Legal English

[Image: Legal texts]

Legal English, also known as legalese when used pejoratively, is the type of English used in legal writing and in the drafting of contracts, statutes, court judgements and other legal documents. It is distinguished from ordinary English by its specialised vocabulary, formal and archaic expressions, Latin and French loanwords, long and complex sentences, and the precise — sometimes idiosyncratic — meanings assigned to common words.

History

The character of legal English reflects the history of English law. In the centuries after the Norman Conquest, court proceedings were conducted in Law French, while records were kept in Latin; many French-derived words survive, such as attorney, plaintiff, defendant, estoppel and tort, along with Latin terms like habeas corpus, bona fide and prima facie. Statutes requiring English in pleadings (notably the Pleading in English Act 1362 and later measures in 1730) shifted drafting into English, but generations of precedent have preserved archaic forms such as hereby, heretofore, witnesseth, and the use of shall to impose obligations.

Characteristics

Key features of legal English include terms of art with fixed meanings; ordinary words used in special senses (for example consideration in contract law); doublets and triplets inherited from multilingual drafting ("null and void", "give, devise and bequeath"); nominalisations; passive constructions; and exhaustive qualification intended to close loopholes. Because law is now practised internationally, legal English functions as a lingua franca of cross-border commerce, and proficiency tests such as the former ILEC examination were developed for non-native speakers working in law.

Plain language movement

Since the twentieth century, reformers in many jurisdictions have promoted plain English in law, arguing that obscure drafting harms consumers and citizens. Consumer-protection and securities legislation in countries such as the United Kingdom, the United States and Australia now often requires documents to be written clearly and intelligibly, and plain-language redrafting of statutes and contracts has become common, though traditional legal English remains pervasive in practice.
