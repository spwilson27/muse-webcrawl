Polysynthetic language

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polysynthetic languages are highly synthetic languages in which words are composed of many morphemes (meaningful components). In such languages, a single verb form can encode information that would require an entire sentence in English — subject, object, tense, aspect, adverbial notions, and even incorporated nouns. The term was popularized by the linguist Peter Stephen Du Ponceau and later Edward Sapir, and forms the extreme end of the morphological typology that runs from analytic (isolating) languages, through agglutinative and fusional synthetic languages, to polysynthetic ones.

Characteristics

Polysynthetic languages typically show a very high morpheme-per-word ratio, extensive agreement marking on the verb for multiple arguments (polypersonal agreement), noun incorporation, and productive derivational processes that let speakers coin complex new words freely. Because verbs carry so much information, word order is often relatively free, and separate pronouns or nouns may be omitted. Distinguishing criteria proposed by Mark Baker include a requirement that every argument of a verb be marked on the verb itself by agreement or incorporation (the "polysynthesis parameter").

Distribution and examples

Polysynthetic languages are found especially among the indigenous languages of the Americas — for example Inuktitut and other Inuit languages, Yupik, Mohawk and other Iroquoian languages, Nahuatl, Mapudungun, and many languages of Amazonia — as well as in Siberia (Chukchi, Koryak), the Caucasus (e.g., Abkhaz), and among Australian Aboriginal languages such as Tiwi and Bininj Kunwok. A famous example is the Inuktitut word tusaatsiarunnanngittualuujunga ("I can't hear very well"), a complete sentence in one word.

Study and endangerment

Polysynthetic morphology poses challenges for linguistic theory, language description, and machine translation, and the languages involved are nearly all endangered as speaker communities shift to majority languages. The "Sapir–Whorf" era of American linguistics devoted particular attention to such languages through the work of Franz Boas and his students.
