Lewis Madison Terman (January 15, 1877 – December 21, 1956) was an American psychologist and author. As a prominent educational psychologist at Stanford University, he was a pioneer in intelligence testing, revising the Binet–Simon scale into the widely used Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales and popularizing the intelligence quotient (IQ) in the United States.

Career

Terman earned his PhD at Clark University in 1905 and joined Stanford's faculty in 1910, chairing its psychology department for two decades. His 1916 revision of Alfred Binet's test introduced William Stern's IQ formula — mental age divided by chronological age, multiplied by 100 — and standardized the test on American schoolchildren. During the First World War he helped develop the Army Alpha and Beta group intelligence tests used to classify nearly two million recruits.

Genetic Studies of Genius

In 1921 Terman began a landmark longitudinal study of more than 1,500 California children with IQs of 135 or above, nicknamed the "Termites". Published in the multi-volume Genetic Studies of Genius, the project followed participants for decades and found that gifted children generally grew into healthy, well-adjusted, and successful adults, challenging the stereotype of the sickly child prodigy. The study also documented biases: teachers nominated the children, missing several high scorers — famously, future Nobel laureates Luis Alvarez and William Shockley were tested but scored below the cutoff.

Criticism

Terman was a prominent member of the American eugenics movement, supporting immigration restriction and the segregation of those he judged intellectually unfit; these views, reflected in his early writings, are now widely condemned and complicate his legacy. Historians also note the self-fulfilling advantages enjoyed by his study's largely white, middle-class sample.
