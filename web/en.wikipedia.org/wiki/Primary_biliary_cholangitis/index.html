Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), previously known as primary biliary cirrhosis, is an autoimmune disease of the liver. It results from the slow, progressive destruction of the small bile ducts of the liver — a chronic non-suppurative destructive cholangitis — causing bile and other toxins to build up in the liver (cholestasis). Over years this can lead to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and liver failure. The name was changed from "cirrhosis" in 2015 because most patients never develop cirrhosis and to reduce stigma.

Signs and symptoms

Many people are asymptomatic at diagnosis and are identified by abnormal liver blood tests. The most characteristic symptoms are profound fatigue (affecting up to 80% of patients) and pruritus (itching), which can be severe. As disease progresses, patients may develop fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, osteoporosis, hypercholesterolemia with xanthelasma, portal hypertension, and eventually jaundice and decompensated cirrhosis. PBC frequently coexists with other autoimmune conditions, particularly Sjögren syndrome, autoimmune thyroid disease, and systemic sclerosis.

Cause and diagnosis

The cause is thought to combine genetic susceptibility with environmental triggers in a loss of immune tolerance to the mitochondrial pyruvate dehydrogenase complex. About 90–95% of patients have anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA) in the blood. Diagnosis generally requires two of three criteria: persistently elevated alkaline phosphatase, presence of AMA (or PBC-specific antinuclear antibodies), and liver biopsy showing destructive cholangitis. The disease predominantly affects women, with a female-to-male ratio around 9:1, usually presenting between ages 40 and 60.

Treatment and prognosis

The first-line treatment is ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), which improves biochemistry and survival, particularly when started early and when alkaline phosphatase normalizes. For patients with an inadequate response, second-line therapies include obeticholic acid and the PPAR agonists elafibranor and seladelpar (approved in 2024); off-label fibrates are also used. Pruritus may be treated with cholestyramine, rifampicin, or naltrexone. Liver transplantation is definitive for end-stage disease, and outcomes after transplant are excellent, although PBC can recur in the graft. With modern therapy, many patients now have a normal life expectancy.
