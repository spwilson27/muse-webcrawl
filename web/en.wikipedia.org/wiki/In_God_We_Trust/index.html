"In God We Trust" (also rendered "In God we trust") is the official motto of the United States, adopted by Act of Congress in 1956, and the state motto of Florida. The phrase first appeared on US coinage during the American Civil War and has appeared on all US coins since 1938; it was added to paper currency beginning in 1957.

History

In 1861, the Reverend Mark R. Watkinson petitioned the Treasury to recognise God on American coinage amid the Civil War. Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase ordered the motto prepared, and "In God We Trust" first appeared on the two-cent piece in 1864. Its wording may echo the final stanza of "The Star-Spangled Banner" ("And this be our motto: 'In God is our trust'").

In 1956, during the Cold War, Congress replaced E pluribus unum as the statutory national motto (though that phrase remains on the Great Seal), and the following year the phrase began appearing on paper money. It is displayed in the chambers of the US Senate and House of Representatives.

Legal controversies

The motto has repeatedly been challenged under the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment. Federal courts have upheld it, treating the phrase as having a secular, ceremonial purpose — a doctrine sometimes called "ceremonial deism" — most notably in Aronow v. United States (1970) and later cases challenging its presence on currency.
