Archē

Archē (Ancient Greek: ἀρχή; sometimes transliterated as arkhē) is a Greek word with primary senses "beginning", "origin", or "source of action" (from ἀρχομαι, "to begin, rule, govern"), and later "first principle" or "element". The concept entered philosophy with the Presocratic thinkers of Miletus, who sought a single underlying substance or principle from which all things arise and to which they return, and it was systematized by Aristotle.

The Milesian archai

Thales of Miletus (c. 624 – c. 548 BC) is traditionally held to have proposed water as the archē of all things. His successor Anaximander replaced water with the apeiron, an indefinite, boundless substrate, reasoning that no single determinate element could generate its opposite. Anaximenes chose air, explaining change by rarefaction and condensation. Later thinkers offered other candidates: Heraclitus fire and measure, the Pythagoreans number, Empedocles the four roots (earth, air, fire, water), and Democritus atoms and void.

Aristotle and later use

In the Metaphysics and Physics, Aristotle defines an archē as the first thing from which something either exists, comes into being, or is known — the starting point of a thing, motion, or inquiry. His doctrine of the four causes generalized the Presocratic search. The word also carried political meaning: rule, office, and sovereignty, from which derive terms such as "monarchy" and "anarchy" (absence of rule). In the Greek New Testament archē opens the Gospel of John in the phrase "In the beginning" (Ἐν ἀρχῇ).

Legacy

The search for an archē marks the beginning of Western philosophy and science: the attempt to explain plurality and change by a rational, unitary principle rather than myth. The concept survives in modern words like "archaic," "archetype," and "architect."
