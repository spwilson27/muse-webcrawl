Danish language

Danish (dansk, pronounced [ˈtænˀsk]) is a North Germanic language spoken by about six million people, principally in Denmark, where it is the official language. It is also spoken in Greenland, the Faroe Islands, and in northern Germany and Danish immigrant communities abroad. It is closely related to Norwegian and Swedish, with which it forms a dialect continuum of mutually intelligible languages.

History

Danish developed from Old East Norse, the eastern branch of Old Norse. During the Middle Ages it diverged from Swedish, underwent extensive phonetic simplification, and absorbed a large Low German vocabulary through Hanseatic trade. The modern standard language is based on the dialects of Copenhagen.

Phonology and grammar

Danish is noted for its large vowel inventory and the stød, a glottal prosody that distinguishes word pairs. Its consonant system has undergone extensive lenition, making spoken Danish difficult for other Scandinavians to parse despite shared vocabulary. Grammatically, Danish has two genders (common and neuter), deflected verbs by tense only, and definite articles suffixed to nouns (e.g. huset, "the house").

Alphabet

Danish uses the Latin alphabet with the additional letters æ, ø and å — 29 letters in total. The letters å (formerly written aa) and ø have been part of the orthography since 1948 spelling reforms.
