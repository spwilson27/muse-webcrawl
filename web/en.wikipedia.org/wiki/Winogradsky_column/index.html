Winogradsky column

Named after the microbiologist Sergei Winogradsky.

Winogradsky column
Type: Laboratory culture device
Inventor: Sergei Winogradsky
Era: 1880s
Used for: Culturing anaerobic and photosynthetic bacteria

The Winogradsky column is a simple device for culturing a large diversity of microorganisms, invented in the 1880s by the Russian microbiologist Sergei Winogradsky. A glass column is filled with pond mud and water supplemented with a carbon source (such as newspaper or egg yolk), a sulfur source (such as gypsum or egg yolk) and calcium carbonate, then left in sunlight for several weeks or months.

Gradients and communities

Over time, gradients of oxygen and sulfide develop from top to bottom. Anaerobic organisms such as Clostridium ferment the cellulose at the bottom, producing organic acids and hydrogen that feed sulfate-reducing bacteria such as Desulfovibrio; the hydrogen sulfide they generate reacts with iron to form black bands and supplies sulfur-oxidizing and purple and green sulfur bacteria, which form strikingly coloured layers. Near the surface, cyanobacteria and algae oxygenate the water and produce organic matter that sustains the whole system, making the column a self-contained miniature ecosystem.

Use

Because it displays microbial succession, biogeochemical cycling of carbon and sulfur, and the dependence of communities on chemical gradients — all in a jar — the Winogradsky column remains a classic demonstration and teaching tool in microbiology and ecology, and a model for studying natural microbial ecosystems such as microbial mats and sediments.
