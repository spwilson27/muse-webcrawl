Sanskrit epic poetry

Sanskrit epic poetry refers to the tradition of epic verse in Sanskrit, whose founding works are the two great itihāsa ("history") epics, the Mahābhārata and the Rāmāyaṇa. Composed and expanded over many centuries, they stand at the head of the classical Sanskrit literary tradition and have profoundly shaped the cultures of South and Southeast Asia.

The two great epics

Mahābhārata

The Mahābhārata, traditionally attributed to the sage Vyāsa, is one of the longest poems ever written — roughly 100,000 ślokas in its critical edition. It narrates the dynastic struggle between the Pāṇḍava and Kaurava cousins, culminating in the Kurukṣetra War, and embeds vast didactic, legal and philosophical material, including the Bhagavad Gītā.

Rāmāyaṇa

The Rāmāyaṇa, attributed to Vālmīki — honoured as the ādikavi, "first poet" — consists of about 24,000 ślokas telling of Prince Rāma's exile, the abduction of his wife Sītā by the demon-king Rāvaṇa, and his victorious return. Its polished style marks it as the first kāvya (courtly poem).

Later court epics

The epics founded a tradition of ornate court poetry, the mahākāvya ("great poem"), perfected by Kālidāsa (Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasambhava), Bhāravi, Māgha and others. Sanskrit epics also travelled: versions of the Rāma story spread throughout Southeast Asia, and Sanskrit poetic conventions influenced regional literatures across India, including Tamil poets such as Kampaṉ, author of a Tamil Rāmāyaṇa.
