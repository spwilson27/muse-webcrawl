William Tecumseh Sherman

William Tecumseh Sherman (February 8, 1820 – February 14, 1891) was an American soldier, businessman, educator, and author. He served as a general in the Union Army during the American Civil War (1861–1865), earning recognition for his command of military strategy as well as infamy for the harshness of his scorched-earth policies, which he defended as necessary to end the war.

Early life and career

Sherman was born in Lancaster, Ohio. After his father's early death he was raised by Senator Thomas Ewing. He graduated from West Point in 1840, served in Florida and California, and resigned in 1853 for banking and business ventures that included running a military academy in Louisiana.

Civil War

Returning to the army in 1861, Sherman fought at First Bull Run and Shiloh, and served under Ulysses S. Grant at Vicksburg. Succeeding Grant in command of the Military Division of the Mississippi in 1864, he captured Atlanta, then launched his famous March to the Sea (November–December 1864) to Savannah, Georgia, waging "total war" against Confederate infrastructure and supplies. In 1865 he marched north through the Carolinas and accepted Joseph E. Johnston's surrender in North Carolina in April 1865.

Post-war

Sherman succeeded Grant as Commanding General of the United States Army (1869–1883), overseeing campaigns against Plains Indian nations in the West. Famous for despising politics, he flatly refused a presidential candidacy in 1884: "I will not accept if nominated and will not serve if elected." His Memoirs (1875) remain a classic of American military literature. He died in New York City on 14 February 1891.
