Prediction market

Prediction markets, also known as betting markets, information markets, decision markets, idea futures or event derivatives, are open markets that enable the trading of the outcome of events. The market prices can indicate what the crowd thinks the probability of the event is. A prediction market contract that trades between 0% and 100% is a binary option that expires at the price of 0 or 100%. Research suggests that prediction markets are at least as accurate as other institutions predicting the same events with a similar pool of participants.

Mechanism

Participants buy and sell contracts whose payoff depends on the outcome of a future event — for example, whether a candidate wins an election, or whether a product ships by a given date. If a contract pays 100 units if the event occurs, its current price can be read as the market's implied probability. Because traders profit by correcting prices, they are incentivised to research and reveal information, aggregating dispersed knowledge into the price in a way related to the efficient-market hypothesis; market makers or automated scoring rules can provide liquidity.

History and examples

Informal political betting markets existed by the 19th century. The modern academic field began with the Iowa Electronic Markets, run by the University of Iowa since 1988 for research on election forecasting, which have often outperformed opinion polls in predicting US presidential vote shares. The (cancelled) Policy Analysis Market proposed by DARPA in 2003, intended to forecast geopolitical events, was abandoned amid controversy over "terrorism futures". Commercial and crypto-based platforms have included Intrade (closed 2013), PredictIt (launched 2014 under a no-action letter from the CFTC), the event-contract exchange Kalshi (regulated by the CFTC from 2020; its election markets were allowed after a 2024 court ruling), and Polymarket (founded 2020), which saw very large volumes during the 2024 US election cycle. Companies have also run internal prediction markets to forecast sales, deadlines and product success.

Accuracy and research

Studies by economists such as Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz find prediction markets generally well calibrated and often more accurate than polls or expert panels, though they can suffer from thin trading, manipulation attempts (rarely successful for long), favourite–longshot bias and feedback effects when forecasts influence the events forecast.

Legal status

Real-money prediction markets face gambling and derivatives regulation that varies by country. In the United States, most operate under Commodity Futures Trading Commission oversight or no-action relief, while offshore and crypto platforms are restricted for US users; elsewhere they may be treated as gambling or prohibited. Play-money markets are widely legal.
