Trace fossil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trace fossil, also known as an ichnofossil, is a fossil record of biological activity by an organism but not the preserved remains of the organism itself. Trace fossils contrast with body fossils, which are the fossilised remains of parts of organisms' bodies, usually altered by later geological activity. The study of traces is called ichnology, and trace fossils receive their own taxonomic names (ichnotaxa) based on their morphology, since one organism can produce many different traces and one trace form can be made by many different organisms.

Types

Trace fossils include burrows, borings, footprints and trackways, feeding marks, root cavities, coprolites (fossil dung), and structures such as nests. Common ichnogenera include Chondrites (small branching burrows), Skolithos (simple vertical tubes), Thalassinoides (branching burrow systems made by crustaceans), Rusophycus (resting traces of trilobites) and Cruziana (trilobite walking and feeding trails). Dinosaur footprints are among the most famous trace fossils, and sites such as Dinosaur State Park in Connecticut preserve thousands of them.

Behavioural classification

In the 1950s Adolf Seilacher proposed classifying traces by the behaviour that produced them: domichnia (dwelling structures), fodinichnia (feeding burrows), pascichnia (grazing trails), cubichnia (resting traces), repichnia (locomotion traces) and fugichnia (escape structures). Later additions include praedichnia (predation traces) and agrichnia (farming or trapping structures). Traces can also be grouped into ichnofacies — recurring assemblages characteristic of particular environments, such as the Skolithos ichnofacies of high-energy shallow seas and the Nereites ichnofacies of deep-sea fans.

Importance

Trace fossils record behaviour rather than anatomy, so they often reveal what body fossils cannot: how fast an animal moved, whether it travelled in herds, how it fed, and what environments it inhabited. Because they are almost always found where they were made (unlike shells or bones, which may be transported), trace fossils are excellent indicators of ancient environments, water depth and oxygen levels. Traces also extend the fossil record: soft-bodied organisms that rarely fossilise leave traces, and the earliest evidence of animal life in the Ediacaran and early Cambrian periods consists largely of burrows and trails. The marked increase in burrowing complexity across the Precambrian–Cambrian boundary is a key marker of the Cambrian explosion.

Interpretation difficulties

Because trace makers are rarely preserved with their traces, the producer of a given ichnotaxon is often uncertain or known only by inference from modern analogues. The same animal also makes different traces when behaving differently or moving through different substrates, and different animals may produce nearly identical traces, which is why ichnotaxonomy is deliberately separate from biological taxonomy.
