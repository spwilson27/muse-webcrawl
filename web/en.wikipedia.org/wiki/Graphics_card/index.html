Graphics card

A modern discrete graphics card, designed for a PCIe x16 slot.

A graphics card (also called a video card, display card, graphics adapter, or GPU) is a computer expansion card that generates a feed of graphics output to a display device such as a monitor. Graphics cards are sometimes called discrete or dedicated graphics cards to emphasize their distinction from integrated graphics processors built into the CPU or chipset.

Components

At the heart of the card is the graphics processing unit (GPU), which performs the calculations needed to render images, video and 3D scenes. The card also carries dedicated video memory (VRAM, typically GDDR6 or GDDR7 in current models), a cooling solution of heatsinks and fans, power connectors, and output ports such as HDMI and DisplayPort. Modern cards connect to the motherboard through a PCI Express x16 slot.

History

Early personal computers used simple display adapters such as IBM's MDA (1981) and CGA, followed by EGA and VGA standards. In the 1990s, 2D GUI acceleration and then 3D acceleration (pioneered by 3dfx's Voodoo Graphics in 1996) transformed the market. NVIDIA coined the term "GPU" with the GeForce 256 in 1999. Today the discrete GPU market is led by NVIDIA and AMD, with Intel entering with its Arc line in 2022.

Uses

Beyond video games, graphics cards are used for video editing, 3D rendering, scientific computing and machine learning — their massively parallel architecture suits tensor and matrix computation, making high-end GPUs the principal engines of modern AI training. They were also widely used for cryptocurrency mining, which caused major price surges in 2017–2018 and 2020–2022.
