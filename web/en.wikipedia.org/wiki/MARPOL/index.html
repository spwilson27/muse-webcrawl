The International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships, 1973, as modified by the Protocol of 1978 (MARPOL 73/78, MARPOL being short for marine pollution and 73/78 short for the years 1973 and 1978) is one of the most important international marine environmental conventions. It was developed by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) with the objective of minimizing pollution of the oceans and seas, including dumping, oil and air pollution.

The original MARPOL convention was signed on 17 February 1973, but did not come into force. The combination of the 1973 Convention and the 1978 Protocol is referred to as MARPOL 73/78, and it entered into force on 2 October 1983. As of recent years, around 160 states, representing more than 98 per cent of the world's shipping tonnage, are parties to the convention.

Provisions

MARPOL is divided into regulations aimed at preventing and minimizing pollution from ships — both accidental pollution and that from routine operations — and currently includes six technical annexes. Special Areas with particularly strict discharge controls are designated under several annexes.

AnnexSubject
IPrevention of pollution by oil (entered into force 1983)
IIControl of pollution by noxious liquid substances in bulk

VIPrevention of air pollution from ships (entered into force 2005)

Annex I requires oil tankers to be fitted with double hulls, regulates discharges of oily water through approved equipment such as oily-water separators, and requires ships to keep an Oil Record Book. Annex V prohibits the discharge of all plastics into the sea. Annex VI limits sulfur oxide and nitrogen oxide emissions from ship exhausts and led to the global 0.50% sulfur cap on marine fuels that took effect on 1 January 2020, often called "IMO 2020".

Implementation and enforcement

Flag states are responsible for certifying that their ships comply, issuing certificates such as the International Oil Pollution Prevention (IOPP) Certificate. Port states may inspect foreign ships under port state control regimes to verify compliance, detain deficient vessels, and prosecute illegal discharges — notably deliberate bypassing of pollution-control equipment using a so-called "magic pipe", which has resulted in substantial criminal penalties for shipping companies in the United States and elsewhere.

Amendments

The convention is amended through the IMO's Marine Environment Protection Committee, generally under a tacit-acceptance procedure by which amendments enter into force unless objected to by a specified proportion of parties. Notable amendments include the phasing-out of single-hull tankers accelerated after the Erika and Prestige spills, energy-efficiency requirements for ships (EEDI and EEXI), and the designation of emission control areas in the Baltic Sea, North Sea, North American and United States Caribbean waters.
