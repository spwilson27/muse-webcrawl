Slate is a fine-grained, foliated metamorphic rock formed from an original shale-type sedimentary rock composed of clay or volcanic ash through low-grade regional metamorphism. It is the finest-grained foliated metamorphic rock. Its defining property is slaty cleavage: the alignment of microscopic platy minerals under directed pressure, which allows the rock to be split into thin, durable sheets quite distinct from the original bedding of the parent shale.

Formation and mineralogy

Slate forms when shale or mudstone is subjected to the heat and, above all, the compressive stress of mountain building. Clay minerals recrystallize into tiny flakes of muscovite (sericite) and chlorite aligned perpendicular to the direction of maximum compression; quartz is the other main constituent, with smaller amounts of hematite, pyrite, and other minerals. Colors range from the familiar blue-grey and black through green, purple, and red, depending on iron oxidation state and mineral content. With higher metamorphic grade, slate grades into phyllite and then schist.

Uses

Because it splits into smooth, waterproof, and durable sheets, slate has been quarried for centuries for roofing tiles, flooring, and flagstones. Historically it was the standard material for school writing slates and blackboards, and it is still used for billiard-table beds, electrical panels (historically), memorials, and laboratory bench tops. Famous producing regions include North Wales (once the world's largest source, centered on Blaenau Ffestiniog and Bethesda), the Slate Valley of Vermont and New York, Spain (today the largest exporter of roofing slate), and parts of Germany, France, and China.

Quarrying

Slate is extracted in open quarries or underground mines, then split by hand or machine along the cleavage and trimmed to size. The Welsh slate landscape of northwest Wales was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021 in recognition of its industrial heritage.
