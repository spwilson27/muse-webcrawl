Hardy–Littlewood circle method

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the Hardy–Littlewood circle method is a technique in analytic number theory. It is named for G. H. Hardy and John Edensor Littlewood, who developed it in a series of papers on Waring's problem beginning in 1920.

Idea of the method

The number of representations of an integer as a sum of powers (or of primes) is extracted, via Cauchy's integral formula, as an integral of a generating function over the unit circle. The circle is divided into "major arcs" — neighbourhoods of rational points with small denominators, where the generating function can be approximated precisely and yields the predicted main term — and "minor arcs," where one must show the contribution is comparatively negligible. This major/minor arc decomposition remains the template for the method.

Applications

The circle method has been applied to Waring's problem, to the ternary Goldbach problem (Vinogradov's theorem that every large odd number is a sum of three primes), and, in the work of Harald Helfgott, to the complete proof of the weak Goldbach conjecture. A variant by Kloostermann and later refinements are central to modern analytic number theory.
