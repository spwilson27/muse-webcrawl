Crucifix

A crucifix (from the Latin cruci fixus meaning "(one) fixed to a cross") is an image of Jesus on the cross, as distinct from a bare cross. The representation of Jesus himself on the cross is referred to in English as the corpus (Latin for "body").

Usage

The crucifix is a principal symbol for many groups of Christians, and one of the most common forms of the crucifixion in the arts. It is especially important within the Catholic Church, but is also used in the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Assyrian Church of the East, and Lutheran churches, as well as in some Anglican and Methodist churches. Most Reformed, evangelical and nondenominational churches generally prefer to use a cross without the corpus, emphasising the resurrection rather than the death of Jesus.

History

Crucifixes were used as devotional objects from the early Middle Ages; early examples include the Gero Cross of Cologne Cathedral (c. 965–970), one of the oldest large-scale depictions of the crucified Christ. In Catholic tradition, crucifixes are carried in processions, displayed in churches and homes, and worn as pendants or on rosaries. A crucifix placed before a dying person, and the use of a small crucifix in confession, are long-standing devotional practices.

Related forms

Related forms include the crucifix with a skull, cross with corpus in various styles, and regional variants such as the San Damiano cross of Assisi and the "Christus Rex" depicting Christ reigning from the cross.
