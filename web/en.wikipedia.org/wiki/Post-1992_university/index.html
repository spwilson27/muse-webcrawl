Post-1992 university

A typical post-1992 university campus in the United Kingdom.

A post-1992 university, also known as a new university or modern university, is a British university that was granted university status in or after 1992. The term derives from the Further and Higher Education Act 1992, which abolished the binary divide between universities and polytechnics in England, Scotland and Wales, allowing the polytechnics and a number of other higher education institutions to adopt the title of university.

Background

Before 1992, British higher education was divided into two tiers: the universities, which awarded their own degrees, and the polytechnics and colleges, whose degrees were validated by the Council for National Academic Awards (CNAA). The Further and Higher Education Act 1992, introduced by the Conservative government of John Major, dissolved the CNAA and permitted polytechnics to award their own degrees under the university title. Thirty-five polytechnics became universities in 1992 alone, including institutions in Birmingham, Coventry, Leeds, Manchester, Middlesex, Northumbria, Portsmouth, Sheffield, Teesside and the University of the West of England.

Later conversions

The Further and Higher Education Act's provisions and subsequent legislation allowed further institutions to gain university status. University colleges that met criteria on student numbers and taught degree awarding powers could apply, and the requirement to conduct research was relaxed in 2004. Institutions such as the University of Northampton, the University of Winchester and a number of former teacher-training colleges joined the sector in the 2000s and 2010s.

Characteristics and debate

Post-1992 universities have often been characterised by a stronger vocational orientation, larger proportions of part-time, mature and local students, and a teaching-led mission compared with older research-intensive universities. The grouping is sometimes contrasted with the Russell Group of research-intensive universities, although individual post-1992 universities perform strongly in particular subjects and in measures of teaching quality. Many are represented by the Million+ and University Alliance mission groups. Critics of the "new/old" terminology argue that it reinforces an outdated hierarchy, while supporters note differences in funding, research income and entry tariffs that remain statistically significant.
