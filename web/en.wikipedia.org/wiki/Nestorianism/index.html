Nestorianism

Nestorianism is a term used in Christian theology and Church history to refer to several mutually related but doctrinally distinct sets of teachings regarding the person of Jesus Christ. The teachings were first attributed to the fifth-century Patriarch of Constantinople Nestorius (c. 386–450), whose writings and doctrine became the centre of controversy. Opponents, led by Cyril of Alexandria, charged that the Antiochene emphasis on distinguishing the full humanity and full divinity of Christ amounted to teaching that Jesus was two persons. Nestorianism was condemned at the Council of Ephesus in 431, and again at the Council of Chalcedon in 451.

The term is often associated, frequently in a pejorative way, with the Church of the East in Persia, whose Christology developed along Antiochene lines. Modern scholarship generally holds that many of Nestorius's actual views were closer to orthodoxy than his accusers allowed, and the Church of the East has historically rejected the label as a mischaracterisation of its faith.

History

Nestorius and the controversy

Nestorius, a monk from Antioch, became Patriarch of Constantinople in 428. He preached against the use of the title Theotokos ("God-bearer", "Mother of God") for the Virgin Mary, arguing that Christotokos ("Christ-bearer") was more appropriate, since Mary bore the human nature of Christ rather than his divine nature. The dispute quickly became entangled in the rivalry between the sees of Alexandria and Constantinople. Cyril of Alexandria circulated his Twelve Anathemas against Nestorius, and the emperor Theodosius II convened a council at Ephesus in 431.

Council of Ephesus

At Ephesus, Cyril's party opened the council before the Antiochene bishops arrived, deposed Nestorius, and condemned his teaching. The late-arriving party, led by John of Antioch, held a rival session deposing Cyril. A formula of reunion was reached in 433, but the controversy did not end. Nestorius was eventually exiled, first to a monastery and then to the Egyptian desert, where he wrote his defence, the Bazaar of Heracleides.

The Church of the East

In the Sasanian Empire, the Church of the East adopted the theology of Theodore of Mopsuestia and the Antiochene tradition. Cut off from the Roman Empire by political frontiers and mutual suspicion, it developed independently, sending missionaries along the Silk Road to India, Central Asia and China — a mission famously recorded on the Nestorian Stele erected at Xi'an in 781. The 1994 Common Christological Declaration signed by Pope John Paul II and Patriarch Dinkha IV clarified that the historic differences were largely terminological.

Doctrine

The essential issue was the union of the divine and human natures in Christ. Nestorius and the Antiochene school stressed the integrity of each nature (Greek physis) and spoke of a union in one prosopon (person or "face"). Alexandrian theology, following Cyril, emphasised the single subject of the incarnation: the eternal Word himself, hence "one incarnate nature of God the Word" (mia physis). The Chalcedonian Definition of 451 — Christ in two natures, "without confusion, change, division or separation" — became the standard of orthodoxy for the Roman and Byzantine churches, but was not accepted by either the Church of the East or the Oriental Orthodox churches.
