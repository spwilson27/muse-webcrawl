The peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus), known simply as the peregrine and historically as the duck hawk in North America, is a cosmopolitan bird of prey (raptor) in the family Falconidae. A large, crow-sized falcon, it has a blue-grey back, barred white underparts, and a black head. The peregrine is renowned for its speed: it can reach over 320 km/h (200 mph) during its characteristic hunting stoop (high-speed dive), making it the fastest animal on the planet.

Description

The peregrine falcon has a body length of 34 to 58 cm and a wingspan from 74 to 120 cm. Males and females have similar markings and plumage, but as with many birds of prey, the female is up to 30% larger than the male. The back and wings of adults are usually bluish black to slate grey with indistinct darker barring; the underparts are white to rusty with thin dark bars. The head is black with a characteristic broad, black "moustache" mark contrasting with the white cheeks and throat.

Distribution and habitat

The peregrine is one of the most widely distributed birds in the world, breeding on every continent except Antarctica. It inhabits a wide range of environments, from Arctic tundra to tropical coasts, though it generally avoids dense rainforest and very dry deserts. Northern populations are strongly migratory — the name peregrinus means "wanderer" — while southern populations are resident. Natural nest sites are typically cliff ledges, and in many areas peregrines now nest on tall buildings, bridges, and other structures in cities, where they feed on abundant pigeons.

Hunting and diet

Peregrines feed almost exclusively on medium-sized birds, which they catch in flight, striking with a clenched foot after a steep stoop from great height. They also take small mammals, and occasionally insects and reptiles. Pairs may hunt cooperatively, and the male frequently brings food to the female during courtship and incubation.

Reproduction

Peregrines mate for life and return to the same breeding territory each year. The female lays three to five (usually four) eggs in a simple scrape on a cliff ledge or in an artificial nest site; incubation, mainly by the female, lasts about a month, and the young fledge at 42 to 46 days. Mortality of first-year birds is high, but adults may live for more than 15 years in the wild.

Decline and recovery

The species declined dramatically in many regions during the mid-20th century because of organochlorine pesticides, especially DDT, which caused eggshell thinning and widespread breeding failure; the peregrine was extirpated as a breeding bird from the eastern United States. Bans on DDT in the 1970s, captive breeding, and large-scale reintroduction programs produced one of the most celebrated conservation recoveries. The peregrine was removed from the United States endangered species list in 1999, and it is globally listed as Least Concern.

Falconry

The peregrine has been used in falconry for more than 3,000 years and remains the classic falconer's bird, valued for its stoop and its willingness to wait on high above the falconer. Captive-bred peregrines and peregrine hybrids are widely used for hunting and for bird-abatement work at airports and farms.
