Giant otter

The giant otter or giant river otter (Pteronura brasiliensis) is a South American carnivorous mammal. It is the longest member of the weasel family, Mustelidae, a globally successful group of predators, reaching up to 1.8 m (5.9 ft). Atypical of mustelids, the giant otter is a social species, with family groups typically supporting three to eight members.

Kingdom: Animalia
Family: Mustelidae
Genus: Pteronura
Species: P. brasiliensis
Conservation status: Endangered

The groups are centered on a dominant breeding pair and are extremely cohesive and cooperative. The species is also known for its noisy vocal repertoire, with at least nine distinct calls identified, used for alarms, greetings, and coordination while hunting fish.

Distribution and habitat

The giant otter lives mainly in and along the Amazon River and in the Pantanal wetlands. Its range once covered much of north-central South America, but poaching for its velvety pelt in the mid-20th century drastically reduced populations. It has been listed as endangered since the 1990s, and remaining populations are threatened by habitat degradation, mercury pollution from gold mining, and disease from domestic animals.

Diet and behaviour

Giant otters feed almost exclusively on fish, particularly characins and catfish, supplementing their diet with small caimans and snakes when fish are scarce. Family groups maintain communal latrines and campsites along riverbanks, reinforcing social bonds through grooming and play. An individual may eat 3–4 kg (7–9 lb) of fish daily.
