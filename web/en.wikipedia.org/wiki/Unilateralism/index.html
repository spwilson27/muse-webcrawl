Unilateralism

Unilateralism is any doctrine or agenda that supports one-sided action. Such action may be in disregard for other parties, or as an expression of a commitment toward a direction which other parties may find disagreeable. In brief, unilateralism is a tendency or policy to conduct foreign affairs in a way that minimises consultation with allies, partners or international institutions, in contrast to multilateralism, which favours coordinated action.

In foreign policy

Kofi Annan, the former UN Secretary-General, warned that no nation can build its security by itself; advocates like Hans_Morgenthau emphasised power realities, but the term "unilateralism" is most often used critically, to describe a powerful state acting without broad international support or legal authorisation. Frequently cited modern examples involving the United States include the rejection of the Kyoto Protocol, withdrawal from the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty in 2002, and — most prominently — the 2003 invasion of Iraq without an explicit authorising UN Security Council resolution, which France, Germany and others denounced as unilateral. Defenders of such policies reply that multilateral approval can paralyse necessary action, that sovereignty permits a state to defend itself as it judges best, and that coalitions in practice often follow rather than precede a determined lead state.

Other forms

  • Unilateral disarmament — renouncing weapons, especially nuclear weapons, without waiting for reciprocal concessions; championed in Britain by the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament.
  • Unilateral declaration of independence — a territory's proclamation of statehood without the consent of the state from which it secedes, as by Rhodesia in 1965 and Kosovo in 2008.
  • Unilateral trade measures — tariffs or sanctions imposed by one country on its own authority.
