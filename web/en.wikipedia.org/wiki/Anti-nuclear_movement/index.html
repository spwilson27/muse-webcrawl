Anti-nuclear movement

The anti-nuclear movement is a social movement that opposes various nuclear technologies. Some direct action groups, environmental movements, and professional organisations have identified themselves with the movement at the local, national, or international level. Major anti-nuclear groups include the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, Friends of the Earth, Greenpeace, the International Physicians for the Prevention of Nuclear War, and the Nuclear Information and Resource Service.

History

The movement began in the aftermath of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945 and grew during the 1950s in response to atmospheric nuclear weapons testing. The 1958 founding of the Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament in Britain and its Aldermaston marches became emblematic of the era. From the 1970s, opposition increasingly focused on nuclear power, galvanised by the Three Mile Island accident in 1979 and the Chernobyl disaster in 1986.

Nuclear power opposition

Critics of nuclear power cite risks of reactor accidents, the unresolved problem of long-lived radioactive waste, proliferation concerns, and high costs relative to alternatives. Large protests were held against plants in Germany, France, the United States, and elsewhere; in Germany the movement helped bring about a national phase-out of nuclear power, completed in 2023. The Fukushima Daiichi accident in 2011 revived anti-nuclear activism worldwide, particularly in Japan and Germany.

Nuclear weapons opposition

Disarmament campaigns peaked again in the early 1980s with mass demonstrations against the deployment of cruise and Pershing II missiles in Europe, including the Greenham Common Women's Peace Camp in England. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, adopted in 2017 and championed by the International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons (ICAN), which received the Nobel Peace Prize that year, reflects the movement's humanitarian framing.
