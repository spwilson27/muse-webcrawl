Law of definite proportions

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In chemistry, the law of definite proportions, sometimes called Proust's law or the law of constant composition, states that a given chemical compound always contains its component elements in fixed ratio by mass, and does not depend on its source or method of preparation. For example, in any sample of pure water, about 8/9 of the mass consists of oxygen and 1/9 of hydrogen; the ratio of the mass of oxygen to the mass of hydrogen is therefore about 8 to 1.

History

The law was formulated by the French chemist Joseph Proust on the basis of experiments conducted between 1797 and 1804, chiefly on copper carbonate and the oxides of tin and iron. Proust's conclusion was disputed by Claude Louis Berthollet, who argued that elements could combine in continuously varying proportions; Proust's position prevailed and became, together with the law of multiple proportions, key evidence for John Dalton's atomic theory.

Non-stoichiometric compounds

Some compounds, known as non-stoichiometric or berthollide compounds, do not obey the law: their composition can vary over a range because of defects in their crystal structures. Examples include wüstite (iron(II) oxide), whose iron-to-oxygen ratio varies appreciably. Large molecules such as polymers also need not have exactly fixed composition.
