Vipassanā

Vipassanā (Pāli) or vipaśyanā (Sanskrit) literally means "insight" or "clear-seeing"—in Buddhism, insight into the true nature of reality, namely the three marks of existence: impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and non-self (anattā). Together with samatha (calm-abiding), it is one of the two main categories of Buddhist meditation.

Meditators practicing at a Vipassanā retreat of the kind popularized by S. N. Goenka.

Practice

Vipassanā practice typically develops mindfulness (sati) of body, feelings, mind, and phenomena, as set out in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta. Practitioners observe sensations and mental events as they arise and pass, cultivating equanimity and progressively deeper stages of insight described in the commentaries as the "progress of insight" (vipassanā-ñāṇa), culminating in the realization of nirvana. Methods include noting physical sensations, as in Burma's Mahasi tradition, body-scanning, and breath awareness.

Modern movements

The modern "Vipassanā movement" arose in 19th–20th century Burma, Sri Lanka, and Thailand, where teachers such as Ledi Sayadaw, Mahasi Sayadaw, and U Ba Khin opened intensive meditation training to laypeople. U Ba Khin's student S. N. Goenka spread a secularized ten-day retreat format worldwide from 1969. In the West, teachers including Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg, and Jack Kornfield founded the Insight Meditation Society (1975), and Jon Kabat-Zinn adapted mindfulness practices into the clinical Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction program, feeding the broader modern mindfulness movement.
