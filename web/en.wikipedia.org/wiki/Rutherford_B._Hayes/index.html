Rutherford Birchard Hayes (October 4, 1822 – January 17, 1893) was the 19th president of the United States, serving from 1877 to 1881. A lawyer and staunch abolitionist from Ohio, he served in the Union Army and the House of Representatives before becoming governor of Ohio. His presidency followed one of the most contentious elections in American history and saw the end of Reconstruction.

Early life and career

Hayes was born in Delaware, Ohio, educated at Kenyon College and Harvard Law School, and practised law in Cincinnati, where he defended fugitive slaves in court. In the Civil War he rose to brevet major general, serving with the 23rd Ohio Infantry — whose ranks also included future president William McKinley — and was wounded several times. Elected to Congress in 1864, he supported Radical Republican Reconstruction measures. He served as governor of Ohio from 1868 to 1872 and again from 1876, building a reputation for honesty in an era of corruption.

Election of 1876

As the Republican nominee in 1876, Hayes faced Democrat Samuel J. Tilden, who won the popular vote and led in the Electoral College, but returns from Florida, Louisiana and South Carolina were disputed. A congressionally appointed Electoral Commission awarded all the disputed electors to Hayes, giving him victory by a single electoral vote, 185–184. The informal Compromise of 1877 that accompanied the outcome committed Hayes to withdraw the remaining federal troops from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction.

Presidency

True to his pledge, Hayes served one term only. He withdrew federal troops from Southern statehouses in 1877, allowing the last Reconstruction governments to fall. Domestically he confronted the Great Railroad Strike of 1877, using federal troops to restore order; pursued civil-service reform against the spoils system, clashing with Senator Roscoe Conkling's Stalwart faction; and defended the gold standard against Greenback and free-silver movements. His administration also began modest efforts to protect the rights of African Americans in the South, largely through moral suasion, with limited effect.

Later life

Hayes retired to Spiegel Grove in Fremont, Ohio, devoting himself to philanthropy, education and prison reform. He died in 1893. His home later became the site of the first presidential library in the United States.
