House price index

A house price index (HPI) measures the price changes of residential housing over time, typically as a percentage change from a base period. Because houses differ in size, quality and location, statisticians use methods such as repeat-sales regression or hedonic pricing to isolate pure price change from changes in the mix of properties sold.

Major indices

In the United States, the best-known measures are the S&P CoreLogic Case–Shiller indices, developed by economists Karl Case and Robert Shiller using repeat sales of single-family homes, and the FHFA House Price Index, compiled from mortgages purchased or securitized by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. In the United Kingdom, indices are published by Nationwide, Halifax, and the Office for National Statistics; other countries maintain similar series through national statistics offices and central banks.

Methods

The repeat-sales method compares successive sale prices of the same property, controlling for quality by construction but suffering from small samples and selection bias toward frequently traded homes. The hedonic method regresses price on property characteristics (floor area, bedrooms, location) and estimates the value of a standardized dwelling. Appraisal-based and simple median-price measures are also used, though medians are distorted by changes in the composition of sales.

Uses

House price indices inform monetary policy, financial stability monitoring, mortgage underwriting and real estate investment. Sustained rapid growth in an index is often watched as a sign of a housing bubble, as in the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis, when the Case–Shiller national index fell sharply from its 2006 peak.
