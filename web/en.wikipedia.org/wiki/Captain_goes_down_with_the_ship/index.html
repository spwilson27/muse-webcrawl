The captain goes down with the ship

"The captain goes down with the ship"
TypeMaritime tradition / ethical principle
Origin19th-century British maritime culture
Related conceptWomen and children first

"The captain goes down with the ship" is a maritime tradition and saying holding that the captain of a ship holds ultimate responsibility for the vessel and everyone embarked on it, and in an emergency should either save them or die trying. Closely associated with the tradition is the expectation that the captain will be the last person to leave a sinking ship, or will remain aboard entirely.

History

The tradition crystallised in 19th-century Britain alongside the "women and children first" protocol famously observed during the wreck of HMS Birkenhead in 1852. It is not generally a requirement of maritime law, which instead obliges captains to exercise care for passengers and crew and to use their best judgement; some jurisdictions, however, penalise a captain who abandons ship prematurely.

Notable examples

Captain Edward Smith of RMS Titanic (1912) is the most famous embodiment of the tradition, dying with his ship after overseeing the evacuation. Captain William John Rogers of the Ocean Monarch and numerous naval commanders in both world wars similarly stayed aboard. Conversely, Captain Francesco Schettino's early abandonment of the Costa Concordia in 2012 drew worldwide condemnation and a criminal conviction, and Yiannis Avranas was criticised for leaving the cruise ship Oceanos in 1991 before all passengers were rescued.

By extension

The phrase is used metaphorically of leaders in business, politics and aviation who accept responsibility for failure rather than abandoning those who depend on them.
