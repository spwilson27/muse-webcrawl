The Critique of Practical Reason (German: Kritik der praktischen Vernunft), published in 1788, is the second of Immanuel Kant's three Critiques and his principal work of moral philosophy. Where the first Critique examined reason in its theoretical use, this work examines reason as it determines the will — "practical" reason — and defends the reality of moral freedom.

Summary

Kant argues that pure reason can be practical: the moral law, expressed in the categorical imperative developed in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), binds rational beings independently of their desires. Moral worth lies in acting from duty and respect for the law, and the autonomy of the rational will — its giving the law to itself — is the supreme principle of morality.

The book's first part, the Analytic, establishes the moral law as a "fact of reason" and deduces freedom from it: we know ourselves free because we know the moral law. Its Dialectic treats the highest good (virtue combined with proportionate happiness) and argues that morality leads to the "postulates of pure practical reason" — freedom, the immortality of the soul, and the existence of God — which cannot be known theoretically but must be assumed from the practical standpoint. The famous conclusion declares: "Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe... the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me."

Influence

The second Critique is the fullest statement of Kant's ethics and a founding text of deontological moral theory, influencing later thinkers from Fichte and Hegel to Rawls and contemporary Kantians. Its doctrine of the postulates shaped subsequent philosophy of religion and debates about faith and reason.
