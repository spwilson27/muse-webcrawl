Sewn boat

A sewn boat is a type of wooden boat in which the hull planks are held together by stitching, sewing or lacing them with fibre cordage — such as coir, palm fibre or sinew — rather than by metal fasteners or wooden pegs. The planking is usually edge-joined (carvel) and the seams caulked with fibre and resin or oil. Sewn construction was used for thousands of years in regions where iron was scarce or where flexible hulls were advantageous.

Distribution and history

Sewn boats are best known from the Indian Ocean, where Arab and Indian dhows — including the Omani boom and the Maldivian dhoni — were sewn with coir cordage well into the modern era. Marco Polo remarked on the sewn ships of Hormuz, and the 9th-century Belitung shipwreck, an Arab-style sewn vessel found off Indonesia carrying Tang dynasty cargo, demonstrates the technique's antiquity. The ancient Egyptian "Abydos boats" (c. 3000 BC) are among the earliest surviving examples.

In northern Europe, sewn construction appears in Bronze Age Britain in the Ferriby boats from the Humber estuary and the Dover Bronze Age boat (c. 1550 BC), whose oak planks were stitched with yew withies. In the Pacific, the lashed-lug construction of Austronesian outrigger canoes is a related technology, and in North America the birchbark canoes of Algonquian peoples were sewn with spruce root.

Advantages

A sewn hull is flexible and can survive groundings and surf landings that would damage a rigidly fastened boat; repairs require no metal or specialist tools, only fibre cordage. Its disadvantages are the need for periodic re-stitching and relatively lower structural strength, which limited the size of sewn vessels.
