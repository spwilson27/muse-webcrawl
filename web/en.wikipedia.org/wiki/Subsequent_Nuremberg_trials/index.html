The subsequent Nuremberg trials (formally the Trials of War Criminals before the Nuremberg Military Tribunals) were a series of twelve military tribunals for war crimes conducted by the United States in Nuremberg, Germany, after World War II, between December 1946 and April 1949. They followed the better-known trial of the major war criminals before the International Military Tribunal (IMT), which concluded in October 1946.

Unlike the IMT trial, the subsequent trials were held before United States military courts rather than an international tribunal, under Control Council Law No. 10. The defendants were 177 members of the political, military, medical, industrial, legal and ministerial elite of Nazi Germany, charged with war crimes, crimes against humanity, and crimes against peace in connection with the Holocaust, slave labour programmes, medical experiments, and the machinery of aggression.

Background

The four Allied powers had agreed that leading Nazis not tried by the IMT should face further proceedings. The United States appointed Brigadier General Telford Taylor as Chief of Counsel for War Crimes, succeeding Robert H. Jackson. Because the other occupying powers showed limited enthusiasm for further international trials, the Americans proceeded alone under the authority of the Allied Control Council.

The twelve trials

  1. Doctors' Trial (United States v. Karl Brandt, et al.) — 23 physicians and officials charged over human experimentation and the Aktion T4 euthanasia programme. Seven defendants were executed.
  2. Milch Trial — Field Marshal Erhard Milch, charged with slave labour and experiments on prisoners.
  3. Judges' Trial — 16 jurists of the Nazi legal system, including officials of the People's Court and Special Courts.
  4. Pohl Trial — Oswald Pohl and 17 other officers of the SS Main Economic and Administrative Office (WVHA), which administered the concentration camps.
  5. Flick Trial — industrialist Friedrich Flick and associates, charged with slave labour and plunder.
  6. IG Farben Trial — 24 executives of the chemical conglomerate IG Farben.
  7. Hostages Trial — generals who commanded in the Balkans, charged with reprisal killings of hostages.
  8. RuSHA Trial — officials of the SS Race and Settlement Main Office, over racial resettlement and kidnapping of children.
  9. Einsatzgruppen Trial — 24 leaders of the mobile killing squads; 14 death sentences were handed down, the most of any subsequent trial.
  10. Krupp Trial — 12 directors of the Krupp armaments firm.
  11. Ministries Trial (Wilhelmstrasse Trial) — 21 officials of German government ministries.
  12. High Command Trial — 14 senior Wehrmacht officers.

Outcomes and clemency

Of the 177 indicted, 142 were tried (the remainder were dropped, unfit, or died before judgment). Twenty-five defendants were sentenced to death, though only twelve of those not reprieved were ultimately executed, at Landsberg Prison in 1951. During the early 1950s, amid the Cold War and West German rearmament, U.S. High Commissioner John J. McCloy commuted many sentences, and nearly all surviving defendants were released by the mid-1950s — decisions that remain controversial.

Legacy

The trials produced an enormous documentary record published as the "Green Series" (Trials of War Criminals before the Nuernberg Military Tribunals, 15 volumes). The Doctors' Trial gave rise to the Nuremberg Code on medical ethics. The proceedings helped develop international criminal law on crimes against humanity, superior responsibility, and the criminal liability of corporate and professional elites.
