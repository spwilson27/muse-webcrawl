Drinking water quality in the United States is generally safe by international standards, with more than 90 percent of the population served by public water systems that comply with federal health-based standards. The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) regulates public drinking water under the Safe Drinking Water Act of 1974, setting legally enforceable limits—called maximum contaminant levels—for over 90 chemical, radiological, and microbiological contaminants.

Regulation

The Safe Drinking Water Act (SDWA) applies to roughly 148,000 public water systems, which are divided into community water systems serving the same population year-round and non-community systems such as schools and campgrounds. The EPA sets standards, while states usually hold primary enforcement responsibility ("primacy"). The 1996 amendments added a requirement for annual consumer confidence reports describing local water quality. Private wells, serving about 13 percent of the population, are not federally regulated, though the EPA publishes guidelines for owners.

Contaminants of concern

Lead enters water mainly through corrosion of service lines and household plumbing. The Lead and Copper Rule, first issued in 1991, requires utilities to control corrosion and sample tap water; the 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require most lead service lines to be replaced within ten years. Disinfection byproducts such as trihalomethanes form when chlorine reacts with natural organic matter. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a group of synthetic "forever chemicals," were regulated nationally for the first time in 2024, when the EPA set maximum contaminant levels for six compounds including PFOA and PFOS. Other regulated contaminants include arsenic, nitrate (chiefly an agricultural runoff problem), fluoride, and microbial pathogens such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium.

Notable incidents

The 1993 Milwaukee cryptosporidiosis outbreak sickened an estimated 403,000 people, the largest waterborne disease outbreak in documented U.S. history. The Flint water crisis, beginning in 2014, exposed thousands of Michigan residents to elevated lead after a source-water switch without adequate corrosion control, becoming a defining case study in regulatory failure and environmental justice. In 2022, Jackson, Mississippi suffered a complete water-system failure following flooding and decades of underinvestment.

Monitoring and violations

Utilities must test water at prescribed intervals and report results to state regulators. EPA analyses have found that at any given time roughly 7–8 percent of community systems are in "significant noncompliance," and studies by organizations such as the Natural Resources Defense Council have argued that violations are under-reported and disproportionately affect low-income communities and communities of color. Infrastructure needs are estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars; the 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act allocated about $55 billion for water infrastructure.
