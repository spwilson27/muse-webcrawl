The Communist International (Comintern), also known as the Third International, was an international organisation founded in Moscow in 1919 that advocated world communism. Led initially by Vladimir Lenin and the Russian Communist Party, it required member parties to accept the "Twenty-one Conditions" (1920), including democratic centralism and the formation of disciplined, Bolshevik-style parties — splitting the world socialist movement from the reformist Second International.

The Comintern held seven World Congresses between 1919 and 1935.

History

After the failure of post-war revolutions in Germany and Hungary, the Comintern alternated strategies: the "united front" of the early 1920s; the ultra-left "Third Period" after 1928, which denounced social democrats as "social fascists" and fatally divided the German left before Nazism; and, from the Seventh Congress of 1935 under Georgi Dimitrov, the Popular Front against fascism. The Comintern organised aid to Republican Spain, including the International Brigades. Many of its cadres perished in Stalin's Great Purge.

Dissolution

Joseph Stalin dissolved the Comintern in May 1943, partly to reassure the USSR's wartime allies that Moscow was not directing revolutions abroad. Its functions passed informally to the Soviet party apparatus; a weaker successor, the Cominform, was created in 1947. The opening of the Comintern archives after 1991 transformed scholarly understanding of its finances, espionage links, and control over national parties.
