Union between Sweden and Norway

The Union between Sweden and Norway was a personal union of the separate kingdoms of Sweden and Norway under a common monarch, lasting from 1814 until its peaceful dissolution in 1905. The two states shared a king and foreign policy, but retained their own constitutions, parliaments, laws, currencies, armies and administrative institutions.

Background

During the Napoleonic Wars, Denmark–Norway sided with France while Sweden lost Finland to Russia in 1809. By the Treaty of Kiel in January 1814, Denmark ceded Norway to the king of Sweden. Norwegians resisted a simple transfer, declared independence, adopted a constitution at Eidsvoll on 17 May 1814 and elected Prince Christian Frederik as king. A short war with Sweden followed, ending in the Convention of Moss. Norway accepted a personal union with Sweden while keeping its constitution, with amendments needed for the union.

Structure

Charles XIII of Sweden became king of Norway in November 1814, and the Norwegian parliament, the Storting, revised the constitution to permit the union. The Act of Union (Riksakten) of 1815 set out the constitutional basis. The monarch was represented in Christiania (now Oslo) by a viceroy or governor-general at times, and Norwegian affairs were handled through a Norwegian government and council. Foreign representation was largely controlled by the Swedish foreign minister, an arrangement that became increasingly controversial.

Each country kept its own parliament—the Swedish Riksdag and the Norwegian Storting—and its own legal and military systems. Norway's politics became steadily more democratic and assertive, especially after parliamentarism was established in 1884.

Growing tensions

Nineteenth-century disputes concerned trade, the union arms and flags, the role of the king's veto, and Norway's demand for a separate consular service to serve its large merchant fleet. The consular question symbolized a broader wish for full equality within the union. In 1905 the Storting passed a consular law; King Oscar II refused sanction, the Norwegian government resigned, and the king could not form a replacement. On 7 June 1905 the Storting declared the union dissolved.

Dissolution

A Norwegian plebiscite in August 1905 overwhelmingly supported dissolution. Negotiations at Karlstad produced an agreement managing the separation, border fortifications and demilitarized arrangements. Sweden recognized Norwegian independence, Oscar II renounced the Norwegian crown for himself and his house, and Norway elected Prince Carl of Denmark as King Haakon VII. The settlement is often cited as a notable example of a major constitutional separation achieved without war.
