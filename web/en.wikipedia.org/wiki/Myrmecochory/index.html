Myrmecochory

Myrmecochory (from Ancient Greek mýrmēx "ant" and khorá "dispersal") is the dispersal of seeds by ants, an ant–plant mutualism found in more than 11,000 plant species worldwide. Plants that rely on this strategy are called myrmecochores.

An ant transporting a seed; the attached elaiosome provides the reward.

Mechanism

Myrmecochorous seeds bear a fleshy, lipid-rich appendage called an elaiosome. Ants are attracted to elaiosomes by chemical signals, including oleic acid, and carry the intact diaspore back to the nest. There, workers consume the elaiosome or feed it to larvae, while the undamaged seed is discarded, typically in underground middens or refuse chambers rich in nutrients.

Benefits to the plant

  • Directed dispersal — seeds are moved to protected, nutrient-enriched microsites favourable for germination.
  • Escape from predators — buried seeds avoid seed-eating rodents and birds.
  • Escape from fire — underground storage protects seeds in fire-prone habitats such as the South African fynbos and Australian bush.
  • Reduced competition — seeds are carried away from beneath the parent plant.

Distribution and examples

Myrmecochory is especially prominent in the sclerophyllous shrublands of Australia and South Africa, and in temperate deciduous forests of the Northern Hemisphere, where spring ephemerals such as bloodroot (Sanguinaria canadensis), trilliums, violets, and Dutchman's breeches depend on ants. It has evolved convergently in many plant families, including the Fabaceae, Euphorbiaceae, and Boraginaceae.

Disruption

Invasive ants, particularly the Argentine ant (Linepithema humile), do not disperse large myrmecochorous seeds effectively and can displace native disperser ants, causing declines in recruitment of ant-dispersed plants in invaded ecosystems.
