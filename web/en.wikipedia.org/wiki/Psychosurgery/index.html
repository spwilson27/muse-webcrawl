Psychosurgery

Psychosurgery is the neurosurgical treatment of mental disorder. It has been controversial since its inception, in part because of its historical misuse and serious side effects. The term is most associated with the lobotomy (prefrontal leucotomy), developed in 1935 by António Egas Moniz and Almeida Lima in Lisbon, for which Moniz received the Nobel Prize in 1949.

History

Lobotomy spread rapidly in the 1940s, especially in the United States, where Walter Freeman popularised the transorbital ("ice pick") procedure and performed thousands of operations. Tens of thousands of patients were lobotomised worldwide, often with devastating effects on personality, cognition and independence. The practice declined sharply in the 1950s with the arrival of antipsychotic drugs such as chlorpromazine, and growing public and professional opposition; the Soviet Union banned it in 1950.

Modern practice

Contemporary psychosurgery is rare, highly regulated, and reserved for severe, intractable conditions. It uses precise stereotactic lesions — anterior cingulotomy, anterior capsulotomy, subcaudate tractotomy and limbic leucotomy — for refractory obsessive–compulsive disorder and depression, typically requiring multidisciplinary review and informed consent. Deep brain stimulation, being adjustable and reversible, is increasingly preferred where applicable.
