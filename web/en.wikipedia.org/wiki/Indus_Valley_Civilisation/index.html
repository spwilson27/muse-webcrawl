Indus Valley Civilisation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro, a large watertight brick tank on the citadel mound]

The Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3300–1300 BC), also called the Harappan civilisation, was a Bronze Age civilisation in the north-western regions of South Asia, one of the three earliest cradles of civilisation alongside Egypt and Mesopotamia — and by area the largest of them. Its cities, including Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira and Lothal, display remarkably standardised town planning, brickwork, weights and water engineering across a million square kilometres. Its script remains undeciphered, and its famously 'faceless' society — no clear palaces, temples or royal tombs have been found — continues to puzzle archaeologists.

Cities and trade

Mature Harappan cities (c. 2600–1900 BC) were laid out on grid plans with covered drains, wells and bathing platforms; Mohenjo-daro's Great Bath suggests ritual importance of water. Standardised fired bricks, chert weights and stamp seals (often carved with animal motifs and the still-unread signs) attest to coordinated organisation. Lothal had a dockyard; Harappan goods and seals reached Mesopotamia, whose texts speak of trade with a land called Meluhha, very likely the Indus region.

Decline

After 1900 BC the cities were gradually abandoned, writing and standard weights disappeared, and settlement shifted eastward. Causes debated include weakening monsoons and the drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra river system, floods, disease and the breakdown of trade — gradual transformation rather than a single catastrophe. Later Vedic culture developed in the same broad region, though the relationship between the two remains contested.
