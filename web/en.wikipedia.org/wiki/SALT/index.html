SALT

[Image: US president Jimmy Carter and Soviet leader Leonid Brezhnev signing the SALT II treaty in Vienna, June 1979]

The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) were two rounds of bilateral negotiations between the United States and the Soviet Union on limiting strategic nuclear weapons during the Cold War. The talks produced the SALT I agreements of 1972 and the SALT II treaty of 1979.

SALT I

Negotiations opened in Helsinki in November 1969. On 26 May 1972, Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev signed in Moscow the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty, which limited each side to two (later one) ABM deployment sites, and an Interim Agreement freezing the numbers of intercontinental and submarine-launched ballistic missile launchers for five years. SALT I did not limit warheads or multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles (MIRVs), a key criticism, but it established arms control as a continuing superpower process and slowed the race in missile defence.

SALT II

The second round, from 1972 to 1979, sought a comprehensive limit on strategic delivery vehicles. Jimmy Carter and Brezhnev signed the SALT II treaty in Vienna on 18 June 1979, capping each side at 2,400 (later 2,250) strategic delivery vehicles and placing sub-limits on MIRVed systems. Following the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979, Carter withdrew the treaty from Senate consideration and it was never ratified, though both sides largely observed its terms until the mid-1980s.

Legacy

SALT was succeeded by the START negotiations under Ronald Reagan, shifting the emphasis from launchers to warhead reductions. Critics of SALT argued it codified high arsenals rather than reducing them (see list of states with nuclear weapons); supporters credit it with stabilising the arms race and creating verification practices on which later treaties built.
