Jetsun Milarepa (c. 1052 – c. 1135) was a Tibetan siddha and yogi, one of the most famous practitioners of Tibetan Buddhism and a central figure of the Kagyu school. His life story — from youthful sorcerer who killed dozens of people with black magic, to repentant disciple, to enlightened hermit-poet — is among the best-known narratives in Tibetan culture. He is renowned for the Hundred Thousand Songs of Milarepa, a vast collection of spiritual songs and teachings.

Life

According to the traditional biography, composed by his disciple Tsangnyön Heruka in the 15th century, Milarepa was born into a wealthy family in western Tibet. After his father's early death, his uncle and aunt seized the family property and reduced Milarepa's mother and sister to servitude. At his mother's urging, he studied sorcery and took revenge, killing 35 people at a wedding feast by causing a house to collapse and destroying crops with hailstorms.

Overcome by remorse, Milarepa sought Buddhist instruction and eventually found the translator Marpa, who subjected him to years of gruelling ordeals — most famously ordering him to build and demolish stone towers repeatedly — to purify his past misdeeds. Only then did Marpa transmit to him the tantric teachings he had brought from India, including the practice of tummo (inner heat).

Milarepa spent the rest of his life meditating in mountain caves, subsisting for long periods on nettle soup that was said to have turned his skin green, and taught through improvised songs. His chief disciples were the monk Gampopa, who systematised his teachings within the Kagyu lineage, and the yogi Rechungpa.

The Hundred Thousand Songs

The Songs of Milarepa (Milarepa's Hundred Thousand Songs, Tibetan: Mi la'i mgur 'bum) present his teachings in verse, often in the form of dialogues with hunters, disciples, patrons, and even wild animals. The songs emphasise renunciation, devotion to the guru, the practice of meditation in solitude, and the realisation of the nature of mind. Together with his biography, they remain among the most beloved works of Tibetan literature.
