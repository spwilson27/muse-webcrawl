Kidney failure

SynonymsRenal failure, end-stage renal disease (ESRD)
SpecialtyNephrology
TypesAcute kidney injury; chronic kidney disease stage 5
CausesDiabetes, hypertension, glomerulonephritis, sepsis, ischemia, toxins
TreatmentTreat cause; dialysis; kidney transplantation

Kidney failure, also known as renal failure, is a medical condition in which the kidneys can no longer adequately filter waste products and excess fluid from the blood. It may arise suddenly as acute kidney injury (formerly acute renal failure) over hours to days, or as the end stage of gradually progressive chronic kidney disease. Failure is typically detected by reduced urine output (oliguria) and rising blood levels of creatinine and urea.

Causes

Acute kidney injury is commonly caused by reduced blood flow to the kidneys (sepsis, hemorrhage, heart failure), direct kidney injury from drugs (such as aminoglycosides, NSAIDs, or contrast dye), severe infection or inflammation, and obstruction of urinary outflow (enlarged prostate, stones, tumors). Chronic kidney failure most often results from diabetes mellitus and hypertension, and also from glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and autoimmune disorders.

Signs and symptoms

Accumulation of waste products (uremia) can produce nausea, fatigue, loss of appetite, itching, confusion, and, in severe cases, pericarditis or encephalopathy. Fluid retention causes swelling and breathlessness; electrolyte disturbances, especially high potassium (hyperkalemia), can cause dangerous cardiac arrhythmias. Chronic failure also leads to anemia (from reduced erythropoietin) and bone disease (from disordered calcium and phosphate metabolism).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on blood tests (creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate or eGFR, urea, electrolytes), urine tests, and imaging such as ultrasound; kidney biopsy may identify specific diseases. Chronic kidney disease is staged by eGFR, with stage 5 (eGFR below 15 mL/min/1.73 m²) denoting kidney failure.

Treatment

Acute cases require treating the underlying cause, careful fluid and electrolyte management, medication review, and sometimes temporary dialysis. For irreversible failure, long-term options are kidney replacement therapy — hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation — or conservative (palliative) care. Kidney failure affects millions of people worldwide, and its advanced treatment consumes a large share of health budgets; prevention centers on controlling diabetes and blood pressure.
