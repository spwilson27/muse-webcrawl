Punk fashion

Punk fashion is the clothing, hairstyles, cosmetics, jewellery and body modifications of the punk subculture. Emerging in the mid-1970s in London and New York alongside punk rock, punk fashion was deliberately confrontational and anti-materialistic, assembling garments from charity shops, military surplus and household objects — ripped jeans held together with safety pins, bin-liner tops, dog collars, razor blades and lavatory chains worn as jewellery — to signal rejection of mainstream culture and of the perceived excesses of earlier rock fashion.

Origins

In London, the boutique SEX (later Seditionaries) run by Malcolm McLaren and Vivienne Westwood on the King's Road became the style's engine room, supplying the Sex Pistols and their followers with bondage trousers, mohair jumpers, "Anarchy" shirts and T-shirts printed with provocative slogans and imagery — including the swastika and images of Karl Marx or the Queen defaced with a safety pin, used as shock tactics rather than political endorsement. In New York, the scene around CBGB favoured a leaner look of ripped jeans, leather jackets and band T-shirts associated with the Ramones.

Elements

  • Hair — dyed in unnatural colours, shaved at the sides, spiked or teased into mohawks and liberty spikes, often using soap, egg white or gelatin.
  • Clothing — deliberately torn and re-pinned garments, tartan, leather and PVC, fishnet stockings, Dr. Martens boots, creepers and Converse sneakers.
  • Customisation — band logos and slogans painted or stencilled onto jackets; studs, spikes and patches; the DIY ethic of making rather than buying fashion.
  • Body modification — safety-pin piercings, multiple ear piercings and, increasingly in later decades, tattoos.

Later developments and influence

As punk fragmented, styles diversified: the military boots and shaved heads of Oi! and street punk, the darker romanticism of goth (an offshoot of post-punk), the patched "crust" aesthetic of anarcho-punk, and the studded leather of hardcore. From the 1980s onward, mainstream and high fashion repeatedly absorbed punk motifs — from Westwood's own catwalk career to Jean Paul Gaultier — a commercialisation many punks regard as betrayal of the subculture's DIY, anti-consumerist ethos. Punk fashion was the subject of the Metropolitan Museum's 2013 exhibition Punk: Chaos to Couture.
