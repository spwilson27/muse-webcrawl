Cadenza

In music, a cadenza (from Italian cadenza, "cadence") is a passage, usually in a concerto, in which the orchestra stops and the soloist plays alone in free time, without a strict regular pulse, displaying virtuosity and improvisational flair. It customarily falls near the end of a movement, at a cadential point — classically over a cadential six-four chord resolving to the dominant, held under a fermata.

History

Cadenzas developed from the improvised ornamentation of cadences in Baroque and earlier vocal music. In the Classical concerto the cadenza was normally improvised by the performer — Mozart and Beethoven both dazzled audiences with their own — though Mozart also wrote cadenzas out for pupils. Beethoven's Emperor Concerto (1809) famously instructs the soloist not to improvise, printing the cadenza in the score, a landmark in composers taking control of the passage.

Famous cadenzas

Joseph Joachim's cadenza for the Brahms Violin Concerto and Fritz Kreisler's for the Beethoven Violin Concerto remain standard choices. Some cadenzas became compositions of independent fame: the cadenza-like solo in Rimsky-Korsakov's Scheherazade, Rachmaninoff's weighty cadenza for the first movement of his Third Piano Concerto, and the written cadenzas in Shostakovich's First Cello Concerto. Solo cadenzas also occur in arias, in works without orchestra, and in jazz, where extended unaccompanied improvisations are still called cadenzas.

Notation

Improvised cadenzas are traditionally signalled by a fermata over the cadential chord, with the orchestra re-entering on a signalled trill and final flourish. Written cadenzas are often printed in small notes. Modern performers may choose among historical cadenzas or compose their own in period style.
