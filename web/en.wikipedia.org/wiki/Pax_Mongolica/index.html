Pax Mongolica

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Mongol relay station with fresh horses for imperial couriers]

The Pax Mongolica (Latin for 'Mongol Peace'), modelled on Pax Romana, describes the stabilising effects of the Mongol conquests on Eurasian trade and communication in the 13th and 14th centuries. For the first time since antiquity, a single political order — after 1260, four cooperating khanates — stretched from Korea and China to Hungary and Anatolia, securing the Silk Road with relay stations (yam), passports (paiza) and protected caravan routes. Merchants, missionaries, technologies and — in the darker reading — plague travelled the reopened Eurasian arteries: Marco Polo eastward, Rabban Bar Sauma westward, gunpowder, printing and astronomy between worlds.

Trade and exchange

The Mongol peace cut the costs and dangers of overland trade: merchants such as the Muslim and Uyghur ortogh associations, Venetian and Genoese houses, and Chinese maritime traders flourished. The yam network — tens of thousands of horses and stations across the khanates — moved officials, envoys and news at unprecedented speed. Yuan China's sea trade linked Quanzhou to India and Arabia; Persian and Chinese astronomy, medicine and art exchanged motifs from Tabriz to Beijing.

Limits and end

The peace was built on conquest's terror and rested on khanates that fought each other (Berke–Hulagu war from 1262, Kaidu's wars) and slowly diverged religiously and politically; Mongol China fell in 1368, the Ilkhanate in 1335, and the plague of the 1330s–40s travelled the same roads west. Historians debate how 'peaceful' and how integrated the system truly was — but the century undeniably reconnected Eurasia as no era had before.
