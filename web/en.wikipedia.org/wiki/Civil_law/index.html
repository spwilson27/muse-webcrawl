Civil law

Civil law is a legal system originating in Italy during the Middle Ages and later adopted and developed across mainland Europe, and from there spread around the world. The civil law system is derived from Roman law, especially the Corpus Juris Civilis of Emperor Justinian I, as later developed through the work of medieval legal scholars. Its primary feature is that its core principles are codified into a referable system, which serves as a primary source of law.

Overview

Civil law is sometimes referred to as neo-Roman law, Romano-Germanic law or Continental law. It is the most widespread system of law in the world, in force in various forms in about 150 countries. It stands in contrast to the common law system, originating in England, which relies heavily on precedent and judicial decision-making. In civil law jurisdictions, courts base their decisions primarily on statutes and comprehensive codes, such as the French Civil Code of 1804 (the Napoleonic Code) and the German Civil Code (BGB) of 1900.

Subgroups and scope

Major subgroups include the French, German, Scandinavian, and Chinese civil law traditions. Within a given jurisdiction, "civil law" may also denote the branch of law dealing with private relations between individuals — such as contract, property, and family law — as distinct from criminal law. Courts in civil law systems have traditionally followed a more inquisitorial procedure, with judges taking an active role in investigating the facts.
