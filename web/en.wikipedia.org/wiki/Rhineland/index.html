The Rhineland (German: Rheinland) is a loosely defined region of western Germany along the middle and lower reaches of the River Rhine, extending roughly from the Dutch border and the Ruhr in the north to the wine-growing valleys of the Moselle and the Nahe in the south. It encompasses the great cities of Cologne, Düsseldorf, Bonn, Koblenz, Mainz, Wiesbaden, and Aachen, and is one of Germany's most densely populated and economically significant regions.

Geography and economy

The region includes the Lower Rhine plain, the volcanic Eifel hills, the slate mountains of the Middle Rhine gorge with its castles and terraced vineyards (famed for Riesling wines), and the industrial conurbations of the Ruhr fringe. The Rhineland's coal, steel, and chemicals built Germany's industrial power in the 19th and 20th centuries; today its economy is led by services, media, logistics, chemicals, and technology.

History

The Rhine was the frontier (limes) of the Roman Empire; Cologne (Colonia Claudia Ara Agrippinensium) and Mainz (Mogontiacum) are Roman foundations. In the Middle Ages the region fragmented among powerful ecclesiastical electorates (Cologne, Mainz, Trier) and duchies such as Jülich and Berg. Revolutionary and Napoleonic France annexed the left bank in the 1790s; after 1815 the Congress of Vienna awarded most of the region to Prussia, which formed its Rhine Province in 1822.

After the First World War the Rhineland was demilitarised and occupied by Allied troops under the Treaty of Versailles; France occupied the Ruhr in 1923 over reparations defaults. On 7 March 1936 Nazi Germany remilitarised the Rhineland in defiance of the Versailles and Locarno treaties — a decisive step toward the Second World War. After 1945 the region was divided between the new Länder of North Rhine-Westphalia and Rhineland-Palatinate, and Bonn in the Rhineland became the capital of West Germany from 1949 to 1990.

Culture

The Rhineland is celebrated for its Carnival (Karneval), especially in Cologne, Düsseldorf, and Mainz, for its Kölsch and Altbier beer traditions, for the Cologne Cathedral — Germany's most visited landmark — and for composers such as Beethoven (born in Bonn) and Schumann. Its people are stereotypically portrayed in Germany as cheerful and sociable, summed up in the Rhenish saying "Et kütt wie et kütt" ("it comes as it comes").
