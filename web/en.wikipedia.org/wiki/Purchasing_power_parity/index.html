Purchasing power parity (PPP) is a measure of the price of specific goods in different countries, used to compare the absolute purchasing power of currencies. In its absolute form it rests on the law of one price: in the absence of transaction costs and trade barriers, identical goods should cost the same everywhere when expressed in a common currency, so the exchange rate between two currencies should equal the ratio of their price levels for a comparable basket of goods and services.

Uses

International agencies such as the World Bank, IMF and OECD use PPP conversion factors — rather than volatile market exchange rates — to compare GDP, incomes and poverty across countries, because non-traded goods and services (haircuts, housing, local labour) are systematically cheaper in poorer countries. By PPP, China's economy became the world's largest in the mid-2010s, and India's ranks third, well above its position at market rates.

Deviations and theory

In practice exchange rates deviate persistently from PPP. The Balassa–Samuelson effect explains part of the gap: rich countries' higher productivity in traded goods raises wages and prices economy-wide. Relative PPP — that exchange-rate changes should offset inflation differentials — holds somewhat better over long horizons. The Economist's light-hearted Big Mac index, published since 1986, gauges currency misalignment from the local price of a McDonald's hamburger. The unit of PPP comparison is the international dollar (Geary–Khamis dollar).
