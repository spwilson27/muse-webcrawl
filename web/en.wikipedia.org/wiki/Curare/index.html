Curare

Curare is a common name for various plant extract alkaloid arrow poisons originating from indigenous peoples in Central and South America. Used as a paralysing agent by the native peoples for hunting, curare only becomes active when it enters the bloodstream; animals killed with curare-tipped arrows can be safely eaten because the poison is harmless by mouth.

Sources and types

Classic curares were prepared from the bark of plants such as Strychnos toxifera and Chondrodendron tomentosum. Depending on the container in which it was stored, three main types were distinguished: tube curare (in bamboo tubes), pot curare (in clay pots), and calabash curare (in gourds). The principal active alkaloid of tube curare is tubocurarine.

Mechanism

Curare alkaloids are neuromuscular blocking agents. They compete with the neurotransmitter acetylcholine at nicotinic receptors on the motor end plate of skeletal muscle, preventing the muscle from contracting. The result is flaccid paralysis that progresses to the respiratory muscles; death, if it occurs, is by asphyxiation, while consciousness remains intact because the alkaloids do not cross the blood–brain barrier.

Medical use

After its active principle was isolated and standardised, tubocurarine was introduced into anaesthesia in 1942, revolutionising surgery by allowing muscle relaxation at lighter levels of anaesthesia. Curare research by Claude Bernard in the nineteenth century was foundational to modern pharmacology. Synthetic successors such as atracurium and vecuronium are now more commonly used, and their effects can be reversed with acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as neostigmine.
