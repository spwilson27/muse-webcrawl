The white-rumped vulture (Gyps bengalensis) is an Old World vulture native to South and Southeast Asia. It has been listed as Critically Endangered on the IUCN Red List since 2000, as the population has declined severely and extremely rapidly. Once described in the 1980s as possibly the most abundant large bird of prey in the world — with a global population estimated at many millions — it suffered a decline of more than 99% in India and neighbouring countries during the 1990s and early 2000s.

Description

It is a medium-sized vulture, 75 to 93 cm (30 to 37 in) long with a wingspan of 1.92 to 2.6 m (6.3 to 8.5 ft) and a weight of 3.5 to 7.5 kg (7.7 to 16.5 lb). Adults are darker than other Gyps species, with blackish plumage, a whitish neck ruff and the white lower back and rump patch that gives the species its name and is conspicuous in flight. The underwing coverts are white, contrasting with the dark flight feathers. Like other vultures it is a scavenger, feeding almost entirely on carrion, which it locates by soaring on thermals and watching other scavengers.

The diclofenac crisis

The collapse was traced in 2004 to poisoning by diclofenac, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug widely used to treat livestock. Vultures feeding on carcasses of recently treated cattle absorbed the drug and died of kidney failure and visceral gout. India, Nepal and Pakistan banned veterinary diclofenac in 2006 and promoted the vulture-safe alternative meloxicam. Captive breeding centres in India (notably at Pinjore, Haryana), Nepal and Cambodia, run by conservation organisations including the Bombay Natural History Society and the RSPB, have since bred the species and begun releases, and wild declines have slowed or partially stabilised in some areas — though numbers remain a tiny fraction of their former abundance.

Ecological significance

Vultures provide a vital sanitation service by disposing of carcasses. Their disappearance in India was linked to increases in feral dog populations and the associated risk of rabies, and to the difficulties faced by the Parsi community, whose traditional sky burials at Towers of Silence depended on vultures.
