The conventional Egyptian chronology is the scholarly consensus framework of regnal dates for ancient Egyptian history, dividing it into the Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms separated by Intermediate Periods.

Sources

Egyptologists combine king lists (the Abydos and Saqqara lists, the Turin Royal Canon), the dynastic history of Manetho (3rd century BC), astronomical references such as heliacal risings of Sirius in the Sothic cycle, and synchronisms with Near Eastern chronologies.

Framework

Under the conventional scheme the Old Kingdom begins around 2686 BC, the Middle Kingdom around 2055 BC, and the New Kingdom around 1550 BC. Absolute dates become firm only in the Late Period; earlier dates may float by decades, and by up to a century for the earliest dynasties. Radiocarbon studies broadly support the conventional framework.

Alternative chronologies

Revisionist schemes — David Rohl's "New Chronology," the Glasgow Chronology, and Immanuel Velikovsky's reconstruction — propose lowering dates by centuries, but remain outside mainstream Egyptology.
