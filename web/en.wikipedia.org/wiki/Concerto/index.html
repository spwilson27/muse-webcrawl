Concerto

A piano soloist with orchestra.

A concerto is a musical composition in which one or more solo instruments are set against an orchestral ensemble, highlighting the contrast between the individual voice and the collective and showcasing the soloist's virtuosity. Since the Classical era the standard concerto has three movements in a fast–slow–fast arrangement, the first usually in sonata form with a double exposition, and often includes a cadenza—an unaccompanied passage of virtuoso display near the end of a movement.

History

The term derives from the Italian concertare, with connotations of both contending and joining together. In the Baroque era the concerto grosso contrasted a small group of soloists (the concertino) with the full ensemble (the ripieno), as in works by Corelli and Handel, while Vivaldi standardized the solo concerto in three movements and ritornello form. Mozart raised the piano and violin concertos to central Classical genres, and Beethoven expanded their scale and drama. The nineteenth century produced the archetypal virtuoso concerto (Mendelssohn, Chopin, Liszt, Brahms, Tchaikovsky), and the form has remained vital through the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, for virtually every orchestral instrument.

Structure

Classical first movements present the main themes first in the orchestra, then in the soloist, followed by development, recapitulation, and cadenza. Slow movements are lyrical and song-like; finales are frequently rondos with brilliant, folk- or dance-inflected themes. Some works alter the scheme—four movements, continuous single movements, or cadenzas written out by the composer instead of improvised.
