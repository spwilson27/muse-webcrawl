Catecholamine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A catecholamine is a monoamine neurotransmitter, an organic compound that has a catechol (benzene with two hydroxyl side groups next to each other) and a side-chain amine. The most abundant catecholamines are epinephrine (adrenaline), norepinephrine (noradrenaline) and dopamine, all of which are produced from the amino acid tyrosine.

Synthesis

Catecholamines are synthesized in the brain, the adrenal medulla and sympathetic nerve fibres. Tyrosine is first hydroxylated to L-DOPA by tyrosine hydroxylase — the rate-limiting step — then decarboxylated to dopamine. In noradrenergic neurones dopamine is further converted to norepinephrine, and in the adrenal medulla norepinephrine is methylated to epinephrine.

Function

Dopamine acts as a neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, involved in reward, motivation and motor control. Norepinephrine and epinephrine function both as neurotransmitters and as hormones released into the bloodstream by the adrenal glands during the fight-or-flight response, increasing heart rate, blood pressure and blood glucose availability.

Breakdown

Catecholamines are degraded by the enzymes monoamine oxidase (MAO) and catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT), producing metabolites such as vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and homovanillic acid (HVA), which are excreted in urine and measured clinically.

Clinical significance

Abnormal catecholamine levels are associated with several conditions: excess secretion by a pheochromocytoma causes episodic hypertension; dopamine deficiency in the substantia nigra underlies Parkinson's disease. Many psychiatric and cardiovascular drugs act by altering catecholamine synthesis, release or reuptake.
