Georgic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The georgic is a type of poem that deals with agriculture and rural labour, presenting practical instruction on farming within verse. The genre takes its name from the Georgics of the Roman poet Virgil, written around 29 BCE, which in turn drew on Hesiod's Works and Days. Unlike the pastoral, which idealises shepherds' leisure, the georgic emphasises work — the effort of cultivating land, keeping livestock and enduring weather.

Virgil's Georgics

Virgil's four books treat, in turn, crop husbandry, the care of trees and vines, livestock farming, and beekeeping. Ostensibly a manual in hexameter verse, the poem constantly broadens into reflections on labour, nature, civil war and the human condition, and it closes its fourth book with the myth of Aristaeus and the story of Orpheus and Eurydice.

Later georgics

The mode was revived in the Renaissance and flourished in eighteenth-century England amid agricultural improvement, in works such as John Philips's Cyder (1708), James Thomson's The Seasons (1726–1730), and John Dyer's The Fleece (1757). Modern criticism reads the georgic as a vehicle for thinking about work, empire, land management and humanity's relation to nature.
