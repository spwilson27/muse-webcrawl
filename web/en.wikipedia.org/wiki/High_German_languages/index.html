High German languages

The High German languages (German: hochdeutsche Mundarten) are a family of West Germanic languages and dialects spoken in central and southern Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg, and neighbouring areas. They are distinguished by having undergone the High German consonant shift, which separates them from Low German and Low Franconian.

High German
Region: Central Europe
Family: Indo-European > Germanic > West Germanic
Includes: Standard German, Yiddish, Luxembourgish (disputed), Alemannic, Bavarian, Franconian dialects

History

Old High German (c. 750–1050), documented in glosses and texts such as the Hildebrandslied, developed into Middle High German (1050–1350), the language of courtly epic, and Early New High German, from which modern Standard German emerged, influenced by Martin Luther's Bible translation and East Central German chancery language.

Classification

Central German

Includes West Central German (Ripuar­ian, Moselle Franconian, Luxembourgish) and East Central German (Thuringian, Upper Saxon, Silesian German), the basis of Standard German.

Upper German

Includes Alemannic (Swiss German, Swabian, Alsatian), Bavarian (Austro-Bavarian), and East Franconian and South Franconian. In the far south the shift is most complete.

Yiddish

Yiddish developed from Middle High German varieties spoken by Ashkenazi Jews, written in Hebrew alphabet, with Hebrew-Aramaic and Slavic components.

Consonant shift

The Second Sound Shift changed voiceless stops p, t, k to fricatives or affricates in certain positions — e.g., English apple vs German Apfel, water vs Wasser, make vs machen. The Benrath line (maken/machen) and Uerdingen line divide Low from High German.
