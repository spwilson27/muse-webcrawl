Operant conditioning, also called instrumental conditioning, is a type of learning in which behaviour is modified by its consequences: actions followed by favourable outcomes (reinforcement) become more likely to recur, and actions followed by unfavourable outcomes (punishment) become less likely. Developed by the American psychologist B. F. Skinner from the earlier work of Edward Thorndike, operant conditioning became the core of the behaviorist school of psychology and remains a foundational framework in behavioural science, animal training, education, and clinical therapy.

History

Edward Thorndike, working with cats in puzzle boxes in the 1890s, formulated the law of effect: responses followed by satisfaction are "stamped in," and those followed by discomfort are "stamped out." B. F. Skinner, from the 1930s, distinguished operant behaviour — emitted by the organism and operating on the environment — from the respondent behaviour elicited in classical conditioning, and built the operant chamber ("Skinner box"), in which a rat's lever press or a pigeon's key peck could be precisely related to delivered consequences. Skinner expounded the approach in The Behavior of Organisms (1938) and popularised it in works such as Walden Two (1948) and Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971).

The four contingencies

Consequences are classified along two axes — whether a stimulus is added or removed, and whether behaviour increases or decreases. Positive reinforcement adds a desirable stimulus (food, praise) to strengthen behaviour. Negative reinforcement removes an aversive stimulus (an electric shock ceases, a seatbelt buzzer stops) and likewise strengthens behaviour. Positive punishment adds an aversive stimulus to weaken behaviour, while negative punishment removes a desirable stimulus (a fine, loss of privileges). When reinforcement ceases altogether, the behaviour undergoes extinction, often after a temporary "extinction burst." New behaviour can be built by shaping — reinforcing successive approximations to the target response.

Schedules of reinforcement

Skinner showed that the pattern (schedule) by which reinforcers are delivered powerfully determines response rate and resistance to extinction. Continuous reinforcement rewards every response; partial schedules reward some responses — on fixed or variable ratios (per number of responses) or intervals (per elapsed time). Variable-ratio schedules, as in slot machines, produce the highest and steadiest response rates and the greatest resistance to extinction, a finding with obvious relevance to gambling and game design.

Applications and criticism

Operant principles underpin applied behavior analysis (ABA), token economies in classrooms and institutions, animal training (including clicker training), programmed instruction, and behavioural therapies for phobias, addiction, and developmental disorders. Critics — from Chomsky's famous 1959 review of Skinner's Verbal Behavior, to research on latent learning, preparedness, and instinctive drift — have argued that operant conditioning cannot by itself explain language, insight, or species-specific behaviour. Nevertheless, its concepts remain among the most replicated and applied in psychology.
