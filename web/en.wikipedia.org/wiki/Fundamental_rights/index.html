Fundamental rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fundamental rights are a group of rights that have been recognised by a high degree of protection from encroachment. These rights are specifically identified in a constitution, or have been found under due process of law. They are distinguished from ordinary legal rights in that they are usually entrenched — difficult to amend or repeal — and in that legislation infringing them is subjected to heightened judicial scrutiny.

In the jurisprudence of the United States, fundamental rights are those freedoms the Supreme Court of the United States has held to be so basic that government infringement triggers strict scrutiny: the state must show a compelling interest pursued by the least restrictive means. In India, the Fundamental Rights set out in Part III of the Constitution of India — including equality before the law, freedoms of speech and religion, and protection against untouchability and exploitation — are directly enforceable against the state, and Dr B. R. Ambedkar described Article 32, the right to move the Supreme Court directly for their enforcement, as the “heart and soul” of the constitution.

Sources and scope

The category descends from the tradition of natural rights and the rights declarations of the American and French revolutions, including the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789. Internationally, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the two 1966 covenants — the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights — enumerate rights that most constitutional systems treat as fundamental, though the two families are often given different levels of judicial enforceability.

Enforcement

Because fundamental rights bind the state, courts may strike down legislation that violates them. The strength of enforcement varies: some systems allow any aggrieved person to petition a constitutional court directly, while others channel claims through ordinary litigation. In emergencies some rights may be suspended or limited, but most frameworks treat a core — such as the prohibitions of torture and slavery — as non-derogable.
