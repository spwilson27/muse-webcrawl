Tulip Period

The Tulip Period (Ottoman Turkish: Lâle Devri) was a phase in Ottoman history conventionally dated from the Treaty of Passarowitz in 1718 to the Patrona Halil rebellion in 1730. It corresponds to the later reign of Sultan Ahmed III and the grand vizierate of Nevşehirli Damat Ibrahim Pasha, and is remembered for peace, courtly luxury, cultural openness, and a flowering of architecture, poetry, and garden culture centred on the tulip.

Background

After the Treaty of Passarowitz ended war with Austria and Venice, the Ottoman court entered a period of relative peace. Grand Vizier Ibrahim Pasha pursued diplomacy with European states, sending embassies such as that of Yirmisekiz Çelebi Mehmed Efendi to Paris in 1720–21, whose reports on French court life, gardens, and manufactures influenced Ottoman tastes.

Culture and architecture

The era saw the construction of palaces and pleasure pavilions along the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn, most famously the Sadâbâd palace at Kâğıthane, modelled in part on French precedents. Istanbul's elite enjoyed tulip festivals, poetry gatherings, and illuminated night entertainments. The period also brought significant technical firsts: the first Ottoman Turkish printing press was established by İbrahim Müteferrika in 1727, and a fire-fighting corps and new waterworks were organised.

End

War with Persia, economic grievances, and resentment of court extravagance culminated in the Patrona Halil rebellion of 1730. Ahmed III was deposed, Ibrahim Pasha was executed, and many of the era's palaces were destroyed, bringing the Tulip Period to an abrupt close.
