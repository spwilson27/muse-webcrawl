Spectral geometry

Spectral geometry is a field in mathematics that concerns the relationships between geometric structures of manifolds and spectra of canonically defined differential operators, most often the Laplace–Beltrami operator. The field is summarized by Mark Kac's famous 1966 question, "Can one hear the shape of a drum?" — that is, whether the eigenvalues of the Laplacian on a domain determine the domain's geometry.

Background

The eigenvalues of the Laplacian correspond physically to the resonant frequencies of a vibrating membrane or cavity. Hermann Weyl showed in 1911 that the asymptotic growth of the eigenvalues determines the volume of the domain (and, in later refinements, its surface area and integrated curvature). Such quantities recoverable from the spectrum are called spectral invariants.

Isospectral manifolds

Two manifolds or domains sharing the same spectrum are called isospectral. John Milnor found the first example in 1964: a pair of non-isometric 16-dimensional flat tori with identical spectra. In 1992 Gordon, Webb, and Wolpert constructed two non-congruent planar domains with the same spectrum, giving a negative answer to Kac's question for drums. Nevertheless, many geometric features — such as volume, perimeter, and the number of holes for certain classes — are audible, and positive results hold under additional hypotheses on curvature or symmetry.

Methods

Spectral geometry uses the heat kernel and its asymptotic expansion, wave-trace formulas relating the spectrum to the lengths of closed geodesics, and zeta-function regularization. The subject connects differential geometry, partial differential equations, and mathematical physics, and has offshoots in spectral graph theory and inverse problems.
