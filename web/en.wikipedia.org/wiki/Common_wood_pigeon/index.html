The common wood pigeon (Columba palumbus), known in Ireland as the colúr coille and in older English as the ring dove, is a large species in the dove and pigeon family, Columbidae, native to the western Palearctic. It is the largest and most abundant pigeon across most of its range in Europe, North Africa, and western Asia.

Description

Adults are grey above and pinkish-grey below, with a distinctive white (or, in one subspecies, cream) neck patch edged with iridescent green and pink, white wing patches conspicuous in flight, and a black-tipped tail. The song is a familiar five-syllable cooing, often rendered "take two cows, Taffy." Juveniles lack the neck patch and have paler bills.

Behaviour and diet

Wood pigeons inhabit woodland, farmland, parks, and gardens. They feed mainly on plant matter — cereals, oilseed rape, brassicas, clover, acorns, beech mast, and berries — and in autumn and winter form large flocks that descend on arable fields, where the species is regarded as an agricultural pest and is widely shot as game. The flimsy twig nest is built in trees; two white eggs are laid, and both parents feed the young ("squabs") on crop milk. Several broods may be raised between April and September.

Population

The European population is estimated in the tens of millions of pairs and is increasing in many areas, helped by intensive cereal and oilseed cultivation providing year-round food.
