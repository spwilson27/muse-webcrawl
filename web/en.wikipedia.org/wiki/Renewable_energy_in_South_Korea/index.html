Renewable energy in South Korea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Although South Korea is the world's ninth-largest energy consumer and has considerable domestic manufacturing capacity in clean-energy technologies, it has the lowest share of renewable electricity production among major economies. Renewables have historically supplied under 10 percent of South Korea's electricity, with the balance dominated by coal, natural gas, and nuclear power. In recent years the government has promoted solar and offshore wind power as part of plans to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and reach carbon neutrality by 2050.

Policy framework

South Korea introduced a feed-in tariff in 2002, replaced in 2012 by a Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS) obliging large power generators to source a rising share of their electricity from renewables, supported by tradable Renewable Energy Certificates. In 2020 South Korea declared a goal of carbon neutrality by 2050 and adopted a "Green New Deal" promoting clean energy investment. Successive Basic Plans for Electricity Supply and Demand have set renewable targets, generally aiming for renewables to provide roughly 20 percent or more of generation by 2030, though targets and the balance between renewables and nuclear power have shifted between administrations.

Solar power

Solar photovoltaics is the largest renewable source in South Korea, growing rapidly from the mid-2010s as panel costs fell. Installed solar capacity passed 20 gigawatts in the early 2020s. Projects range from rooftop installations to large ground-mounted arrays and floating solar plants on reservoirs. Expansion has faced constraints from the country's mountainous terrain, high land prices, grid connection queues, and local siting rules.

Wind power

Onshore wind development has been limited by terrain and permitting. The government has therefore emphasized offshore wind, announcing plans for large projects off the southwest coast, including an 8.2-gigawatt complex near Sinan County described as among the world's largest planned offshore wind developments, and setting goals of around 12 gigawatts of offshore wind by 2030.

Other sources

Hydropower, including pumped storage, has long contributed a small share of supply. Bioenergy and waste-to-energy account for a significant portion of officially counted "renewable energy" under Korean statistics. South Korea has also promoted a hydrogen economy strategy, supporting fuel-cell generation and hydrogen vehicles, and operates the world's largest tidal power station, the Sihwa Lake Tidal Power Station (254 MW), opened in 2011.

Challenges

Analysts point to limited grid flexibility, curtailment of solar output in regions such as Jeju Island and the southwest, interconnection delays, and an electricity market structure dominated by the state utility KEPCO as obstacles. Energy security concerns are heightened because South Korea imports nearly all of its fossil fuels.
