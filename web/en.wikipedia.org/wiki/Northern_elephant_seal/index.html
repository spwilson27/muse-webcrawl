Northern elephant seal

The northern elephant seal (Mirounga angustirostris) is one of two species of elephant seal, the other being the southern elephant seal. It is a member of the family Phocidae (true seals) and is named for the large proboscis of the adult male, which resembles an elephant's trunk and is used to produce loud roaring sounds, especially during the mating season.

Description

Northern elephant seals exhibit extreme sexual dimorphism. Adult males can reach 4–5 metres (13–16 ft) in length and weigh 1,500–2,300 kg (3,300–5,100 lb), and possess an inflatable proboscis and a thick chest shield of calloused skin. Females are much smaller, typically about 3 metres (10 ft) long and 400–900 kg (880–1,980 lb), lacking the proboscis.

Distribution and behaviour

The species ranges across the eastern North Pacific, breeding on islands and mainland beaches from Baja California, Mexico, to British Columbia, Canada. Major rookeries include Año Nuevo and Piedras Blancas in California and Isla Guadalupe in Mexico. Elephant seals spend most of the year — up to 8–10 months — at sea, making repeated deep dives of 300–800 metres lasting around 20 minutes to feed on squid, fish and sharks, and coming ashore twice a year to breed and to moult.

Conservation

Hunted nearly to extinction for their oil-rich blubber in the 19th century, the population may have fallen to fewer than 100 individuals, all on Isla Guadalupe. Protected by Mexico and the United States, the species recovered dramatically, and the population now exceeds 200,000, though the genetic bottleneck left the population with low genetic diversity. It is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
