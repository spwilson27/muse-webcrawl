River Weaver

River Weaver
Location: Cheshire, England
Length: approx. 96 km (60 miles)
Source: Peckforton Hills, Cheshire
Mouth: River Mersey, near Frodsham

The River Weaver is a river in Cheshire, England, that flows for about 96 kilometres (60 miles) from its source in the Peckforton Hills to its confluence with the River Mersey near Frodsham. The river passes through or near the towns of Nantwich, Winsford, and Northwich. Its name is thought to derive from an Old English word meaning "winding stream".

Course

The Weaver rises in the Peckforton Hills in southern Cheshire and flows northwards through Audlem and Nantwich. It continues past Winsford, where the river was historically broadened into the Winsford Flashes — lakes formed by subsidence caused by salt extraction — then flows on to Northwich, where it is joined by the River Dane, its principal tributary. Below Northwich the river passes under the Anderton Boat Lift and enters the Weaver Navigation, before joining the Manchester Ship Canal and ultimately the River Mersey at Weston Marsh.

Weaver Navigation

The lower part of the river was made navigable in the 18th century to serve the salt industry of mid-Cheshire. Acts of Parliament in 1720 led to the Weaver Navigation, with locks and cuts that allowed vessels carrying salt to reach Liverpool via the Mersey. The navigation was later enlarged and remains navigable for leisure craft as far as Winsford. The Anderton Boat Lift, built in 1875, connects the navigation with the Trent and Mersey Canal and is a notable feat of Victorian engineering.

Salt industry

The valley of the Weaver was central to the British salt industry, with brine extraction and salt works at Nantwich, Winsford, and Northwich. Land subsidence from brine pumping created the "flashes" along the river, now valued as wildlife habitats and sites for sailing and angling.
