Hundred Days

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hundred Days (French: les Cent-Jours), also known as the War of the Seventh Coalition, was the period between Napoleon Bonaparte's return from exile on the island of Elba to Paris on 20 March 1815 and the second restoration of King Louis XVIII on 8 July 1815 (a period of 110 days). Napoleon, who had abdicated in 1814, landed in southern France on 1 March with about a thousand men; troops sent to arrest him — including his old marshal Ney, who had promised to bring him back "in an iron cage" — joined him instead, and he entered Paris without a shot.

Waterloo and aftermath

The European powers at the Congress of Vienna declared Napoleon an outlaw and mobilised the Seventh Coalition. Napoleon struck first into Belgium, defeating the Prussians at Ligny and the Anglo-Allied army's rearguard at Quatre Bras, but on 18 June 1815 he was decisively beaten at the Battle of Waterloo by Wellington's Anglo-Allied army and Blücher's arriving Prussians. Hindered by rain-soaked ground, divided command and the stubborn defence of Hougoumont and La Haye Sainte, the French army broke in the evening; Napoleon abdicated four days later. The restored Bourbons and the allies occupied Paris; Napoleon surrendered to the British and was exiled to remote Saint Helena, where he died in 1821. The Hundred Days closed 23 years of Revolutionary and Napoleonic wars and opened a century of (relative) great-power peace in Europe.
