Cyclic guanosine monophosphate

Cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) is a cyclic nucleotide derived from guanosine triphosphate (GTP). Like its relative cyclic AMP, cGMP acts as a second messenger in intracellular signal transduction, relaying signals from cell-surface and gaseous messengers to downstream effector proteins.

Synthesis and degradation

cGMP is synthesised by guanylate cyclase enzymes. One form is activated by the signalling gas nitric oxide (NO); another, found in intestinal and retinal cells, is activated by peptide hormones such as guanylin and by bacterial enterotoxins. cGMP is broken down by phosphodiesterases (PDEs). Inhibition of the PDE5 enzyme, which degrades cGMP in vascular smooth muscle, is the mechanism of drugs such as sildenafil.

Functions

In vascular smooth muscle, cGMP activates protein kinase G (PKG), which lowers intracellular calcium and causes relaxation of the muscle, producing vasodilation — the basis of NO-mediated blood pressure regulation and of treatments for erectile dysfunction and pulmonary hypertension. In the retina, cGMP keeps ion channels open in photoreceptor cells; light triggers its hydrolysis, closing the channels and generating the visual signal. In the intestine, cGMP regulates fluid and electrolyte secretion, a pathway exploited by the drug linaclotide.

Effectors

The principal effectors of cGMP are protein kinase G, cGMP-gated ion channels, and cGMP-regulated phosphodiesterases, which create cross-talk with the cAMP pathway.
