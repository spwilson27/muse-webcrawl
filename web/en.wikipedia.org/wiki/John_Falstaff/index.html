Sir John Falstaff is a fictional character who appears in three plays by William Shakespeare and is mentioned in a fourth. A fat, vain, boastful and cowardly knight, he leads the young Prince Hal and other characters in tavern scenes in Henry IV, Part 1 and Part 2, and is the would-be seducer in The Merry Wives of Windsor. His death is movingly described in Henry V.

The Henriad

In the Henry IV plays, Falstaff is the companion of Prince Hal (the future Henry V) in the taverns of Eastcheap, centred on the Boar's Head Inn and its hostess Mistress Quickly. He robs travellers at Gad's Hill, lies extravagantly about it, plays dead at the Battle of Shrewsbury, and debates honour in the famous "catechism" speech. At the end of Part 2, the newly crowned Henry V rejects him: "I know thee not, old man."

Origins

The character appears to have been renamed from Sir John Oldcastle, a real Lollard knight whose descendants objected; Shakespeare's epilogue to Part 2 insists "Oldcastle died a martyr, and this is not the man." Falstaff may also draw on the Vice of morality plays and on Robert Greene.

Legacy

Falstaff is among Shakespeare's most popular creations. He is the central figure of Verdi's final opera Falstaff (1893), Vaughan Williams's Sir John in Love, Holst's At the Boar's Head, and Orson Welles's film Chimes at Midnight (1965). Harold Bloom praised him as representing "a kind of immortality" of wit and vitality.
