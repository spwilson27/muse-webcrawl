Paradox

The Penrose triangle, a visual paradox

A paradox is a statement, argument or situation that, despite apparently sound reasoning from apparently true premises, leads to a conclusion that seems self-contradictory, absurd or impossible. Paradoxes can expose hidden errors in reasoning, reveal limits of concepts and theories, or simply play on ambiguity in language. The word comes from the Greek paradoxos, "contrary to expectation".

Classification

The philosopher W. V. Quine distinguished three kinds. A veridical paradox yields a result that seems impossible but is true — as in the Banach–Tarski theorem or the birthday problem. A falsidical paradox seems to prove something false but rests on a concealed fallacy, such as many false proofs that 1 = 2. An antinomy is a genuine contradiction arising from accepted modes of reasoning — the deepest kind, forcing revisions of theory.

Famous examples

Zeno's paradoxes argue against motion and plurality. The liar paradox ("this sentence is false") and Russell's paradox of the set of all sets that do not contain themselves shook the foundations of logic and mathematics. In physics, the twin paradox of relativity and the grandfather paradox of time travel remain staples of discussion. Economics and decision theory contribute the paradox of thrift, the Allais and Ellsberg paradoxes; statistics offers Simpson's paradox; probability the Monty Hall problem and two-envelopes paradox.

Role in inquiry

From Greek philosophy through Gödel's incompleteness theorems — which exploit self-reference in the manner of the liar — paradoxes have repeatedly driven conceptual progress by showing where intuitions or formal systems break down.
