Plant stem

The plant stem is one of the two main structural axes of a vascular plant, the other being the root. It supports leaves, flowers, and fruits, transports water, minerals, and photosynthates between roots and shoots, and in many species stores nutrients or carries out photosynthesis itself.

Structure

A stem is divided into nodes, where leaves and buds attach, and internodes between them. Internally, conducting tissues — xylem (carrying water upward) and phloem (carrying sugars) — are arranged in vascular bundles: in a ring in most dicots, scattered through the ground tissue in monocots such as grasses and palms. Growth in length comes from apical meristems at the stem tip and buds; growth in thickness in woody plants comes from the vascular cambium, which produces new xylem inward (accumulating as wood, with visible annual rings) and new phloem outward, beneath the bark produced by the cork cambium.

Modifications and uses

Stems are widely modified: rhizomes, tubers (potatoes), bulbs, and corms store food underground; runners and stolons spread plants horizontally; tendrils climb; thorns defend; and the succulent stems of cacti store water and photosynthesise. Stems are economically important as timber, paper pulp, fibres (flax, hemp, jute), sugar (sugarcane), cinnamon bark, and vegetables such as asparagus and celery, and are the axis manipulated in grafting, pruning, and taking cuttings.
