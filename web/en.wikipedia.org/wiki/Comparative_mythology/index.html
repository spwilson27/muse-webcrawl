Comparative mythology

Comparative mythology is the comparison of myths from different cultures in an attempt to identify shared themes and characteristics. Comparative mythology has served a variety of academic purposes. For example, scholars have used the relationships between different myths to trace the development of religions and cultures, to propose common origins for myths from different cultures, and to support various psychological theories.

Approaches

Different schools of comparative mythology take different approaches. Nineteenth-century comparativists such as Max Müller interpreted myths as distorted reflections of natural phenomena, especially solar mythology. In the 20th century, Georges Dumézil proposed the trifunctional hypothesis for Indo-European myth and society; Claude Lévi-Strauss analysed myth structurally, as a system of oppositions; and Joseph Campbell popularized the idea of the monomyth, or hero's journey, in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949). Other comparativists, following historical linguistics, use the comparative method to reconstruct the mythology of ancestral cultures such as the Proto-Indo-Europeans.

Recurring motifs

Common subjects of comparison include creation myths, flood myths, dying-and-rising gods, trickster figures, and myths of a world tree or cosmic axis.
