Scapegoating

The scapegoat: a goat symbolically laden with the sins of the people (Leviticus 16)

Scapegoating is the practice of singling out a person or group for unmerited blame and consequent negative treatment. The term derives from the ancient Israelite ritual described in Leviticus, in which a goat was symbolically burdened with the sins of the people and driven into the wilderness on Yom Kippur — the goat for Azazel. William Tyndale coined the English word "scapegoat" in his 1530 Bible translation.

Psychological mechanisms

Psychologists explain scapegoating through several mechanisms. Frustration–aggression theory proposes that blocked goals produce aggression that is displaced onto convenient, less powerful targets. Projection involves attributing one's own unacceptable feelings or failings to others. Scapegoating also serves group cohesion: shared blame of an outsider can unify an in-group, a dynamic examined in René Girard's theory of the scapegoat mechanism, in which communal violence against a victim restores social order.

In groups and organisations

In families, family therapists describe an "identified patient" — often a child — who is blamed for the family's problems. In workplaces, scapegoating may target whistleblowers or minority employees, shielding leadership from accountability. Political scapegoating directs public anger at minorities, immigrants, or foreign nations during crises such as epidemics and economic downturns.

Historical examples

  • Medieval pogroms blaming Jewish communities for the Black Death
  • Witch trials in early modern Europe and colonial America
  • The Dreyfus affair in France (1894–1906)
  • Internment and public blame directed at ethnic groups during wartime

Related concepts

Scapegoating is related to bullying, mobbing, victim blaming, moral panic, and the fundamental attribution error. Countering it involves critical evaluation of evidence, protection of minority rights, and institutional checks on collective blame.
