Negative feedback amplifier

A negative feedback amplifier (or feedback amplifier) is an electronic amplifier that subtracts a fraction of its output from its input, so that the negative feedback opposes the original signal. The concept was invented by Harold Stephen Black at Bell Laboratories in 1927 and patented in 1937. Negative feedback is fundamental to modern electronics, control systems, and the design of operational amplifiers.

Principle

If an amplifier has open-loop gain A and a fraction β of the output is fed back and subtracted from the input, the closed-loop gain is approximately A/(1 + Aβ). When the loop gain Aβ is large, the closed-loop gain approaches 1/β, making the overall gain depend mainly on the feedback network rather than on the amplifier itself. This trades excess gain for accuracy and stability.

Advantages

  • Reduced distortion: nonlinearities in the amplifier are suppressed by the loop gain.
  • Extended bandwidth: the gain–bandwidth product is redistributed, widening the usable frequency range.
  • Controlled input and output impedances: feedback topology can raise or lower impedances as required.
  • Desensitivity: gain becomes less dependent on component variations, temperature, and ageing.

Stability

Feedback amplifiers can become unstable and oscillate if phase shifts around the loop convert the feedback from negative to positive at some frequency. Harold Nyquist's stability criterion (1932) and the gain and phase margin concepts developed by Hendrik Bode provide the standard tools for analysing and ensuring stability, typically by compensation techniques.

Applications

Negative feedback is used in audio amplifiers, radio and instrumentation amplifiers, servo systems, voltage regulators, and virtually all operational amplifier circuits, where external resistor networks set precise gains and implement functions such as summing, integration, and filtering.
