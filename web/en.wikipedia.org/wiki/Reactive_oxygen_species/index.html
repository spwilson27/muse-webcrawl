Reactive oxygen species

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive chemicals formed from molecular oxygen. They include free radicals such as superoxide (O2•−) and the hydroxyl radical (•OH), and non-radical oxidants such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and singlet oxygen. ROS are natural by-products of aerobic metabolism — especially electron leakage from the mitochondrial electron transport chain and the activity of enzymes such as NADPH oxidases — and also arise from radiation, pollution and redox-active drugs.

Biological roles

At controlled levels ROS are essential signalling molecules: hydrogen peroxide regulates growth-factor signalling, vascular tone, insulin action and immune function. Phagocytes deliberately generate a "respiratory burst" of superoxide via NADPH oxidase to kill engulfed microbes; patients lacking this enzyme suffer chronic granulomatous disease. Plants use ROS in defence signalling and stomatal closure.

Damage and oxidative stress

When ROS production overwhelms antioxidant defences, the result is oxidative stress: radicals attack lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins and DNA, producing lesions such as 8-oxoguanine. Oxidative stress has been implicated in ageing and in the pathogenesis of cancer, atherosclerosis, neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, diabetes complications and inflammation. Cells defend themselves with enzymes — superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase — and small-molecule antioxidants including glutathione and vitamins C and E.

Measurement and therapy

ROS are difficult to measure directly because of their short lifetimes; researchers use fluorescent probes (e.g. DCFH-DA), spin-trapping electron paramagnetic resonance, and biomarkers of oxidative damage. Despite the oxidative-stress theory's popularity, large trials of antioxidant supplements have mostly failed to show benefit and sometimes showed harm, leading researchers to view ROS as Janus-faced: damaging in excess, indispensable in physiology, and deliberately exploited in some cancer therapies that raise tumour ROS beyond lethal thresholds.
