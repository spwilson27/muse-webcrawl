Adjudication

Adjudication is the legal process by which a judge, arbitrator, or other authorised decision-maker reviews evidence and argumentation, including legal reasoning, set forth by opposing parties to come to a decision determining rights and obligations between the parties involved.

Forms

Adjudication takes several forms depending on the legal system and the kind of dispute:

  • Judicial adjudication — determination of disputes by courts, resulting in a judgment enforceable by the state.
  • Administrative adjudication — determination by government agencies, for example in benefits, licensing, or immigration cases, often through tribunals such as an employment tribunal.
  • Arbitration — a private form of adjudication in which parties agree to be bound by the decision of an arbitrator.
  • Statutory adjudication — a rapid dispute-resolution procedure, notably in United Kingdom construction law, where an adjudicator decides payment disputes quickly, with the decision temporarily binding.

Process

A typical adjudication involves the initiation of a claim, disclosure of evidence, a hearing at which the parties present argument, and a reasoned decision. Decisions are ordinarily subject to appeal or review, although in arbitration the grounds of challenge are much narrower.

Adjudication versus other methods

Adjudication differs from mediation and negotiation in that the outcome is imposed by a third party rather than agreed by the parties. It is valued for producing finality and enforceability, but criticised for cost, formality, and delay, which has encouraged the growth of alternative dispute resolution.
