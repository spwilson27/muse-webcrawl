Plant physiology

Plant physiology is the branch of botany that studies how plants function: the physical and chemical processes by which they capture energy, take up water and nutrients, grow, move, reproduce and respond to their environment. It underlies much of agriculture, horticulture and forestry, and overlaps with biochemistry, cell biology and ecology.

Key processes: photosynthesis, transpiration, transport

Major topics

Photosynthesis and respiration

Photosynthesis converts light energy, water and carbon dioxide into sugars and oxygen in chloroplasts, via light-dependent reactions (producing ATP and NADPH) and the Calvin cycle, which fixes carbon. Variants such as C4 photosynthesis and CAM concentrate CO2 to limit photorespiration in hot or dry conditions. Cellular respiration releases the stored energy for growth and maintenance.

Water and mineral nutrition

Water is absorbed by roots, rises through the xylem largely by the cohesion–tension mechanism driven by transpiration from leaves, and is lost through stomata, whose opening is regulated by guard cells. Plants require macronutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur) and micronutrients (iron, manganese, zinc and others), obtained from soil or, in some species, via symbiotic mycorrhizal fungi or nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

Transport and growth regulation

The phloem translocates sugars and other assimilates from sources (leaves) to sinks (roots, fruits, seeds). Growth and development are coordinated by plant hormones: auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, abscisic acid and ethylene, together with light receptors such as phytochromes that govern photoperiodism, germination and flowering.

Responses and reproduction

Plant physiologists also study tropisms and nastic movements, seed dormancy and germination, vernalisation, senescence, and defence and stress responses to drought, salinity, pathogens and herbivores.

History

The field began with experiments such as Jan Baptist van Helmont's willow-tree weighing (1640s) and Stephen Hales's Vegetable Staticks (1727), and matured in the nineteenth century with Julius von Sachs, who unified the study of plant function in the laboratory.
