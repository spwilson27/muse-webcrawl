Extraterritoriality

Extraterritoriality, or extraterritorial application, is the state of being exempted from the jurisdiction of local law, usually as the result of diplomatic negotiation. Historically it most often applied to individuals and companies; in modern practice it may refer to the exercise of a state's law beyond its territory.

Historical cases

In the Ottoman Empire, capitulations granted Europeans exemption from local courts. In 19th- and early 20th-century China, unequal treaties gave foreign nationals in treaty ports the right to be tried by their own consular courts; similar regimes existed in Japan (ended 1899) and Siam. Extraterritorial rights in China were largely relinquished by the US and UK in 1943.

Modern forms

Diplomatic immunity under the Vienna Convention, status of forces agreements for military bases, and the application of domestic laws abroad — for example U.S. sanctions and anti-corruption law — are modern analogues. International organizations and their premises are also inviolable.
