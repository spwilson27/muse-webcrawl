Herbert George Wells (21 September 1866 – 13 August 1946) was an English writer. Prolific in many genres, he wrote more than fifty novels and dozens of short stories. His non-fiction output included works of social commentary, politics, history, popular science, satire, biography and autobiography. Wells is now best remembered for his science fiction novels and has been called the "father of science fiction".

Early life

Wells was born in Bromley, Kent, to a working-class family; his father was a shopkeeper and professional cricketer and his mother a lady's maid. A childhood accident that left him bedridden sparked his love of reading. He was apprenticed as a draper, an experience he drew on in novels such as Kipps, before winning a scholarship to the Normal School of Science in London, where he studied biology under Thomas Henry Huxley. He later worked as a teacher and journalist.

Science fiction

Wells's early books, called "scientific romances", invented a number of themes now classic in science fiction. The Time Machine (1895) introduced time travel by machine; The Island of Doctor Moreau (1896) explored vivisection and human-animal hybrids; The Invisible Man (1897) treated invisibility; and The War of the Worlds (1898) depicted an invasion of Earth by Martians and remains one of the most influential alien-invasion stories ever written. He also foresaw, in various works, aircraft, tanks, space travel, nuclear weapons, satellite television and something resembling the World Wide Web.

Later work and ideas

In his later career Wells concentrated on novels of social realism such as Tono-Bungay (1909), on history — his Outline of History (1920) was a bestseller — and on political advocacy. A lifelong socialist of a Fabian hue, he campaigned for a world state and universal education, and in 1940 published The Rights of Man, an early draft of what became the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. He died in London in 1946.
