Renal cell carcinoma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a kidney cancer that originates in the lining of the proximal convoluted tubule, part of the nephron. It is the most common type of kidney cancer in adults, accounting for roughly 90% of cases. The most common subtype is clear cell RCC, characterised by loss of the VHL tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 3p.

SpecialtyOncology, urology, nephrology
SymptomsBlood in urine, flank pain, abdominal mass, weight loss
Risk factorsSmoking, obesity, hypertension, VHL disease
TreatmentSurgery, targeted therapy, immunotherapy

Signs and symptoms

The classic triad of haematuria (blood in urine), flank pain and a palpable abdominal mass occurs in a minority of patients; many cases are now found incidentally on imaging performed for other reasons. RCC is known for paraneoplastic syndromes, including hypercalcaemia, polycythaemia and fever, and for unusual patterns of metastasis to the lungs, bones and brain. It also has a tendency to grow into the renal vein and inferior vena cava.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by contrast-enhanced CT imaging, with biopsy reserved for selected cases. Localised disease is treated surgically by partial or radical nephrectomy; small tumours may be managed by ablation or active surveillance. Advanced disease, historically resistant to chemotherapy, is treated with VEGF-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (such as sunitinib and pazopanib), mTOR inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors such as nivolumab, often in combination regimens.
