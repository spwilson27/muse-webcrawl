Whale

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Whales are fully aquatic marine mammals of the infraorder Cetacea, which also includes dolphins and porpoises. They descend from land mammals that returned to the sea about 50 million years ago — Fossil intermediates such as Pakicetus and Ambulocetus document the transition — and are the closest living relatives of hippopotamuses. The blue whale is the largest animal known ever to have lived, reaching around 30 metres and 150+ tonnes.

Whales divide into baleen whales (mysticetes), which filter krill and small fish through baleen plates — blue, humpback, right and gray whales — and toothed whales (odontocetes), which hunt with echolocation, including sperm whales, orcas and the dolphins. All breathe air, are warm-blooded, suckle their young, and many undertake the longest migrations of any mammal.

Whaling and recovery

Industrial whaling in the 19th–20th centuries killed millions of large whales and drove several species to the edge of Extinction. The International Whaling Commission's moratorium (1986) allowed some populations, notably humpbacks, to recover strongly; others, like the North Atlantic right whale (a few hundred left), remain critical. Japan, Norway and Iceland continue limited whaling under various arrangements, an ongoing controversy.

Intelligence and culture

Whales and dolphins have large brains, complex social lives and learned vocal traditions — humpback song that changes seasonally across oceans, orca dialects and hunting specialisations passed between generations — among the clearest cases of animal culture.
