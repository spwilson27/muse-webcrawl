Social engineering (security)

In the context of information security, social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information. Rather than attacking technology directly, a social engineer exploits human trust, helpfulness, fear or curiosity — often described as hacking the "human operating system". The term was popularised in security circles by former hacker Kevin Mitnick, whose book The Art of Deception (2002) catalogued such techniques.

Techniques

  • Phishing — fraudulent messages, usually email, impersonating a trusted sender to harvest credentials or deliver malware; targeted variants include spear phishing and "whaling" against executives.
  • Pretexting — inventing a scenario (the pretext), such as posing as IT support or an auditor, to extract information.
  • Baiting — leaving an infected USB drive or tempting download where a victim will find and use it.
  • Tailgating — following an authorised person through a secured door without credentials.
  • Vishing and smishing — voice and SMS equivalents of phishing.
  • Quid pro quo — offering a service or benefit in exchange for access or information.

Defence

Because social engineering targets people rather than systems, defences centre on security awareness training, strict identity-verification and call-back procedures, multi-factor authentication, and organisational cultures in which challenging an unusual request is safe. Penetration testers routinely include social engineering in "red team" engagements to test these human defences.
