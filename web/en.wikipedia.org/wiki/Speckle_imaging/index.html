Speckle imaging

Speckle imaging is a family of techniques in observational astronomy used to recover high-resolution images from ground-based telescopes despite the blurring effects of atmospheric turbulence. By taking many short-exposure images and processing them computationally, astronomers can approach the diffraction limit of the telescope, far exceeding the resolution imposed by astronomical "seeing".

Principle

Atmospheric turbulence distorts incoming wavefronts on timescales of milliseconds. A long exposure averages these distortions into a blurred disc, but a very short exposure (10–20 ms) "freezes" the turbulence, producing an image broken into many small bright patches called speckles. Each speckle is an interference pattern carrying information about the object at the telescope's diffraction limit. Statistical processing of thousands of such frames can reconstruct a high-resolution image.

Techniques

Speckle interferometry, proposed by Antoine Labeyrie in 1970, computes the power spectrum of many short exposures to recover the object's autocorrelation, which yields separations and brightness ratios of binary stars but not a full image. Later methods recovered phase information: the Knox–Thompson algorithm and bispectrum (triple correlation) analysis allow true image reconstruction. "Shift-and-add" and lucky imaging select and align the sharpest frames.

Applications

Speckle imaging has been used to measure the orbits of thousands of binary stars, resolve the surfaces and surroundings of nearby giant stars, detect faint companions and exoplanet host-star companions, and image asteroids. Modern speckle cameras on telescopes such as Gemini Observatory's 'Alopeke and Zorro instruments achieve resolutions of tens of milliarcseconds and are used to vet exoplanet candidates found by missions such as Kepler and TESS.
