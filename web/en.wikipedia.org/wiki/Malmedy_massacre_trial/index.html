The Malmedy massacre trial (officially United States vs. Valentin Bersin et al.) was a war crimes trial held from May to July 1946 at the Dachau internment camp in occupied Germany. Seventy-four members of the Waffen-SS, most of them from Kampfgruppe Peiper of the 1st SS Panzer Division Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler, were tried by a United States military court for their part in the Malmedy massacre of 17 December 1944 and related killings during the Battle of the Bulge.

Background

On 17 December 1944, at the Baugnez crossroads near Malmedy in Belgium, soldiers of Kampfgruppe Peiper machine-gunned a group of American prisoners of war, killing 84 men of Battery B, 285th Field Artillery Observation Battalion. Peiper's unit was also accused of murdering American prisoners at other locations along its route and of killing more than 100 Belgian civilians. News of the massacre spread rapidly among American forces and hardened attitudes toward the SS.

The trial

The trial opened on 16 May 1946. The prosecution's case rested heavily on sworn statements taken from the accused during interrogations at Schwäbisch Hall. The chief prosecutor was Colonel Burton F. Ellis; the defence was led by Colonel Willis M. Everett Jr. On 16 July 1946 the court convicted 73 of the 74 defendants. Forty-three, including Joachim Peiper and former SS general Josef "Sepp" Dietrich, were sentenced to death, 22 to life imprisonment, and the remainder to prison terms of ten to twenty years. One defendant was acquitted.

Controversy and reviews

Everett pursued appeals alleging that confessions had been extracted by mock trials, solitary confinement and other improper interrogation methods. A review by the Simpson Commission in 1948 found some interrogation techniques improper but rejected claims of systematic torture. Amid Cold War political pressure and lobbying in Germany, the death sentences were progressively commuted; Peiper was released in December 1956, the last of the convicted men to leave Landsberg Prison.
