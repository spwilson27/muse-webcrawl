The Conseil d'État (French: [kɔ̃sɛj deta]; "Council of State") is a French public body established by Napoleon Bonaparte in 1799 which serves two functions: it is the supreme court for administrative justice, hearing appeals against acts of the government and the administration, and it is the government's chief legal adviser, reviewing draft legislation and decrees before they are enacted. It sits in the Palais-Royal in Paris.

History

The Conseil descends from the medieval king's council (curia regis) and the Conseil du Roi of the Ancien Régime. Napoleon recreated it in the Constitution of Year VIII (1799), and its members drafted the Civil Code of 1804. Given judicial independence progressively during the nineteenth century — landmark rulings such as Blanco (1873) founded French administrative law as a body separate from ordinary civil law — the Conseil d'État became, and remains, the apex of France's dual court system.

Functions

As legal adviser, the Conseil gives opinions on all government bills and major decrees; its advice, long secret, has been published since 2015. As a court, it acts as first and last instance for the most important administrative disputes (challenges to decrees, ministerial decisions and elections) and as a court of cassation over the administrative courts of appeal. Its membership of roughly 300 — auditeurs, maîtres des requêtes and conseillers d'État — is recruited mainly from the top graduates of the national administration school (ENA, now INSP). The Prime Minister is its nominal president; in practice it is led by its vice-president.

Notable influence

French-style councils of state were imitated in many countries, and the Conseil's jurisprudence on public liability, public service and the legality of administrative action remains foundational to administrative law worldwide. During the COVID-19 pandemic and states of emergency, its urgent applications judge (juge des référés) played a prominent role reviewing government restrictions.
