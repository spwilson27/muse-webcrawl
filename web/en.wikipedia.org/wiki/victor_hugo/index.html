Victor-Marie Hugo (26 February 1802 – 22 May 1885) was a French Romantic author, poet, essayist, playwright, and politician. During a literary career that spanned more than sixty years, he wrote in a variety of genres and forms. He is considered to be one of the greatest writers of all time, and his fame abroad rests principally on his novels The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831) and Les Misérables (1862).

Life

Hugo was born in Besançon, the youngest son of Joseph Léopold Sigisbert Hugo, a general in Napoleon's army. A leading figure of French Romanticism after the success of his play Hernani (1830), he was elected to the Académie française in 1841. Exiled by Napoleon III after the coup of 1851, he spent nearly two decades in the Channel Islands, first in Jersey and then in Guernsey, where he wrote Les Misérables. He returned to Paris in triumph in 1870.

Works and legacy

His poetry collections include Les Contemplations (1856) and La Légende des siècles (1859). Hugo campaigned against the death penalty and championed social causes throughout his life. His state funeral in 1885 drew millions of mourners; he is buried in the Panthéon. See also Romanticism.
