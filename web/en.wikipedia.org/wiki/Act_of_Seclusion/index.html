Act of Seclusion

The Act of Seclusion (Dutch: Acte van Seclusie) was a secret annex to the Treaty of Westminster of 1654, by which the States of Holland, led by Grand Pensionary Johan de Witt, undertook to exclude the young Prince William of Orange — the future William III — from the office of stadtholder of Holland.

Background

The Treaty of Westminster ended the First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–1654) between the Dutch Republic and the English Commonwealth under Oliver Cromwell. Cromwell, fearing that the House of Orange — related by marriage to the exiled Stuart royal family — might return to power in the person of William, the posthumous son of William II, pressed for a guarantee that the young prince would never become stadtholder or captain-general.

Terms and adoption

Because the demand touched the sovereignty of the provinces, it was kept secret from the States General and embodied in a separate act adopted by the States of Holland on 4 May 1654. In it, Holland pledged that it would never elect William or his descendants as stadtholder of the province, nor support his appointment as captain-general or admiral-general of the Republic.

Consequences

The act, once leaked, provoked outrage among Orangists, who saw it as a national humiliation dictated by a foreign ruler. De Witt defended it in his famous Deduction of 1654, arguing it served the interest of Dutch freedom. The Act of Seclusion became a cornerstone of the "True Freedom" of the First Stadtholderless Period. After the Restoration of Charles II, pressure mounted to annul it, and in 1660 the States of Holland formally abolished the act — though the exclusion of the Prince of Orange was pursued by other means, notably the Perpetual Edict of 1667, until the crisis year 1672 swept the republican regime away.
