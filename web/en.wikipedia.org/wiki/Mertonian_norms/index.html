Mertonian norms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Mertonian norms are the four rules — communism, universalism, disinterestedness and organised scepticism — that the American sociologist Robert K. Merton described in 1942 as comprising the "ethos of modern science". They are often remembered by the acronym CUDOS (Communism, Universalism, Disinterestedness, Organized Scepticism). Merton argued that these institutional imperatives, rather than merely the individual scientist's character, sustain science as a self-correcting social institution.

The four norms

  • Communism (later often called communalism): scientific discoveries are common heritage; secrecy is the antithesis of science, and scientists owe their findings to the community, receiving recognition and esteem in return.
  • Universalism: claims to truth are evaluated by pre-established impersonal criteria, independent of the race, class, religion or nationality of their proponents.
  • Disinterestedness: scientists are accountable to their peers and should pursue knowledge rather than personal gain; fraud is held in check by rigorous policing by fellow experts.
  • Organised scepticism: science involves the suspension of judgment and the detached scrutiny of beliefs by empirical and logical criteria; nothing is sacred from inquiry.

Discussion

Merton presented the norms in his 1942 essay "Science and Technology in a Democratic Order" (later republished as "The Normative Structure of Science"), written partly in reaction to the politicisation of science under Nazism and Stalinism. Later sociologists, notably Ian Mitroff, documented "counter-norms" (particularism, interestedness, organised dogmatism) that scientists also invoke, and historians have treated CUDOS as an idealisation of mid-20th-century academic science rather than a description of all scientific practice. Nonetheless the norms remain the canonical statement of the scientific ethos and a reference point in debates about open science and commercialisation.
