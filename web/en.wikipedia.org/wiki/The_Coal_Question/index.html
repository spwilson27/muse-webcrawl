AuthorWilliam Stanley Jevons
Published1865
SubjectEconomics, energy, resource depletion

The Coal Question: An Inquiry Concerning the Progress of the Nation, and the Probable Exhaustion of Our Coal-Mines is a book by the English economist William Stanley Jevons, published in 1865. It argued that British industrial prosperity rested on cheap coal, that consumption was growing exponentially, and that the country's economically recoverable reserves would be exhausted within roughly a century, with rising costs arriving much sooner.

Argument

Jevons observed that British coal consumption had multiplied decade by decade alongside population, iron production, and steam power. Extrapolating this exponential growth, he calculated that reserves workable at acceptable cost would not long sustain it. He rejected the notion that improved efficiency would save coal, arguing the opposite: more efficient steam engines lowered the cost of power and so increased total consumption — the proposition now known as the Jevons paradox, an early statement of the rebound effect.

He also dismissed substitutes such as petroleum and electricity as incapable of replacing coal's role, and warned that rising coal prices would erode Britain's manufacturing supremacy and transfer industry to rivals such as the United States.

Influence

The book attracted wide attention, including from Prime Minister William Gladstone, and prompted a Royal Commission on coal supplies. It is regarded as a founding work of energy economics and of the economics of exhaustible resources, foreshadowing later analyses such as the Hubbert peak theory of oil production. Although the timing of British coal's decline differed from Jevons's projections — output peaked in 1913 for economic and geological reasons broadly related to his argument — the book remains a landmark in thinking about growth and finite resources.
