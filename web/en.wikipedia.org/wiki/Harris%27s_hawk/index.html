FamilyAccipitridae
RangeSouthwestern United States to Chile and Argentina

The Harris's hawk (Parabuteo unicinctus), formerly known as the bay-winged hawk, is a medium-large bird of prey that breeds from the southwestern United States south through Mexico and Central America to much of South America. It is notable for its cooperative hunting behaviour, unusual among raptors.

Description

Harris's hawks are dark brown overall with chestnut shoulders, thighs, and wing linings, a white rump and tail base, and a white tip to the tail. They are 46–59 cm long with a wingspan of about 103–120 cm. Females are noticeably larger than males. The species is named after the American naturalist Edward Harris, a companion of John James Audubon.

Behaviour

Unlike most birds of prey, Harris's hawks live in social groups of two to seven individuals, typically an extended family. Groups hunt cooperatively, with members taking turns chasing and ambushing prey such as rabbits, rodents, and birds, which increases their hunting success. They also engage in "back-standing", perching stacked one on another on cacti or poles.

Falconry

Because of their intelligence, trainability, and social disposition, Harris's hawks are among the most popular birds in falconry, and they are widely used for bird control at airports and other sites.
