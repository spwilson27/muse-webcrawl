Daguerreotype

InventorLouis Daguerre
Announced1839
MediumSilver-plated copper plate
In common usec. 1839–1860

The daguerreotype was the first publicly available photographic process; it was widely used during the 1840s and 1850s. Invented by Louis-Jacques-Mandé Daguerre, working with the legacy of Nicéphore Niépce, the process creates a unique image on a silvered copper plate with a mirror-like surface. The French government purchased the patent and released the invention "free to the world" in August 1839.

The process

A copper plate thinly coated with silver is polished to a mirror finish and sensitized with iodine vapour, forming light-sensitive silver iodide. The plate is exposed in a camera — early exposures took several minutes, though improved lenses and chemical acceleration soon reduced sittings to under a minute. The latent image is then developed over heated mercury vapour, fixed with sodium thiosulfate, and often gilded with gold chloride to improve tone and durability. Because there is no negative, each daguerreotype is a unique object, and the image appears laterally reversed unless a mirror or prism is used.

Popularity and use

Daguerreotype portrait studios proliferated in Europe and the United States during the 1840s; in America the process was embraced with particular enthusiasm, and millions of portraits were produced annually by the early 1850s. The images were commonly mounted in hinged leather or thermoplastic cases lined with velvet. Notable practitioners included Southworth and Hawes in Boston and Mathew Brady, while in Britain Richard Beard opened Europe's first daguerreotype portrait studio in 1841.

Decline

The daguerreotype was gradually displaced during the 1850s by cheaper and more convenient processes — the ambrotype, the tintype, and above all the albumen print from glass negatives, which allowed multiple copies. By about 1860 it had fallen out of mainstream commercial use, though the process has been revived by a small number of contemporary artists.
