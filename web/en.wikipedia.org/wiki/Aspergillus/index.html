Aspergillus is a genus of several hundred species of mould (fungi) found worldwide in many climates. The genus was first catalogued in 1729 by the Italian priest and biologist Pier Antonio Micheli, who named it for the resemblance of its spore-bearing structures to an aspergillum, the brush used to sprinkle holy water. Aspergilli are common in soil, decaying vegetation, dust and indoor air, and grow as networks of hyphae producing asexual spores (conidia) on characteristic stalked heads.

Importance

Some species are industrially valuable: Aspergillus oryzae is used to ferment soy sauce, sake and miso, and A. niger produces most of the world's citric acid and many commercial enzymes. Others spoil food and produce potent toxins, notably the aflatoxins made by A. flavus, among the most powerful known natural carcinogens.

Disease

Inhaled spores are normally cleared by the immune system, but in immunocompromised people A. fumigatus and other species can cause invasive aspergillosis, a frequently fatal infection. In people with asthma or cystic fibrosis the fungus can trigger allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, and it can colonise old lung cavities to form a fungal ball, or aspergilloma — conditions grouped under aspergillosis.
