A clear-channel station is an AM radio station in North America that has the highest protection from interference from other stations on the same frequency. Most such stations transmit at the maximum permitted power of 50,000 watts. Their signals, especially at night when medium-wave skywave propagation carries them hundreds or thousands of miles, can serve vast rural areas — the original purpose of the regulatory scheme.

History

The clear-channel system grew out of the Federal Radio Commission's General Order 40 of 1928, which reorganized the AM band and reserved certain frequencies for a single dominant high-power station, intended to deliver programming to sparsely populated regions with no local service. The Federal Communications Commission maintained the system after 1934. In 1980 the FCC voted to "break down" the clear channels, permitting additional stations to operate on formerly protected frequencies with reduced power and directional antennas at night.

Classes

Under the current North American arrangement, Class A stations (formerly Class I) are the unlimited-time, dominant clear-channel stations. Class B and D stations may share their frequencies subject to protecting the Class A station's nighttime skywave service area. Canada and Mexico also maintain clear-channel allocations under regional agreements (the North American Regional Broadcasting Agreement).

Famous stations

Historic clear-channel "blowtorches" became cultural institutions: WSM in Nashville carried the Grand Ole Opry across much of the continent; WLW in Cincinnati briefly experimented with 500,000 watts in the 1930s; border-adjacent super-power Mexican stations such as XERA famously exceeded even those levels. Farm reports, religious broadcasts, and nighttime sports reached audiences across dozens of states from single transmitters.
