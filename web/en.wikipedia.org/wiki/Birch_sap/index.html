Birch sap, or birch water, is the sap directly obtained from birch trees such as the silver birch, river birch, and paper birch. It is collected in early spring, when root pressure drives nutrient-rich xylem sap upward before the leaves unfold, and drunk as a traditional tonic beverage across northern Eurasia and, increasingly, as a commercial health drink.

Collection

A small hole is drilled into the trunk and a spile or tube inserted; a healthy mature tree may yield several litres per day during a flow season of a few weeks. The hole is plugged after tapping to protect the tree. A single tree typically provides sap for a household's spring consumption without lasting harm, though repeated intensive tapping is avoided.

Composition and uses

Fresh birch sap is a clear, slightly sweet liquid containing sugars (mainly glucose and fructose, at far lower concentration than maple sap), amino acids, enzymes, and minerals including potassium, calcium, magnesium, and manganese. It spoils within days unless refrigerated or pasteurized, and can be fermented into birch wine or beer, or boiled down—impractically large volumes are required—into birch syrup. In folk medicine it has been used as a diuretic and spring "blood purifier"; modern markets sell it as birch water, a low-calorie alternative to coconut water.
