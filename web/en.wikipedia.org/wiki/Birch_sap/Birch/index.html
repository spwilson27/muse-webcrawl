Birch as the source of birch sap

Birches in early spring, the tapping season for birch sap.

Within the subject of birch sap, the birch tree is the producer: birch sap exists as a drink and commodity only because of how birches move water and stored carbohydrates through their trunks each spring.

Sap flow in birch

Birches are among the genera that develop strong positive root pressure in late winter and early spring. Before leaf-out, the tree hydrolyzes starch stored in its roots and trunk into sugars, and osmotic uptake of soil water generates pressure that drives xylem sap upward to swell the buds. This flow — lasting roughly two to three weeks and ending when leaves open — is what tappers intercept. Species commonly tapped include silver birch (Betula pendula) and downy birch (B. pubescens) in Europe, paper birch (B. papyrifera) and yellow birch (B. alleghaniensis) in North America, and Erman's birch (B. ermanii) in East Asia.

Effect on the tree

A single taphole of a few centimetres' depth removes only a small fraction of a mature tree's rising sap, and the tree seals the wound with new growth within a season or two. Nevertheless, sap is the tree's own spring energy reserve: guidelines in tapping countries typically recommend one tap for a medium trunk, rest years between tappings, and plugging holes after the season. Dead or stressed trees are not tapped, since diversion of sap can worsen decline. Commercial birch sap production in Latvia, Finland, Russia and China therefore depends on managing whole birch stands, not merely the harvest from individual trees.
