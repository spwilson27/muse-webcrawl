Elephanta Island


The Trimurti sculpture in the main Elephanta cave

Elephanta Island (also called Gharapuri, "city of caves") is one of a number of islands in Mumbai Harbour, east of Mumbai, India. It is best known for the Elephanta Caves, a complex of rock-cut cave temples dedicated mainly to the Hindu god Shiva, which were designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

Name and geography

The island covers about 16 km² (6 sq mi) at high tide and lies roughly 10 kilometres (6.2 mi) east of the Gateway of India, reachable by an approximately one-hour ferry ride. Portuguese explorers of the sixteenth century named it Elephanta after a large stone statue of an elephant they found there; that statue was later moved and now stands in Mumbai's Dr. Bhau Daji Lad Museum.

The caves

The cave complex consists of two groups of rock-cut caves hewn into basalt hills, generally dated between the 5th and 8th centuries CE. The great main cave (Cave 1) contains monumental Shaivite sculpture, most famously the 7-metre (23 ft) Trimurti Sadashiva — a three-faced Shiva representing the creator, preserver, and destroyer — along with panels of the marriage of Shiva and Parvati, Shiva as Nataraja, and Ravana lifting Mount Kailasa.

History and residents

The island was ruled successively by various Indian dynasties, then by the Gujarat Sultanate, the Portuguese from 1534, and the British from the eighteenth century. Today a small population of a few thousand lives in three villages on the island, and the caves are a major tourist attraction, with an annual Elephanta Festival of music and dance held in February.
