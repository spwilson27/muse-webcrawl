Nori (Japanese: 海苔) is a dried edible seaweed used in Japanese cuisine, made from red algae species of the genus Pyropia, including P. yezoensis and P. tenera. It is most commonly pressed into sheets and used to wrap sushi rolls and onigiri, or shredded as a garnish. The equivalent product in Korea is gim; in Britain, related wild laver is traditionally made into laverbread in Wales.

Production

Nori farming is a major aquaculture industry in Japan, Korea and China. The algae are grown on nets suspended in shallow bays; the life cycle of Pyropia, with its microscopic shell-boring conchocelis phase, was famously elucidated in 1949 by the British phycologist Kathleen Drew-Baker, whose work revived the Japanese industry after a series of failed harvests — she is honoured in Japan as the "Mother of the Sea". Harvested fronds are chopped, washed, spread in frames like paper, dried, and graded; top-grade nori is dark, glossy and almost black-green.

Culinary use

Nori sheets wrap makizushi and temaki hand rolls and bind onigiri rice balls. Toasted and seasoned nori is eaten as a snack; flakes (aonori is a different, powdered green algae) garnish dishes such as okonomiyaki and yakisoba. Nori is rich in iodine, protein (up to roughly 40% by dry weight), vitamins A and B12 analogues, and umami compounds.

History

Seaweed was taxed and offered as tribute in Japan as early as the 8th century; sheet nori production developed in Asakusa, Edo, during the 18th century using papermaking techniques. Japan, Korea and China together produce the vast majority of the world's supply.
