Microsoft Excel

Microsoft Excel is a spreadsheet application developed by Microsoft for Windows, macOS, Android, iOS and the web. It features calculation, graphing tools, pivot tables, and a macro programming language called Visual Basic for Applications (VBA). Excel forms part of the Microsoft 365 / Microsoft Office suite and is the industry-standard spreadsheet program, widely used in business, finance, and data analysis.

History

Excel was first released in 1985 for the Apple Macintosh, two years before its Windows debut in 1987 — a launch famously promoted with the help of Steve Jobs. It succeeded Microsoft's earlier Multiplan. On Windows, Excel overtook the market leader Lotus 1-2-3 in the early 1990s and became a flagship justification for buying the Office suite. Excel 5.0 (1993) introduced VBA, giving users a full programming environment within the spreadsheet. The .xlsx format, based on Office Open XML, arrived with Excel 2007, which also expanded the grid to 1,048,576 rows by 16,384 columns.

Features

Data is organized in workbooks of worksheets: grids of cells identified by column letters and row numbers (e.g. B7). Cells may contain values or formulas that recalculate automatically when their inputs change. Excel provides hundreds of built-in functions (SUM, VLOOKUP and its successor XLOOKUP, IF, INDEX/MATCH), conditional formatting, data validation, sorting and filtering, charts, and pivot tables for summarizing data interactively. Power Query and Power Pivot add data-import and modelling capability, and recent versions integrate Python scripting and AI-assisted analysis.

Impact and criticism

Excel's ubiquity makes it a de facto platform for financial modelling and business processes, but errors in large spreadsheets have caused notable losses and research problems — including the Reinhart–Rogoff coding error debate and the loss of thousands of COVID-19 test results in England in 2020 when an old file format's row limit was exceeded. Studies of operational spreadsheets consistently find high rates of material error, prompting a small discipline of spreadsheet risk management.
