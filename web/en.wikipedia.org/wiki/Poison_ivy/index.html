Poison ivy

Poison ivy (Toxicodendron radicans)
Family: Anacardiaceae
Native range: North America and Asia

Poison ivy is an allergenic flowering plant in the genus Toxicodendron, native to Asia and North America. It is known for causing an irritating, itchy, and sometimes painful rash in people sensitive to urushiol, an oil found in all parts of the plant. Despite its name, poison ivy is not a true ivy (genus Hedera), and its "poison" is an allergen rather than a toxin in the usual sense.

Identification

The plant grows as a groundcover, shrub, or climbing vine. Its compound leaves consist of three almond-shaped leaflets — the basis of the mnemonic "leaves of three, let it be." Leaf colour varies from light green in spring, through dark green in summer, to bright red, orange, or purple in autumn. Mature vines develop clusters of small, dull white berries and thick, hairy aerial rootlets. Related species include western poison ivy (Toxicodendron rydbergii) and eastern poison ivy (T. radicans).

Urushiol-induced contact dermatitis

On contact, urushiol binds to skin proteins and triggers a delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction in sensitised individuals — roughly 85% of adults develop a rash upon sufficient exposure. Symptoms usually appear 12 to 72 hours after contact: redness, swelling, itching, and fluid-filled blisters. The rash is not contagious; blister fluid contains no urushiol. Washing exposed skin promptly with soap and water or specialised washes can reduce severity. Severe cases may require corticosteroids. Scratching does not spread the rash, though urushiol lingering on clothing, tools, or pet fur can cause new exposures, and the oil remains active on dead plants and surfaces for years.

Ecology

Many animals eat poison ivy without ill effect, and its berries are an important winter food for birds, which disperse the seeds. The plant thrives at forest edges and in disturbed ground, and studies suggest it grows more vigorously with elevated atmospheric carbon dioxide.
