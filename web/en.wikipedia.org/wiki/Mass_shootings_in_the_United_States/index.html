Mass shootings in the United States are incidents in which several people are injured or killed by firearm violence in a single event. There is no single agreed definition: the FBI has used a threshold of four victims killed (excluding the perpetrator) for "mass murder", while databases such as the Gun Violence Archive count incidents with four or more people shot, whether injured or killed, producing far higher annual totals.

Definitions and counting

Counts differ dramatically by methodology. Narrow definitions focusing on public, indiscriminate shootings yield a handful of incidents per year; broader definitions that include gang-related and domestic incidents yield several hundred. Researchers and journalists therefore specify their criteria carefully, and debates over the phenomenon often turn on which definition is in use.

History

Although mass shootings occurred throughout American history, the modern era is often dated to the 1966 University of Texas tower shooting, in which Charles Whitman killed 16 people. High-profile incidents in subsequent decades included the 1999 Columbine High School shooting, which shaped public debate about school shootings; the 2007 Virginia Tech shooting; the 2012 Sandy Hook Elementary School shooting; the 2016 Pulse nightclub shooting in Orlando; the 2017 Las Vegas shooting, the deadliest in modern U.S. history; and the 2022 Robb Elementary School shooting in Uvalde, Texas.

Perpetrators and motives

Studies by the U.S. Secret Service's National Threat Assessment Center and others find that most attackers are male, many have histories of grievances, personal crises, or suicidal intent, and many plan their attacks in advance and display concerning behaviors beforehand. Motives range from workplace grievances and domestic conflict to extremist ideology.

Policy debate

Mass shootings are a central flashpoint in the American debate over gun politics. Responses have included federal and state legislation on background checks, assault weapons, and red flag laws, as well as school security measures and threat-assessment programs. The Second Amendment frames the constitutional boundaries of such measures.
