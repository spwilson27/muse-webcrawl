History of Russia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Saint Basil's Cathedral, built under Ivan the Terrible.

The history of Russia begins with the East Slavs and the medieval state of Kievan Rus', founded in the 9th century. After the Mongol invasion of the 13th century, the Grand Duchy of Moscow rose to dominate, becoming the Tsardom of Russia and then, under Peter the Great, the Russian Empire — the largest contiguous empire in the world. The empire collapsed in the revolutions of 1917, giving way to the Soviet Union, which emerged victorious from the Second World War as a superpower before dissolving in 1991. The Russian Federation that followed passed through economic shock therapy, recentralisation under Vladimir Putin, and wars in Chechnya, Georgia and Ukraine.

Kievan Rus' and the Mongol period

According to the Primary Chronicle, the Varangian Rurik founded a dynasty at Novgorod in 862; his successors ruled from Kiev, adopted Orthodox Christianity in 988 under Vladimir the Great, and built a federation of principalities. Mongol armies destroyed Kiev in 1240; the northeast principalities paid tribute to the Golden Horde for two centuries, while Novgorod flourished as a trading republic.

Tsardom and empire

Ivan III of Moscow ended tribute to the Horde in 1480; Ivan IV ("the Terrible") was crowned first Tsar in 1547 and conquered Kazan and Astrakhan. After the Time of Troubles, the Romanov dynasty (from 1613) expanded across Siberia to the Pacific. Peter I (r. 1682–1725) westernised the state, founded St Petersburg and proclaimed the empire in 1721. Catherine the Great extended its borders to the Black Sea and partitioned Poland. The empire defeated Napoleon in 1812, abolished serfdom in 1861 under Alexander II, industrialised under Sergei Witte, and entered the First World War in 1914.

Revolution, Soviet Union, and federation

The February Revolution of 1917 toppled the Tsar; the Bolsheviks seized power in October. Lenin's and then Stalin's USSR collectivised agriculture, industrialised through five-year plans at the cost of famine and the Great Terror, and bore the brunt of defeating Nazi Germany (1941–1945) with some 27 million dead. The Cold War superpower rivalry ended with Gorbachev's perestroika and the USSR's dissolution in December 1991. Under Boris Yeltsin Russia underwent market transition and war in Chechnya; Vladimir Putin (president from 2000) recentralised power, annexed Crimea in 2014, and launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.
