The art and industry of filmmaking in Spain has produced some of world cinema's most distinctive voices, from surrealist pioneer Luis Buñuel to Pedro Almodóvar, the country's most internationally celebrated modern director. Spanish cinema developed through the upheavals of the Civil War, the censorship of the Franco era, and the creative explosion that followed the transition to democracy.

Early years and the Civil War

Film exhibitions reached Spain in 1896 and domestic production began soon after, centred in Barcelona. Segundo de Chomón became a pioneering special-effects artist. The silent era's most famous Spanish production, however, was made in exile: Buñuel and Salvador Dalí's surrealist short Un Chien Andalou (1929) and L'Âge d'Or (1930), made in Paris. The Spanish Civil War (1936–39) devastated the industry and turned film into propaganda on both sides.

Franco era

Under Franco, cinema was heavily censored but subsidised; state-backed studio CIFESA produced lavish historical epics. Dissenting filmmakers worked within constraints: Juan Antonio Bardem (Death of a Cyclist, 1955) and Luis García Berlanga (Welcome Mr. Marshall!, 1953; Plácido, 1961) smuggled social criticism into comedy and drama, while Buñuel returned briefly to make Viridiana (1961), which won the Palme d'Or at Cannes and was banned in Spain. Carlos Saura emerged in the 1960s–70s with allegorical works such as The Hunt (1966) and Cría Cuervos (1976).

Democracy and Almodóvar

Censorship ended after Franco's death, unleashing the countercultural movida madrileña, whose emblematic filmmaker was Pedro Almodóvar (Women on the Verge of a Nervous Breakdown, 1988; All About My Mother, 1999, Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film; Talk to Her, 2002). Other major figures include Víctor Erice (The Spirit of the Beehive, 1973), José Luis Garci (whose Begin the Beguine won Spain's first Oscar in 1982), Fernando Trueba (Belle Époque, 1992), and Alejandro Amenábar (The Others, 2001; The Sea Inside, 2004). Actors such as Javier Bardem and Penélope Cruz achieved global stardom.

Industry today

Spain produces over two hundred features a year, with strong regional cinemas in Catalan and Basque as well as Castilian Spanish, successful popular comedies and thrillers, and internationally recognised genre filmmaking. The Goya Awards, presented by the Spanish Academy since 1987, are the national film honours. Government support, co-production treaties, and global streaming platforms have expanded Spanish-language film's worldwide reach.
