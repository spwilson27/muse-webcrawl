Cerium

A sample of cerium metal.

Cerium is a chemical element; it has symbol Ce and atomic number 58. It is a soft, ductile, and silvery-white metal that tarnishes when exposed to air. Cerium is the most abundant of the rare-earth elements and the second element of the lanthanide series. It was discovered in 1803 by Jöns Jakob Berzelius and Wilhelm Hisinger in Sweden, and independently by Martin Heinrich Klaproth in Germany; it was named after the dwarf planet Ceres, itself named for the Roman goddess of agriculture.

Cerium is unusual among the lanthanides in having an accessible +4 oxidation state in addition to the common +3 state. Cerium(IV) oxide (ceria, CeO2) is a pale yellow-white powder used in polishing compounds, catalytic converters, and as an abrasive, while cerium(III) compounds find use in flints for lighters. The metal is pyrophoric and oxidizes rapidly in air, igniting spontaneously when scratched or struck as part of the alloy misch metal.

Occurrence and production

Despite the "rare earth" label, cerium is about as abundant in Earth's crust as copper. Its principal ores are monazite and bastnäsite, from which it is separated by solvent extraction and ion-exchange techniques. China is the dominant producer of cerium compounds.

Applications

Important uses include ferrocerium lighter flints, self-cleaning oven coatings, glass polishing powders, phosphors, and three-way catalytic converters, where ceria's oxygen-storage capacity helps oxidize carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons. Cerium compounds are also used to decolorize glass and in the mantles of gas lanterns historically prepared with thorium and cerium oxides.

Biological role and safety

Cerium has no known biological role. Its compounds are of low to moderate toxicity; cerium oxide nanoparticles have been studied both as potential antioxidants in medicine and as environmental pollutants from diesel fuel additives.
