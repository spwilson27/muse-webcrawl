Second Empire architecture

Second Empire, in the United States and Canada often called the Second Empire style, is an architectural style most popular in the latter half of the 19th century. It is named for the French Second Empire under Napoleon III, whose ambitious rebuilding of Paris — including the Palais Garnier opera house — set the fashion. The style's signature element is the mansard roof, a steep, nearly vertical roof that allows a full additional storey of living space beneath it.

Characteristics

Second Empire buildings typically combine a mansard roof pierced by dormer windows with classical ornament drawn from French Renaissance and Baroque sources: paired columns, bracketed cornices, quoins, pedimented windows and doors, and often a central tower or pavilion. In North America the style was applied to houses, public buildings and commercial blocks; prominent American examples include the Eisenhower Executive Office Building in Washington, D.C., and Philadelphia's City Hall, one of the largest Second Empire buildings in the world.

History

The style flourished in the United States from about 1855 to 1885, encouraged by pattern books and by the work of architects such as Alfred B. Mullett, who designed numerous federal buildings in the style. Its association with the Grant administration led to the nickname "General Grant style." After the 1880s the style fell from favour, displaced by the Queen Anne style and other picturesque modes, though many Second Empire houses survive in older American and Canadian neighbourhoods.
