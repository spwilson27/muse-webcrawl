Freiburg School

Freiburg School
OriginUniversity of Freiburg, Germany, 1930s
Key figuresWalter Eucken, Franz Böhm, Hans Großmann-Doerth

The Freiburg School (German: Freiburger Schule) was a group of economists and lawyers centred on the University of Freiburg in Germany in the 1930s and 1940s who developed the economic school of thought known as ordoliberalism, the intellectual foundation of West Germany's post-war social market economy.

History and ideas

The school formed around the economist Walter Eucken and the jurists Franz Böhm and Hans Großmann-Doerth, who in 1936/37 issued a joint manifesto, Unsere Aufgabe ("Our Task"), calling for an economic constitution (Wirtschaftsverfassung) in which a strong state sets and enforces the legal framework — stable money, open markets, private property, freedom of contract, liability and consistent policy — within which competition can flourish. The group worked partly in opposition to the Nazi regime; some members had links to resistance circles.

Unlike laissez-faire liberalism, the Freiburg School held that markets do not automatically produce competitive order: monopolies, cartels and other concentrations of private power had to be restrained by the state, just as state power itself had to be limited by law. After 1945 these ideas were developed further in the journal ORDO (founded 1948 by Eucken and Böhm) and influenced Ludwig Erhard's economic policy in the Federal Republic of Germany, where ordoliberalism was blended with social policy into the "social market economy".

Legacy

Ordoliberal principles — sound money, competition policy, an independent central bank and rule-bound economic governance — are often cited as influences on German economic preferences and on the design of European institutions, including the European Central Bank and EU competition law.
