Chromatic adaptation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chromatic adaptation is the ability of the human visual system to discount the color of the illumination and preserve the appearance of objects, a key component of color constancy. A white sheet of paper, for example, looks roughly white both in daylight and under warm tungsten light, even though the light reaching the eye differs greatly in spectral composition.

Mechanism

Adaptation occurs partly in the retina, where the sensitivities of the three cone types are independently scaled (a process modeled by the von Kries transform), and partly through cortical mechanisms. The adjustment is usually incomplete, which is why scenes lit by strongly colored light retain a slight tint.

Chromatic adaptation transforms

In color science, a chromatic adaptation transform (CAT) predicts how a color measured under one illuminant will appear under another. Algorithms such as Bradford and CAT02 are used in color management, ICC profiles and color appearance models such as CIECAM02 to map colors between, for example, the D65 and D50 standard illuminants.

In imaging

Digital cameras mimic chromatic adaptation through automatic white balance, scaling the red, green and blue channels so that neutral objects are recorded as neutral.
