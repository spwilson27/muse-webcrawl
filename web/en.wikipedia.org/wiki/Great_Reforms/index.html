The Great Reforms were a series of sweeping liberal reforms enacted in the Russian Empire during the reign of Emperor Alexander II (1855–1881), prompted above all by Russia's defeat in the Crimean War, which exposed the backwardness of its serf-based economy and military. The centrepiece was the Emancipation Reform of 1861, which abolished serfdom and freed some 23 million privately held serfs, who received personal freedom and land allotments, though on terms — 49 years of "redemption payments" and communal (mir) control of allotments — that many historians regard as burdensome and a source of lasting rural grievance.

Principal reforms

  • Zemstvo reform (1864) — created elected local assemblies (zemstvos) at district and provincial level, responsible for roads, schools, public health, and famine relief.
  • Judicial reform (1864) — established an independent judiciary with jury trials, public proceedings, an organized bar, and justices of the peace; widely considered the most complete and successful of the reforms.
  • Municipal reform (1870) — elected city dumas with responsibility for urban services.
  • Military reform (1874) — under Dmitry Milyutin, replaced 25-year recruitment levies with universal conscription of six years' active service, modernized officer education and the general staff.
  • Education and press — the 1863 university statute restored university autonomy; censorship was relaxed by the 1865 press rules; primary schooling expanded under zemstvo initiatives.

Assessment

The reforms transformed Russian law, local government, and the armed forces but stopped short of national representative institutions. Alexander II's assassination by members of Narodnaya Volya on 13 March 1881 — the day he had approved a modest consultative proposal — was followed by the counter-reforms of Alexander III, which rolled back parts of the zemstvo and judicial settlements. Historians continue to debate whether the reforms, had they continued, could have averted revolution.
