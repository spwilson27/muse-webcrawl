Inverse scattering transform


Field: Mathematical physics
Introduced: Gardner, Greene, Kruskal & Miura (1967)

The inverse scattering transform is a method for solving certain non-linear partial differential equations, discovered in 1967 by Clifford Gardner, John Greene, Martin Kruskal, and Robert Miura in their study of the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation. It is a non-linear analogue of the Fourier transform: instead of decomposing a function into sinusoids, the initial data are converted into scattering data for an associated linear (Sturm–Liouville / Schrödinger) problem, the scattering data evolve in a simple explicit way, and the solution at later times is recovered by solving an inverse problem, typically a Gel'fand–Levitan–Marchenko integral equation.

Method

The scheme has four steps: (1) direct scattering — from the initial condition, compute the reflection coefficient and bound-state (eigenvalue) data; (2) time evolution — advance the scattering data according to simple formulas; (3) inverse scattering — reconstruct the potential from the evolved data; (4) read off the solution. The method explains solitons: discrete eigenvalues correspond to solitary waves that pass through one another unchanged in shape.

Applications

The transform and its generalisations (via Lax pairs and the AKNS / Zakharov–Shabat system) solve the KdV, modified KdV, nonlinear Schrödinger, sine-Gordon, and many other integrable equations, with applications in shallow-water waves, nonlinear optics, and plasma physics.
