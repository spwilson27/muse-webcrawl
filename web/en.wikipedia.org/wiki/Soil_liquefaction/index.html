Soil liquefaction

Soil liquefaction is a phenomenon in which saturated or partially saturated soil substantially loses strength and stiffness in response to applied stress — usually earthquake shaking or other sudden loading — and behaves like a liquid instead of a solid. It occurs mainly in loose, water-saturated sandy soils at shallow depth.

Mechanism

Loose sand tends to contract (densify) when sheared. If the soil is saturated and the loading is rapid, the water cannot drain, so pore-water pressure rises. By the principle of effective stress, as pore pressure approaches the total stress, the effective stress — and thus the soil's strength — approaches zero. The ground can then no longer support foundations: buildings sink or tilt, buried tanks float upward, and sand boils erupt at the surface. After shaking stops, the soil reconsolidates, often with significant settlement.

Effects

  • Lateral spreading — horizontal displacement of ground toward a free face such as a riverbank.
  • Flow liquefaction — large deformations and flow slides where static shear stress exceeds the residual strength.
  • Sand boils and ejecta, ground settlement, and foundation bearing failure.

Notable occurrences

Liquefaction caused dramatic damage in the 1964 Niigata earthquake (apartment buildings toppled largely intact) and the 1964 Alaska earthquake, events that spurred modern research led by engineers such as H. Bolton Seed. It also featured prominently in the 1989 Loma Prieta earthquake (San Francisco's Marina District), the 1995 Kobe earthquake, the 2010–2011 Christchurch earthquakes, and the 2018 Sulawesi earthquake, where flowslides in Palu killed many people.

Assessment and mitigation

Susceptibility is assessed from soil type, density (via the standard penetration test or cone penetration test), groundwater depth, and expected shaking. Mitigation includes ground improvement (vibro-compaction, stone columns, deep soil mixing, grouting), drainage, and deep foundations extending to firm strata.
