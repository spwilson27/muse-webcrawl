Ferrocerium

Composition~70% mischmetal, ~30% iron
PropertyPyrophoric
InventorCarl Auer von Welsbach (1903)

Ferrocerium (also known in Europe as Auermetall) is a man-made metallic alloy that produces large numbers of hot sparks when rapidly oxidised by scraping or striking — a property called pyrophoricity. It is the material of the "flint" in most lighters and of survival fire-starter rods.

Composition and history

Ferrocerium consists of about 70% mischmetal — a mixture of rare-earth metals, mainly cerium with lanthanum, neodymium and praseodymium — alloyed with roughly 30% iron to give hardness and durability. It was invented in 1903 by the Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach, who noticed that his cerium-rich alloys sparked strongly when scratched; the cerium component lowers the ignition temperature so that shavings burn at around 165 °C, producing sparks of up to 3,000 °C.

Uses

Uses include lighter flints, the striking wheels of gas welding torches, toy spark guns, and "ferro rods" carried by hikers and bushcraft practitioners, who scrape them with a steel striker to ignite tinder. Despite the name, lighter flints contain no flint, and modern survival ferrocerium rods are a related but distinct product used without any fuel.
