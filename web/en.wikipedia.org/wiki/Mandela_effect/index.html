Mandela effect

The Mandela effect is a phenomenon in which a large group of people share a false memory of a past event or detail. It is a form of confabulation — the production of fabricated or misinterpreted memories — occurring at a collective scale.

Origin of the term

The term was coined by Fiona Broome, a self-described paranormal researcher, who reported that she and many others distinctly remembered the South African anti-apartheid leader Nelson Mandela dying in prison in the 1980s. In reality, Mandela was released from prison in 1990, served as President of South Africa from 1994 to 1999, and died in 2013. Broome and others proposed paranormal or parallel-universe explanations, which have no scientific support.

Common examples

Frequently cited examples include remembering the children's book series as the "Berenstein Bears" rather than the Berenstain Bears; believing the Monopoly mascot wears a monocle (he does not); misremembering the Fruit of the Loom logo as containing a cornucopia; and recalling a movie called "Shazaam" starring the comedian Sinbad, which does not exist. Such errors typically arise from similar real stimuli, schemas, suggestion and post-event misinformation.

Scientific explanations

Psychologists explain the Mandela effect through well-established features of human memory: memory is reconstructive rather than a recording, it is vulnerable to false memories and source-monitoring errors, and social reinforcement can spread and entrench an incorrect recollection through a community. The phenomenon illustrates that confidence in a memory is a poor guide to its accuracy, even when the memory is widely shared.
