The haddock (Melanogrammus aeglefinus) is a saltwater ray-finned fish in the true cod family, Gadidae, and the only species in its genus. A popular food fish of the North Atlantic, it is easily recognised by the distinctive black "thumbprint" — sometimes called St Peter's mark — on each side of its body behind the pectoral fin, and by its dark lateral line against a silvery-grey body.

Distribution and habitat

Haddock are found on both sides of the North Atlantic, from the Strait of Belle Isle and Newfoundland to New England in the west, and from Iceland and the Barents Sea to the Bay of Biscay in the east. They are demersal, living near the seabed in depths of roughly 40–300 metres over rock, sand or gravel, and feed on small invertebrates, crustaceans, molluscs and small fish.

Life cycle

Spawning takes place mainly from January to June depending on the stock, with females releasing hundreds of thousands to millions of buoyant eggs. Year classes vary enormously in strength, and the species is known for occasional exceptionally large cohorts. Most haddock mature at around 30–40 cm; adults commonly reach 50–70 cm and can exceed one metre.

Fisheries

Haddock has long been one of the most important commercial fish in the North Atlantic, landed especially by the United Kingdom, Norway, Iceland, Russia and Canada. It is sold fresh, frozen and smoked — the cold-smoked Finnan haddock of Scotland is a celebrated preparation — and is a classic fish for fish and chips. Stocks have fluctuated under fishing pressure and environmental change, and the species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN.
