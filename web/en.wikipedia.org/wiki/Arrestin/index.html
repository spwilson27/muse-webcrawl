Arrestins (abbreviated Arr) are a small family of proteins important for regulating signal transduction at G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). They were first discovered as part of the mechanism that "arrests" signalling by activated, phosphorylated receptors — hence the name.

Function

Following agonist activation, GPCRs are phosphorylated by G protein-coupled receptor kinases (GRKs). Arrestin binds the phosphorylated receptor, sterically blocking further G protein coupling (desensitisation) and targeting the receptor to clathrin-coated pits for endocytosis and internalisation. Arrestin binding can also direct receptors toward recycling back to the cell surface or toward degradation in lysosomes.

Subtypes

Mammals express four arrestin subtypes. Visual arrestin (ARR1, S-antigen) and cone arrestin (ARR4) are found mainly in the retina, where ARR1 quenches light-activated, phosphorylated rhodopsin in rod photoreceptor cells; mutations in the S-antigen gene are associated with Oguchi disease, a form of congenital stationary night blindness. β-arrestin-1 (ARR2) and β-arrestin-2 (ARR3) are expressed ubiquitously and regulate most GPCRs.

Signalling roles

Beyond turning receptors off, β-arrestins act as scaffolds that assemble signalling complexes, activating pathways such as ERK/MAP kinase cascades independently of G proteins. This dual behaviour underlies the concept of "biased agonism": drugs may be designed to favour G protein signalling or β-arrestin signalling at a receptor, potentially separating therapeutic effects from adverse effects, an active area of pharmaceutical research involving receptors such as the μ-opioid receptor.
