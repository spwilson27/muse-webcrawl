The Fitzwilliam Virginal Book is a primary source of keyboard music from the late Renaissance and early Baroque periods in England, containing 297 separate pieces. It was copied between about 1609 and 1619, most probably by Francis Tregian the Younger, a Catholic recusant who may have assembled the collection while imprisoned in the Fleet Prison. The manuscript is now held by the Fitzwilliam Museum in Cambridge, to which it was bequeathed by its founder, Richard Fitzwilliam, 7th Viscount Fitzwilliam, in 1816.

Contents

The book contains music by the leading English virginalists, including John Bull (about 65 pieces), William Byrd (more than 70 pieces), and Orlando Gibbons, together with Thomas Morley, Giles Farnaby, William Tisdale, and others. It also includes English copies of pieces by continental composers such as Jan Pieterszoon Sweelinck, and arrangements of madrigals, dances (pavans, galliards, almains, and courants), variation sets on popular tunes, fantasias, and character pieces such as Byrd's “The Bells” and Bull's monumental variations on “Walsingham”.

History and scholarship

In the 19th century the manuscript was romantically, but inaccurately, known as “Queen Elizabeth's Virginal Book” — a name used for the first complete edition, edited by J. A. Fuller Maitland and W. Barclay Squire and published by Breitkopf & Härtel in 1899. A facsimile edition appeared in 1980. The attribution of the copying to Tregian, first proposed by musicologists in the 20th century and supported by handwriting and concordance evidence, links the book to other important Tregian manuscripts, including the Egerton manuscript of Byrd's music and the so-called Tregian manuscript of madrigals. The collection remains the single richest source for the English virginalist school and is still widely recorded and performed.
