Indo-European studies

Indo-European studies (German: Indogermanistik) is a field of linguistics and an interdisciplinary field dealing with the Indo-European languages, their history and reconstruction, and the cultures and migrations of the peoples who spoke Proto-Indo-European (PIE). It combines comparative linguistics with archaeology, textual philology, mythology and, increasingly, archaeogenetics.

History

The field began with Sir William Jones's 1786 observation to the Asiatic Society in Calcutta that Sanskrit bore to Greek and Latin "a stronger affinity... than could possibly have been produced by accident". In the 19th century, Franz Bopp, Rasmus Rask and Jacob Grimm formalised the comparative method and the systematic sound correspondences known as Grimm's law; August Schleicher produced the first reconstructed PIE "fable"; and Ferdinand de Saussure's 1879 hypothesis of "sonantic coefficients" was vindicated when Jerzy Kuryłowicz linked them to attested Hittite consonants — founding laryngeal theory.

Reconstruction and homeland

By comparing daughter languages — Indo-Iranian, Hellenic, Italic, Celtic, Germanic, Balto-Slavic, Armenian, Albanian, Tocharian and Anatolian — scholars reconstruct the vocabulary, phonology and grammar of PIE, including features such as ablaut, mobile pitch accent and the thematic conjugation. The leading theory of the PIE homeland is the Kurgan hypothesis (Marija Gimbutas), which places it on the Pontic–Caspian steppe in the 4th millennium BCE and associates its spread with Yamnaya pastoralists — a model strongly supported by ancient DNA research published from 2015 onward. The main rival, the Anatolian hypothesis (Colin Renfrew), links the dispersal to the spread of farming around 7000 BCE.

Branches of study

Beyond linguistics, Indo-European studies investigates reconstructed mythology and social structure (such as Georges Dumézil's trifunctional hypothesis), poetic formulae (studied by Calvert Watkins), and the archaeology of the Eurasian steppe. Academic centres include universities in Germany, the Netherlands (Leiden), France, and the United States (notably UCLA and Harvard).
