Jaggies

Jaggies are visual artifacts that occur in raster graphics when diagonal or curved lines appear as a series of small square or rectangular steps, resembling a staircase. They are a form of aliasing, caused when an image is displayed at a resolution too low to represent smooth edges faithfully.

Cause

A raster display can only colour whole pixels. When a diagonal edge crosses the pixel grid, the renderer lights the pixels closest to the ideal line, producing a stepped approximation. The effect is most noticeable on high-contrast edges and shallow angles, where the steps are long and regular. Jaggies were especially prominent in early computer and video game graphics, where low resolutions and the absence of edge smoothing made them ubiquitous.

Reduction

The standard remedy is anti-aliasing, which shades edge pixels with intermediate colours proportional to how much of the pixel the ideal edge covers, making the transition appear smooth. Modern techniques include multisample anti-aliasing (MSAA), fast approximate anti-aliasing (FXAA), temporal anti-aliasing (TAA), and supersampling (SSAA), in which the image is rendered at a higher resolution and downsampled. Simply increasing display resolution also reduces the visibility of jaggies by making the steps smaller.
