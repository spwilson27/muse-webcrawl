Social structure

Social structure is the distinctive, stable arrangement of institutions and patterned relationships whereby members of a society interact and live together. The term, central to sociology since the 19th century, covers both the large-scale organisation of society — its economy, polity, class system, kinship arrangements and culture — and the statuses, roles and networks through which individuals are positioned within it. Structures both constrain and enable action, a relationship theorised in the structure–agency debate.

Development of the concept

Herbert Spencer popularised structural analogies between society and organisms. Karl Marx located structure in the economic "base" of relations of production, with legal and political forms as superstructure; Émile Durkheim emphasised collective representations and the division of labour; Max Weber analysed stratification by class, status and party. In the 20th century, Talcott Parsons's structural functionalism treated society as a system of interdependent institutions meeting functional needs, while British social anthropologists such as Radcliffe-Brown defined social structure as the network of actually existing relations between persons.

Structure and agency

Later theorists sought to overcome the opposition between structure and individual agency. Anthony Giddens's theory of structuration holds that structure is both the medium and the outcome of action ("duality of structure"); Pierre Bourdieu's concepts of habitus, field and capital similarly link objective positions to embodied dispositions. Network analysts formalise structure as patterns of ties between actors, studied with social network analysis.

Levels

Analysts commonly distinguish macro-structures (institutions, class systems, world-systems), meso-level structures (organisations, communities, networks) and micro-structures (the patterned expectations of face-to-face interaction, as in role theory). Critics note that "structure" can be reified — treated as a thing rather than an abstraction from ongoing processes — and process-oriented sociologies prefer to speak of structuring.
