Cognate

In historical linguistics, cognates or lexical cognates are sets of words in different languages that have been inherited in direct descent from an etymological ancestor in a common parent language. Because they descend from the same source, cognates often have related meanings and similar (though not necessarily identical) forms, altered over time by regular sound changes.

Characteristics

Cognates need not look alike: English wheel and Sanskrit cakra are both descended from Proto-Indo-European *kʷékʷlos. Nor need they share a meaning: the Latin habēre and Germanic habban are cognate, yet Latin developed the sense "to have" differently from its semantic path in English have. Systematic correspondences, such as those described by Grimm's law, allow linguists to establish cognacy even when forms have diverged greatly.

Cognates versus loanwords

Words shared between languages by borrowing are not cognates, even if ultimately traceable to the same root; English paternal (borrowed from Latin) and father (inherited) are "doublets"—two English words from one source by different routes. False cognates are words that merely resemble one another and have similar meanings but unrelated origins, such as English much and Spanish mucho.

Use in language reconstruction

Comparing cognate sets across related languages underlies the comparative method used to reconstruct proto-languages such as Proto-Indo-European. Counting shared core-vocabulary cognates also underlies lexicostatistics and glottochronology, though the reliability of the latter is debated.
