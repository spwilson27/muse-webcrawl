Uxmal (Yucatec Maya: Óoxmáal) is an ancient Maya city of the classical period located in present-day Yucatán, Mexico, about 60 km south of Mérida. It is considered one of the most important archaeological sites of Maya culture, along with Palenque, Chichén Itzá and Calakmul, and is the foremost example of the Puuc architectural style.

Architecture

The name Uxmal is traditionally glossed "built three times". Dominating the site is the Pyramid of the Magician (Adivino), an unusual elliptical-based stepped pyramid about 35 metres high, legendarily built in a single night by a dwarf king. The Nunnery Quadrangle (Cuadrángulo de las Monjas) is a courtyard of four richly carved buildings whose upper façades carry intricate stone mosaics of the rain god Chaac, latticework and serpents. Other major structures include the Governor's Palace — whose 100-metre mosaic façade is often called the finest example of Puuc art — the Great Pyramid, the House of the Turtles and a ball court dated to 906 CE.

History

Uxmal flourished between about 850 and 925 CE as capital of a regional state in the Puuc hills, a region lacking surface water and dependent on rain stored in cisterns (chultunes), hence the prominence of Chaac imagery. It was linked by a sacbé (causeway) to Kabah. The city declined and was largely abandoned by about 1100, though it remained a place of pilgrimage into the Spanish colonial period. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1996.
