Cook–Levin theorem

In computational complexity theory, the Cook–Levin theorem, also known as Cook's theorem, states that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete. That is, any problem in NP can be reduced in polynomial time to an instance of SAT. The theorem was proved by Stephen Cook in his 1971 paper "The complexity of theorem proving procedures" and, independently, by Leonid Levin in 1973.

SAT was the first problem shown to be NP-complete.

Significance

The theorem founded the theory of NP-completeness. Once SAT was known to be NP-complete, thousands of other problems — in graph theory, scheduling, optimisation, and elsewhere — could be shown NP-complete by reduction from SAT or from problems already proven complete. Richard Karp's 1972 paper exhibited 21 such problems. The theorem also frames the P versus NP problem: if any NP-complete problem has a polynomial-time algorithm, then P = NP.

Proof idea

Given any problem in NP, a nondeterministic Turing machine solves it in polynomial time. For a given input, the machine's computation — a tableau of polynomial size — can be encoded as a Boolean formula in conjunctive normal form whose variables describe the machine's tape contents, head position, and state at each time step. The formula is satisfiable exactly when the machine has an accepting computation. The formula's size is polynomial in the input, and it can be written down in polynomial time, establishing the reduction.

History

Cook presented the result at the 3rd ACM Symposium on Theory of Computing in 1971. Levin, working in the Soviet Union, published corresponding results on universal search problems in 1973, unaware of Cook's work; the joint name became standard in the West after Karp publicised both contributions.
