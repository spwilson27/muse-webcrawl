Icelandic cuisine

Icelandic cuisine is the traditional food culture of Iceland, shaped by the island's harsh climate, isolation and reliance on fishing and sheep farming. Historically it centred on preserved foods — dried, salted, smoked, pickled and fermented — needed to survive long winters.

Traditional foods

Well-known traditional dishes include hákarl (fermented shark), hangikjöt (smoked lamb), harðfiskur (dried fish eaten with butter), svið (singed sheep's head), lifrarpylsa (liver sausage) and skyr, a cultured dairy product similar to strained yogurt that remains a staple. These foods feature in the midwinter þorramatur buffet served during the month of Þorri.

Modern cuisine

Contemporary Icelandic cooking emphasises fresh local ingredients: lamb, cod, haddock, Arctic char, langoustine and root vegetables grown in geothermally heated greenhouses. Reykjavík has developed a notable restaurant scene drawing on New Nordic cuisine.

Beverages

Traditional drinks include brennivín, a caraway-flavoured schnapps nicknamed "Black Death". Coffee is extremely popular, and Iceland's pure tap water is widely praised. Beer was prohibited in Iceland until 1989.
