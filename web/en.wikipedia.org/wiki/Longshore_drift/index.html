Longshore drift

Longshore drift, also known as littoral drift, is a geological process by which sediment is transported along a coast parallel to the shoreline. It results from waves approaching the beach at an oblique angle: the swash carries sand and shingle up the beach in the direction of the incoming wave, while the backwash, under gravity, drags material straight back down to the sea. Successive waves thus move beach material in a zigzag along the shore.

Processes and features

The direction of drift is governed by the prevailing wind and wave approach. Longshore drift builds depositional features where the coastline changes direction or the drift is interrupted: spits (such as Spurn Head and Hurst Castle Spit), bars across river mouths, tombolos and cuspate forelands such as Dungeness. A related process, the longshore current flowing parallel to the shore within the surf zone, also moves suspended sediment.

Human impact

Groynes are built to interrupt drift and hold beaches in place, but by starving the shore down-drift they can accelerate erosion there; harbours and piers act as giant groynes and require regular dredging. Coastal management increasingly treats drift as part of a sediment cell, managing the whole stretch of coast between headlands as one system.
