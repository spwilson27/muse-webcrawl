ATP synthase

ATP synthase is a rotary molecular motor driven by a proton gradient.
EC number7.1.2.2
LocationInner mitochondrial membrane; thylakoid membrane; bacterial plasma membrane
ReactionADP + Pi + H+ gradient → ATP + H2O
SubunitsFO (membrane rotor) and F1 (catalytic head)

ATP synthase is an enzyme that creates the energy storage molecule adenosine triphosphate (ATP). ATP is the most commonly used "energy currency" of cells, formed from adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate (Pi). ATP synthase sits in the inner mitochondrial membrane (in eukaryotes), the thylakoid membrane of chloroplasts, and the plasma membrane of bacteria, and harnesses the energy of a proton (H+) gradient generated by the electron transport chain — a process called chemiosmosis.

Structure and mechanism

The enzyme is a rotary motor of two linked complexes. The membrane-embedded FO part contains a ring of c-subunits that rotates as protons flow through it down their gradient. Torque is transmitted by the central γ shaft to the F1 head, a hexamer of alternating α and β subunits. Rotation forces the three catalytic β subunits through Paul Boyer's "binding change" cycle — open, loose, and tight states — binding ADP and Pi, squeezing them into ATP, and releasing it. Roughly three ATP are made per full rotation, powered by about 8–17 protons depending on the c-ring size of the species.

History

Peter D. Mitchell proposed the chemiosmotic theory in 1961 (Nobel Prize 1978). Paul Boyer developed the binding-change mechanism and John E. Walker determined the atomic structure of the F1 portion; Boyer and Walker shared the 1997 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, and direct observation of the rotating γ shaft by Masasuke Yoshida's group in 1997 confirmed the motor model.
