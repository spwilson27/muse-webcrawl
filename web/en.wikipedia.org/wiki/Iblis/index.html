Iblis (Arabic: إبليس) is the primary figure of evil in Islamic theology, the tempter who whispers doubt and seduction into the hearts of humans. The Quran mentions him frequently; his refusal to bow before Adam — because he considered himself, created of fire, superior to Adam, created of clay — led God to expel him from Heaven. Iblis then obtained respite until the Day of Judgement, declaring that he would mislead humankind, and God answered that he would have no power over the truly faithful.

Names and nature

The Quranic figure is also called al-Shaytan ("the adversary" — Satan) and Rajim ("the accursed/stoned"). Theologians disagree about his nature: the dominant view holds that he is a jinn, citing the verse that describes him as a jinn among the angels, though some traditions make him a fallen angel. His original name is often given as Azazil. The Hajj ritual of stoning the jamarat at Mina symbolises the pilgrims' rejection of Satan, commemorating Abraham's rejection of temptation.

Theology and debate

Islamic thought has long wrestled with Iblis's role. Was his refusal to prostrate itself a sin of pride, or — as Sufi thinkers like al-Hallaj suggested — a tragic fidelity to God's command to worship none but God? Mainstream theology holds firmly to the former. Iblis is contrasted with God, never his rival in power; Islamic tradition rejects any Manichaean dualism. In Sufi literature he sometimes appears as a paradoxical, even melancholy figure, while in popular belief he is simply the enemy of mankind, leading the shayatin (devils).
