The Data Encryption Standard (DES) is a symmetric-key algorithm for the encryption of digital data. Although its short key length of 56 bits makes it too insecure for modern applications, it was highly influential in the advancement of cryptography.

History

DES was developed at IBM in the early 1970s by a team led by Horst Feistel, building on the earlier Lucifer cipher. In 1973 the United States National Bureau of Standards invited proposals for a national encryption standard; IBM's submission, modified in consultation with the National Security Agency (NSA), was adopted as a federal standard (FIPS PUB 46) in 1977. The NSA's involvement — in particular the shortening of the key from 128 to 56 bits and opaque changes to the S-boxes — attracted decades of controversy, though the S-box changes were later shown to have hardened the cipher against differential cryptanalysis, a technique then known only within IBM and the NSA.

Description

DES operates on 64-bit blocks using a 56-bit key (eight bytes with parity bits). The algorithm applies an initial permutation, sixteen Feistel rounds in which half the block is combined with a round subkey derived from the main key, and a final permutation. Encryption and decryption use the same procedure with the subkey schedule reversed.

Security

The 56-bit key space, large in the 1970s, became the algorithm's fatal weakness. In 1998 the Electronic Frontier Foundation built the "Deep Crack" machine for about $250,000, which recovered a DES key in 56 hours; a year later a combined effort reduced this to under a day. DES was withdrawn as a standard and superseded by the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) in 2001. Triple DES, which applies DES three times with independent keys, remained in use in legacy payment systems for many years but has also been deprecated.
