Oskar Barnack (1 November 1879 – 16 January 1936) was a German optical engineer and precision mechanic who designed the first mass-produced 35 mm still camera, the Leica, thereby effectively creating the format that came to dominate photography for the rest of the twentieth century. He worked for the optical firm Ernst Leitz in Wetzlar, where he headed the department for the development of cinematographic equipment.

The Ur-Leica

Barnack, an asthmatic who found the heavy plate cameras of the day burdensome on mountain hikes, sought a small camera that could use 35 mm motion-picture film. Around 1913–14 he built his Liliput-Kamera, later called the "Ur-Leica": rather than the cinema format's 18×24 mm frame, he doubled the frame to 24×36 mm and ran the film horizontally. He used a prototype to take personal photographs around Wetzlar, including images of a flood in 1914 that are among the earliest pictures made with the camera. Development was delayed by the First World War, and the production Leica I did not reach the market until 1925, after Ernst Leitz II took the famous decision "Ich entscheide hiermit: Es wird riskiert" ("I hereby decide: it will be risked").

Legacy

The compact, quiet, high-quality 35 mm camera Barnack created made possible the candid photojournalism of the 1930s and the work of photographers such as Henri Cartier-Bresson; the 24×36 mm "Barnack format" became the standard full-frame size still used in digital photography. Leica's Oskar Barnack Award, founded in 1979, honours photojournalists in his name, and Barnack's own prototype Ur-Leica sold at auction in 2022 for €14.4 million, becoming the most expensive camera ever sold.
