Governor General of Canada

The governor general of Canada (French: gouverneure générale du Canada) is the federal representative of the Canadian monarch, currently King Charles III, and carries out the monarch's constitutional and ceremonial duties within Canada. The governor general is appointed by the monarch on the advice of the prime minister of Canada, conventionally for about five years, and acts on the advice of the Canadian government in all but exceptional reserve-power situations. Since 2021 the office has been held by Mary Simon, the first Indigenous person to hold the post.

Role

The governor general grants royal assent to legislation, summons, prorogues and dissolves Parliament, reads the Speech from the Throne, appoints the prime minister and (on advice) senators and judges, and serves as commander-in-chief of the Canadian Armed Forces. The office also has a prominent ceremonial role: presenting national honours such as the Order of Canada, welcoming heads of state, and representing Canada abroad and at commemorations.

History

The office descends from the governors of New France and British North America; the first governor general of the Dominion of Canada, Lord Monck, took office at Confederation in 1867. Early holders were British aristocrats; Vincent Massey (1952) was the first Canadian-born appointee, and since then the office has alternated between anglophone and francophone Canadians, with conventions of appointing women and members of minority communities — including Adrienne Clarkson, Michaëlle Jean, and Mary Simon, an Inuk leader from Nunavik.

Notable episodes

Rare uses or threatened uses of reserve powers mark the office's history: the King–Byng Affair of 1926, when Lord Byng refused Mackenzie King's request for dissolution, and the 2008 prorogation granted to Stephen Harper. The 1947 Letters Patent delegate to the governor general virtually all of the monarch's powers in Canadian matters.
