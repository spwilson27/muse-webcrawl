Land reclamation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Land reclamation, usually known as reclamation, and also known as land fill (not to be confused with a landfill), is the process of creating new land from oceans, seas, riverbeds or lake beds. The land reclaimed is known as reclamation ground, reclaimed land, or artificially filled land. The term is also used, in a broader environmental sense, for restoring damaged land — for example after mining — to a usable state.

Methods

Reclamation can be carried out by several methods. The simplest is to deposit heavy rock and soil directly into the water, often inside a perimeter bund or causeway, a technique called dry reclamation. Hydraulic reclamation uses dredged material, pumped as a slurry through pipelines from borrow areas to the site; trailing suction hopper dredgers and cutter-suction dredgers are the main vessels used. In deep water, reclamation may proceed by deep cement mixing or by filling large caissons. Reclamation of low-lying land protected by dykes and drainage — the creation of polders — is treated in detail in the article on land reclamation in the Netherlands.

History

Reclamation is ancient: harbour works in the Mediterranean, the chinampas of Mesoamerica, and Chinese coastal enclosure all created farmland from shallows. Modern large-scale reclamation expanded with steam dredgers in the nineteenth century and accelerated in the twentieth. Hong Kong, Singapore, the Netherlands, South Korea, Japan, Bahrain, and the coastal cities of China are among the best-known practitioners. Singapore has increased its land area by roughly a quarter since independence; the Netherlands has reclaimed about a fifth of its territory from water, much of it as polders.

Uses

Reclaimed land is used for ports, airports (such as Kansai International Airport and Hong Kong International Airport), industrial estates, housing, agriculture, and beaches. Beach nourishment — the deposit of sand to widen an eroding beach — is a soft form of reclamation.

Environmental impacts

Reclamation destroys intertidal and shallow marine habitat, affects sediment transport and coastal currents, and can raise flood risk elsewhere. Newly filled land may subside and is vulnerable to liquefaction during earthquakes; the 1995 Kobe earthquake severely damaged artificial islands in the port. For these reasons many jurisdictions now subject large reclamation schemes to environmental impact assessment, and some have restricted or banned the practice except for essential infrastructure.
