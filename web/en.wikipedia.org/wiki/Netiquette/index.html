Netiquette


Early guides to online behaviour were circulated as FAQs on Usenet and mailing lists.

Netiquette, a portmanteau of network and etiquette, is the set of social conventions that facilitate interaction over networks such as the Internet and other computer networks, particularly mailing lists, Usenet newsgroups, forums and, later, social media. The term was popularised by early Internet culture and codified in documents such as RFC 1855, Netiquette Guidelines (1995), and Virginia Shea's 1994 book Netiquette.

Common rules

Although conventions vary between communities, widely cited principles include:

  • Remember that a human being is on the other end of the communication.
  • Adhere to the same standards of behaviour online that you follow in real life.
  • Lurk before posting: read a group's archives and FAQ before contributing.
  • Do not type in ALL CAPS, which is read as shouting.
  • Keep messages concise, quote sparingly, and use descriptive subject lines.
  • Do not send unsolicited bulk messages (spam) or engage in flaming.
  • Respect others' privacy — do not forward private mail without permission.
  • Forgive other people's mistakes, especially those of newcomers.

Shea summarised her rules as a "Core Rules of Netiquette" list of ten items, beginning with "Remember the human."

History

Norms of online conduct emerged on ARPANET mailing lists and on Usenet in the 1980s, where communities enforced them socially. Seasonal influxes of new users — most famously the "September" arrival of university students, and after 1993 the permanent influx dubbed the Eternal September when AOL opened Usenet access to its subscribers — strained those norms. Written guidelines multiplied as the network commercialised in the 1990s. In the era of social media, platform-specific community standards and moderation policies have partly replaced the older voluntary etiquette, though the underlying advice remains widely taught in digital-literacy curricula.
