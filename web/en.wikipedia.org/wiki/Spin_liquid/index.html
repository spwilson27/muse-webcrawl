Spin liquid

A quantum spin liquid is a phase of matter that can be formed by interacting quantum spins in certain magnetic materials. In contrast to an ordinary magnet, whose spins freeze into an ordered pattern at low temperature, a spin liquid's spins remain disordered and strongly fluctuating down to the lowest temperatures, even though neighbouring spins are strongly correlated. The name is an analogy: the spins fail to "freeze" just as a liquid fails to solidify, but the underlying physics is quantum mechanical.

Characteristics

Spin liquids are long-range entangled ground states. They typically arise in geometrically frustrated magnets, such as antiferromagnets on triangular or kagome lattices, where the geometry prevents all pairwise interactions from being satisfied simultaneously. Their excitations can be fractionalised: instead of ordinary spin-1 magnons, they support emergent spin-1/2 quasiparticles called spinons, and some spin liquids possess topological order with anyonic excitations.

History and candidates

The concept was introduced by Philip W. Anderson in 1973 in the form of the resonating valence bond (RVB) state, proposed for the triangular-lattice antiferromagnet; he revived the idea in 1987 in connection with high-temperature superconductivity. Candidate materials include herbertsmithite (a kagome-lattice mineral), the organic salts κ-(ET)2Cu2(CN)3 and EtMe3Sb[Pd(dmit)2]2, and α-RuCl3, which is studied as a realisation of the exactly solvable Kitaev honeycomb model. Definitive experimental identification of a spin liquid remains an active and difficult area of condensed matter physics.
