Molecular mechanics

Molecular mechanics uses classical mechanics to model molecular systems: atoms are treated as point masses connected by springs, and the potential energy of a system is calculated from a force field — a set of functions and parameters describing bond stretching, angle bending, torsional rotations, and non-bonded interactions (van der Waals and electrostatic). Because electrons are not treated explicitly, molecular mechanics is computationally cheap and can handle systems of millions of atoms, though it cannot describe bond breaking or formation.

Force fields

A force field assigns atom types and parameters so that energies and forces can be computed quickly. Widely used biomolecular force fields include AMBER, CHARMM, GROMOS and OPLS; materials and small-molecule work uses others such as MMFF and UFF. Parameters are fitted to experimental data and quantum-mechanical calculations. The energy expression sums harmonic bond and angle terms, periodic dihedral terms, and Lennard-Jones plus Coulomb terms between non-bonded atoms.

Applications

Molecular mechanics underlies energy minimization (finding low-energy conformations), molecular dynamics simulations of proteins, nucleic acids, membranes and materials, and much of computer-aided drug design, where docking and free-energy calculations depend on force-field energies. Its accuracy depends entirely on the quality and transferability of parameters, and hybrid QM/MM methods embed a quantum-mechanical treatment of a reactive centre within a molecular-mechanics environment.
