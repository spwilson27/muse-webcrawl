Blackbeard

Edward Teach (or Thatch; c. 1680 – 22 November 1718), better known as Blackbeard, was an English pirate who operated around the West Indies and the eastern coast of Britain's North American colonies. He is the archetypal pirate of the "Golden Age of Piracy" and one of the most famous pirates in history.

Early life

Little is known of Teach's early life. He probably went to sea from Bristol, served as a privateer during the War of the Spanish Succession, and turned to piracy around 1716 under the pirate captain Benjamin Hornigold at New Providence in the Bahamas.

Career

In 1717 Teach captured the French slave ship La Concorde, refitted her with 40 guns and renamed her Queen Anne's Revenge. He cultivated a terrifying image: a tall man with a long black beard tied with ribbons, who reportedly lit slow-burning fuses under his hat to wreathe his face in smoke during battle. In May 1718 he blockaded Charleston, South Carolina, ransoming hostages for a chest of medicine. Soon after, Queen Anne's Revenge ran aground at Beaufort Inlet, and Teach accepted a royal pardon at Bath, North Carolina — though he quickly returned to piracy.

Death

Governor Alexander Spotswood of Virginia sent a naval force under Lieutenant Robert Maynard to hunt him down. On 22 November 1718 Teach was killed in a fierce boarding action at Ocracoke Inlet, reportedly suffering multiple gunshot and cutlass wounds before falling. Maynard had Blackbeard's head hung from his ship's bowsprit as proof.

Legacy

Blackbeard's exploits were popularised by Captain Charles Johnson's A General History of the Pyrates (1724). The wreck of Queen Anne's Revenge was located off North Carolina in 1996, and thousands of artefacts have since been recovered. He remains a staple of pirate fiction, film and folklore.
