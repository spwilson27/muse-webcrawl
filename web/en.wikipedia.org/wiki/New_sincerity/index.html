New sincerity

Key figuresDavid Foster Wallace, Jonathan Franzen, Dave Eggers, Zadie Smith
Era1990s–present
Reaction againstPostmodern irony, metafiction
Related conceptsPost-postmodernism, metamodernism

New sincerity is a term used in cultural criticism to describe works of literature, film, and music that emphasize earnestness, emotional directness, and authentic communication as a reaction against the irony, detachment, and self-consciousness associated with postmodernism. It is closely related to debates about post-postmodernism.

In literature

The concept is most strongly associated with David Foster Wallace, whose 1993 essay "E Unibus Pluram: Television and U.S. Fiction" predicted a generation of "literary rebels" who would dare to endorse "single-entendre principles" and treat ordinary human emotion with reverence rather than ironic distance. Wallace's fiction and essays, with their recursive self-awareness harnessed to moral earnestness, became the movement's touchstone. Other writers frequently grouped under the label include Dave Eggers (A Heartbreaking Work of Staggering Genius, 2000), Jonathan Franzen, Zadie Smith, Michael Chabon, and later authors such as Sally Rooney; scholars have also applied the term to earlier counter-movements in American poetry in the 1980s.

Other media

In film criticism "new sincerity" has been applied to directors such as Wes Anderson and Michel Gondry, whose whimsical surfaces carry earnest emotional cores, and to the work of Charlie Kaufman. In music it has described indie and alternative artists who foreground vulnerability. Critics note the term is loose and contested: some see it as a genuine aesthetic shift, others as a marketing label, and many of its supposed exemplars combine irony and sincerity rather than abandoning irony altogether.
