Bankruptcy is a legal process through which people or other entities who cannot repay debts to creditors may seek relief from some or all of their debts, under the protection and supervision of a court. In most jurisdictions it is imposed by a court order, often initiated by the debtor (a voluntary petition) but sometimes by creditors. Bankruptcy is the principal legal mechanism for resolving insolvency, balancing a "fresh start" for honest debtors against fair, orderly treatment of creditors.

Etymology and history

The word derives from Italian banca rotta, "broken bench" — the moneylender's bench supposedly broken when he defaulted. Roman law provided the cessio bonorum; early English statutes (from 1542) treated bankruptcy as a creditor remedy against absconding traders, and discharge of the debtor only appeared in 1705. The United States Constitution authorises uniform federal bankruptcy laws; the modern US Bankruptcy Code dates from 1978 (with major amendments in 2005, the Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act).

United States

Under Title 11 of the US Code, cases proceed under chapters suited to the debtor: Chapter 7 (liquidation — a trustee sells non-exempt assets and remaining debts are discharged); Chapter 11 (reorganisation, used mainly by businesses, which propose a plan to restructure debts while continuing to operate, as in the cases of General Motors and many airlines); Chapter 13 (a wage-earner's repayment plan over three to five years); Chapter 9 (municipalities, e.g. Detroit in 2013, the largest US municipal bankruptcy); Chapter 12 (family farmers and fishermen) and Chapter 15 (cross-border cases). Filing triggers an automatic stay halting most collection actions. Secured creditors, priority claims (such as certain taxes and support obligations), and the debtor's exempt property are governed by detailed rules.

Other jurisdictions

In the United Kingdom, bankruptcy applies only to individuals (under the Insolvency Act 1986); companies use liquidation, administration or company voluntary arrangements. The Enterprise Act 2002 reduced the usual discharge period to one year. Many civil-law countries have modernised their regimes (e.g. Germany's Insolvenzordnung, in force 1999) to encourage rescue of viable businesses, and the EU Insolvency Regulation and UNCITRAL Model Law coordinate cross-border cases.

Effects and debates

Bankruptcy can allow businesses to reject burdensome contracts, restructure debts, and continue operating, but a filing damages the debtor's credit record (in the US it is reported for up to 7–10 years) and may carry social stigma. Economists debate the trade-off between debtor relief and the cost and availability of credit. Personal bankruptcy filings spike in recessions; well-known corporate filings include Enron (2001), Lehman Brothers (2008, the largest in US history), and General Motors (2009).
