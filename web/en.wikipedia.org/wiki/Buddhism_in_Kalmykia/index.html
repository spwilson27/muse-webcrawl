Buddhism in Kalmykia

Buddhism in Kalmykia is the predominant religion of the Kalmyk people, an Oirat Mongol group whose ancestors migrated from Dzungaria to the lower Volga steppe in the early 17th century. Kalmykia, a republic within the Russian Federation, is the only region in Europe where Buddhism is practised by a majority or plurality of the population, making it geographically the westernmost traditional Buddhist homeland.

History

The Kalmyks brought with them the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism, which had spread among the Oirat tribes through the influence of missionaries such as Zaya Pandita, who in 1648 created the Clear Script (todo bichig) for the Oirat language. Buddhist temples (khuruls) multiplied across the steppe, and lamas maintained close ties with Tibet, sending pilgrims overland to Lhasa.

After the Russian Revolution, Buddhism suffered persecution; temples were closed or destroyed in the 1930s, and in 1943 the entire Kalmyk population was deported to Siberia by Stalin. Buddhism was effectively banned until the Kalmyks were rehabilitated and allowed to return home in 1957. A modest revival began in the late Soviet period.

Revival

Following the collapse of the USSR, Buddhism in Kalmykia underwent a rapid revival, encouraged by republic president Kirsan Ilyumzhinov. The 14th Dalai Lama visited Kalmykia in 1991, 1992 and 2004. In 2005 the Golden Abode of Buddha Shakyamuni (Burkhan Bakshin Altan Sume) was consecrated in the capital, Elista; it houses a nine-metre gilded statue of the Buddha and is among the largest Buddhist temples in Europe. The head lama of Kalmykia, the Shajin Lama, is Telo Tulku Rinpoche (Erdne Ombadykow), born in Philadelphia to Kalmyk émigré parents and recognised as a reincarnation of the saint Tilopa.

Practice

Kalmyk Buddhism follows the Gelug tradition, with practice centred on khuruls in Elista and the districts. Major festivals include Zul (the Kalmyk New Year, marking the parinirvana and birthday of Je Tsongkhapa) and the summer festival of Ur Sar. Hundreds of temples and prayer houses have been built or restored since 1988, and Kalmyk monks study in Tibetan monasteries in India.
