The Battle of Bannockburn (Scottish Gaelic: Blàr Allt nam Bànag) was a decisive victory of a Scottish army under King Robert the Bruce over the much larger English army of Edward II on 23–24 June 1314, during the First War of Scottish Independence. It remains one of the most celebrated events in Scottish history.

Scottish schiltrons on the second day of the battle.

Background

Edward Bruce, the king's brother, had besieged English-held Stirling Castle and agreed that it would surrender if not relieved by midsummer 1314. Edward II marched north with a great army to raise the siege, compelling Robert the Bruce to give battle south of Stirling, on ground he had carefully prepared near the Bannock Burn, with pits and caltrops dug to hinder cavalry.

The battle

On the first day, Bruce famously killed the English knight Henry de Bohun in single combat after de Bohun charged him, and Scottish spearmen repelled an English cavalry probe. On the second day, the Scottish schiltrons — dense formations of pikemen — advanced off their defensive ground and pressed the English army back into the confined, boggy carse between the Bannock Burn and the River Forth, where its archers and cavalry could not deploy. The English host collapsed; Edward II fled the field, Stirling surrendered, and thousands of English soldiers were killed or captured.

Legacy

Bannockburn secured Scotland's de facto independence, confirmed diplomatically by the Declaration of Arbroath (1320) and by treaty in 1328. The battlefield near Stirling is preserved by the National Trust for Scotland, with an iconic equestrian statue of Bruce overlooking the field.
