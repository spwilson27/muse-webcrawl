In computing, FLOPS (floating-point operations per second) is a measure of computer performance, useful in fields of scientific computation that require floating-point calculations. It counts how many addition and multiplication operations on floating-point numbers a machine can perform each second. Because it measures a rate, it is written with SI prefixes — megaFLOPS (10⁶), gigaFLOPS (10⁹), teraFLOPS (10¹²), petaFLOPS (10¹⁵), and exaFLOPS (10¹⁸).

Measurement

Performance figures are usually quoted as a theoretical peak (Rpeak) or as sustained performance on the LINPACK benchmark solving dense linear systems (Rmax), which the TOP500 list uses to rank supercomputers. Achieved performance in real applications depends on memory bandwidth, interconnect, precision, and algorithmic efficiency, and is typically well below peak. Counts also vary with numerical precision: modern AI accelerators advertise very high rates at reduced precision (FP16, BF16, FP8), which are not directly comparable with the double-precision (FP64) figures used in traditional HPC.

Milestones

The CDC 6600 (1964) is often called the first supercomputer, at about 3 MFLOPS. The Cray-2 reached gigaFLOPS-scale peak performance in 1985; ASCI Red broke the teraFLOPS barrier in 1996–97; IBM Roadrunner passed a petaFLOPS in 2008; and in 2022 the US system Frontier became the first officially recognised exascale machine, exceeding 1.1 EFLOPS on LINPACK. Consumer hardware has followed a similar curve: modern GPUs deliver tens of teraFLOPS of single-precision throughput and far higher rates for low-precision tensor operations.

Limitations

FLOPS do not measure integer performance, memory performance, or real-world speed, and can be inflated by counting fused multiply–add operations as two operations or by using very low precision. Alternative metrics (such as FLOPS per watt for energy efficiency, or benchmark suites like HPCG) complement it.
