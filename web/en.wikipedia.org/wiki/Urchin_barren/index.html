Urchin barren


An urchin barren off the California coast

An urchin barren is a shallow ocean area on rocky reefs where unchecked sea urchin populations have grazed away kelp and other macroalgae, leaving bare rock encrusted with coralline algae. Urchin barrens are a classic example of an alternative stable state in marine ecology: once established, they can persist for decades even though the same habitat, under different conditions, supports lush kelp forests.

Formation

Barrens typically form when the predators that control urchins — such as sea otters, spiny lobsters, sheephead, sunflower sea stars, or commercially harvested crabs — are removed by fishing, disease, or environmental change. Freed from predation, urchins multiply and move out of crevices onto open reef, consuming kelp faster than it can regrow. The urchins then survive in a starved "zombie" state on encrusting algae and detritus, eating any kelp recruit that settles and thereby locking the system in the barren state.

Distribution and effects

Urchin barrens occur in temperate waters worldwide, including California, Tasmania, Norway, Japan, New Zealand, and the Aleutian Islands. The long-spined sea urchin Centrostephanus rodgersii, carried south by a warming East Australian Current, has converted large areas of Tasmanian kelp bed into barrens. Barrens support far lower biodiversity and fisheries productivity than kelp forests, which shelter abalone, rockfish, and hundreds of other species.

Restoration

Recovery requires reducing urchin densities below a threshold — through predator reintroduction (notably sea otters in the North Pacific), commercial harvest, or direct culling — so that kelp can re-establish. Because of hysteresis, the urchin density at which kelp recovers is far lower than the density at which it collapsed.
