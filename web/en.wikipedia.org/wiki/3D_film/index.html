3D film

Cinema audience wearing polarised 3D glasses

A 3D film (three-dimensional film) is a motion picture that enhances the illusion of depth perception by presenting two offset images to the viewer's left and right eyes, simulating binocular stereopsis. Audiences wear special glasses — historically red/cyan anaglyph, and in modern cinemas usually polarised or active-shutter lenses — to separate the two images.

History

Stereoscopic filmmaking dates to the earliest years of cinema; the first confirmed 3D feature, The Power of Love, premiered in 1922. The technology's first boom came in the early 1950s with films such as Bwana Devil (1952) and House of Wax (1953), exploiting dual-strip polarised projection. A second wave in the 1980s used IMAX and single-strip systems.

Modern era

Digital projection enabled a third, much larger wave in the 2000s with systems such as RealD. James Cameron's Avatar (2009) became the highest-grossing film of all time and cemented 3D as a mainstream commercial format; studios released many films converted to 3D in post-production, with variable quality. Audience enthusiasm waned in the 2010s, especially on home television, though premium large-format 3D releases such as Avatar: The Way of Water (2022) remained major box-office draws.

Techniques

Films may be shot natively with dual-camera rigs or converted from 2D footage using depth mapping. Projection methods include polarisation (linear or circular), active shutter glasses, and spectral separation (Dolby 3D). Critics note drawbacks including dimmer images, eyestrain for some viewers, and higher ticket prices.
