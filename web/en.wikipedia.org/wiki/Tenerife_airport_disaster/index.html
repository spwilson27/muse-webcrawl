Tenerife airport disaster

Date27 March 1977
SiteLos Rodeos Airport, Tenerife, Canary Islands
AircraftTwo Boeing 747s: KLM Flight 4805 and Pan Am Flight 1736
Fatalities583
Survivors61 (all on the Pan Am aircraft)

The Tenerife airport disaster occurred on 27 March 1977, when two Boeing 747 passenger aircraft collided on the runway at Los Rodeos Airport (now Tenerife North–Ciudad de La Laguna Airport) on the Spanish island of Tenerife. With 583 fatalities, it remains the deadliest accident in aviation history.

Background

Both aircraft had been diverted to the small regional airport of Los Rodeos after a bomb explosion at Gran Canaria Airport, their original destination, forced its closure. Los Rodeos became congested with diverted wide-body jets parked on the taxiway, forcing aircraft to back-taxi on the single runway. Thick fog then rolled in, reducing visibility to a few hundred metres; the control tower could not see the runway and neither crew could see the other aircraft.

Collision

The KLM flight, captained by Jacob Veldhuyzen van Zanten, the airline's chief flight instructor, was cleared to line up and wait. Misunderstood radio communications — compounded by a simultaneous transmission that blocked a key message — led the KLM crew to believe they had take-off clearance, and they began their take-off roll while the Pan Am 747 was still taxiing on the runway, having missed its assigned exit turn in the fog. The Pan Am crew saw the KLM jet's landing lights emerging from the mist seconds before impact and tried to veer off; the KLM crew attempted an early rotation. The KLM aircraft's landing gear and engines struck the Pan Am jet and it crashed back onto the runway, bursting into flames.

All 248 people aboard the KLM aircraft died. Of the 396 aboard the Pan Am flight, 335 died; 61 survived, including the flight crew, escaping from the forward section before it was consumed by fire.

Investigation and legacy

Spanish investigators concluded that the fundamental cause was the KLM captain's decision to take off without clearance; the Dutch authorities emphasised the ambiguous radio phraseology and the pressure on the crew. The disaster transformed aviation communication: standard phraseology was tightened worldwide ("take-off" reserved for actual clearance, "line up and wait" used otherwise), readback requirements were strengthened, and the accident became a foundational case study for crew resource management (CRM), the training discipline that teaches junior crew members to challenge a captain's decisions.
