The Battle of Poitiers was fought on 19 September 1356 between an Anglo-Gascon army commanded by Edward, the Black Prince, and a French army under King John II. It was the second of the three great English victories of the first phase of the Hundred Years' War, after Crécy (1346) and before Agincourt (1415), and it ended with the extraordinary capture of the French king on the battlefield.

Background

In 1356 the Black Prince led a great chevauchée — a mounted raid — north from Bordeaux through France, devastating the countryside. Near Poitiers, a larger French army blocked his laden, retreating force. After a day of failed negotiation mediated by a papal legate, the English took up a strong defensive position among hedges, vineyards and marshy ground near Nouaillé-Maupertuis, longbowmen lining a sunken lane.

The battle

Having learned from Crécy, the French attacked largely on foot, in successive divisions. The first assaults, including a small mounted charge against the archers, were shot down or repulsed at the hedge in hard fighting that at one point nearly broke the Anglo-Gascon line. When the Black Prince committed his reserve in a mounted counterstroke — with a Gascon force under the Captal de Buch circling to strike the French rear — the great division led by King John himself was surrounded. John fought bravely in the mêlée before surrendering, legendarily to the exile Denis de Morbecque. His youngest son Philip, who stayed at his side (prompting his epithet "the Bold" and the famous cry "Father, keep to the right! … to the left!"), was captured with him. French dead included the Constable of France and many great lords; English losses were far lighter.

Aftermath

John II was taken to London and held in honourable captivity. His ransom was set at three million gold crowns, and the resulting Treaty of Brétigny (1360) ceded an enlarged Aquitaine to England in full sovereignty. France, meanwhile, descended into crisis: the Jacquerie peasant revolt and the ascendancy of Étienne Marcel in Paris followed within two years. John, unable to complete his ransom and honour-bound when a hostage son escaped, returned voluntarily to English captivity in 1364 and died there — an episode long celebrated as the epitome of chivalric honour.
