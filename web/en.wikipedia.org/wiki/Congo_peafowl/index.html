The Congo peafowl (Afropavo congensis), also known as the African peafowl, is a species of peafowl native to the Congo Basin of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. It is the only member of the genus Afropavo and the only peafowl found outside Asia.

Discovery

The species remained unknown to science until 1936, when American zoologist James Chapin identified it from feathers in a museum collection and from specimens in the Congo, resolving a puzzle prompted by a single feather he had noticed in 1913 in the headdress of a Congolese man. It is regarded as a notable late discovery among large birds.

Description and behaviour

Males are deep blue with metallic green and violet plumage, a white crest, and bare red throat skin; females are chestnut-brown and green and also bear a short crest. The species is smaller and lacks the long ornamental train of the Indian peafowl. Congo peafowl live in lowland rainforest, forage on the ground for invertebrates and fallen fruit, and are thought to be generally monogamous.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the species as Vulnerable owing to habitat loss from logging and mining and to hunting for food. It is the national bird of the Democratic Republic of the Congo.
