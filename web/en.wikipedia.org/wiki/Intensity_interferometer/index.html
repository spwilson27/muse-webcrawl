Intensity interferometer

An intensity interferometer is an optical instrument that measures the angular diameter of stars by correlating fluctuations in light intensity recorded at two separated telescopes, rather than combining the light waves directly as in amplitude interferometry.

History

The technique was developed by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Twiss in the 1950s, building on the Hanbury Brown and Twiss effect in photon statistics. Their Narrabri Stellar Intensity Interferometer in Australia measured the diameters of 32 stars during the 1960s.

Advantages and limits

Because intensity correlations are insensitive to atmospheric phase distortions, the instrument tolerates poor seeing and imperfect optics. Its main limitation is low sensitivity, requiring bright targets, though modern fast detectors and large Cherenkov telescopes have revived interest in the method.
