Slash-and-burn

Slash-and-burn clearing in a tropical forest

Slash-and-burn agriculture is a farming method that involves the cutting and burning of plants in a forest or woodland to create a field called a swidden. The method begins by cutting down the trees and woody plants in an area. The downed vegetation, or "slash", is then left to dry, usually right before the rainiest part of the year. Then, the biomass is burned, resulting in a nutrient-rich layer of ash which makes the soil fertile, as well as temporarily eliminating weed and pest species.

Practice

After about three to five years, the plot's productivity decreases due to depletion of nutrients along with weed and pest invasion, causing the farmers to abandon the field and move to a new area. The abandoned plot is left fallow and forest regrows; given enough time — traditionally fallow periods of ten years or more — soil fertility is restored.

Distribution

Slash-and-burn is practiced by an estimated 200 to 500 million people worldwide, principally in the tropical rainforests of South America, Central and West Africa, and Southeast Asia. It is known by many local names, including shifting cultivation, milpa in Central America, jhum in South Asia, and swidden agriculture.

Environmental issues

With low population densities and long fallows, slash-and-burn can be sustainable. Under population pressure, shortened fallows degrade soils and the practice becomes a significant driver of deforestation and carbon emissions, releasing the carbon stored in forest biomass and reducing future sequestration.
