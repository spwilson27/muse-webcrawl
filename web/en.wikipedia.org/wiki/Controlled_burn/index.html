Controlled burn

A controlled burn, also known as a prescribed burn, is the planned and deliberate application of fire to a specific area of land, under specified weather and fuel conditions, to achieve land management objectives. It is a tool in forestry, prairie restoration, agriculture, and wildfire risk reduction.

Purposes

Prescribed burning reduces accumulated dead vegetation ("fuel loads") that could otherwise feed severe wildfires; recycles nutrients into the soil; controls invasive species and plant diseases; and maintains fire-adapted ecosystems whose plants — such as longleaf pine or many prairie grasses — depend on periodic fire to germinate and thrive. Indigenous peoples in North America and Australia practiced cultural burning for millennia before European colonization.

Practice

A burn plan specifies the area, weather window (wind, humidity, temperature), staffing, ignition pattern, and contingency resources. Trained crews use drip torches to ignite lines of fire, often burning against the wind (backing fire) for control, and hold the perimeter with firebreaks, water, and patrols. Smoke management is a major consideration because of effects on air quality and nearby communities.

Risks

Escaped prescribed burns occasionally occur; the 2000 Cerro Grande Fire in New Mexico began as a prescribed burn and destroyed hundreds of homes in Los Alamos, an event that reshaped United States burn policies and led to stronger review requirements.
