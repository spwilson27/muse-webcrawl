Fire ecology

Regrowth after fire in a fire-adapted ecosystem

Fire ecology is a scientific discipline concerned with natural processes involving fire in an ecosystem and the ecological effects of those processes. Many ecosystems, particularly prairie, savanna, chaparral and coniferous forests, have evolved with fire as an essential contributor to habitat vitality and renewal.

Fire regimes

A fire regime describes the pattern, frequency, and intensity of the fires that prevail in an area over long periods of time. Regimes range from frequent, low-intensity surface fires in ponderosa pine forests to infrequent, stand-replacing crown fires in boreal forest and lodgepole pine stands.

Adaptations

Many plants have evolved adaptations to fire. Serotinous cones, such as those of lodgepole pine and jack pine, open only when exposed to the heat of a fire. Some plants resprout vigorously from root crowns after burning, and the seeds of certain species germinate only after exposure to smoke or heat. Thick bark protects the cambium of mature trees in fire-frequent forests.

Effects

Fire recycles nutrients locked in dead vegetation, reduces competition from fire-sensitive species, creates habitat mosaics used by many animals, and can suppress insect and disease outbreaks. Conversely, changes in fire regime — through fire suppression, land use change, or climate change — can allow fuel accumulation and lead to unusually severe fires, or allow fire-sensitive vegetation to invade formerly open habitats.
