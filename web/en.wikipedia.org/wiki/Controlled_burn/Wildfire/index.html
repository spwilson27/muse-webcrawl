Wildfire

A wildfire in a coniferous forest

A wildfire, forest fire, or bushfire is an unplanned, uncontrolled and unpredictable fire in an area of combustible vegetation. Depending on the type of vegetation present, a wildfire may be more specifically identified as a bushfire (in Australia), desert fire, grass fire, hill fire, peat fire, prairie fire, vegetation fire, or veld fire. Wildfires can be incited by lightning or by human actions such as arson, discarded cigarettes, or sparks from equipment.

Behaviour

Fire behaviour is governed by the fire environment triangle of weather, topography, and fuels. Wind, low humidity, and high temperatures accelerate spread; slopes allow preheating of upslope fuels; and fuel load, arrangement, and moisture determine intensity. Fires are classified as ground fires, surface fires, and crown fires, the last being the fastest and most intense.

Causes

Lightning is the dominant natural ignition source in remote regions, while in many populated areas the majority of ignitions are human-caused, including power-line failures. Climate change has increased the frequency of fire weather and lengthened fire seasons in many regions, including western North America and Australia.

Effects

Wildfires cause loss of life and property, destroy habitat, and produce large quantities of smoke harmful to health far downwind. At the same time, fire is a natural and necessary process in many ecosystems — see fire ecology. Suppression policy, wildfire suppression, and fuel treatments such as controlled burns are used to manage risk.
