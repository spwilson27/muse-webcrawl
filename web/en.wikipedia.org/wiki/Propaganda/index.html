Propaganda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Propaganda is communication that is primarily used to influence or persuade an audience to further an agenda, which may not be objective and may selectively present facts to encourage a particular perception, or produce an emotional rather than rational response. The term originated with the Catholic Church's Congregation for the Propagation of the Faith (1622) and was neutral until the First World War, after which it acquired strongly negative connotations of manipulation and deceit.

Modern states institutionalised propaganda in both world wars (Britain's Wellington House, America's Creel Committee, Goebbels's Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda in Nazi Germany) and throughout the Cold War (Radio Free Europe, Soviet active measures). Techniques catalogued by analysts include repetition, loaded language, testimonials, bandwagon appeals, scapegoating, censorship of alternatives and, in the digital era, coordinated inauthentic accounts and micro-targeted disinformation.

Study and responses

Jacques Ellul's Propaganda (1962) argued that propaganda is a total sociological phenomenon of technological society, addressed as much to the propagandist's own public as to enemies. Democracies counter hostile propaganda through public diplomacy, fact-checking and media literacy, while struggling with the boundary between persuasion and manipulation in their own strategic communications. Scholars now often prefer narrower terms — disinformation (false and deliberate), misinformation (false but not deliberate) — reserving propaganda for organised state or party persuasion systems.
