Microfilament

Microfilaments, also called actin filaments, are protein filaments in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells that form part of the cytoskeleton. At about 6–8 nanometres in diameter they are the thinnest of the three cytoskeletal filament systems, the others being intermediate filaments and microtubules.

Structure

A microfilament is a helical polymer of the protein actin. Individual actin monomers (G-actin) assemble into long chains (F-actin) in which two strands wind around each other. The filament is polarised: monomers add more rapidly at the fast-growing "barbed" (plus) end than at the "pointed" (minus) end, and ATP hydrolysis by actin powers a process of continuous assembly and disassembly known as treadmilling.

Functions

Microfilaments maintain and change cell shape, form the contractile ring that divides cells in cytokinesis, and underlie structures such as microvilli and filopodia. In muscle cells, actin filaments slide relative to myosin thick filaments to produce contraction. Together with myosin motor proteins they also drive cytoplasmic streaming, amoeboid movement and the trafficking of vesicles. Their assembly is regulated by proteins such as profilin, cofilin and the Arp2/3 complex, which nucleates branched actin networks at the leading edge of migrating cells.
