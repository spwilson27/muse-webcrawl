Teshuvah

Teshuvah (Hebrew: תְּשׁוּבָה, literally "return") is the Jewish practice of repentance — returning to God and to the path of the commandments after transgression. It is a central concept in Judaism, closely associated with the High Holy Days of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, and with the month of Elul that precedes them, though Jewish teaching holds that repentance is possible at any time. A person who undertakes teshuvah and embraces observance is called a baal teshuvah (literally "master of return").

In the Bible and rabbinic teaching

The Hebrew Bible repeatedly calls Israel to "return" (shuv) to God — for example in Deuteronomy 30 and the prophecies of Hosea, Isaiah and Ezekiel, who teaches that God takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked but desires that they turn from their ways and live. The book of Jonah, read on Yom Kippur afternoon, depicts the repentance of Nineveh. Rabbinic literature exalts teshuvah as one of the things created before the world itself, and teaches that repentance done out of love can transform deliberate sins into merits.

Maimonides' formulation

Maimonides codified the laws of repentance in Hilkhot Teshuvah in the Mishneh Torah. His steps include: recognising and ceasing the sin; remorse; verbal confession (vidui); restitution and appeasing any person wronged — for offences against another person, Yom Kippur does not atone until the victim is appeased; and resolve not to repeat the act. Complete teshuvah (teshuvah gemurah) is demonstrated when one faces the same opportunity to sin and refrains. The gates of repentance, Maimonides writes, are never locked.

Practice

During Elul and the Ten Days of Repentance between Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur, Jews traditionally engage in self-examination (cheshbon hanefesh), recite additional penitential prayers (Selichot), give charity, and seek forgiveness from people they have wronged. In modern usage, "teshuvah movement" also denotes the broader phenomenon of secular or less-observant Jews returning to traditional observance, and a baal teshuvah (plural baalei teshuvah) is often simply a Jew who has become Orthodox later in life; outreach organisations such as Chabad and Aish HaTorah focus on this population.
