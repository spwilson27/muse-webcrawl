Post-2008 Irish economic downturn

The post-2008 Irish economic downturn was a severe financial and economic crisis in Ireland that followed the bursting of the Irish property bubble and the global financial crisis of 2007–08. It ended the "Celtic Tiger" boom years and led to a deep recession, a banking collapse, mass unemployment, renewed emigration, and an EU–IMF bailout.

Background

During the late 1990s and 2000s, Ireland enjoyed rapid growth driven by foreign direct investment, EU membership, and increasingly by a credit-fuelled construction and property boom. Irish banks lent heavily to property developers, funded by borrowing on international wholesale markets.

Crisis

When property prices began falling in 2007 and global credit markets froze in 2008, the banks faced insolvency. On 30 September 2008 the government issued a blanket guarantee of the liabilities of the six main Irish banks. The cost of rescuing the banks — eventually about €64 billion, including some €31 billion for Anglo Irish Bank alone — overwhelmed the public finances. The National Asset Management Agency (NAMA) was created in 2009 to take over toxic property loans. Unemployment rose from under 5% to nearly 15%, house prices fell by more than half from their peak, and "ghost estates" of unfinished housing became a symbol of the crash.

Bailout and recovery

In November 2010 Ireland entered an €85 billion EU–IMF programme and implemented years of austerity budgets. Ireland exited the bailout in December 2013, and strong growth resumed in the mid-2010s, though emigration and the social costs of the crisis left lasting effects.
