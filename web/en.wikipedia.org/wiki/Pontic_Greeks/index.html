The Pontic Greeks (Greek: Πόντιοι, romanized: Póndii), also called Pontian Greeks, are an ethnically Greek group indigenous to the region of Pontus, in northeastern Anatolia (in modern Turkey), along the southern coast of the Black Sea. Greeks inhabited the region from at least 700 BC until the population exchange between Greece and Turkey in 1923.

History

Greek colonists from Miletus founded cities such as Sinope and Trapezus (Trabzon) on the Pontic coast in the 8th century BC. The region later formed part of the kingdom of Pontus under the Mithridatic dynasty, and subsequently of the Roman and Byzantine empires. After the fall of Constantinople in 1204, the Empire of Trebizond preserved Greek rule in Pontus until its conquest by the Ottomans in 1461.

Under Ottoman rule the Pontic Greeks maintained a distinct culture, language and Orthodox Christian faith, though a significant minority converted to Islam while retaining the Pontic Greek language. In the late Ottoman period, Pontic Greeks prospered in trade, mining and tobacco cultivation.

Genocide and population exchange

During and after the First World War, the Pontic Greeks suffered deportations, massacres and death marches as part of the wider Greek genocide; hundreds of thousands perished between 1914 and 1923. Under the 1923 Convention Concerning the Exchange of Greek and Turkish Populations, the surviving Orthodox Pontic Greeks were expelled to Greece, while the region's Muslims — including Greek-speaking Muslims — were largely exempted or exchanged in the opposite direction. Greece officially commemorates the Pontic Greek genocide on 19 May.

Language and culture

Pontic Greek is a dialect of Greek preserving many archaic features lost in the standard language; it is now endangered, though still spoken in Greece and by Muslim communities in the Of valley of Turkey. Pontic culture is renowned for its music, centred on the kemenche (lyra), and energetic circle dances such as the serra.
