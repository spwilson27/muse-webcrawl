In vitro

In vitro
EtymologyLatin: "in glass"
ContrastIn vivo, in silico, ex vivo

In vitro (Latin for "in glass"; often styled in italics) refers to studies and procedures performed with biological components — cells, tissues, microorganisms, or biomolecules — outside their normal biological context, typically in laboratory glassware or plastic vessels such as test tubes, flasks, and Petri dishes. It contrasts with in vivo work, performed in a living organism, and in silico work, performed by computer simulation.

Methods and uses

In vitro techniques include cell culture, tissue culture and organ culture, assays of isolated enzymes and receptors, and molecular methods such as the polymerase chain reaction (PCR). They allow precise control of conditions, high-throughput screening of drug candidates, and study of mechanisms that would be impractical or unethical in whole organisms.

Advantages and limitations

The controlled environment of in vitro work gives reproducibility and detail but can misrepresent whole-organism behaviour: isolated cells lack interactions with other tissues, circulation, and metabolism. Results therefore require validation in vivo. The best-known medical application of the term is in vitro fertilisation (IVF), in which fertilisation of an egg by sperm takes place in a laboratory dish.
