Digambara

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digambara ("sky-clad") is one of the two major schools of Jainism, the other being Śvetāmbara ("white-clad"). The Digambara tradition holds that its monks must renounce all possessions, including clothing, in keeping with the practice of Mahavira, the 24th Tirthankara. The two schools differ in their views on the Jain canon, the spiritual status of women, the biography of Mahavira, and details of doctrine and monastic discipline. Digambaras are concentrated chiefly in central and southern India, particularly Maharashtra, Karnataka, and Madhya Pradesh.

Origins and the schism

According to Digambara tradition, after Mahavira's nirvana the community was led by a succession of acharyas. A famine in Magadha, traditionally dated some two centuries after Mahavira, is said to have driven a group under Bhadrabahu south to Karnataka, while those who remained in the north relaxed the rule of nudity. When the southern monks returned, differences in practice and scripture had hardened into a permanent division, conventionally dated to the 1st century CE. Digambaras hold that the original canon (the Purvas and Angas) has been lost, while the Śvetāmbaras preserve it in a form Digambaras do not accept as authentic.

Beliefs and practices

Digambara monks (munis) take the five great vows (mahavratas) and practise complete nudity as a symbol of total renunciation; they carry only a whisk of fallen peacock feathers (pinchi) to gently clear insects from their path, and a wooden water pot (kamandalu). They eat once a day, standing, from their cupped hands in a devotee's home. Digambara nuns (aryikas) wear white or saffron robes. Because full liberation is held to require the male monastic path, Digambara doctrine teaches that a woman must first be reborn as a man to attain moksha — a point of sharp disagreement with the Śvetāmbaras.

Digambara philosophy was shaped by thinkers such as Kundakunda (author of the Samayasara), Samantabhadra, and Akalanka. The Tattvartha Sutra of Umaswati is accepted by both schools. The colossal statue of Bahubali at Shravanabelagola, carved in the 10th century under the Western Ganga dynasty, is among the tradition's most famous monuments.

Sub-sects

The Digambara community includes the Bispanth, Terapanth (which emerged in the 17th century in reaction to the institution of resident clerics called bhattarakas), and the smaller Taran Panth, whose followers worship scriptures rather than images. Bhattarakas — robed, celibate religious administrators — remain influential in some regions, notably Karnataka and Maharashtra.
