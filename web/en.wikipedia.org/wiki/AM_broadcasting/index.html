AM broadcasting is radio broadcasting using amplitude modulation (AM) transmissions. It was the first method developed for making audio radio transmissions and remains in worldwide use, primarily in the medium wave band. AM stations were the dominant form of radio entertainment from the 1920s until FM's rise in the 1970s.

Technical characteristics

An AM signal encodes audio by varying the amplitude of the carrier wave. Medium-wave signals travel by groundwave during the day, typically reaching tens to a few hundred kilometres, and by skywave at night, when ionospheric reflection allows reception hundreds or thousands of kilometres away — necessitating power reductions or directional antennas for many stations after sunset. AM audio bandwidth is limited (often around 5–10 kHz), giving lower fidelity than FM, and the band is vulnerable to static from lightning and electrical interference.

History

Commercial AM broadcasting began around 1920, with stations such as KDKA in Pittsburgh. Networks (NBC, CBS) built national programming around the band. In the United States the FCC designates certain "clear channel" frequencies for high-power (50,000-watt) stations protected over wide areas. Longwave and shortwave AM broadcasts served international audiences.

Decline and status

Music formats largely migrated to FM and then to streaming, leaving AM dominated by talk, news, sports and religious programming. Some countries have switched off medium-wave services, while the United States expanded the band (1610–1700 kHz) in the 1990s. Debate continues over AM's place in car dashboards, with emergency-management officials stressing its unmatched reach during disasters.
