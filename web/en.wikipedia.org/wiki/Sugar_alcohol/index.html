Sugar alcohol

Sugar alcohols (also called polyhydric alcohols, polyalcohols or polyols) are organic compounds, typically derived from sugars, in which a hydroxyl group (–OH) replaces the carbonyl group of an aldose or ketose. They are widely used as sugar substitutes and food additives because they are sweet but are absorbed more slowly and incompletely than sugars, producing a smaller rise in blood glucose.

Sorbitol occurs naturally in stone fruits and is manufactured by hydrogenating glucose; xylitol is found in birch bark and fruit and produced from xylose; erythritol is made by fermentation and is largely excreted unchanged, giving it near-zero usable calories and good digestive tolerance. Many sugar alcohols produce a cooling sensation on dissolving, exploited in mints and chewing gum.

Health aspects

Because oral bacteria ferment sugar alcohols poorly, they do not contribute to dental caries; xylitol in particular is actively anti-cariogenic. Their downside is gastrointestinal: the unabsorbed fraction is fermented by gut bacteria or draws water osmotically, so excessive consumption causes bloating and a laxative effect, which regulators require to be labelled on foods containing them above certain levels. Sugar alcohols are generally recognised as safe food additives in the European Union (E numbers E420–E968) and the United States.
