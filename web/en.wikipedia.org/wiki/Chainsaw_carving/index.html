Chainsaw carving

Chainsaw carving of a bear

Chainsaw carving is the art of using a chainsaw to create sculptures from wood. It emerged as a recognised folk art form in the United States in the 1950s and 1960s and grew rapidly in popularity from the 1980s onward. Carvers produce works ranging from rustic animals, such as bears and eagles, to elaborate furniture, totem poles, and fine-art sculpture. Competitions and festivals devoted to chainsaw carving are held around the world.

History

The earliest chainsaw art is generally dated to the 1950s, when artists and loggers began experimenting with the newly common portable chainsaw as a sculpting tool. In 1961, Ken Kaiser created the "Trees of Mystery" sculptures in California. The art form expanded in the 1980s, with carvers such as Art Moe gaining attention, and the first chainsaw carving competitions being held. The 1988 publication of carving guides and the rise of televised speed-carving events helped popularise the craft.

In the United Kingdom, chainsaw carving developed its own scene, and in Japan the art gained prominence through the Toei Chainsaw Art competition and carvers demonstrating at forestry festivals.

Technique

Carvers typically work on logs or stumps, blocking out the rough form with a large saw fitted with a standard guide bar, then refining detail with smaller saws equipped with specialised carving bars (such as dime-tip and quarter-tip bars) that have smaller noses for fine work and reduced kickback. Details such as eyes and fur texture may be finished with grinders, rotary tools, chisels, and sanders, and the work is often coloured with torches, paints, or stains and sealed with oil or varnish.

Safety

Chainsaw carving carries significant risk. Carvers wear protective equipment including chainsaw chaps or trousers, steel-toe boots, eye and ear protection, and gloves. Competitions enforce safety standards, and carvers train to manage kickback, the sudden upward movement of the bar tip.

Competitions and culture

Speed-carving events, in which artists produce a finished piece in an hour or less before a crowd, are a staple of fairs and logging shows. Major competitions include the Chetwynd International Chainsaw Carving Championship in British Columbia, Canada, and events in the United States, Germany, and Australia. Chainsaw sculptures are commonly seen as roadside attractions, garden ornaments, and public art in timber-producing regions.
