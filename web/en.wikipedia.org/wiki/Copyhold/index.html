Copyhold

Copyhold was a form of customary land ownership — or rather tenure — in England, in use from the late medieval period into the early twentieth century. A copyholder held land from the lord of a manor according to the custom of the manor, and the tenant's title was evidenced by a copy of the entry in the manorial court rolls — hence "copyhold", tenure "by copy of court roll".

History

Copyhold developed from the unfree tenure of villeinage. Over time customary tenants gained protection in the royal courts, and by the sixteenth century copyhold was a secure and heritable form of tenure, though subject to manorial incidents such as fines on admission and heriots (a death duty, often the best beast). Copyholds could be for lives or in fee, and the customs governing them varied from manor to manor.

Abolition

The Law of Property Act 1922 converted all remaining copyhold land into freehold (socage) tenure with effect from 1 January 1926, although certain manorial rights — such as rights to mines and minerals, fairs and markets — could be preserved by registration, and some manorial incidents survived until later reforms.
