Haitian Vodou

Haitian Vodou (also spelled Voodoo, Vodun, Vodoun) is an African diasporic religion that developed in Haiti among enslaved West and Central Africans during the colonial period, blending Fon, Yoruba and Kongo religious traditions with Roman Catholicism and other influences. It centres on veneration of spirits known as lwa (loa), who serve as intermediaries between humanity and the supreme creator, Bondye.

Beliefs and practice

Practitioners, called vodouizan, are led by priests (oungan) and priestesses (manbo). Ceremonies, held in temples (ounfò), typically involve drumming, song, dance and offerings, and commonly culminate in a lwa "riding" or possessing a devotee, who then gives counsel or healing to the community. The lwa are grouped into nations (nanchon), the best known of which are the Rada (benevolent spirits) and the Petwo (more fiery or forceful spirits). Well-known lwa include Papa Legba, guardian of the crossroads; Ogou, spirit of iron and war; Ezili Danto and Ezili Freda; and Baron Samedi, associated with the dead and cemeteries.

History

Vodou took shape in the French colony of Saint-Domingue and played a role in the Haitian Revolution; tradition holds that the August 1791 ceremony at Bois Caïman helped launch the uprising that led to Haitian independence in 1804. Despite long periods of suppression by church and state, including anti-superstition campaigns, Vodou remained central to Haitian culture, and it was officially recognised as a religion by the Haitian government in 2003.
