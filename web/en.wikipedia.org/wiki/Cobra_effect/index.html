Cobra effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The anecdote of cobra breeding in colonial Delhi illustrates perverse incentives.

The cobra effect occurs when an attempted solution to a problem makes the problem worse. It is a type of unintended consequence arising from a perverse incentive, and the term illustrates how well-meaning interventions in complex systems can backfire.

Origin

The term comes from an anecdote set in colonial India. Concerned about the number of venomous cobras in Delhi, the British government offered a bounty for every dead cobra delivered. Initially the scheme reduced cobra numbers, but enterprising residents began breeding cobras to collect the reward. When the government discovered this and cancelled the bounty, the breeders released their now-worthless snakes, and the wild cobra population ended up larger than before.

A similar story is told about Hanoi under French colonial rule, where a bounty on rat tails led to tail-less rats being released to breed, and to rat farming on the city's outskirts.

Examples

  • Bug bounties gone wrong in organizations that reward reported defects can incentivize creating defects to report.
  • Performance targets in schools and hospitals have led to teaching to the test or reclassifying patients rather than improving outcomes (an instance of Goodhart's law).
  • Wells Fargo's sales quotas encouraged employees to open millions of unauthorized accounts, resulting in scandal.
  • The "Streisand effect" is a related phenomenon, in which attempts to suppress information publicize it.

Related concepts

The cobra effect is connected to Goodhart's law ("when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"), Campbell's law, and the broader theory of perverse incentives and moral hazard in economics and systems thinking.
