Obstructive sleep apnea

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a sleep-related breathing disorder in which the upper airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, causing episodes of reduced (hypopnea) or absent (apnea) airflow despite continuing respiratory effort. It is the most common form of sleep apnea, distinct from central sleep apnea, in which the brain temporarily fails to send breathing signals.

SymptomsLoud snoring, witnessed apneas, gasping, unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness
ComplicationsHypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, accidents
Risk factorsObesity, male sex, older age, alcohol, craniofacial anatomy
DiagnosisPolysomnography or home sleep testing (AHI)
TreatmentCPAP, oral appliances, weight loss, surgery

Signs and symptoms

Typical features are loud habitual snoring, pauses in breathing reported by a bed partner, choking or gasping arousals, morning headache, nocturia, and excessive daytime sleepiness. Because events fragment sleep, patients may suffer impaired concentration, memory problems, irritability, and an increased risk of motor vehicle accidents.

Causes and mechanism

During sleep, reduced tone in the muscles that hold open the pharynx allows the soft tissues of the throat to collapse inward, especially in people with a narrow airway, enlarged tonsils or tongue, retrognathia, or excess neck fat. Alcohol and sedatives worsen collapsibility. Each obstruction ends with a brief arousal and a surge in sympathetic activity, blood pressure, and heart rate — repeated hundreds of times a night in severe cases, with accompanying falls in blood oxygen.

Diagnosis

Severity is quantified by the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI): the number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep. An AHI of 5–14 is mild, 15–29 moderate, and 30 or more severe. The reference standard is in-laboratory polysomnography, though home sleep apnea testing is widely used for uncomplicated suspected cases.

Treatment

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), delivered by mask from a bedside device, splints the airway open and is the most effective treatment, though adherence is a persistent challenge. Alternatives include mandibular advancement oral appliances, positional therapy, weight loss (including with modern anti-obesity medications or bariatric surgery), and surgical procedures such as uvulopalatopharyngoplasty or hypoglossal nerve stimulation in selected patients.

Untreated OSA is associated with hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, insulin resistance, and cognitive decline, making it a major public-health concern; prevalence rises with age and obesity, and many cases remain undiagnosed.
