Brushed DC electric motor

A simple two-pole brushed DC motor.

A brushed DC electric motor is an internally commutated electric motor designed to run from a direct current (DC) power source. Brushed motors were the first commercially important application of electric power to driving mechanical energy, and DC distribution systems were used for more than 100 years in commercial and industrial buildings. Because their speed can be varied simply by changing the supply voltage or the strength of the magnetic field, brushed DC motors are widely used in applications from toys and appliances to steel rolling mills.

Structure

A brushed DC motor is made up of four basic components: a stator (either permanent magnets or field windings), a rotor (armature) wound with coils of wire, a commutator, and brushes. The commutator is a rotary electrical switch, typically made of copper segments insulated from one another, mounted on the rotor shaft. Spring-loaded carbon or graphite brushes slide against the commutator, feeding current to the rotor windings.

Operation

When current flows through the rotor windings, the resulting magnetic field interacts with the stator field to produce torque. As the rotor turns, the commutator reverses the direction of current in the windings at precisely the right moment, so the magnetic poles of the rotor are continually attracted and repelled by the stator poles, sustaining rotation. The torque of a DC motor is roughly proportional to armature current, and no-load speed is roughly proportional to supply voltage.

Types

Motors with wound stators are classified by the connection of the field winding: series-wound (high starting torque, used in traction and starters), shunt-wound (good speed regulation), compound-wound, and separately excited. Permanent-magnet brushed motors are common in small appliances, model vehicles and automotive accessories.

Advantages and disadvantages

Brushed motors are simple, inexpensive and easy to control, but their brushes wear out, generate electrical noise and sparking, and limit maximum speed. These drawbacks have led to their replacement in many applications by brushless DC motors, which use electronic commutation.
