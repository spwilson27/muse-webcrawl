Baltic peoples

The Baltic peoples are the speakers of the Baltic languages, a branch of the Indo-European language family, native to the eastern shore of the Baltic Sea in Northern Europe. The surviving Baltic peoples are the Lithuanians and Latvians; historically the group also included the Old Prussians, Curonians, Samogitians, Semigallians, Selonians, Galindians, and Yotvingians, most of whom were assimilated or extinguished by the early modern period.

Languages

The Baltic languages divide into Eastern Baltic—Lithuanian and Latvian, the only survivors—and Western Baltic, represented by extinct Old Prussian, documented in catechisms from the 16th century. Lithuanian is noted for retaining many archaic Indo-European features. The Baltic languages share features with Slavic languages, though the nature of the Balto-Slavic relationship is debated.

History

Baltic-speaking cultures are associated with the Corded Ware horizon and later archaeological cultures of the forest zone west of the upper Dnieper. Tacitus mentioned the amber-gathering Aestii on the Baltic coast. In the 13th century the Teutonic and Livonian Orders conquered and Christianised the western and northern Balts during the Northern Crusades; the Prussians were Germanised over subsequent centuries. The Lithuanians resisted, building the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, one of Europe's largest states, and were the last pagans in Europe, formally Christianised only in 1387. Latvian identity coalesced under German and later Swedish, Polish, and Russian rule. Both nations established independent states in 1918, were annexed by the USSR in 1940, and restored independence in 1990–91.

Culture

Baltic folk culture is renowned for polyphonic songs—Lithuanian sutartinės, inscribed by UNESCO—and for song festivals that became vehicles of national identity. Pre-Christian religion featured deities such as Perkūnas (thunder), Dievas (sky), and Žemyna (earth), and its motifs persist in folklore and neopagan revivals such as Romuva.
