Micropropagation

Micropropagation is the practice of rapidly multiplying plant material using modern plant tissue culture methods to produce large numbers of progeny plants. A small piece of tissue (an explant) — often a shoot tip or meristem — is grown in sterile conditions on a nutrient medium containing plant hormones, where it multiplies into many shoots or plantlets that are genetically identical to the parent plant. The technique is a major commercial industry for orchids, bananas, potatoes, and many ornamentals.

Stages

Micropropagation is usually described in stages: (0) selection and preparation of a healthy mother plant; (1) establishment of a sterile culture from the explant; (2) multiplication, in which cytokinin-rich medium induces masses of shoots that are repeatedly subdivided; (3) rooting of shoots, promoted by auxins; and (4) acclimatization ("hardening off"), in which plantlets are transferred to soil and gradually adapted from near-100% humidity and low light in the vessel to normal growing conditions — the stage with the greatest losses.

Advantages and uses

Because plant cells are totipotent, micropropagation can produce thousands or millions of uniform plants from a single parent within a year, in a small laboratory space, independent of season. Meristem culture can free plants of viruses, since the growing tip often outruns infection, making the method central to producing certified disease-free seed potatoes and fruit stock. It is also used to conserve rare and endangered species, to propagate plants that are sterile or breed slowly (such as many orchids and date palms), and in forestry and pharmaceutical production.

Limitations

The method is labour-intensive and costly, requiring sterile facilities and skilled workers. Genetic fidelity is high but not absolute: prolonged culture, especially through a callus phase, can produce "somaclonal variation". Contamination of cultures by bacteria or fungi is a constant practical problem.
