Tetanus vaccine

Type: Toxoid vaccine
Target: Tetanus (Clostridium tetani)
Route: Intramuscular injection

Tetanus vaccine, also known as tetanus toxoid (TT), is a toxoid vaccine used to prevent tetanus. It consists of the inactivated tetanus toxin (tetanospasmin) produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani, adsorbed onto aluminium salts as an adjuvant. During childhood, five doses are recommended in most schedules, with booster doses advised every ten years in adults.

Medical uses

The vaccine is highly effective: a full series provides protection in nearly all recipients. It is commonly given in combination formulations, including DTaP (with diphtheria and pertussis) for children and Td or Tdap for adolescents and adults. Vaccination of pregnant women with tetanus toxoid is a cornerstone of the global effort to eliminate maternal and neonatal tetanus.

History

Passive immunization with tetanus antitoxin was developed in the 1890s following the work of Emil von Behring and Shibasaburo Kitasato. The toxoid vaccine was developed by Gaston Ramon in the 1920s and came into widespread use during the Second World War, when immunization of soldiers dramatically reduced tetanus cases compared with the First World War. The vaccine is included on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Effectiveness and safety

After a complete series, protection lasts at least a decade, and cases of tetanus in fully vaccinated individuals are extremely rare. Side effects are generally mild and include pain, redness, and swelling at the injection site, low-grade fever, and tiredness; severe allergic reactions are very rare.
