Waste (law)

In property law, waste is damage or destruction to real property committed by a person in possession of it — such as a tenant or life tenant — that diminishes the value of the interest of the owner or of a future interest holder (the remainderman or reversioner). The doctrine protects the inheritance by requiring the possessor to preserve the property for those entitled to it later.

Categories

  • Voluntary (affirmative) waste — deliberate acts that damage the property, such as cutting valuable timber or demolishing buildings.
  • Permissive waste — passive neglect, such as allowing a roof to decay or failing to make ordinary repairs or pay taxes.
  • Ameliorating waste — changes that technically alter the property's character but increase its value, such as converting a house to a more profitable use; historically actionable, it is rarely restrained by modern courts.
  • Equitable waste — wanton destruction, such as stripping a mansion of its fixtures, which equity would restrain even where a lease purported to excuse waste.

Remedies

The injured party may sue for damages — historically trebled under English statutes stemming from the Statute of Gloucester (1278) — or obtain an injunction against threatened waste. Life tenants and tenants for years are generally liable; tenants at will and at sufferance have narrower obligations.
