Reverberation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reverberation (commonly shortened to reverb) is the persistence of sound in a space after the original sound has stopped, produced by a large number of reflections off walls, ceilings, floors, and other surfaces that build up and then decay as the sound is absorbed. It differs from an echo, in which reflections are individually distinct.

Reverberation time

The standard measure is RT60, the time required for the sound pressure level to decay by 60 decibels after the source stops. Wallace Clement Sabine, the founder of architectural acoustics, derived the empirical formula:

T60 ≈ 0.161 · V / A (seconds)

where V is the room volume in cubic metres and A is the total absorption in sabins (area multiplied by absorption coefficient). Concert halls typically aim for an RT60 around 1.8–2.0 seconds for orchestral music, while classrooms require much shorter times (under ~0.6 s) for speech intelligibility.

Effects and simulation

In audio production, artificial reverberation is created with mechanical devices (plate and spring reverbs), acoustic chambers, and digital algorithms. Digital methods include convolution reverb, which applies a recorded impulse response of a real space, and algorithmic designs such as Schroeder reverberators and feedback delay networks. Excessive reverberation masks direct sound and reduces clarity; too little makes a space feel "dead" and unnatural.
