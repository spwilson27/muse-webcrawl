Dhu al-Qarnayn

TitleDhū al-Qarnayn (ذُو ٱلْقَرْنَيْن)
Literal meaning"He of the Two Horns"
SourceQuran, Surah Al-Kahf (18), verses 83–101
Known forBuilding a barrier against Gog and Magog; journeys to the rising and setting places of the sun
Common identificationsAlexander the Great; Cyrus the Great; other legendary kings

Dhu al-Qarnayn (Arabic: ذُو ٱلْقَرْنَيْن, "He of the Two Horns") is a figure who appears in the Quran, in Surah Al-Kahf (chapter 18, verses 83–101), as a righteous ruler who travels to the ends of the earth and constructs a great barrier to hold back the nations of Gog and Magog until the end of time. The identity of Dhu al-Qarnayn has been debated by Muslim scholars and historians for over a millennium.

The Quranic narrative

The passage in Al-Kahf describes three journeys. In the first, Dhu al-Qarnayn travels west to the setting place of the sun, where he finds it setting in a muddy spring and rules a people there. In the second, he travels east to the rising place of the sun, finding a people for whom no shelter had been provided against it. In the third, he journeys to a place between two mountains, whose people ask him to build a barrier against Gog and Magog, who are spreading corruption in the land. Using blocks of iron heated in furnaces and molten copper poured over them, he constructs an impregnable wall, declaring that it will stand until God's promise comes to pass.

The question of identity

Classical commentators most often identified Dhu al-Qarnayn with Alexander the Great, largely because of the popularity of the Alexander Romance in Syriac, Persian and Arabic versions, in which Alexander is depicted with horns and builds a gate against the nations of the north. In the modern era, some scholars have proposed Cyrus the Great, founder of the Achaemenid Empire, pointing to his portrayal as God's anointed in the Hebrew Bible and to the horned imagery on his coinage and monuments. Other candidates advanced over the centuries include various South Arabian (Himyarite) kings and even the Chinese emperor Qin Shi Huang, builder of the Great Wall.

The wall and Gog and Magog

The barrier of Dhu al-Qarnayn became one of the most durable motifs in Islamic geography and eschatology. Medieval geographers located it variously in the Caucasus, at Derbent on the Caspian, or in Central Asia, and it merged with the older Alexander's Gate tradition. In eschatological literature, the breaching of the wall and the release of Gog and Magog (Ya'juj and Ma'juj) is one of the major signs of the approaching Day of Judgment.

Legacy

The figure influenced Persian epic literature, the Alexander Romance tradition, and popular storytelling across the Islamic world. The epithet "the Two-Horned" is still used in some regions as a title for Alexander. Scholars today generally treat the Quranic account as belonging to a late antique storytelling milieu in which Alexander legends circulated widely in Syriac Christian and Jewish literature.
