Grand Banks

The Grand Banks lie on the continental shelf southeast of the island of Newfoundland.

The Grand Banks are a series of underwater plateaus southeast of the island of Newfoundland on the North American continental shelf. The Grand Banks of Newfoundland are considered one of the richest fishing grounds in the world, historically famed for Atlantic cod.

Geography and oceanography

The banks are relatively shallow, ranging from about 15 to 90 metres in depth. The cold Labrador Current mixes here with the warm Gulf Stream, creating conditions that stir up nutrients and support dense concentrations of plankton, capelin, and the fish that feed on them — including cod, haddock, halibut, herring and mackerel. The mixing of currents also produces frequent fog.

History of the fishery

European fishing on the banks began around 1500, shortly after John Cabot's 1497 voyage reported seas so full of cod they could be caught in baskets. For nearly five centuries, fleets from England, France, Spain, Portugal and later Canada and the United States harvested the banks. Disputes over fishing rights contributed to arrangements such as the French Shore on Newfoundland's coast.

After Canada extended its exclusive economic zone to 200 nautical miles in 1977, foreign overfishing on the "nose" and "tail" of the banks outside the zone continued. In 1992, following the collapse of the northern cod stock, Canada declared a moratorium on cod fishing — one of the largest industrial shutdowns in Canadian history, throwing tens of thousands out of work. The 1995 "Turbot War" with Spain followed over Greenland halibut on the banks. The cod moratorium was largely lifted only in 2024, with a small commercial quota.

Petroleum

The Grand Banks also host offshore oil fields — including Hibernia, Terra Nova, White Rose and Hebron — produced since 1997 from fields discovered beginning in 1979. The Ocean Ranger drilling rig capsized on the Grand Banks in a storm in February 1982 with the loss of all 84 crew.
