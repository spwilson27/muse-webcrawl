Tylwyth Teg

The Tylwyth Teg (Welsh for "the Fair Family") are the fairies of Welsh folklore. Described as fair-haired, beautiful and generally benevolent beings of human or smaller stature, they inhabit an otherworld reached through lakes, hills and fairy rings, and feature in a large body of Welsh folk tales collected in the nineteenth century.

Characteristics

Unlike the often malicious fairies of some traditions, the Tylwyth Teg were usually portrayed as kindly, fond of dancing in rings on moonlit nights and of music, though mortals who joined their dances might find that years had passed in the human world. They were said to reward clean houses with gifts, to borrow household items (leaving payment behind), and — in a darker motif shared with other fairy lore — occasionally to exchange human infants for changelings. Regional kinds include the lake-dwelling Gwragedd Annwn, the fairy brides of several famous tales, whose marriages to mortals typically ended when a taboo — often striking the bride with iron — was broken.

In legend

The Tylwyth Teg are associated with the otherworld of Annwn and, in some accounts, are ruled by Gwyn ap Nudd. Nineteenth-century collectors such as Wirt Sikes, in British Goblins (1880), classified the Welsh fairy types, and the folklore influenced later Welsh literature and fantasy writing.
