Stratification (seeds)

In horticulture, stratification is a process of treating seeds to simulate natural conditions that the seeds must experience before germination can occur. Many plant species have physiological dormancy: their seeds will not sprout until they have passed through a period of cold, moist conditions — in nature, winter. Artificial stratification gives gardeners a way to break that dormancy in the refrigerator.

Process

In cold stratification, seeds are mixed with or layered in a moist medium (sand, peat, vermiculite or a damp paper towel), sealed in a bag or container, and refrigerated at about 1–5 °C (33–41 °F) for a species-dependent period of a few weeks to several months. The medium must be damp but not wet, and the seeds checked for mould or premature sprouting. Some species require a period of warm stratification before the cold phase ("warm–cold stratification"), imitating a summer followed by a winter.

Biology

Cold, moist conditions reduce the germination-inhibiting hormone abscisic acid and increase sensitivity to gibberellins inside the seed; enzymes also weaken the seed coat and mobilise reserves. The requirement prevents seeds dispersed in autumn from germinating during a brief warm spell and then dying in winter frosts. Related dormancy-breaking treatments include scarification (nicking or abrading hard coats) and soaking in water or gibberellic acid.

Plants requiring stratification

Many temperate trees and perennials need cold stratification, including apples, pears and other pome fruits, maples, oaks, lavender, milkweeds (Asclepias), echinacea and numerous wildflowers. Seed packets for such species commonly instruct the grower to refrigerate the seed before sowing.
