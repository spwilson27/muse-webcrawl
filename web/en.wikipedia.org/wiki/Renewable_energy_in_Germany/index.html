Renewable energy in Germany

Renewable energy in Germany is a central pillar of the country's Energiewende (energy transition). Germany has moved from a system dominated by coal, nuclear and gas to one in which renewables — principally wind and solar — supply more than half of gross electricity consumption. In 2023 renewables accounted for roughly 52% of electricity consumption, and the share passed 50% again in 2024; the government targets 80% renewable electricity by 2030 and a largely greenhouse-gas-neutral power system by 2035.

History

Growth began with the Electricity Feed-in Act of 1991 and accelerated under the Renewable Energy Sources Act (EEG) of 2000, which guaranteed fixed feed-in tariffs and priority grid access. Citizen-owned wind and solar cooperatives were a distinctive feature of the German transition. The nuclear phase-out, completed in April 2023, and planned coal exit increased the policy emphasis on wind and solar expansion, grid reinforcement, storage and green hydrogen.

Sources

Wind power, onshore and offshore, is the largest renewable source, followed by solar photovoltaics, biomass and hydropower. Germany has among the world's largest installed solar capacities, with over 80 GW of photovoltaics reached in the mid-2020s. Renewables also supply a growing share of heating (via biomass and heat pumps) and transport remains the sector with the lowest renewable share.

Challenges

Challenges include the north–south transmission bottleneck between windy northern states and industrial southern demand centres, permitting delays, intermittency (periods of low wind and sun known as Dunkelflaute), and debates over electricity prices and the cost of the EEG surcharge, which was abolished in 2022 and replaced by federal budget funding.
