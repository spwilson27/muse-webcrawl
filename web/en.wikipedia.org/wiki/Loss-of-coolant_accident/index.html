Loss-of-coolant accident

Loss-of-coolant accident

Abbreviation: LOCA

Field: Nuclear engineering / reactor safety

Severity: Design-basis accident; can lead to core damage

A loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA) is a mode of failure for a nuclear reactor; if not managed effectively, the results of a LOCA could result in reactor core damage. Each nuclear plant's emergency core cooling system (ECCS) exists specifically to deal with a LOCA.

Causes

A LOCA arises from a break in the reactor coolant pressure boundary — for example a rupture of a main coolant pipe, a stuck-open relief valve, or a failed pump seal. Loss of coolant reduces the ability of the core to remove the heat generated by fission and by radioactive decay, so core temperatures rise.

Consequences

If cooling is not restored, fuel cladding can fail, releasing fission products, and in the extreme case the core can melt — a nuclear meltdown. The Three Mile Island accident of 1979 began as a small-break LOCA caused by a stuck-open pilot-operated relief valve, compounded by operator misdiagnosis.

Mitigation

Reactors are designed with redundant emergency core cooling systems — accumulators, high- and low-pressure injection pumps, and containment spray systems — to reflood the core. The large-break LOCA has historically been the "design basis accident" against which light-water reactor safety systems were sized and tested.
