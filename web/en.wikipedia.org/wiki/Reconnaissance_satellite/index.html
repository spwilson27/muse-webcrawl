Reconnaissance satellite

A reconnaissance satellite or intelligence satellite (commonly, although unofficially, referred to as a spy satellite) is an Earth observation satellite or communications satellite deployed for military or intelligence applications. The first generation of reconnaissance satellites (the American Corona and Soviet Zenit programmes) took photographs, then ejected canisters of photographic film which would descend back down into Earth's atmosphere before being retrieved.

Later generations transmit digital images by radio, encrypted. Most information about actual reconnaissance satellites in service remains classified; details of early programmes have been declassified, notably the U.S. Corona, Argon and Lanyard programmes in 1995, and Gambit and Hexagon in 2011.

History

On 16 March 1955, the United States Air Force ordered development of an advanced reconnaissance satellite under the WS-117L programme. Studies by the RAND Corporation had already suggested the concept as a way to observe the Soviet Union without violating its airspace — a pressing need after the 1960 U-2 incident. The Corona programme, publicly disguised as the "Discoverer" scientific series, achieved the first successful recovery of film from orbit in August 1960 (Discoverer 14); its first mission recovered more photographic coverage of Soviet territory than all U-2 flights combined. The Soviet Union flew the similar Zenit series from 1961.

Film-return satellites such as the KH-8 Gambit and the large KH-9 Hexagon ("Big Bird") gave way from 1976 to the KH-11 Kennen, which used electro-optical detectors and relayed images in near real time. Successors include the KH-12/Improved Crystal series; other nations operate the French Helios and Pléiades, German SAR-Lupe and SARah radar satellites, Israeli Ofek, Indian RISAT, Japanese IGS and Chinese Yaogan constellations.

Types

  • Optical imaging — visible-light and infrared photography; modern systems are credited with ground resolutions around 10 cm or better in public estimates.
  • Radar imaging — synthetic-aperture radar producing images through cloud and at night.
  • Signals intelligence (SIGINT) — interception of radar and communications emissions (electronic intelligence, ELINT).
  • Missile early warning — sometimes classed separately; infrared detection of launches (e.g. DSP and SBIRS).
  • Ocean surveillance — locating naval vessels by their emissions.

International law and secrecy

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty does not prohibit military satellites, and reconnaissance from orbit came to be accepted as a stabilising "national technical means of verification" in arms-control treaties such as SALT and START. Satellites pass over other states without legal objection, in contrast to aircraft overflight, a distinction crystallised after 1960 when Soviet protests targeted the U-2 but not the Corona programme's successors.
