Myiasis

Myiasis (/maɪˈaɪəsɪs/) is the infestation of living tissue of a vertebrate animal by fly larvae (maggots) of the order Diptera, which feed on the host's dead or living tissue, body fluids, or ingested food. The name was coined by the entomologist Frederick William Hope in 1840, from the Greek myia ("fly"). Myiasis affects livestock worldwide — causing major economic losses — and occasionally affects humans, especially in tropical regions and in wounds that are neglected.

Classification

Myiasis is classified by the part of the body affected — cutaneous (furuncular, migratory/creeping, and wound/traumatic), nasopharyngeal, ophthalmomyiasis, aural, intestinal, and urogenital — and by the biological relationship between parasite and host:

  • Obligatory myiasis — the larva requires living tissue to complete development (e.g., screwworms, botflies).
  • Facultative myiasis — normally free-living larvae of carrion flies opportunistically infest wounds or necrotic tissue.
  • Accidental myiasis (pseudomyiasis) — larvae or eggs are ingested and pass through the gut without true infestation.

In humans

Furuncular myiasis from the human botfly (Dermatobia hominis) in Central and South America and the tumbu fly (Cordylobia anthropophaga) in Africa presents as boil-like lesions with a central breathing pore; travellers are commonly affected. Wound myiasis occurs in neglected wounds, and the New World screwworm (Cochliomyia hominivorax) — eradicated from the United States and Mexico through the sterile insect technique — can cause rapidly destructive infestations. Treatment consists of removing the larvae (by extraction, occlusion of the breathing pore with petroleum jelly, or surgery), cleaning the wound, and supportive care.

In animals

"Fly strike" of sheep by blowflies such as Lucilia sericata and L. cuprina is a major welfare and economic problem in Australia, New Zealand, and Britain, managed by shearing practices, crutching, tail docking, insecticides, and selective breeding. Cattle grubs (Hypoderma spp.) and the sheep nasal botfly (Oestrus ovis) are other classic veterinary examples.
