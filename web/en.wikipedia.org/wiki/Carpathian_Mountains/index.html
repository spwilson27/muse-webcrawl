The Carpathian Mountains or Carpathians form a great arc of mountain ranges roughly 1,500 kilometres long across Central and Eastern Europe, the third-longest mountain chain in Europe after the Urals and the Scandinavian Mountains. They stretch from the Czech Republic in the west through Slovakia, Poland, Hungary and Ukraine to Romania in the south-east, with a small extension into Serbia, enclosing the Transylvanian plateau and the Pannonian plain.

Geography and divisions

The range is conventionally divided into the Western Carpathians (including the High Tatra, whose Gerlachovský štít at 2,655 m is the highest summit of the whole chain), the Eastern (or Ukrainian) Carpathians, and the Southern Carpathians in Romania, often called the Transylvanian Alps, whose highest peak is Moldoveanu (2,544 m). The mountains are lower and more heavily forested than the Alps; great rivers including the Vistula, Dniester, Tisza and Prut rise in them, and the Danube cuts through at the Iron Gates.

Nature

The Carpathians hold some of Europe's largest remaining areas of virgin forest and its largest populations of brown bears, wolves and lynx outside Russia. Large areas are protected in national parks in Slovakia, Poland, Ukraine and Romania, and beech forests of the region form a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The mountains were raised during the Alpine orogeny, mainly in the Tertiary period; the eastern part includes Europe's only chain of volcanic mountains of that age, now extinct.
