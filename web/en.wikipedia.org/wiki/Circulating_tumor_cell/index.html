Circulating tumor cell

A circulating tumor cell (CTC) is a cancer cell that has shed from a primary tumor into the bloodstream (or lymphatic system) and is carried through the body. CTCs are the seeds of metastasis, the process by which cancer spreads to distant organs; they are extremely rare in blood — often only a few cells among billions of blood cells — yet their enumeration and analysis provide prognostic information and form the basis of "liquid biopsy" approaches.

Biology

To enter the circulation (intravasation), tumor cells typically undergo changes such as the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), loosening cell adhesion and gaining motility. In the blood, most CTCs die quickly from shear stress, anoikis and immune attack; a surviving few may arrest in capillary beds, exit the vessels (extravasation) and found metastatic colonies, or remain dormant for years. Clusters of CTCs, though rarer than single cells, have a much higher metastatic potential in animal studies.

Detection

Because of their rarity, CTCs must first be enriched from a blood sample. Methods exploit physical properties (size, deformability, density, electrical charge) or biological markers — most commonly the epithelial adhesion molecule EpCAM, captured with antibody-coated magnetic beads. The CellSearch system, which counts EpCAM-positive, cytokeratin-positive, CD45-negative cells, was the first FDA-cleared CTC assay; newer microfluidic chips and marker-independent platforms aim to catch cells that have lost epithelial markers through EMT.

Clinical significance

Elevated CTC counts predict shorter progression-free and overall survival in metastatic breast, prostate and colorectal cancers, and changes in count during therapy can precede imaging changes. Beyond counting, molecular analysis of CTCs — mutations, gene expression, and even growth of CTC cultures or xenografts — is an active research area aimed at guiding personalized therapy without invasive tissue biopsies.
