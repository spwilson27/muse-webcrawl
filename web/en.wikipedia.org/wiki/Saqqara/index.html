Saqqara

Saqqara (Arabic: سقارة) is a vast ancient burial ground in Egypt, serving as the necropolis of the ancient capital Memphis. Located about 30 km south of modern Cairo, the site covers roughly 7 by 1.5 km of desert plateau and contains pyramids, mastaba tombs, temples and cemeteries spanning nearly 3,000 years, from the Early Dynastic Period through the Christian era. Its best-known monument is the Step Pyramid of Djoser, the oldest large-scale stone building in Egypt. Saqqara is a component of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Memphis and its Necropolis".

History

Burials at Saqqara began with the kings and nobles of the first dynasties, whose large mudbrick mastabas line the northern edge of the plateau. In the Third Dynasty, the architect Imhotep built for King Djoser a revolutionary complex in stone, centred on a six-stepped pyramid. Later Old Kingdom rulers — including Userkaf, Unas (whose pyramid contains the earliest Pyramid Texts), Teti and Pepi I and II — built smaller pyramids around it, while officials filled the plain with decorated mastabas such as those of Ti and Mereruka.

The necropolis remained in use through the Middle and New Kingdoms (including the tombs of officials near the later Serapeum, where the sacred Apis bulls were buried in enormous granite sarcophagi), the Late Period — when vast shaft tombs and animal catacombs were cut — and into Ptolemaic and Roman times. The Coptic Monastery of Apa Jeremias stood on the site into the early medieval period.

Excavations and discoveries

Saqqara has been excavated since the 19th century, when Auguste Mariette uncovered the Serapeum in 1850. Major work by Jean-Philippe Lauer reconstructed Djoser's complex over seven decades. Recent years have brought spectacular finds, including caches of intact painted wooden coffins and bronze statues announced in 2020–2022, one of the largest discoveries in modern Egyptology.
