Potamides

The potamides were nymphs of rivers and streams.

The potamides (Ancient Greek: Ποταμίδες) are a type of water nymph in Greek mythology. They are the nymphs of rivers and streams, presiding over fresh running water, and are counted among the wider family of naiads. Their name derives from the Greek potamos (ποταμός), meaning "river".

Nature and worship

Like other nymphs, the potamides were considered minor female nature deities, youthful and beautiful, attached to particular localities. Each river's nymphs were associated with that stream's waters, which were thought to inspire prophecy, healing or poetic frenzy in some traditions. The Greeks further classified river nymphs by their particular waters: pegaeae of springs, eleionomae of marshes and wetlands, and others of fountains and wells. Young men and women offered locks of hair to the local river deities on reaching adulthood, and swearing by the waters of one's native stream was a solemn oath.

Relations

The great river gods (potamoi), sons of Oceanus and Tethys, were male deities distinct from the potamides, though the nymphs are sometimes described as their daughters or attendants. Individual potamides appear in myth chiefly in connection with the rivers they inhabit, as companions of greater gods, or as the mothers of heroes by gods and mortals.
