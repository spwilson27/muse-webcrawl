Glycogen is a multibranched polysaccharide of glucose that serves as a form of energy storage in animals, fungi, and bacteria. It is the main storage form of glucose in the human body, functionally analogous to starch in plants, but more extensively branched and compact, allowing rapid mobilization.

Structure

Glycogen consists of chains of glucose units joined by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds, with branches formed by α(1→6) bonds roughly every 8–12 residues. A single granule may contain up to about 30,000 glucose units arranged in tiers around one central molecule of the primer protein glycogenin.

Metabolism

Glycogenesis — synthesis — is carried out by glycogen synthase, which adds UDP-glucose to growing chains; a branching enzyme creates the α(1→6) branch points. Glycogenolysis — breakdown — uses glycogen phosphorylase to release glucose-1-phosphate, with a debranching enzyme handling branch points. In the liver, glucose-6-phosphatase converts the product to free glucose for export to the blood; muscle lacks this enzyme and uses its glycogen only for itself.

Regulation

Insulin promotes glycogen synthesis after meals, whereas glucagon (in liver) and adrenaline stimulate breakdown during fasting and exercise. The enzymes of synthesis and breakdown are reciprocally regulated by phosphorylation cascades and allosteric effectors such as glucose-6-phosphate and AMP.

Clinical significance

Inherited defects in glycogen metabolism cause the glycogen storage diseases, including von Gierke disease (glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency), Pompe disease (lysosomal acid maltase deficiency), and McArdle disease (muscle phosphorylase deficiency). Liver glycogen stores (~100 g) can maintain blood glucose for roughly a day of fasting; muscle stores (~400 g) fuel exercise.
