T–V distinction

The T–V distinction is the use of different second-person pronouns depending on the social relationship between speaker and addressee, most famously the contrast between a familiar form (from Latin tu) and a polite or formal form (from Latin vos). It exists in many languages, including French (tu/vous), Spanish (tú/usted), German (du/Sie), Italian (tu/Lei), and Russian (ty/vy).

Usage

Generally, the T form is used with friends, family, children, and in informal settings, while the V form conveys respect, distance, or formality toward elders, strangers, and superiors. Sociolinguists Roger Brown and Albert Gilman described the choice in terms of "power" and "solidarity": asymmetrical use signals hierarchy, while reciprocal T use signals intimacy and reciprocal V use signals respectful distance. English largely lost the distinction when thou (the T form) fell out of general use in favour of you.

Variation

Norms vary widely: some societies have expanded informal address (such as Sweden's du reform), while some languages have more than two levels of politeness, including elaborate honorific systems.
