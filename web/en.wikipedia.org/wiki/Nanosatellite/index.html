A nanosatellite (or nanosat) is an artificial satellite with a wet mass between 1 and 10 kilograms. Nanosatellites form the lower end of the small-satellite classes, above picosatellites (0.1–1 kg) and below microsatellites (10–100 kg). Because they are built from miniaturised commercial components and launched as secondary payloads, nanosatellites have dramatically lowered the cost of access to space for universities, start-ups, and developing space programmes.

CubeSat standard

Most nanosatellites follow the CubeSat specification, created in 1999 by Jordi Puig-Suari of California Polytechnic State University and Bob Twiggs of Stanford University. A CubeSat is built in units (U) of a 10 cm cube with a mass of up to about 2 kg per unit; common configurations are 1U, 2U, 3U, and 6U. Standardised "P-POD" and later deployers allow the satellites to ride as secondary payloads on many launch vehicles, and since 2013 large numbers have been deployed from the International Space Station via the NanoRacks system. The first CubeSats were launched in June 2003 on a Russian Rokot vehicle.

Applications

Early nanosatellites were mainly educational and technology-demonstration projects — Canada's CanX programme and Norway's NCUBE series among them. Commercial uses expanded rapidly in the 2010s: Planet Labs built a constellation of 3U CubeSats ("Doves") imaging the whole Earth daily, and Spire Global operates CubeSats collecting ship-tracking (AIS) and weather data. Scientific missions include BRITE (asteroseismology), LightSail (solar sailing), and MarCO, two 6U CubeSats that flew to Mars with NASA's InSight lander in 2018 — the first interplanetary CubeSats.

Characteristics and challenges

Nanosatellites are constrained by small power budgets (a few watts to tens of watts), limited communications bandwidth, and minimal or no propulsion, though miniaturised electric and cold-gas thrusters are increasingly flown. Their low cost permits rapid iteration and constellation deployment, but high failure rates among amateur-built spacecraft, short orbital lifetimes, and their contribution to space debris are recurring concerns; regulators increasingly require deorbit plans and trackability.
