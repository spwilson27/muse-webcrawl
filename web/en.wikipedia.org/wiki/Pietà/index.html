Pietà

The Pietà: the Virgin Mary cradling the dead body of Jesus after the Crucifixion.

The Pietà (Italian for "pity" or "compassion") is a subject in Christian art depicting the Virgin Mary cradling the mortal body of Jesus Christ after his Crucifixion and Deposition. It is a specific form of the Lamentation of Christ, in which Jesus is mourned by Mary alone, or by Mary and other figures.

Origins and development

The theme arose in German sculpture around 1300, in the tradition of the Vesperbild — an image for evening prayer on Good Friday — where Mary is often shown gaunt with grief, holding a shrunken, emaciated Christ. From Germany the subject spread to France, the Low Countries and Italy during the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, becoming one of the most popular devotional images of the late Middle Ages and Renaissance.

Michelangelo

The most famous Pietà is Michelangelo's marble sculpture (1498–1499) in St Peter's Basilica in the Vatican, carved when the artist was only 24. In a striking departure from the German tradition, his Mary is youthful and serene, and the composition arranges the two figures in a harmonious pyramid. Michelangelo returned to the subject in old age in the unfinished Florentine Pietà and the Rondanini Pietà.

Other examples

Other celebrated Pietàs include works by Giovanni Bellini, Titian (his last painting), El Greco, and Annibale Carracci in painting, and in sculpture the Röttgen Pietà (c. 1300) and the many expressive wooden Vesperbilder of the German late Gothic. The subject remains a staple of Catholic iconography and cemetery art.
