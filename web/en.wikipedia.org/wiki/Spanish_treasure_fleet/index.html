The Spanish treasure fleet (Spanish: Flota de Indias, "Fleet of the Indies"), was the convoy system adopted by the Spanish Empire from 1566 to 1790 to transport valuable goods — above all silver and gold — from Spanish America to Spain, while carrying European manufactures and passengers to the colonies. The system linked Spain with its American territories and, via the Manila galleon, with the Philippines and Asia.

Organization

Trade with the Indies was a royal monopoly administered by the Casa de Contratación (House of Trade) in Seville (later Cádiz). Because of the danger from pirates, privateers, and rival powers, merchant ships were required to sail in escorted convoys. Ordinarily two fleets sailed each year:

  • The Flota de Indias (New Spain fleet), bound for Veracruz in Mexico, collecting silver from the mines of Zacatecas and Guanajuato, as well as cochineal and other goods.
  • The Galeones de Tierra Firme, bound for Cartagena and Nombre de Dios (later Portobelo) in Panama, collecting South American silver from Potosí, which was carried across the isthmus by mule train, together with gold, emeralds, and pearls.

The fleets rendezvoused at Havana to make the return crossing together through the Bahama Channel, riding the Gulf Stream past Florida. Asian luxuries — silk, porcelain, spices — reached Mexico aboard the Manila galleon from the Philippines and were transferred to the Veracruz fleet.

History

The convoy system was formalized under Pedro Menéndez de Avilés in the 1560s. At its height in the late 16th century the fleets carried enormous quantities of silver, which financed Habsburg Spain's wars in Europe and, through trade, flowed onward to China. Remarkably few fleets were lost to enemy action: the Dutch admiral Piet Heyn captured the entire New Spain fleet at the Battle in the Bay of Matanzas in 1628, and an English fleet destroyed or captured much of the fleet at Cádiz in 1656 and at Vigo Bay in 1702 (though most treasure had been unloaded). Far more ships were lost to hurricanes, notably the fleets of 1622 (including the Nuestra Señora de Atocha), 1715, and 1733 off Florida.

Decline

Sailings became irregular in the 18th century as the system decayed; the last regular fleet sailed in 1776, and the system was formally ended around 1790 as Spain liberalized colonial trade. The great silver wrecks of the fleet, such as the Atocha (found by Mel Fisher in 1985) and the 1715 fleet, later became famous in salvage and treasure-hunting lore.
