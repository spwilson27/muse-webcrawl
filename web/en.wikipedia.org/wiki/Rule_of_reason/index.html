Rule of reason

The rule of reason is a legal doctrine used to interpret the Sherman Antitrust Act, the cornerstone of United States antitrust law. Under the doctrine, a restraint of trade is unlawful only if it unreasonably restricts competition; courts weigh the restraint's anticompetitive effects against its procompetitive justifications rather than condemning it categorically.

Origin

The rule was articulated by the Supreme Court in Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States (1911), in which Chief Justice Edward Douglass White held that Section 1 of the Sherman Act prohibits only unreasonable restraints of trade. Justice Brandeis expanded on the analysis in Chicago Board of Trade v. United States (1918), listing factors such as the nature of the restraint, the business conditions before and after it was imposed, and the purpose sought to be attained.

Analysis

Modern rule-of-reason analysis is usually structured as a burden-shifting framework:

  1. The plaintiff must show that the restraint produces significant anticompetitive effects in a relevant market.
  2. The defendant must then offer a procompetitive justification.
  3. The plaintiff may show that the justification could be achieved by substantially less restrictive means.

The rule contrasts with per se illegality, under which certain practices — price fixing, market allocation and some group boycotts — are condemned without inquiry into their effects. Most restraints, including vertical agreements after Leegin Creative Leather Products v. PSKS (2007), are judged under the rule of reason.
