Moon god

A moon god or lunar deity is a god or goddess of the Moon, or a divine figure associated with the Moon and its phases. Lunar deities occur in many cultures and religions around the world, in both male and female forms. Because the Moon governs tides, the menstrual cycle, planting calendars, and the measurement of time, lunar gods are frequently associated with water, fertility, time, madness, change, and the cycle of death and rebirth.

Mesopotamia

In ancient Mesopotamian religion, the moon god was known as Nanna in Sumerian and Sin (or Suen) in Akkadian. Nanna was the tutelary deity of the city of Ur, where his great ziggurat temple, the É-kiš-nu-gál, stood. Nanna was believed to travel across the night sky in a boat, measuring time and determining the fates of men. His symbol was the crescent moon, sometimes shown as a bull's horns. Sin was the father of the sun god Shamash (Utu) and of Ishtar (Inanna), goddess of Venus.

Egypt

In ancient Egyptian religion, the Moon was represented by several deities. Khonsu, whose name means "traveller", was a youthful god depicted with a crescent and full moon disk upon his head; he was part of the Theban triad with Amun and Mut. Earlier, Iah (also rendered Aah) was a lunar god whose name simply means "moon", and Thoth, god of writing and wisdom, also had lunar aspects as measurer of time.

Greco-Roman religion

In Greek mythology the Moon was personified by Selene, a Titaness who drove her moon chariot across the sky each night; her Roman counterpart was Luna. Selene was later gradually eclipsed by Artemis, twin sister of Apollo, who acquired lunar associations alongside her role as goddess of the hunt; the Roman equivalent was Diana. The goddess Hecate was also associated with the Moon, particularly its darker aspects, crossroads, and magic.

Norse and Germanic religion

In Norse mythology, the Moon is personified as the male god Máni, who drives the Moon's chariot across the sky and is chased by the wolf Hati, who will catch and devour him at Ragnarök. His sister Sól personifies the Sun — a reversal of the more common Mediterranean pattern in which the sun is male and the moon female.

East and South Asia

In Hindu tradition, Chandra is the male moon god, one of the Navagraha (nine planetary deities), who rides a chariot drawn by white horses or an antelope. In Chinese mythology, Chang'e is the goddess who lives on the Moon after consuming an elixir of immortality; she is celebrated at the Mid-Autumn Festival. The Japanese god of the Moon is Tsukuyomi, a male deity born from the right eye of Izanagi. In Shinto, the moon god is a brother of the sun goddess Amaterasu and the storm god Susanoo.

Americas

In Maya mythology, the Moon goddess (often identified with Ix Chel) was associated with childbirth, medicine, weaving, and water. In Aztec religion the dismembered goddess Coyolxauhqui represented the Moon, while the male god Tecciztecatl became the Moon after hesitating to sacrifice himself in the fire that created the fifth sun. In Inca religion, Mama Quilla ("Mother Moon") was the sister and wife of the sun god Inti and a protectress of women.

Gender and symbolism

The gender of lunar deities varies by culture. Linguists note that in many Indo-European languages the word for moon is masculine, and several early traditions (Mesopotamian, Norse, Japanese, Hindu) have male moon gods; the feminine lunar deity, linked with the menstrual and tidal cycles, is prominent in Greco-Roman, Chinese, and many other traditions. The Moon's waxing and waning made it a near-universal symbol of cyclic renewal, inconstancy, and hidden knowledge.
