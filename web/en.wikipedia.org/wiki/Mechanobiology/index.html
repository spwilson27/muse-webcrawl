Mechanobiology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mechanobiology is an emerging field of science at the interface of biology, engineering, chemistry and physics. It focuses on how physical forces and changes in the mechanical properties of cells and tissues contribute to development, cell differentiation, physiology, and disease.

Overview

Cells continuously sense and respond to mechanical cues from their environment — substrate stiffness, stretch, compression, fluid shear stress — through a process called mechanotransduction, in which mechanical stimuli are converted into biochemical signals. Key molecular machinery includes integrin-based focal adhesions, the actomyosin cytoskeleton, mechanosensitive ion channels such as Piezo1 and Piezo2, and the transcriptional regulators YAP and TAZ, which shuttle between cytoplasm and nucleus depending on the mechanical state of the cell.

Key findings

Landmark studies showed that mesenchymal stem cells differentiate toward neurons, muscle, or bone depending simply on the elasticity of the substrate they are grown on, mimicking the stiffness of brain, muscle, or bone tissue. Mechanobiology also explains how bone remodels in response to load (Wolff's law), how blood vessels adapt to shear stress, and how increased tissue stiffness in tumors can promote cancer progression.

Applications

The field informs tissue engineering, where scaffold mechanics guide regeneration; regenerative medicine; the design of biomaterials; and understanding of diseases including fibrosis, atherosclerosis, muscular dystrophy, and osteoporosis. Techniques central to the field include atomic force microscopy, traction force microscopy, micropatterning, and engineered hydrogels of tunable stiffness.
