William Duane (physicist)

FieldsPhysics, biophysics

William Duane (February 17, 1872 – March 7, 1935) was an American physicist who made pioneering contributions to the study of X-rays and radioactivity. He is best known for the Duane–Hunt law, which relates the minimum wavelength of X-rays produced in an X-ray tube to the accelerating voltage applied to the tube.

Education and career

Duane studied at the University of Pennsylvania and Harvard University, and spent time in Europe working in the laboratories of Walther Nernst in Göttingen and Marie Curie in Paris. He became a professor at the University of Colorado and later returned to Harvard, where he was professor of biophysics and worked with the Harvard Cancer Commission on medical applications of radiation.

Duane–Hunt law

In 1915, Duane and Franklin L. Hunt published the empirical relation λmin = hc/eV, connecting the short-wavelength limit of the bremsstrahlung X-ray spectrum to the applied voltage V. The law provided one of the most precise early determinations of Planck's constant and strong confirmation of the quantum theory of radiation.

Other work

Duane also researched the ionization of gases, radon (which he called "radium emanation") for cancer treatment, and proposed a quantum theory of diffraction by crystals and gratings for X-rays — the Duane hypothesis of 1923. He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and received honors including the John Scott Medal.
