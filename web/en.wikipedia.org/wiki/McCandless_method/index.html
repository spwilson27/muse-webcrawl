McCandless method

The McCandless method is a technique in stage lighting design, described by Stanley McCandless in his 1932 book A Method of Lighting the Stage, which remains a foundational approach to lighting actors on stage. McCandless, who taught at the Yale School of Drama, is regarded as one of the founders of modern lighting design education in the United States.

The method

The stage is divided into a grid of "acting areas", typically six to twelve zones. Each area is lit by a pair of spotlights positioned at roughly 45 degrees above and to either side of the area, one from the front-left and one from the front-right. One instrument of the pair carries a warm colour filter (such as a pale amber or pink) and the other a cool filter (such as a pale blue or lavender). The two beams overlap on the actor, modelling the face and body with complementary tones that imitate the warmth of sunlight and the coolness of ambient skylight, and giving a sense of three-dimensionality that a single flat front light cannot.

These paired area lights form the base "wash" of the design, which the designer then supplements with background lighting, side light, specials and effects. Colour, intensity and angle for each area can be controlled independently, allowing the designer to blend or isolate areas as the action of the play requires.

Legacy

The method's systematic division of the stage into controllable areas, and its warm/cool pairing, became standard practice in educational and professional theatre and influenced subsequent approaches such as Tharon Musser's work on Broadway. Though modern designers often depart from it freely, the McCandless method is still taught as the starting grammar of stage lighting.
