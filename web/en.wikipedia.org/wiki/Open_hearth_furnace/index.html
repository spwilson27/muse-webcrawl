Open hearth furnace

Tapping an open hearth furnace.

An open hearth furnace is a furnace in which a charge of pig iron, scrap steel and iron ore is melted and refined into steel on a shallow hearth by the flame and hot gases passing over it. The Siemens–Martin process, developed in the 1860s, made the open hearth the dominant steelmaking method of the first half of the twentieth century before being displaced by the basic oxygen furnace.

Principle

The furnace's key innovation is the regenerative preheating system devised by Carl Wilhelm Siemens: waste exhaust gases pass through brick checkerwork chambers, which then preheat incoming air and fuel gas in alternating cycles, achieving the very high flame temperatures needed to melt steel — hotter than a blast-furnace-derived charge and beyond the reach of earlier reverberatory furnaces. Pierre-Émile Martin first applied Siemens' furnace to steelmaking at Sireuil, France, in 1864.

Operation

A heat takes many hours: the charge melts down, ore and limestone additions oxidise carbon, silicon, manganese and phosphorus into the slag, and the melter can sample and adjust composition precisely before tapping — an advantage over the fast, all-or-nothing Bessemer converter. Open hearths could also consume large proportions of scrap. Furnaces grew to several hundred tonnes capacity and typically burned gas or oil.

Decline

Although the process produced most of the world's steel by the 1950s, its slowness (heats of 8–12 hours against under an hour for basic oxygen steelmaking) made it uncompetitive once the Linz–Donawitz (basic oxygen) process spread after 1952. The last open hearths in Western Europe and the United States closed in the 1980s–1990s; a few lingered longer in the former Soviet Union and China.
