The Louvre Pyramid (French: Pyramide du Louvre) is a large glass-and-metal pyramid designed by Chinese-American architect I. M. Pei. The pyramid is located in the main courtyard (Cour Napoléon) of the Louvre Palace in Paris, surrounded by three smaller pyramids, and serves as the main entrance to the Louvre Museum. Completed in 1989, it has become a celebrated landmark of the city.

Design and construction

The structure consists of glass segments — 603 rhombus-shaped and 70 triangular panes of specially manufactured extra-clear glass — supported by a steel framework. It rises 21.6 metres with a square base of 34 metres per side, proportions close to those of the Great Pyramid of Giza. Below it lies an underground lobby (the Hall Napoléon) linking the museum's three wings and relieving congestion at the historic entrances.

History

The pyramid was the centrepiece of President François Mitterrand's "Grand Louvre" project, commissioned in 1981 and awarded to Pei in 1983 without a competition. The design was fiercely controversial at the time: critics denounced a futuristic glass pyramid as an intrusion into the classical palace courtyard. A life-size cable mock-up erected in 1985 helped win over public and official opinion, and the pyramid was inaugurated on 29 March 1989. A smaller inverted pyramid (La Pyramide Inversée), suspended over the underground shopping gallery, followed in 1993.

In popular culture

The pyramid features prominently in art, photography, and fiction — most famously in Dan Brown's novel The Da Vinci Code (2003), which wove a popular (and apocryphal) legend around its pane count and the inverted pyramid.
