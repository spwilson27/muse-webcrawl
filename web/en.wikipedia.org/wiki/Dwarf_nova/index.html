Dwarf nova

A dwarf nova (pl. dwarf novae), or U Geminorum type variable, is one of several types of cataclysmic variable star, consisting of a close binary star system in which one of the components is a white dwarf that accretes matter from its companion. Dwarf novae are dimmer and repeat much more often than classical novae: outbursts recur on timescales of days to decades, brightening the system by roughly 2 to 5 magnitudes as the accretion disc around the white dwarf temporarily becomes much hotter and brighter.

Mechanism

In a dwarf nova system, a low-mass companion star fills its Roche lobe and transfers hydrogen-rich gas through the inner Lagrange point. The gas forms an accretion disc around the white dwarf. The disc is subject to a thermal instability: hydrogen in the disc partially ionises, sharply increasing the disc's viscosity and causing material to fall inward at a much higher rate. The resulting release of gravitational energy brightens the disc dramatically for days to weeks, until the disc cools and returns to its quiescent state.

Subtypes

  • U Geminorum (UG) stars: the prototype class, with outbursts every few weeks to months.
  • Z Camelopardalis (UGZ) stars: show "standstills" — extended periods at intermediate brightness between outbursts.
  • SU Ursae Majoris (UGSU) stars: short orbital periods and occasional much brighter, longer "superoutbursts" accompanied by superhumps.
  • WZ Sagittae stars: extreme systems with very long intervals (decades) between superoutbursts.

Observation

Dwarf novae are popular targets for amateur astronomers, whose monitoring of outbursts contributes to professional research databases such as that of the American Association of Variable Star Observers. Well-known examples include SS Cygni, U Geminorum and IP Pegasi.
