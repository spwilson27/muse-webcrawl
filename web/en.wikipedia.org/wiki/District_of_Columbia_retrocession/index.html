District of Columbia retrocession

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

District of Columbia retrocession is the act of returning some or all of the land that had been ceded to the federal government of the United States for the purpose of creating its federal district for the new national capital of the United States, the District of Columbia. The land was originally ceded to the federal government by Virginia and Maryland in 1790, forming a square of 100 square miles (259 km²) straddling the Potomac River.

The 1847 retrocession to Virginia

In 1846, Congress passed legislation retroceding the portion of the District south-west of the Potomac — Alexandria County, including the city of Alexandria — to Virginia, following a referendum of its residents. President James K. Polk signed the act, and Virginia formally took possession in 1847. Motives included economic decline and neglect in Alexandria, the desire of its residents for representation in a state legislature and Congress, and — historians note — the wish of Alexandria's slave traders and their allies to protect the city's slave market from possible abolition in the federal district. The retrocession reduced the District to its present area of about 68 square miles, all on the Maryland side of the Potomac.

Modern proposals

Retrocession has periodically been proposed as an alternative to D.C. statehood: the residential parts of the modern District would be returned to Maryland, while a small federal enclave containing the Capitol, White House and other federal buildings would remain the District of Columbia. Supporters argue this would give District residents full voting representation as Maryland citizens without creating a new state; opponents, including most D.C. statehood advocates and Maryland officials at various times, argue that residents want statehood rather than absorption into Maryland, and that Maryland's consent would be constitutionally required. Bills to that effect have been introduced in Congress but not enacted.
