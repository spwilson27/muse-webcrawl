Thai Forest Tradition

The Thai Forest Tradition (Pali: Kammaṭṭhāna, lit. "basis of work") is a branch of Theravada Buddhism in Thailand, Cambodia and Sri Lanka that emphasizes meditative practice and strict adherence to the monastic discipline (vinaya) as the central elements of the Buddhist path. Practitioners, known as forest monks, typically live in remote forests, mountains and caves, subsisting on alms food and devoting themselves to meditation and the study of early Buddhist texts.

History

The tradition traces its modern revival to the venerable Ajahn Mun Bhuridatta (1870–1949), a monk from Ubon Ratchathani in northeastern Thailand. Dissatisfied with the bookish, ritual-oriented Buddhism he encountered, Ajahn Mun took up a wandering ascetic life in the forests, seeking teachers who could guide him in meditation. His practice centered on the dhutanga (ascetic) practices described in the Pali Canon, including eating one meal a day, dwelling at the root of a tree, and using robes made from discarded cloth.

Ajahn Mun's most famous disciple, Ajahn Chah (1918–1992), founded Wat Nong Pah Pong in 1954 and later established Wat Pah Nanachat, a monastery for Western disciples. Through students such as Ajahn Sumedho, Ajahn Amaro and Ajahn Pasanno, the Thai Forest Tradition spread to England, where Amaravati Buddhist Monastery was founded, and to North America, where Abhayagiri Monastery in California continues the lineage.

Practice

Forest monks observe the 227 training rules of the bhikkhu Patimokkha with unusual strictness. Daily life revolves around communal work periods, evening chanting, and long hours of sitting and walking meditation. Meditation instruction characteristically begins with mindfulness of breathing (anapanasati), often using the mantra Buddho coordinated with the breath, and develops into insight meditation (vipassana) directed at the contemplation of impermanence, suffering and non-self.

The tradition places particular emphasis on investigating the body, including contemplation of its unattractive aspects (asubha) and of corpses in various stages of decomposition, as an antidote to sensual desire. Teachers stress that liberation is attainable in this very life through diligent practice.

Notable teachers

  • Ajahn Mun Bhuridatta (1870–1949) — founder of the modern tradition
  • Ajahn Sao Kantasilo (1861–1941) — Ajahn Mun's teacher
  • Ajahn Chah (1918–1992) — founder of Wat Nong Pah Pong
  • Ajahn Lee Dhammadharo (1907–1961)
  • Ajahn Maha Bowa (1913–2011) — biographer of Ajahn Mun
  • Ajahn Sumedho (b. 1934) — first Western abbot in the lineage
