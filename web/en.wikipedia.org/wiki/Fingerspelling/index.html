Fingerspelling

Fingerspelling (or dactylology) is the representation of the letters of a writing system, and sometimes numeral systems, using only the hands. Fingerspelling alphabets — manual alphabets — are a standard component of sign languages used by deaf communities, where they are used to spell out proper names, technical terms, and words for which no conventional sign exists.

One-handed and two-handed alphabets

Manual alphabets divide into one-handed systems, such as those used in American Sign Language (ASL) and French Sign Language, and two-handed systems, such as that used in British Sign Language (BSL) and its relatives (Auslan and New Zealand Sign Language). The ASL alphabet, derived from the French system, uses a single hand with distinct handshapes for each letter; the British two-handed alphabet represents most letters by touching or pointing at the fingers of the other hand. Alphabets for scripts other than Latin exist, including systems for Japanese kana, Korean Hangul, Cyrillic, Arabic, Hebrew, and Devanagari characters.

Use in signing

Fingerspelling is used sparingly in ASL discourse — chiefly for names, places, titles, and emphasis — and fluent receivers read whole-word patterns rather than individual letters. In BSL-family languages, fingerspelling also serves names and borrowings. Some words spelled frequently become lexicalised, with modified movement and handshapes, becoming signs in their own right.

History

Manual alphabets were described as early as the 17th century, in works such as John Wallis's teaching of deaf pupils in England and the Spanish tradition associated with Juan Pablo Bonet, whose 1620 book included an alphabet for teaching deaf people to speak. Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet brought the French system to the United States in the early 19th century, where it evolved into the modern ASL alphabet.
