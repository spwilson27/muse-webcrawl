Cyclin

Placeholder image: Cyclin

Cyclins are a family of proteins that control the progression of a cell through the cell cycle by activating cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). Cyclins were originally named because their concentration varies in a cyclical fashion during the cell cycle; they were discovered by Tim Hunt and colleagues in the early 1980s from studies of sea urchin eggs. The discovery of cyclins, together with work on CDKs and cell cycle checkpoints, was recognized by the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, awarded to Leland Hartwell, Paul Nurse, and Tim Hunt.

Function

Each cyclin associates with specific CDKs, and the resulting complex phosphorylates target proteins that drive particular cell cycle events: G1 cyclins (cyclin D) promote entry into the cycle in response to growth signals; cyclin E–CDK2 drives the G1/S transition; cyclin A acts in S phase and G2; and B-type cyclins (cyclin B–CDK1, the maturation-promoting factor) trigger mitosis. Cyclin levels are controlled by regulated synthesis and by ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis — mediated during mitosis by the anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C) — so that complexes form and are destroyed in an orderly sequence.

Structure

Cyclins share a conserved region of about 100 amino acids called the cyclin box, which binds the kinase. Beyond this region they diverge, reflecting their phase-specific roles and localization signals, including destruction boxes targeted by ubiquitin ligases.

Major groups

  • G1/S cyclins (A, E): commit the cell to DNA replication.
  • S-phase cyclins: maintain replication progression.
  • Mitotic cyclins (A, B): control entry into and progression through mitosis.
  • G1 cyclins (D): integrate extracellular growth signals.

Clinical relevance

Deregulation of cyclins, especially cyclin D1 overexpression and amplification, is common in cancers. CDK4/6 inhibitors (such as palbociclib) that block cyclin D–dependent kinases are approved treatments for certain breast cancers.
