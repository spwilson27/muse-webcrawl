Fundamental attribution error

In social psychology, the fundamental attribution error (also known as correspondence bias or the overattribution effect) is the tendency for people, when explaining the behaviour of others, to overemphasise dispositional and personality-based explanations while underemphasising situational and environmental ones. Conversely, people tend to explain their own behaviour more in terms of the situation — an asymmetry known as the actor–observer asymmetry.

Classic studies

In the quizmaster study of Ross, Amabile and Steinmetz (1977), participants who watched a quiz game rated the questioners as much more knowledgeable than the contestants, even though the questioners' advantage came entirely from their role — they had written the questions themselves. In the earlier Jones and Harris (1967) experiment, readers judged that students who had written essays defending Fidel Castro genuinely held pro-Castro attitudes, even when told the essay position had been assigned by a coin toss.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. Observers focus attention on the actor rather than the background situation, making dispositional causes more salient; adjusting an initial dispositional inference to account for situational constraints requires cognitive effort, which may be skipped under time pressure or cognitive load. Cultural differences also matter: the bias is typically stronger in individualistic cultures than in collectivist ones, where situational explanations are more habitual.

Criticism and scope

Some researchers have questioned whether the error is truly "fundamental", noting that attributions are sometimes accurate, that the effect size varies with the valence of behaviour, and that observers do use situational information when it is made salient. The concept nevertheless remains one of the most influential ideas in attribution theory and a foundation for research on stereotyping, blame, and the just-world hypothesis.
