Quercus calliprinos

Kingdom: Plantae
Order: Fagales
Family: Fagaceae
Genus: Quercus
Species: Q. calliprinos
Common names: Palestine oak, common oak (Åalon matzui)

Quercus calliprinos, the Palestine oak, is an oak tree or large shrub in the beech family (Fagaceae), native to the eastern Mediterranean basin, from Greece and Turkey through the Levant. Some authorities treat it as a subspecies of the kermes oak, Quercus coccifera, under the name Q. coccifera subsp. calliprinos.

Description

The Palestine oak is an evergreen tree growing 5–18 metres tall, though often shrubby where grazed or cut. The leaves are small, 3–5 cm long, leathery, glossy dark green, and edged with spiny teeth. The acorns are 3–4 cm long, maturing in the second year in large cups covered in long, reflexed scales.

Distribution and ecology

It is one of the most common and widespread trees of the Levant, forming the dominant species in much of the region's maquis shrubland and open woodland alongside terebinth (Pistacia palaestina) and carob. It tolerates drought, poor limestone soils, and heavy grazing, resprouting vigorously after fire or cutting.

Uses and cultural significance

The tree's hard wood was traditionally used for charcoal and tool handles, and its acorns for animal fodder. Ancient, large specimens are often revered as sacred trees at shrines and tombs in Israel and Palestine; the famous oak associated with traditions of Abraham near Hebron (the "Oak of Mamre" site) is of this species complex. In Hebrew it is called alon matzui (“common oak”).
