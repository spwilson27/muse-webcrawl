Extrapyramidal symptoms

Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) are symptoms that are classically associated with the extrapyramidal system of the brain's cerebral cortex. When such symptoms are caused by medications or other drugs, they are also known as extrapyramidal side effects (EPSE). The symptoms can be acute (short-term) or tardive (long-term). They include movement dysfunction such as dystonia (continuous spasms and muscle contractions), akathisia (motor restlessness), parkinsonism (characteristic symptoms such as rigidity), bradykinesia (slowness of movement), tremor, and tardive dyskinesia (irregular, jerky movements).

Extrapyramidal symptoms are a reason why subjects drop out of clinical trials of antipsychotics; of the 213 (14.6%) subjects dropped out of phase II trials of olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, or ziprasidone, 58 (27.2%) had extrapyramidal symptoms.

Causes

Extrapyramidal symptoms are most commonly caused by typical antipsychotic drugs that antagonize dopamine D2 receptors. The most common typical antipsychotics associated with EPS are haloperidol and fluphenazine. Atypical antipsychotics have lower D2 receptor affinity or higher serotonin receptor affinity, leading to lower rates of EPS. Other causes include certain antidepressants, metoclopramide, and drugs that deplete dopamine stores such as reserpine and tetrabenazine.

Types

Acute dystonic reactions involve sustained, painful muscular spasms of the head, neck, limbs or trunk, developing within hours to days of starting or increasing the dose of a neuroleptic medication. Akathisia is a subjective sense of restlessness, often accompanied by observed restless movements. Parkinsonism mimics Parkinson's disease with rigidity, tremor and bradykinesia. Tardive dyskinesia develops after months or years of treatment and involves involuntary, repetitive movements, particularly of the mouth, tongue and face.

Treatment

Anticholinergic drugs such as benztropine and diphenhydramine are commonly used to treat acute EPS. Benzodiazepines may also be helpful. Lowering the dose of the antipsychotic, or switching to an atypical agent with lower EPS liability, is often effective. Tardive dyskinesia may be treated with the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 inhibitors valbenazine and deutetrabenazine, approved in 2017.
