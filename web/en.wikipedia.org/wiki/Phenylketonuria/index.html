Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an inborn error of metabolism that results in decreased metabolism of the amino acid phenylalanine. Untreated, PKU can lead to intellectual disability, seizures, behavioral problems, and mental disorders. It is caused by mutations in the PAH gene, which encodes the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase needed to convert phenylalanine into tyrosine.

History

PKU was discovered in 1934 by Norwegian physician Asbjørn Følling, who detected phenylpyruvic acid in the urine of two siblings with intellectual disability—giving the condition its original name, "imbecillitas phenylpyruvica." In the 1950s, Horst Bickel and colleagues showed that a diet low in phenylalanine could prevent damage. Robert Guthrie developed a simple bacterial inhibition screening test in 1961, making PKU the model condition for universal newborn screening.

Signs and symptoms

Babies born with PKU appear normal at birth. Without treatment, phenylalanine accumulates in the blood and brain, causing progressive intellectual disability, microcephaly, epilepsy, eczema, and a characteristic musty odor in the breath, sweat, and urine from phenylacetate. With early treatment, children typically develop normally.

Treatment

The mainstay of treatment is a lifelong diet restricted in phenylalanine, avoiding high-protein foods and the sweetener aspartame, which is a source of phenylalanine; products containing aspartame carry the warning "Phenylketonurics: contains phenylalanine." Special medical formulas supply the other amino acids. The drug sapropterin (Kuvan) helps a subset of patients whose residual enzyme responds to the cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), and the enzyme therapy pegvaliase (Palynziq) is approved for adults. Sweeteners such as saccharin and stevia do not contain phenylalanine and are safe for people with PKU.

Maternal PKU

Women with PKU must follow a strict diet before and during pregnancy, because high maternal phenylalanine levels are teratogenic and can cause congenital heart disease and intellectual disability in the child, even if the child does not have PKU.

Genetics

PKU is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern; both parents must pass on a mutated PAH gene, located on chromosome 12. More than 1,000 pathogenic variants have been described, and severity varies with the degree of residual enzyme activity.
