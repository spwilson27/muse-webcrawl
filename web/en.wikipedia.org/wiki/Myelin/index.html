Myelin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Myelin is a lipid-rich insulating substance that forms a sheath around the axons of many neurons, dramatically increasing the speed of the action potentials they conduct — from about 1 m/s uninsulated to as much as 120 m/s in large myelinated fibres, by saltatory conduction between the gaps (nodes of Ranvier) in the sheath.

Myelin is made by glial cells: Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system (one cell, one axon segment) and oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system (one cell myelinating many axons). It is about 70–80% lipid — hence the white appearance of "white matter" — and its synthesis and maintenance continue throughout life, remodelling with learning.

Demyelinating diseases destroy it: multiple sclerosis (autoimmune attack on CNS myelin), Guillain–Barré syndrome (peripheral), and leukodystrophies (inherited defects). Conduction slows or fails, producing the scattered neurological deficits — vision loss, weakness, incoordination — of MS. The Hodgkin–Huxley equations (see action potential) describe the bare membrane; myelin is evolution's insulation wrapped around it, and its breakdown a measure of the membrane potential's fragility.
