Mitotic spindle

In cell biology, the mitotic spindle is the cytoskeletal structure of eukaryotic cells that forms during mitosis (and meiosis) to separate sister chromatids between daughter cells. It is built from microtubules polymerised from tubulin, together with hundreds of associated proteins — motor proteins such as kinesins and dynein, and microtubule-organising centres, in animal cells the centrosomes.

Structure and dynamics

Three classes of microtubules make up the spindle: kinetochore microtubules, which attach to chromosomes at the kinetochore and pull them poleward; polar microtubules, which interdigitate from opposite poles and push the poles apart; and astral microtubules, which anchor the poles to the cell cortex and position the spindle. Microtubules are dynamically unstable, growing and shrinking by addition and loss of tubulin subunits, which allows the spindle to "search and capture" chromosomes during prometaphase.

Function and checkpoints

Once every chromosome is bi-oriented — sister kinetochores attached to opposite poles — the spindle assembly checkpoint is satisfied, the anaphase-promoting complex triggers destruction of securin and cohesion, and sister chromatids separate in anaphase A while the poles move apart in anaphase B. Errors produce aneuploid daughter cells. Because the spindle is essential for division, it is a major target of chemotherapy: taxanes stabilise microtubules while vinca alkaloids depolymerise them, both arresting dividing cancer cells. The meiotic equivalent is the meiotic spindle; in oocytes it is acentrosomal.
