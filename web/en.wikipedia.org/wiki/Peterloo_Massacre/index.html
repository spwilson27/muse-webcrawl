Peterloo Massacre
The charge at St Peter's Field
Date16 August 1819
LocationSt Peter's Field, Manchester, England
Participants60,000 reform demonstrators; Manchester and Salford Yeomanry; 15th Hussars
Outcome18 killed (disputed estimates), 400–700 injured

The Peterloo Massacre took place at St Peter's Field, Manchester, on 16 August 1819, when cavalry charged into a crowd of around 60,000 people who had gathered to demand parliamentary reform and hear the radical speaker Henry Hunt. Eighteen people were killed and several hundred injured. The name "Peterloo" — an ironic echo of the Battle of Waterloo four years earlier — was coined by the Manchester Observer.

Background

After the Napoleonic Wars, economic depression, the Corn Laws, and a franchise restricted to a small propertied minority fuelled radical agitation in the industrial North. Lancashire's cotton workers, many in severe distress, organised mass meetings for universal male suffrage and annual parliaments. Local magistrates, alarmed by talk of "equality" and fearing revolution, resolved to arrest Hunt as he spoke.

The charge

When the arrest attempt stalled in the crowd, the magistrates ordered in the volunteer Manchester and Salford Yeomanry, followed by the regular 15th Hussars. The yeomanry slashed at the crowd with sabres as they tried to reach the hustings; in the panic and crush that followed, men, women and children — many present in their Sunday best, led by contingents of female reformers — were cut down or trampled. Among the dead were a two-year-old boy and a former soldier who had fought at Waterloo.

Aftermath and legacy

The government of Lord Liverpool praised the magistrates and responded with the repressive Six Acts. The massacre radicalised opinion, helped spur the founding of the Manchester Guardian in 1821, and became a defining symbol in the long campaign for democratic reform — Percy Shelley responded with his poem The Mask of Anarchy, written in the spirit of the revolutionary age that also produced Prometheus Unbound. A memorial in Manchester was unveiled in 2019, the bicentenary year.
