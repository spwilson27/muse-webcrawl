Craniosynostosis

Craniosynostosis is a condition in which one or more of the fibrous joints (sutures) between the bones of an infant's skull fuse prematurely, before the brain has finished growing. The skull then grows in a distorted, compensatory pattern, producing characteristic abnormal head shapes. It occurs in roughly one in 2,000 to 2,500 live births.

Types

The pattern of deformity depends on which suture fuses:

  • Sagittal synostosis (the most common) produces scaphocephaly, a long, narrow head.
  • Coronal synostosis produces plagiocephaly or, if bilateral, a short, wide head (brachycephaly).
  • Metopic synostosis produces trigonocephaly, a triangular forehead.
  • Lambdoid synostosis, the rarest, distorts the back of the head.

Causes and associations

Most cases are isolated and sporadic, but craniosynostosis is a feature of more than 150 syndromes, including Apert syndrome, Crouzon syndrome and Pfeiffer syndrome, many caused by mutations in fibroblast growth factor receptor genes (FGFR2 and FGFR3). Growth of the skull is driven by the expanding brain and normally occurs perpendicular to each suture, as described by Virchow's law.

Treatment

Treatment is usually surgical, performed in infancy: the fused suture is released and the skull remodelled (by open cranial vault reconstruction or, in selected cases, endoscopic strip craniectomy followed by helmet therapy) to allow normal growth, correct the head shape and prevent raised intracranial pressure, which can otherwise impair development and vision.
