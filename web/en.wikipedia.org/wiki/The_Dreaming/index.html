The Dreaming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Aboriginal art often depicts Dreaming stories and ancestral journeys across Country.

The Dreaming, also referred to as Dreamtime, is a term devised by early anthropologists to refer to a religio-cultural worldview attributed to Aboriginal Australian beliefs. It describes the period and ongoing reality in which ancestral beings — often called totemic ancestors — travelled across the land, creating the features of the landscape, its plants and animals, and establishing the laws, languages, and customs of Aboriginal society.

Concepts

The Dreaming is not confined to the distant past: it is a continuing dimension of reality in which ancestral beings remain present in sacred sites, in the land, and in living people. Stories of the ancestors' journeys, known as songlines or "Dreaming tracks", cross the continent and encode law, genealogy, navigation, and ecological knowledge, transmitted through story, song, dance, and art. Different Aboriginal language groups have their own terms for the concept, such as Tjukurrpa among the Pitjantjatjara and Wapar in other traditions; the English word "Dreaming", popularised by anthropologist W. E. H. Stanner, only approximates these ideas.

Relation to land and law

Connection to "Country" — a particular tract of land to which a group belongs and for which it holds responsibilities — is central to Aboriginal identity and underpins customary law. The Dreaming supplied the philosophical foundation for Aboriginal claims to land rights, recognised in part by the High Court's Mabo decision of 1992, which overturned the doctrine of terra nullius.

Art and storytelling

Dreaming stories are represented in bark paintings, rock art, ground sculpture, and the acrylic "dot paintings" of the Western Desert art movement, which became internationally celebrated from the 1970s. Certain designs and stories are restricted by traditional law as to who may see or tell them.
