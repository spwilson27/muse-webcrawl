The Comptroller General of the United States is the director of the Government Accountability Office (GAO), a legislative-branch agency established by Congress in 1921 to investigate and oversee the use of public money. The comptroller general is appointed to a 15-year term by the president of the United States with the advice and consent of the Senate, chosen from a list of candidates proposed by a congressional commission. The long, single, non-renewable term is intended to insulate the office from political pressure.

Role and responsibilities

As head of the GAO, the comptroller general oversees audits, evaluations, and investigations of federal programs and expenditures. The GAO is often called the "congressional watchdog," and the comptroller general reports findings and recommendations to Congress. The office also issues legal decisions on the use of appropriated funds, sets generally accepted government auditing standards (the "Yellow Book"), and issues decisions on bid protests of federal contracts. The comptroller general can be removed from office only by impeachment or by joint resolution of Congress for specified causes, such as inefficiency or malfeasance.

History

The position was created by the Budget and Accounting Act of 1921, which transferred auditing responsibilities from the Treasury Department to the new General Accounting Office (renamed the Government Accountability Office in 2004). The first comptroller general was John R. McCarl (1921–1936). Notable holders include Lindsay C. Warren, Elmer B. Staats, Charles A. Bowsher, David M. Walker, and Gene L. Dodaro, who became acting comptroller general in 2008 and was confirmed in 2010. In addition to leading the GAO, the comptroller general issues a regular "High Risk List" identifying federal programs vulnerable to fraud, waste, abuse, and mismanagement, and publishes the Financial Report of the United States Government audit.
