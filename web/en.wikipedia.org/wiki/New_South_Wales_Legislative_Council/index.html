The New South Wales Legislative Council, often referred to as the upper house, is one of the two chambers of the Parliament of New South Wales, the legislature of the Australian state of New South Wales; the other is the Legislative Assembly. Its 42 members (MLCs) are elected from a single statewide electorate by proportional representation, serving eight-year terms with half facing election every four years.

History

The Council originated in 1823 as an appointed advisory body to the Governor of the then-colony of New South Wales — the oldest legislative body in Australia. With responsible government in 1856 it became the upper house of a bicameral legislature, initially appointed by the Governor on ministerial advice. Labor premier Jack Lang's attempts to abolish or swamp the Council in the 1920s–30s led to reforms, and in 1978, under Premier Neville Wran, the Council became directly elected by proportional representation. A 1991 referendum, associated with the campaign of Euthanasia No campaigner Fred Nile's opponents and supporters alike, reduced terms and confirmed the system of 42 members with staggered elections.

Role and powers

The Legislative Council reviews and amends legislation, initiates bills other than money bills, and cannot block supply. Its proportional election system means governments rarely hold a majority in the chamber, giving it a strong committee system and a reputation as an effective "house of review." Members serve on standing committees — including the long-running General Purpose Standing Committees — that scrutinise government administration and expenditure.
