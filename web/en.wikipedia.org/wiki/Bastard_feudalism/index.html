Bastard feudalism

A late-medieval lord's livery badge, worn by his retained followers.

Bastard feudalism is a term used by historians to describe the form of feudal society in late medieval England, roughly from the late thirteenth to the late fifteenth century, in which the bond between lord and follower was based less on the grant of land (a fief) in return for service and more on money, written contracts and personal affinity. The phrase was coined by the historian Charles Plummer in 1885 and carried a pejorative sense — a degenerate or "bastardised" version of an older, land-based feudalism.

Features

Under bastard feudalism, magnates recruited followers through indentures — written contracts promising an annual fee or annuity, robes, food and lodging, and the lord's "good lordship" (patronage and protection) in return for the retainer's service in peace and war. Retainers wore the lord's livery (uniform colours and badges) and formed his affinity, the network of servants, tenants, lawyers and gentry through which magnate power operated in the localities. Lords also exercised maintenance — supporting their men's lawsuits, sometimes corruptly — which contemporaries regarded as one of the system's abuses.

Historiography

Early historians blamed bastard feudalism for disorder, private armies and the Wars of the Roses. K. B. McFarlane, in influential work from the 1940s published posthumously in England in the Fifteenth Century (1981), argued instead that the system was a rational and largely functional adaptation: money retaining met the crown's own needs for armies (the king retained soldiers by indenture too), supplied local governance in an age of thin royal administration, and was no more intrinsically disorderly than the tenure it replaced. Later historians have refined McFarlane's rehabilitation, emphasising the crown's management of the affinities and the system's gradual curtailment under Edward IV, Henry VII (whose statutes restricted retaining) and the Tudors.
