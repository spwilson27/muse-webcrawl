Differential scanning calorimetry

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) is a thermoanalytical technique in which the difference in the amount of heat required to increase the temperature of a sample and a reference is measured as a function of temperature. Both are maintained at nearly the same temperature throughout a programmed temperature scan; when the sample undergoes a physical transformation, more or less heat must flow to it than to the reference to keep them equal.

Measurements

DSC detects transitions such as melting, crystallization, glass transitions, curing, oxidation, and denaturation of proteins. The resulting thermogram plots heat flow against temperature; peaks and steps yield transition temperatures, enthalpies (from peak areas), and heat capacities. Two main instrument designs exist: heat-flux DSC and power-compensated DSC.

Applications

DSC is widely used in polymer science (characterizing glass transition and crystallinity), pharmaceuticals (polymorphism, purity, stability), food science, and materials research. Variants include modulated-temperature DSC, which separates overlapping reversible and non-reversible events, and fast-scanning chip calorimetry for very small samples.
