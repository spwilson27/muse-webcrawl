Batesian mimicry

Batesian mimicry is a form of mimicry in which a harmless species (the mimic) has evolved to imitate the warning signals of a harmful species (the model) directed at a common predator. It is named after the English naturalist Henry Walter Bates, who described the phenomenon in butterflies of the Amazon rainforest in an 1862 paper that provided early support for Darwin's theory of natural selection.

A hoverfly (mimic) resembling a wasp (model).

Mechanism

Batesian mimicry is a "parasitic" relationship between signal and receiver: the mimic benefits from the predator's learned avoidance of the model without paying the cost of producing toxins or other defences. The system works only while mimics remain relatively uncommon compared with models; if mimics become too numerous, predators encounter palatable prey bearing the signal often enough that the warning loses its effect — an example of frequency-dependent selection.

Examples

Classic examples include hoverflies that resemble wasps and bees; the viceroy butterfly, long thought to mimic the toxic monarch (though the viceroy is now known to be unpalatable itself, making the pair partly Müllerian); scarlet kingsnakes mimicking venomous coral snakes; and numerous moths, spiders and even plants that copy warning patterns. Acoustic Batesian mimicry also occurs, as when harmless moths produce the ultrasonic clicks of toxic tiger moths to deter bats.

Contrast with Müllerian mimicry

In Müllerian mimicry, two or more genuinely defended species converge on a shared warning signal, mutually reinforcing predator learning. Batesian mimics, by contrast, are "sheep in wolves' clothing" and erode the signal's reliability.
