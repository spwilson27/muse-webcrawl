Bryozoa (also known as the Polyzoa, Ectoprocta or commonly as moss animals) are a phylum of simple, aquatic invertebrate animals, nearly all living in sedentary colonies. Typically about 0.5 millimetres long, individual bryozoans (zooids) filter feed with a lophophore — a crown of tentacles that whips up water currents to draw in food particles. Colonies range from encrusting sheets on rocks and seaweed to delicate lace-like fans and gelatinous freshwater blobs; most secrete a protective skeleton of calcium carbonate or chitin.

Biology

Colonies grow by asexual budding from a single founding zooid (ancestrula) produced by sexual reproduction. Zooids within a colony may be specialised: feeding autozooids, defensive avicularia with snapping jaws, and cleaning vibracula. Bryozoans lack respiratory, circulatory and excretory systems; gas exchange and waste removal occur by diffusion, with ammonia waste managed by the lophophore. Freshwater species produce durable dormant statoblasts that survive drying and freezing.

Fossil record

Bryozoans have an extensive fossil record, abundant from the Ordovician period onward, and were important reef-builders and contributors to limestone deposits in the Palaeozoic and Mesozoic. Their colony growth forms are widely used by geologists as indicators of palaeoenvironment.
