Golem

In Jewish folklore, a golem (Hebrew: גולם) is an animated anthropomorphic being created entirely from inanimate matter, usually clay or mud. The word appears in the Bible (Psalm 139:16) and in Talmudic literature, where it denotes an embryonic or incomplete substance. In medieval and later legends, a golem is brought to life by ritual and the power of the Hebrew word — most famously by inscribing the word emet ("truth") on its forehead, or by placing a slip of paper bearing the Name of God in its mouth. Erasing the first letter to leave met ("dead") deactivates it.

The Golem of Prague

The best-known golem narrative involves Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal of Prague, who is said in the late 16th century to have fashioned a golem from the clay of the Vltava River to defend the Prague ghetto against antisemitic attacks and blood libel accusations. In the legend, the golem grew violent or uncontrollable and had to be deactivated; its body was stored in the attic of the Old New Synagogue, where, according to folklore, it remains and can be reawakened if needed. The specific stories were largely recorded and popularised in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Other traditions

Earlier legends attach golem-making to figures such as Rabbi Elijah Ba'al Shem of Chełm. Medieval commentaries on the Sefer Yetzirah (Book of Creation) described the combination of Hebrew letters by which sages could imitate God's creation of Adam — though such a creature typically lacked the power of speech, since speech was God's gift alone.

Cultural influence

The golem has become a widespread cultural metaphor for created things that escape their creator's control, influencing works from Paul Wegener's film The Golem: How He Came into the World (1920) to Gustav Meyrink's novel The Golem (1915). The figure is often cited as a precursor of the robot — Karel Čapek's R.U.R. emerged from the same Prague milieu — and of modern discussions about artificial intelligence.
