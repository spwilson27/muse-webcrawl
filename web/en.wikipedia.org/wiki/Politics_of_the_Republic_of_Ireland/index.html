Politics of the Republic of Ireland

The politics of the Republic of Ireland take place in a framework of a parliamentary representative democratic republic, in which the President of Ireland is head of state and the Taoiseach is head of government, operating within a multi-party system. Executive power is exercised by the government, legislative power is vested in the Oireachtas (parliament), and the judiciary is independent.

Institutions

The Oireachtas consists of the President and two houses: Dáil Éireann (the lower house, whose members, TDs, are directly elected by proportional representation using the single transferable vote) and Seanad Éireann (the Senate, largely indirectly elected and nominated). The Taoiseach is nominated by the Dáil and appointed by the President. The President, elected directly for a seven-year term, has mostly ceremonial functions but retains certain discretionary powers, such as referring bills to the Supreme Court.

Political parties

For most of the state's history, politics was dominated by two parties born of the Civil War split of 1922–23: Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael, usually described as centrist or centre-right. The Labour Party is the third oldest party. Sinn Féin rose sharply in the 2010s and 2020s, becoming the largest party by first-preference votes in the 2020 general election. In 2020 Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael formed a government together for the first time, in coalition with the Green Party, with the office of Taoiseach rotating between Micheál Martin and Leo Varadkar. Following the 2024 general election, Fianna Fáil and Fine Gael again formed a coalition, supported by independent TDs, with Micheál Martin returning as Taoiseach.

Constitution and referendums

The Constitution of Ireland (Bunreacht na hÉireann), adopted in 1937, can be amended only by referendum. Referendums have produced major social changes, including legalization of divorce (1995), marriage equality (2015), and repeal of the constitutional ban on abortion (2018). Ireland is a member of the European Union, and European integration has been a central axis of policy since accession in 1973.
