Convention on Cluster Munitions

An unexploded cluster submunition

The Convention on Cluster Munitions (CCM) is an international treaty that prohibits the use, transfer, and stockpiling of cluster munitions, a type of explosive weapon which scatters submunitions ("bomblets") over an area. The convention was adopted on 30 May 2008 in Dublin, Ireland, opened for signature in Oslo in December 2008, and entered into force on 1 August 2010.

Provisions

States parties undertake never under any circumstances to use, develop, produce, acquire, stockpile, retain, or transfer cluster munitions, and to destroy existing stockpiles within eight years of entry into force. The treaty also requires clearance of contaminated areas, assistance to victims, and transparency reporting. Certain munitions with a small number of heavier, self-destructing submunitions are excluded from the definition.

Negotiation

The treaty was negotiated through the "Oslo Process", launched by Norway in 2007 outside the deadlocked UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons framework, following concern over civilian harm from cluster munition use in the 2006 Lebanon War. It was modelled in part on the 1997 Ottawa Treaty banning anti-personnel landmines.

Signatories and non-parties

More than one hundred states have joined the convention. Major military powers including the United States, Russia, China, India, Pakistan, and Israel have not signed, although several have adopted restrictive policies on use and export. The treaty's norm against cluster munitions drew renewed attention during the Russo-Ukrainian War, when the United States transferred cluster munitions to Ukraine in 2023 and Lithuania moved to withdraw from the convention.
