Regular and irregular verbs

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A regular verb is a verb whose conjugation follows the typical, predictable pattern of its language. An irregular verb is one whose conjugation deviates from that pattern, usually through changes to the verb stem, suppletion, or endings inherited from older forms of the language.

English

In English, regular verbs form their past tense and past participle by adding the suffix -ed (or -d) to the base form, as in walk–walked, with three spoken allomorphs /t/, /d/ and /ɪd/. Irregular verbs, numbering roughly 200 in common use, instead change their internal vowel (sing–sang–sung), change both vowel and ending (teach–taught), show no change (cut–cut), or are fully suppletive (go–went; the verb be). Although irregular verbs are a minority of the vocabulary, they include the most frequently used verbs in the language.

Acquisition and change

Children learning English typically over-regularise irregular verbs at one stage, producing forms such as "goed" or "holded", evidence that the regular rule is productive. Over centuries, infrequent irregular verbs tend to regularise (as chide/chid gave way to chided), while very frequent verbs resist change, which is why the commonest English verbs are overwhelmingly irregular.
