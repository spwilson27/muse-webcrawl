Cytokinin

Cytokinins (CKs) are a class of plant hormones that promote cell division (cytokinesis) in plant roots and shoots. They are involved primarily in cell growth and differentiation, but also affect apical dominance, axillary bud growth, nutrient mobilisation, and the delay of leaf senescence.

History

Cytokinins were discovered in the 1950s through experiments by Folke Skoog and Carlos Miller on tobacco tissue culture, in which a substance from autoclaved herring sperm DNA — kinetin — was found to stimulate cell division in the presence of auxin. The first naturally occurring cytokinin, zeatin, was isolated from immature maize (corn) kernels in 1963. Most natural cytokinins are derivatives of the purine base adenine.

Functions

Working in a balance with auxin, cytokinins regulate organ formation in tissue culture (a high cytokinin-to-auxin ratio promotes shoots; the reverse promotes roots), release lateral buds from apical dominance, stimulate chloroplast development, and delay ageing of leaves by drawing nutrients toward treated tissue. They act against stress responses and participate in circadian and light signalling. Cytokinins are synthesised mainly in root tips and transported upward in the xylem, as well as being produced in developing seeds and fruits.

Signalling and uses

Cytokinin perception uses a two-component phosphorelay system similar to bacterial signalling: membrane-localised histidine kinase receptors activate response regulators that switch on cytokinin-responsive genes. In agriculture and horticulture, synthetic cytokinins such as benzyladenine are used to promote branching and fruit set, and cytokinin treatment is a standard step in plant micropropagation. Genetic work has shown that reducing cytokinin levels in rice can increase grain yield, a notable result in crop improvement.
