Crimean Goths

The Crimean Goths were a Germanic people who inhabited the mountains of the southwestern Crimea from late antiquity into the early modern era — the longest-lived Gothic community, outlasting the Ostrogothic and Visigothic kingdoms by a thousand years. They spoke Crimean Gothic, an East Germanic language attested as late as the sixteenth century.

History

Goths settled in the Crimea during the third century AD, and after the Hunnic invasions a population remained in the mountainous southwest, centred on the fortress settlement of Doros (later Mangup). They lived under successive Byzantine, Khazar, and local influence, organised in the region the Byzantines called Gothia. In the later Middle Ages their territory formed part of the Principality of Theodoro, a small Byzantine successor state that fell to the Ottoman Empire in 1475.

Under Ottoman rule the Crimean Goths gradually assimilated into the surrounding Greek- and Tatar-speaking populations, but their language survived remarkably long. The Flemish diplomat Ogier de Busbecq, ambassador to Constantinople, recorded a word list of Crimean Gothic in the 1560s — about eighty words and a fragment of a song — the principal evidence for the language. Travellers' reports suggest Gothic may still have been spoken in remote villages into the eighteenth century.

Language

Crimean Gothic is the only East Germanic language with post-medieval attestation. Busbecq's list shows both clear kinship with Biblical Gothic and differences suggesting either dialectal divergence or influence from West Germanic. Words recorded include broe ("bread"), plut ("blood"), and stul ("stool/chair").
