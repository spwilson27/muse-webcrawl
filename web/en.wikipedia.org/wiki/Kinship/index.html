Kinship

In anthropology, kinship is the web of social relationships that form an important part of the lives of all humans in all societies, although its exact meanings vary even within this discipline. It typically refers to the patterns of relationship built from ties of descent (consanguinity) and marriage (affinity), which structure families, inheritance, residence, obligations, and identity. The comparative study of kinship has been central to anthropology since its founding, from Lewis Henry Morgan's Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family (1871) onward.

Descent and alliance

Kinship systems are commonly analysed along two axes. Descent traces membership of kin groups through ancestors: patrilineal descent through men, matrilineal descent through women, bilateral or cognatic descent through both, and double descent combining the two for different purposes. Corporate descent groups — lineages and clans — may hold property, regulate marriage, and organise ritual. Alliance theory, associated with Claude Lévi-Strauss's The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949), instead emphasises marriage exchange between groups, grounded in the near-universal incest taboo, as the foundation of kinship.

Kinship terminology

Societies classify relatives with distinctive terminologies. Following Morgan and later Alfred Kroeber and George Peter Murdock, anthropologists commonly distinguish six patterns: Hawaiian (generational), Eskimo (lineal, as in English), Iroquois (bifurcate merging), Crow and Omaha (bifurcate merging with generational skewing), and Sudanese (descriptive). Which relatives are called by the same term reveals how a society groups kin for purposes of behaviour and obligation.

Residence, marriage and family

Kinship is bound up with marriage rules (including endogamy and exogamy, polygyny and polyandry, and preferential cousin marriage), post-marital residence patterns (patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal, avunculocal), and the forms of the family and household. Kin terms and obligations also extend to fictive kin — godparents, blood brothers, milk kin in Islamic tradition — and to adoptive and chosen families.

Critiques and new directions

From the 1970s, David Schneider's critique of the assumption that kinship everywhere rests on "blood" ties prompted rethinking; feminist anthropology, the study of new reproductive technologies, same-sex families, and transnational migration have further broadened the field toward a study of "relatedness" — how people create and live bonds of belonging — rather than formal genealogy alone.
