Makars

The Makars
Periodc. 1450 – early 1600s
LanguageMiddle Scots
RegionKingdom of Scotland

The Makars were the poets of the Scottish Renaissance, writing in Middle Scots during the 15th and early 16th centuries. The term — from the Scots verb mak, "to make" — parallels the English "maker" used for poets. They consciously modelled themselves on Geoffrey Chaucer while developing a distinctively Scottish courtly and comic literature.

Principal figures

  • Robert Henryson (c. 1425–c. 1500) — author of the Morall Fabillis and The Testament of Cresseid.
  • William Dunbar (c. 1460–c. 1513) — court poet to James IV, known for the allegorical The Goldyn Targe, the satirical Dance of the Sevin Deidly Synnis, and the elegiac line "I that in heill wes and gladnes" from Lament for the Makaris.
  • Gavin Douglas (c. 1474–1522) — bishop and translator whose Eneados (1513) was the first complete translation of Virgil's Aeneid into an Anglic language.
  • David Lyndsay (c. 1490–1555) — herald and dramatist, author of Ane Satyre of the Thrie Estaitis.
  • James I of Scotland — credited with The Kingis Quair.

Legacy

Dunbar's Lament for the Makaris, with its refrain Timor mortis conturbat me, preserves the names of many lesser figures of the circle. The tradition waned after the Union of the Crowns in 1603 shifted the court to London. In modern times "Makar" has been revived as an official title: Scotland appoints a national poet, the Scots Makar, and several Scottish cities appoint their own makars.
