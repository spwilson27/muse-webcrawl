Fred Hoyle

Sir Fred Hoyle (24 June 1915 – 20 August 2001) was an English astronomer who formulated the theory of stellar nucleosynthesis and research into the chemical elements formed in stars. He coined the term "Big Bang" in a 1949 BBC radio broadcast, while advocating his own steady-state model of the universe.


Born: 24 June 1915, Gilstead, Yorkshire, England
Died: 20 August 2001, Bournemouth
Known for: Steady-state theory, stellar nucleosynthesis, coining "Big Bang"
Institutions: University of Cambridge

Early life and education

Hoyle was born in Gilstead, West Yorkshire, and studied mathematics at Emmanuel College, Cambridge. During the Second World War he worked on radar at the Admiralty.

Stellar nucleosynthesis

In a landmark 1957 paper with Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge and William Fowler — often called B2FH — Hoyle and colleagues showed how most elements heavier than hydrogen and helium are forged in stars. Hoyle famously predicted a resonance in carbon-12 needed for carbon to form, later confirmed experimentally. See Fusion power and Friedrich Bessel for related astronomy.

Steady state and Big Bang

With Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold, Hoyle developed the steady-state theory in 1948, proposing continuous creation of matter as the universe expands. Evidence for a hot early universe, especially the cosmic microwave background, eventually favoured the FLRW model.

Later work and writing

Hoyle founded the Institute of Astronomy at Cambridge, wrote popular science and science fiction including The Black Cloud (1957), and held controversial views on panspermia and evolution. He was knighted in 1972.
