Cloisonné

TechniqueVitreous enamel on metal
Name originFrench cloison, "partition"

Cloisonné is an ancient technique for decorating metalwork objects with coloured material held in place by partitions (cloisons) formed from metal wires or strips soldered to the surface. In recent centuries vitreous enamel has been the usual material.

Process

The artisan bends wire into the outlines of the design and solders it edge-on to a metal base, creating compartments. Powdered enamel is packed into each compartment and fired, a process repeated to fill and level the surface, which is then polished.

History

The technique was developed in the ancient Near East and refined in Byzantium, where enamelled plaques adorned crowns and reliquaries. Chinese cloisonné enamel, perfected during the Ming dynasty, became famous for large vessels with bright colours on a turquoise ground, and Japanese artists later produced highly detailed work.
