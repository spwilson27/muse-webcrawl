Congenital cytomegalovirus infection

Congenital CMV is the most common infectious cause of birth defects in developed countries.

Congenital cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection refers to a condition where an infant is infected with cytomegalovirus before birth. It occurs when a pregnant person has a primary CMV infection, or less commonly a reactivation or reinfection, and the virus crosses the placenta to the fetus. Congenital CMV is the most common congenital viral infection and a leading non-genetic cause of childhood sensorineural hearing loss and neurodevelopmental disability.

Signs and symptoms

Most infected newborns (about 85–90%) are asymptomatic at birth, although some later develop hearing loss. Symptomatic infants may show intrauterine growth restriction, microcephaly, jaundice, a petechial "blueberry muffin" rash, hepatosplenomegaly, seizures and intracranial calcifications. Long-term sequelae include sensorineural hearing loss, intellectual disability, vision loss from chorioretinitis, and cerebral palsy.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is confirmed by detecting CMV DNA by PCR in the infant's urine or saliva within the first three weeks of life. Antiviral treatment with valganciclovir, begun in the first month of life in symptomatic infants, can improve hearing and developmental outcomes. Prevention centres on hygiene measures for pregnant people, especially avoiding contact with the saliva and urine of young children; there is no licensed vaccine.
