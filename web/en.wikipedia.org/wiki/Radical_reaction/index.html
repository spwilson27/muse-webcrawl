Radical reaction

A radical reaction is any chemical reaction involving radicals — atoms or molecules with an unpaired valence electron. Radical reactions proceed through homolytic bond cleavage (each fragment keeping one electron of the broken bond) and are typically divided into three phases: initiation, propagation and termination. They are important in combustion, atmospheric chemistry, polymer chemistry and biochemistry.

Mechanism

Initiation creates radicals from stable molecules using heat, light or redox chemistry — for example the photolysis of chlorine into two chlorine atoms. Propagation steps consume one radical and produce another, sustaining a chain: in the chlorination of methane, a chlorine radical abstracts hydrogen to give HCl and a methyl radical, which attacks Cl₂ to form chloromethane and regenerate a chlorine radical. Termination occurs when two radicals combine to form a stable molecule, ending the chain.

Examples and importance

Radical chain reactions drive the combustion of hydrocarbons and the explosive reaction of hydrogen with oxygen. In the stratosphere, chlorine radicals from chlorofluorocarbons catalytically destroy ozone. In biology, reactive oxygen species such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals damage proteins, lipids and DNA, and are countered by antioxidants. Industrially, radical reactions underpin free-radical polymerization, autoxidation of oils and fats, and halogenation processes.

Detection and control

Radicals are usually short-lived and are detected by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy or trapped with spin traps. Reactions can be suppressed by radical inhibitors (scavengers) such as hydroquinone or hindered phenols, which intercept chain-carrying radicals.
