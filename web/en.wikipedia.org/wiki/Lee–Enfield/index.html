The Lee–Enfield is a bolt-action, magazine-fed repeating rifle that served as the main firearm of the military forces of the British Empire and Commonwealth during the first half of the 20th century, and was the standard service rifle of the British Army from its official adoption in 1895 until 1957. The name derives from its designer, James Paris Lee, and the factory where it was designed, the Royal Small Arms Factory at Enfield.

The rifle was noted for its fast-operating bolt action and large magazine capacity, which allowed a trained rifleman to fire 20 to 30 aimed rounds a minute, making it the fastest military bolt-action rifle of the era. Over 17 million Lee–Enfield rifles were produced in all variants, making it one of the most widely produced military rifles in history.

History

The Lee–Enfield lineage began with the Lee–Metford rifle of 1888, which combined James Paris Lee's bolt action and detachable magazine with a barrel rifled to a pattern designed by William Ellis Metford. The Lee–Enfield proper was adopted in November 1895 as the Rifle, Magazine, Lee–Enfield Mark I, chambered for the .303 British cartridge and fitted with Enfield-pattern rifling suited to cordite propellant.

Experience in the Second Boer War led to the famous Short Magazine Lee–Enfield (SMLE), introduced in 1903, a single "short" rifle intended for both infantry and cavalry. The SMLE Mk III of 1907 became the standard British rifle of the First World War. Its successor, the Rifle No. 4, was mass-produced during the Second World War with a simplified, stronger action and an aperture rear sight.

Design

The Lee bolt system uses rear-locking lugs and a short bolt throw with a cock-on-closing action, contributing to its speed of operation. The detachable box magazine holds ten rounds of .303 ammunition — twice the capacity of most contemporary rifles — and was normally loaded by two five-round charger clips through the top of the action. The bolt handle is positioned close to the trigger, enabling rapid cycling without the shooter losing sight picture.

Variants and later use

Major variants include the SMLE Mk III, the Rifle No. 4 Mk I and Mk I*, the jungle carbine No. 5 Mk I with its distinctive flash hider, and the L42A1 sniper rifle converted from No. 4 receivers and chambered in 7.62×51mm NATO. Canada, Australia, and India all produced Lee–Enfields domestically; India's Ishapore factory produced the Rifle 2A/2A1 in 7.62mm NATO. The rifle remains in ceremonial and reserve use in several Commonwealth countries and is prized by collectors and target shooters.
