Wampum

Wampum is a traditional shell bead of the Eastern Woodlands tribes of Native North America. It consists of small tubular beads made from the shells of the quahog clam (Mercenaria mercenaria), which yield purple beads, and the North Atlantic channeled whelk (Busycon canaliculatum), which yield white beads. Wampum was used by the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), Wampanoag, Narragansett, and other nations for ornament, ceremony, diplomacy, and record-keeping; woven into belts, patterns of purple and white beads encoded agreements and narratives.

A wampum belt; patterned belts served as mnemonic records of treaties and councils.

Manufacture

Making wampum was laborious: shell pieces were ground into cylinders on stone, drilled — before European metal drills, with stone or bone tools — polished, and strung. Purple beads, cut from the small purple patch of the quahog shell, were rarer and typically valued at about twice the white. The word derives from Massachusett wampumpeag, "white strings of shell beads".

Uses

Diplomatic and mnemonic records

Among the Haudenosaunee, wampum strings and belts authenticated messages and solemnised councils. Belts commemorated foundational events and agreements: the Hiawatha Belt records the union of the Five Nations; the Two Row Wampum (Guswenta, 1613) symbolises the parallel, non-interfering coexistence of Indigenous nations and Dutch settlers. Designated keepers recited the associated oral records at councils; belts are therefore often described as a form of writing or archive.

Currency

In the 17th century, colonial New Netherland and New England adopted wampum as a medium of exchange — it was legal tender in parts of the colonies — because coin was scarce. Mass production with European tools inflated the supply, and depreciation, counterfeiting with dyed shells, and the growing availability of coin ended its monetary role by the late 1600s.

Ceremonial and personal use

Wampum strings are still exchanged in condolence ceremonies, marriages, and adoptions, and belts remain potent symbols of nationhood; repatriation claims have returned important belts from museums to the Onondaga Nation and other keepers.
