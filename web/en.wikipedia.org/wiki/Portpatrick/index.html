Portpatrick (Scottish Gaelic: Port Phàdraig) is a village and civil parish in the Rhins of Galloway, Dumfries and Galloway, Scotland. It lies on the west coast of the Rhins peninsula, roughly 8 miles (13 km) southwest of Stranraer, and faces the North Channel towards Ireland.

History

The village grew around its sheltered natural harbour, and for centuries the shortest sea crossing between Scotland and Ireland ran from Portpatrick to Donaghadee in County Down, a distance of about 21 miles (34 km). The route became the principal mail and packet station between Great Britain and Ireland after 1662, and in the 18th and 19th centuries large numbers of Irish migrants, cattle and soldiers passed through the port. The old church, built in 1629 and notable for its unusual round tower, served a parish that included many Irish mariners.

Portpatrick's exposed harbour was dangerous in southwesterly storms; the lifeboat John Buchanan disaster of 1900 and repeated wrecking of vessels led to diminishing use. When the packet route transferred to Stranraer and later Cairnryan with larger vessels, Portpatrick declined as a commercial port. A railway reached the village in 1862 via the Portpatrick Railway, later part of the Portpatrick and Wigtownshire Joint Railway; the line closed in 1965.

Today

The village is now a small holiday resort and harbour for pleasure craft and inshore fishing. It is the starting point of the Southern Upland Way, Scotland's coast-to-coast long-distance footpath, which ends at Cockburnspath on the North Sea. Dunskey Castle, a ruined tower house on the cliffs just south of the village, dates from the 16th century and is a local landmark. The surrounding coastline is noted for dramatic cliffs, sea fishing, and views across to the Antrim coast of Northern Ireland on clear days.
