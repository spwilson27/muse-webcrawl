Siberian High

The Siberian High (also known as the Siberian Anticyclone) is a massive collection of cold, dry air that accumulates over the northeastern part of Eurasia from September until April or May. It is generally centred on Lake Baikal. It reaches its greatest size and strength in the winter when the air temperature near the centre of the high pressure area is often below −40 °C (−40 °F). The atmospheric pressure is often above 1,040 millibars (31 inHg). The Siberian High is the strongest semi-permanent high in the Northern Hemisphere and is responsible for both the lowest temperature recorded in the Northern Hemisphere, of −67.7 °C (−89.9 °F) on 15 January 1885 at Verkhoyansk, and the highest pressure, 1,083.8 mbar (32.01 inHg) at Agata, Krasnoyarsk Krai on 31 December 1968, ever recorded. The Siberian High is responsible for severe winter cold in a large part of northern Asia, Europe, and parts of North America.

Formation

The Siberian High forms mostly due to the Siberian Arctic air cooling in the clear sky, as well as dry air descending and warming over the region. In the summer, it is replaced by a low-pressure system. The high generally starts to form at the end of August, reaches its peak in January, and then starts to weaken toward the end of April. Its genesis at the end of the summer is caused by the convergence of summer air flows and the cooling of the Eurasian landmass.

Effects

The Siberian High affects the weather patterns in most parts of the Northern Hemisphere: it influences wind flow and precipitation amounts across Asia, Europe, and North America. It is the major driver of the East Asian winter monsoon, bringing bitterly cold, dry air southward over China, Korea, and Japan, and it can send polar air masses southwest into Europe, producing cold waves. When the high interacts with moist air from the oceans, it can also contribute to heavy snowfall on the western coast of Japan (the "snow country") and in parts of the Russian Far East.
