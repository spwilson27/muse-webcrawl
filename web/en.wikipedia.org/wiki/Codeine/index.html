Codeine

Codeine is an opiate and prodrug of morphine used to treat pain, coughing, and diarrhea. It is typically used to treat mild to moderate degrees of pain and is often combined with paracetamol (acetaminophen), as in co-codamol, or with aspirin. It is found naturally in opium, the latex of the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum), of which it constitutes roughly 0.3–3% of the dried latex.

Pharmacology

Codeine itself has weak affinity for opioid receptors; most of its analgesic effect comes from its conversion by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 into morphine. Genetic variation in CYP2D6 means that "poor metabolizers" obtain little benefit, while "ultra-rapid metabolizers" convert codeine quickly and may suffer dangerous morphine overdose—especially infants exposed through breastfeeding, leading regulators to restrict its use in children.

History

Codeine was isolated in 1832 by the French chemist Pierre Jean Robiquet, who also extracted caffeine. Its name derives from the Greek kodeia, meaning "poppy head". Most codeine used today is synthesized by methylating morphine rather than being extracted directly from opium. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Adverse effects and dependence

Common side effects include constipation, nausea, vomiting, dry mouth, itching, and drowsiness. As with other opioids, use can lead to tolerance, physical dependence, and misuse; high doses may cause respiratory depression. In the United States codeine is a Schedule II drug alone and Schedule III/V in low-dose combination products; recreational use of codeine cough syrup mixtures ("lean" or "purple drank") has been widely reported.
