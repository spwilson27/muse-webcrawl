Hirudo medicinalis

Hirudo medicinalis
Class: Clitellata
Order: Arhynchobdellida
Family: Hirudinidae
Conservation: Near Threatened (IUCN)

Hirudo medicinalis, the European medicinal leech, is a species of jawed leech famed for its use in medicine from antiquity to the present. It inhabits freshwater ponds and slow streams across Europe and western Asia, attaching to amphibians and mammals to feed on blood.

Biology

The leech can reach 20 cm in length and is olive-green to brown with longitudinal stripes. Its body of 34 segments bears a sucker at each end; the anterior sucker surrounds a mouth armed with three jaws, each carrying dozens of tiny teeth that make a Y-shaped incision. Saliva injected as it feeds contains hirudin and other anticoagulant and anaesthetic compounds, so a single meal of several times the leech's body weight can sustain it for months. Like other clitellates, it is hermaphroditic and lays eggs in cocoons in damp soil near the water's edge.

Medical use

Leeches were applied for bloodletting throughout ancient, medieval and early modern medicine, peaking in nineteenth-century Europe, when tens of millions were imported annually and wild populations collapsed — the species remains protected, including listing in CITES Appendix II. Modern medicine has revived them for a quite specific purpose: relieving venous congestion in reconstructive and microsurgery, such as reattached fingers and skin flaps, and in 2004 the US Food and Drug Administration cleared medicinal leeches as a medical device. Research has also shown that many leeches sold commercially as H. medicinalis in fact belong to the closely related species Hirudo verbana.
