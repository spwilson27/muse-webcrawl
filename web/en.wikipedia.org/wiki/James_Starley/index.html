James Starley

Born21 April 1830, Albourne, Sussex, England
Died17 June 1881 (aged 51), Coventry, England
OccupationInventor, industrialist
Known forDevelopment of the bicycle; tangent-spoked wheel; differential gear

James Starley (21 April 1830 – 17 June 1881) was an English inventor and father of the bicycle industry. He was one of the most innovative and successful builders of bicycles and tricycles, and his inventions include the differential gear and the perfected design of the penny-farthing (high-wheel) bicycle, as well as the introduction of the tangent-spoked tension wheel.

Early life

Starley was born at Albourne, Sussex, the son of a farmer. As a teenager he ran away to Lewes, and later worked as a gardener and repaired clocks and gadgets in his spare time. He moved to London and became a gardener for Josiah Turner, a partner in a sewing-machine firm; in 1859 Turner, Starley and others founded the Coventry Sewing Machine Company (later the Coventry Machinists Company) in Coventry, then the centre of Britain's machine trades.

Bicycle development

When a French Michaux velocipede ("boneshaker") was brought to the works in 1868, Starley saw its potential and began improving it. With William Hillman he patented the "Ariel" in 1870 — an all-metal machine with solid rubber tyres and the tangent-spoked wheel, in which spokes are angled so as to transmit torque, allowing lighter, larger wheels and higher speeds. The Ariel is regarded as the first true penny-farthing design. Starley's designs steadily increased the front wheel's size to maximise speed, culminating in machines with wheels over 1.5 m (5 ft) in diameter.

Later work

In the 1870s Starley founded his own firm, Starley & Sutton Co of the Meteor Works, Coventry, and turned increasingly to tricycles, including the "Salvo" — and an improved differential gear that allowed the drive to be applied evenly to a pair of wheels, a device later fundamental to the motor car. His nephew John Kemp Starley later designed the Rover Safety Bicycle (1885), the direct ancestor of the modern bicycle.

Death and legacy

James Starley died suddenly in 1881, aged 51. A monument in Coventry commemorates him as the father of the bicycle industry; the city's cycle industry, and later motor industry, grew directly from the firms he and his associates founded.
