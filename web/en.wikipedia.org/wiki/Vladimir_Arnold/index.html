Vladimir Igorevich Arnold (12 June 1937 – 3 June 2010) was a Soviet and Russian mathematician. While he is best known for the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem in dynamical systems, he made fundamental contributions across an unusually wide range of subjects, including topology, algebraic geometry, classical mechanics, and singularity theory.

Early work

Arnold was a student of Andrey Kolmogorov at Moscow State University. While still an undergraduate, in 1957 at the age of nineteen, he solved Hilbert's thirteenth problem, showing that any continuous function of several variables can be constructed as a finite superposition of continuous functions of a single variable and the binary operation of addition.

Career

Arnold worked at Moscow State University and the Steklov Mathematical Institute, and later also at Paris-Dauphine University. He developed, with Kolmogorov and Jürgen Moser, the KAM theory of quasi-periodic motion in Hamiltonian systems, studied the geometry of integrable systems, founded much of modern singularity theory and symplectic topology, and posed the problems collected in his famous problem lists. He was a prolific and combative writer on mathematics education, famously critical of excessive abstraction in the Bourbaki style, and the author of widely used textbooks such as Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics and Ordinary Differential Equations.

Recognition

Arnold received the Lenin Prize in 1965, the Wolf Prize in Mathematics in 2001, and the Shaw Prize in 2008. He was widely regarded as one of the greatest mathematicians of the 20th century, though in his lifetime he was controversially never awarded the Fields Medal.
