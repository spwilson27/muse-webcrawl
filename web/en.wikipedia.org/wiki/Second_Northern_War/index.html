The Second Northern War (1655–1660), also known in Polish historiography as the Deluge (Polish: Potop), was fought between the Swedish Empire and its adversaries the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Tsardom of Russia, Brandenburg-Prussia, the Habsburg monarchy, and Denmark–Norway. It was part of the wider series of Northern Wars over control of the Baltic Sea.

Background

Sweden, dominant in the Baltic after the Thirty Years' War, sought to extend its position, while the Polish Vasa kings continued to claim the Swedish throne. In 1655 Charles X Gustav of Sweden invaded Poland-Lithuania, whose forces were already engaged against Russia and the Cossack uprising of Bohdan Khmelnytsky.

Course of the war

Swedish armies overran much of Poland in 1655, occupying Warsaw and Kraków, in a catastrophe remembered in Poland as the Deluge. Swedish brutality and looting provoked widespread resistance, symbolized by the successful defence of the Jasna Góra monastery at Częstochowa. King John II Casimir returned from exile and Polish forces recovered much of the country. Denmark entered the war against Sweden in 1657 but was defeated after Charles X Gustav marched his army across the frozen Danish straits in the winter of 1658, forcing the Treaty of Roskilde.

Settlement

The war ended with the Treaty of Oliva (1660) between Sweden, Poland, Brandenburg, and Austria; the Treaty of Copenhagen (1660) between Sweden and Denmark–Norway; and the Treaty of Cardis (1661) with Russia. Poland recognized Swedish sovereignty in Livonia and renounced claims to the Swedish crown, while Denmark's cessions of Scania, Halland, and Blekinge were largely confirmed. Brandenburg-Prussia secured full sovereignty over the Duchy of Prussia.
