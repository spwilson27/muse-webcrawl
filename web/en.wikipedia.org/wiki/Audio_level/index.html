Audio level

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Audio level is the strength of an audio signal, expressed relative to a reference and measured in decibels. Correct management of levels through a signal chain — gain staging — maximises signal-to-noise ratio while avoiding overload distortion. Audio levels are grouped by nominal strength: microphone level, instrument level, line level and speaker level.

Standard levels

  • Microphone level — the very low output of a microphone, typically −60 to −40 dBu, requiring a preamplifier.
  • Instrument level — the output of an electric guitar or bass, around −20 dBu.
  • Line level — the standard for interconnecting equipment: +4 dBu (1.228 V RMS) in professional systems, −10 dBV (0.316 V) in consumer equipment.
  • Speaker level — the high-voltage, low-impedance output of a power amplifier.

Measurement

Levels are indicated by meters with differing characteristics: the VU meter approximates perceived loudness with slow ballistics; the PPM (peak programme meter) responds quickly to transients; and digital systems measure sample peaks in dBFS, where 0 dBFS is the maximum representable level and any excess causes clipping. Modern broadcast practice additionally uses loudness metering in LUFS under standards such as EBU R 128 and ATSC A/85.

Alignment

Alignment level links the analogue and digital domains: in European broadcasting 0 dBu commonly corresponds to −18 dBFS, leaving headroom for peaks, while alignment tones (typically 1 kHz) are used to calibrate chains end to end.
