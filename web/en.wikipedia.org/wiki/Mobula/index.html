Mobula is a genus of rays in the family Mobulidae, found worldwide in tropical and warm temperate seas. Its members are commonly known as devil rays, flying mobulas or simply mobulas. Together with the closely related manta rays, they are the only members of their family. Mobulas resemble mantas but are generally smaller, with a disc width of up to about 3 to 3.5 metres in the largest species, and most species possess a tail spine.

Description

Mobulas have a flattened, diamond-shaped body with long, pointed pectoral fins that they flap like wings when swimming. On the head are two cephalic fins which, when rolled up, resemble horns — the origin of the name "devil ray". The mouth is located on the underside or at the front of the head depending on the species, and is flanked by gill slits used in filter feeding. Unlike manta rays, most mobulas retain a serrated caudal spine near the base of their long, whip-like tail.

Behaviour and ecology

Mobulas are filter feeders, straining zooplankton — especially krill and small schooling fishes — from the water. They are famous for spectacular leaping behaviour, launching themselves clear of the surface, sometimes in large aggregations in places such as the Sea of Cortez. The reason for breaching is not fully understood; proposed functions include communication, courtship, parasite removal and play. Many species form large seasonal aggregations and undertake long migrations following plankton blooms.

Species

Following taxonomic revisions that subsumed the former genus Manta into Mobula, the genus contains about eleven recognised species, including the reef manta ray (Mobula alfredi), the giant oceanic manta ray (M. birostris), the Chilean devil ray (M. tarapacana), the spinetail devil ray (M. japanica, often treated as M. mobular), the lesser devil ray (M. hypostoma), the pygmy devil rays (M. kuhlii, M. eregoodoo, M. munkiana), the Atlantic pygmy devil ray (M. rochebrunei) and the bentfin devil ray (M. thurstoni).

Conservation

Most mobula species are listed as endangered or vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. They are threatened by targeted fisheries for their gill plates, which are used in traditional medicine, and by bycatch in tuna fisheries. Their slow reproduction — typically a single pup after a long gestation — makes populations slow to recover. Mobulids are protected internationally under CITES Appendix II and the Convention on Migratory Species.
