Franco-Flemish School

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Franco-Flemish School — also called the Netherlands School, Burgundian School, or Flemish School, names varying by period — denotes the generations of composers from the Low Countries (northern France, present-day Belgium, and the southern Netherlands) who dominated European polyphonic music from about 1420 to the end of the 16th century. Trained in the choir schools of cathedrals such as Cambrai, Antwerp, and Bruges, these composers carried their art to courts and chapels across Europe, above all to Italy.

Generations

Music history conventionally divides the school into five generations:

  • First (c. 1420–1470) — the Burgundian generation of Guillaume Du Fay, Gilles Binchois, and Antoine Busnois, who fused English sweetness (contenance angloise) with French and Italian styles.
  • Second (c. 1470–1500) — Johannes Ockeghem, Jacob Obrecht, and Heinrich Isaac, masters of complex canon and the cyclic cantus-firmus mass.
  • Third (c. 1500–1520) — Josquin des Prez, the era's most celebrated composer, with Pierre de la Rue, Antoine Brumel, and Loyset Compère.
  • Fourth (c. 1520–1560) — Nicolas Gombert, Jacob Clemens non Papa, and Adrian Willaert, who took the style to Venice and founded the Venetian School; texture becomes pervasive imitation throughout all voices.
  • Fifth (c. 1560–1600) — Orlande de Lassus, Philippe de Monte, and Giaches de Wert, working in an international late-Renaissance idiom that gives way to the Baroque.

Style and influence

Franco-Flemish composers perfected imitative counterpoint, the cyclic mass, the motet, and the French chanson. The invention of music printing (Petrucci, 1501) spread their works everywhere; for roughly a century and a half, the "Netherlanders" set the technical standard against which all European art music was measured.
