In American politics, the Southern strategy was a Republican Party electoral strategy to increase political support among white voters in the South by appealing to racism against African Americans. It is widely credited with transforming the once solidly Democratic "Solid South" into a Republican stronghold over the later twentieth century.

Background and development

The Democratic Party had dominated the South since Reconstruction, but its mid-century embrace of civil rights — the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965, signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson — alienated many white Southerners. Johnson reportedly remarked that the Democrats had "lost the South for a generation". In 1964 Republican Barry Goldwater opposed the Civil Rights Act and carried five Deep South states; in 1968 George Wallace's third-party segregationist campaign and Richard Nixon's law-and-order "Southern strategy", articulated by strategist Kevin Phillips in The Emerging Republican Majority (1969), competed for those voters.

Mechanism and debate

Analysts describe increasingly coded appeals — "states' rights", opposition to busing, "law and order", and later welfare and crime rhetoric — replacing explicit segregationism. Republican strategist Lee Atwater's candid 1981 description of this evolution is often quoted in the debate. Scholars and politicians continue to argue over the strategy's weight relative to other causes of Southern realignment, such as economic change, suburbanisation and religious conservatism; the Republican Party formally apologised for exploiting racial polarisation in a 2005 speech by RNC chairman Ken Mehlman.
