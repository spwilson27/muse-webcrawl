Naga (mythology)

GroupingSerpent deities / mythical beings
RegionSouth and Southeast Asia
ReligionsHinduism, Buddhism, Jainism
AbodePatala; rivers, lakes and seas

In Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism, the Naga (Sanskrit: nāga) are a class of divine or semi-divine beings, half human and half serpent, who dwell in the underworld realm of Patala and in bodies of water. They are regarded as potent, handsome and potentially dangerous beings, and are associated with rain, fertility, treasure and the protection of waters.

In Hindu tradition

Hindu texts name many nagas, including Shesha, upon whose coils the god Vishnu rests; Vasuki, who served as the churning rope in the Samudra Manthan, the churning of the ocean of milk; and Takshaka, a king of serpents in the Mahabharata. The epic also describes the naga princess Ulupi, who married the hero Arjuna. Nagas are both revered and feared: they may guard treasure and grant boons, but their venom can be deadly, and myths tell of their longstanding enmity with the bird Garuda.

In Buddhism and Southeast Asia

In Buddhist legend the naga king Mucalinda sheltered the meditating Buddha from a storm by spreading his hoods over him. Naga imagery is pervasive in the art and architecture of Cambodia, Laos and Thailand: the seven-headed naga Muchalinda guards the causeway at Angkor Wat, and in Laos the nagas are believed to inhabit the Mekong River, where the phenomenon of the Naga fireballs is attributed to them. In Jainism, the tirthankara Parshvanatha is traditionally depicted sheltered by the many-headed naga Dharanendra.

Worship

The festival of Naga Panchami, celebrated across India and Nepal, is dedicated to the worship of snakes and nagas, with offerings of milk made at temples and snake burrows.
