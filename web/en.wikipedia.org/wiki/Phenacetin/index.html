Phenacetin

Phenacetin (acetophenetidin, N-(4-ethoxyphenyl)acetamide) is a pain-relieving and fever-reducing drug that was widely used from its introduction in 1887 until it was withdrawn in many countries in the late 20th century because of its link to kidney damage and cancer.

IUPAC nameN-(4-ethoxyphenyl)acetamide
FormulaC10H13NO2
Legal statusWithdrawn in many countries

History

Phenacetin was introduced in 1887 and became a common ingredient in headache and analgesic mixtures, often combined with aspirin and caffeine (the "APC" combination). It was one of the first synthetic antipyretics derived from aniline dyes.

Metabolism and toxicity

The body metabolizes phenacetin largely to paracetamol (acetaminophen), which accounts for most of its analgesic effect. Chronic heavy use was associated with analgesic nephropathy, a form of kidney damage, and with carcinogenic effects, leading regulators including the U.S. FDA to withdraw it in 1983.

Legacy

Phenacetin's safer metabolite, paracetamol, replaced it in most over-the-counter analgesics worldwide.
