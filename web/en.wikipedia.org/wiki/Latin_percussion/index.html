Latin percussion

Latin percussion refers to the family of percussion instruments and rhythmic practices used in Latin American music, Latin jazz, salsa, Afro-Cuban music, and related genres, as well as to the standard percussion setup found in modern dance bands and studios. Its instruments are largely of African and Afro-Cuban origin — descendants of drums and idiophones brought to the Americas during the slave trade — and its playing is organised around repeating rhythmic patterns called claves and cascara, over which other parts interlock. A "Latin percussionist" in a pop, rock, or jazz band typically covers congas, bongos, timbales, and a table of hand instruments, colouring the music of artists from Santana to modern pop productions.

A typical Latin percussion station: congas, bongos, timbales, cowbell, and shakers.

Principal instruments

  • Congas (tumbadoras): tall, single-headed hand drums, usually played in sets of two or three (quinto, conga, tumba).
  • Bongos: a pair of small single-headed drums joined together, held between the knees and played with the fingers; the player is the bongosero.
  • Timbales: shallow metal-shelled drums played with sticks, usually with a cowbell and woodblock mounted nearby; the timbalero also drives the band with shell patterns (cascara) and fills (abaneo).
  • Claves: two hardwood sticks struck together, playing the five-stroke clave pattern that is the rhythmic key of Afro-Cuban music.
  • Güiro and güira: scrapers — the ridged gourd güiro of Cuba and Puerto Rico and the metal güira of Dominican merengue.
  • Maracas and shakers: rattles used throughout the Caribbean and Brazil, including the cabasa and shekere.
  • Cowbells (cencerro): including the hand-held bongo bell and larger bells used in salsa.
  • Batá drums, cajón, surdo and other Brazilian instruments — used in Afro-Cuban religious music, Afro-Peruvian music, and samba respectively.

Rhythmic foundations

Afro-Cuban Latin percussion is organised by clave, a five-stroke pattern in two bars that exists in son and rumba forms and in 6/8 variants; every other instrument's part must align with its direction (2–3 or 3–2). Dance band rhythms such as the mambo, cha-cha-cha, bolero, and salsa each have standard patterns for congas (tumbao), bongos (martillo), and timbales. Brazilian styles, by contrast, are organised around surdo and caixa patterns in samba and the agogô bell in many Afro-Brazilian genres.

Notable players

Figures such as Mongo Santamaría, Tito Puente (the "King of Timbales"), Ray Barretto, Giovanni Hidalgo, and Poncho Sanchez carried Latin percussion into jazz and popular music, while percussionists like Alex Acuña and Luis Conte became fixtures of the Los Angeles studio scene. The instrument manufacturer Latin Percussion (LP), founded in 1964 by Martin Cohen to supply Cuban instruments unavailable during the US embargo, gave the field its best-known brand name.
