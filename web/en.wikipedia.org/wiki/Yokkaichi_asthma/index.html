Yokkaichi asthma (四日市ぜんそく, Yokkaichi zensoku) is a form of pollution-related respiratory disease that afflicted residents of Yokkaichi, a city in Mie Prefecture, Japan, beginning in the early 1960s. Caused by sulfur oxide emissions from the city's large petrochemical complexes, it is counted among the Four Big Pollution Diseases of Japan and became a landmark case in Japanese environmental law and policy.

Background

From the late 1950s, petrochemical plants were built on reclaimed land along Yokkaichi's port, burning high-sulfur crude oil and venting large quantities of sulfur dioxide and other pollutants. By 1960–1961 residents of nearby districts such as Isozu were complaining of foul air, dead fish, and a sharp rise in bronchial asthma, chronic bronchitis, and emphysema. Medical surveys documented asthma prevalence many times higher near the complexes than in control areas.

Litigation and response

In 1967 nine patients sued six companies of the Showa Yokkaichi Oil complex. In its 1972 judgment, the Tsu District Court found for the plaintiffs, holding the companies jointly liable without requiring proof of which individual plant caused each illness — a ruling that helped establish modern Japanese pollution law. The case, alongside Minamata disease and the other big pollution suits, spurred the creation of the Environment Agency in 1971, a national pollution-related health damage compensation system in 1973, and strict sulfur oxide controls; emissions from Yokkaichi's complexes subsequently fell dramatically and air quality improved.

Legacy

The city's experience is preserved in educational materials and a municipal museum devoted to the pollution and its lessons, and "Yokkaichi asthma" remains a standard term in Japanese medicine and environmental history.
