The area postrema is a paired structure in the medulla oblongata of the brainstem, located at the caudal floor of the fourth ventricle, around the obex. It is one of the circumventricular organs: its capillaries are fenestrated and it lacks a normal blood–brain barrier, allowing its neurons to sample chemicals circulating in the blood. Because of this role it is often called the "chemoreceptor trigger zone" for vomiting.

Function

The area postrema detects toxins, drugs, and hormonal signals in the bloodstream and cerebrospinal fluid that cannot cross the blood–brain barrier elsewhere. On detecting emetic substances — such as certain chemotherapy agents, apomorphine, or bacterial toxins — it signals the nucleus tractus solitarius and the dorsal vagal complex to initiate nausea and vomiting, a protective reflex that expels ingested poisons. Its neurons bear receptors for many circulating peptides and neurotransmitters, including dopamine D2 receptors (the target of several antiemetic drugs), serotonin 5-HT3 receptors, and receptors for gut hormones such as GLP-1 and amylin.

Other roles

Beyond emesis, the area postrema participates in cardiovascular regulation, responding to angiotensin II to influence blood pressure and thirst; in appetite and energy balance, through signals including amylin and GLP-1; and in conditioned taste aversion learning. It may also serve as a route by which immune signals reach the brain during sickness. Lesions of the area postrema impair toxin-induced vomiting while leaving motion sickness — mediated by the vestibular system — largely intact.

Clinical significance

Antiemetic therapy, especially for chemotherapy-induced nausea, is built largely around blocking area postrema signalling. In neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, autoantibodies against aquaporin-4, which is richly expressed in the area postrema, can produce intractable nausea, vomiting, and hiccups — the "area postrema syndrome", a recognized presenting feature of the disease.
