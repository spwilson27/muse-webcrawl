Platonic solids and Platonism

The five Platonic solids occupy a special place in Platonism because Plato himself used them, in the dialogue Timaeus, as the geometrical building blocks of the material world — the clearest ancient example of the Platonic conviction that reality is at bottom mathematical. The solids are the only convex polyhedra whose faces are congruent regular polygons meeting identically at every vertex: the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron.

The Timaeus cosmology

In the Timaeus (c. 360 BC), Plato's speaker describes the demiurge fashioning the cosmos according to eternal models, and assigns the four elements of Empedocles to four of the solids: fire to the sharp tetrahedron, earth to the stable cube, air to the octahedron, and water to the smooth-rolling icosahedron. The fifth solid, the dodecahedron, "the god used for the whole universe" — for embroidering the constellations on the heavens. Because the faces of the fire, air, and water solids are all equilateral triangles, Plato could describe the transmutation of elements as the rearrangement of basic triangles, an early atomism built from geometry rather than matter.

Why the solids exemplify Platonism

The solids are discovered, not invented, features of geometry: it is a theorem, already proved in the final book of Euclid's Elements, that exactly five such solids can exist. For Platonists, including many modern mathematical Platonists, this necessity is evidence that mathematical objects and truths belong to an eternal realm independent of human minds — the same realm of Forms to which Plato assigned the Beautiful and the Good. Kepler, a self-conscious heir of this tradition, built his first model of the solar system, the Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596), by nesting the five solids between the spheres of the six known planets, and even after abandoning the model he continued to seek geometrical harmonies in the heavens.

Duality and symmetry

The solids fall into dual pairs — cube with octahedron, dodecahedron with icosahedron, and the tetrahedron with itself — and their symmetry groups are among the fundamental finite symmetry groups of three-dimensional space. They recur in nature, from the cubic crystals of pyrite and the icosahedral shells of viruses to radiolarian skeletons, a recurrence that later writers in the Platonic tradition have taken to illustrate the world's mathematical intelligibility.
