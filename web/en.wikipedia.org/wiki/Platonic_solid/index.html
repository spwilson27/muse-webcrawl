Platonic solid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geometry, a Platonic solid is a convex, regular polyhedron in three-dimensional Euclidean space. Its faces are congruent regular polygons, with the same number of faces meeting at each vertex. Exactly five solids satisfy these conditions: the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron and icosahedron. They are named for the ancient Greek philosopher Plato, who associated each of the four classical elements with one of the solids in his dialogue Timaeus.

History

The solids were known to the ancient Greeks. In Plato's Timaeus (c. 360 BC), fire was assigned to the tetrahedron, earth to the cube, air to the octahedron and water to the icosahedron, while the dodecahedron was said to be used by the god for arranging the constellations. Euclid gave the first complete mathematical treatment in Book XIII of the Elements, proving that only five such solids exist. In 1596, Johannes Kepler proposed in Mysterium Cosmographicum that the distances of the six known planets could be modelled by nesting the five solids between their spheres.

Properties

Platonic solids possess a high degree of symmetry: their symmetry groups are the finite subgroups of rotations generated by reflections in the faces. Each solid has a dual obtained by interchanging faces and vertices — the cube and octahedron are duals, as are the dodecahedron and icosahedron, while the tetrahedron is self-dual. For every Platonic solid, the numbers of vertices V, edges E and faces F obey Euler's formula V − E + F = 2.

In nature and art

Platonic forms occur in crystals, in the radial skeletons of radiolaria, and in the protein shells (capsids) of many viruses, which are frequently icosahedral. The solids have also been used as dice and as motifs in art and architecture since the Renaissance.
