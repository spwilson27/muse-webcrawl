Polar stratospheric cloud

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) are clouds in the winter polar stratosphere at altitudes of 15,000–25,000 metres (49,000–82,000 ft). They are best observed during civil twilight, when the Sun is between 1° and 6° below the horizon, as well as in winter and at higher latitudes. One main type of PSC is made mainly of supercooled droplets of water and nitric acid and is implicated in the formation of the ozone hole. PSCs are sometimes called nacreous clouds (from nacre, mother of pearl) because of their striking iridescent colours.

Formation and types

PSCs form only at the extremely low temperatures of the polar winter stratosphere, roughly below −78 °C. They are classified into types:

  • Type I — contain nitric acid and water, as nitric acid trihydrate (NAT) particles or supercooled ternary solution droplets; form below about −78 °C.
  • Type II — consist of water ice only and require still colder temperatures, below about −85 °C; these are the bright, iridescent nacreous clouds.

The Arctic stratosphere is generally warmer than the Antarctic, so PSCs are rarer and shorter-lived in the north, and Arctic ozone losses are correspondingly smaller — though unusually cold Arctic winters, such as 2010–11 and 2019–20, have produced extensive PSCs and significant ozone depletion.

Effects on ozone

The surfaces of PSC particles provide sites for heterogeneous chemical reactions that convert stable chlorine reservoir species (HCl and ClONO2) into reactive forms (notably Cl2) that are photolysed when sunlight returns in spring, releasing chlorine radicals that catalytically destroy ozone. Sedimentation of NAT particles also removes nitrogen oxides from the stratosphere (denitrification), prolonging ozone destruction. These processes are the central chemical mechanism of the Antarctic ozone hole.
