Opposition (politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In politics, the opposition comprises one or more political parties or other political organisations that oppose, principally ideologically, the government (or, in American English, the administration) of a state, city, region or polity. A parliamentary opposition — in Westminster systems dignified as His Majesty's Loyal Opposition, led by a salaried Leader of the Opposition with a shadow cabinet mirroring ministers — holds the government to account through questions, debates, committees and the standing offer of an alternative government.

Role and types

The concept of "loyal opposition" — opposing the government while accepting the constitutional order — is a defining achievement of democratic politics, distinguishing it from anti-system opposition (which seeks the regime's overthrow) and from suppressed or exiled opposition under authoritarianism. Opposition parties scrutinise legislation, staff committees, and in presidential systems control chambers or governorships as platforms; their fragmentation or unity is a perennial strategic question, especially under plurality electoral rules. In one-party and dominant-party systems, debates concern whether meaningful opposition exists at all.
