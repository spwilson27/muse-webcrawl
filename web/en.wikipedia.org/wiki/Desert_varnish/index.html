Desert varnish or rock varnish is an orange-yellow to black coating found on exposed rock surfaces in arid environments. Desert varnish is usually around one micrometer thick and represents nanometre-scale layering. It forms over thousands of years from wind-blown clay dust cemented by manganese and iron oxides, with manganese-oxidising microorganisms thought to concentrate the dark manganese compounds.

Formation and composition

The coating consists of roughly 70% clay minerals, with the colour coming from oxides: iron oxides produce reddish hues and manganese oxides produce black. Because varnish grows extremely slowly (on the order of micrometres per thousand years) on stable surfaces, it can record environmental history and has been studied as a possible dating tool and even as an analogue for rock coatings observed on Mars.

Petroglyphs

Many ancient petroglyphs are created by pecking or scraping through desert varnish to reveal the lighter rock beneath — as at Newspaper Rock in Utah and in the Coso Range of California. The degree of re-varnishing of a carved surface is used, cautiously, to estimate a petroglyph's relative age.
