Group C

Group C was a category of motorsport, introduced by the FIA in 1982 along with Group A for touring cars and Group B for GTs, for sports-prototype racing cars. It governed the World Sportscar Championship from 1982 until its replacement in the early 1990s and is widely regarded as a golden era of endurance racing.

Regulations

Instead of limiting engine size directly, Group C restricted fuel consumption: cars were allocated a fixed amount of fuel per race distance (600 litres per 1,000 km in the original formula), pushing manufacturers toward efficient engine design while allowing enormous freedom — turbocharged and normally aspirated engines of many configurations competed. Cars had to be closed-wheel two-seat prototypes with minimum weights around 800–900 kg.

History

Porsche dominated the early years with the 956 and 962, challenged by Lancia, Jaguar, Mercedes-Benz (with Sauber), Nissan, Toyota and Mazda, whose rotary-engined 787B won the 24 Hours of Le Mans in 1991 — the only victory by a Japanese manufacturer and a rotary engine at Le Mans for decades. Costs and political disputes over a new 3.5-litre formula after 1991 caused manufacturer withdrawals, and the World Sportscar Championship was cancelled after 1992, ending the category.
