Gauss–Bonnet theorem

The Gauss–Bonnet theorem is a fundamental result in differential geometry linking the geometry of a surface — its curvature — to its topology, expressed by the Euler characteristic. For a compact two-dimensional Riemannian manifold M without boundary, it states that the integral of the Gaussian curvature K over the whole surface equals 2π times the Euler characteristic χ(M):

∬M K dA = 2π χ(M)

Examples

For a sphere of radius R, K = 1/R² everywhere and the total area is 4πR², so the integral is 4π, matching χ = 2. For a torus, regions of positive and negative curvature cancel, giving 0, and indeed χ(torus) = 0. For a closed orientable surface of genus g, the theorem yields 2π(2 − 2g): the more "holes" the surface has, the more negative its total curvature must be.

Surfaces with boundary

For a manifold with boundary, the formula adds the integral of the geodesic curvature of the boundary curve and the exterior angles at any corners: ∬ K dA + ∫ kg ds + Σ θi = 2π χ(M). Applied to a geodesic triangle, this recovers Girard's theorem: the angle sum of a spherical triangle exceeds π by exactly the area of the triangle (on a unit sphere).

History

Special cases were known to Harriot and Girard in the 17th century. Carl Friedrich Gauss understood a version for geodesic triangles around 1827 in connection with his theorema egregium, and Pierre Ossian Bonnet published the general form in 1848. The theorem was later extended to higher dimensions in the Chern–Gauss–Bonnet theorem by Shiing-Shen Chern in the 1940s, becoming a prototype for index theorems relating analysis, geometry and topology.

Significance

The theorem shows that total curvature is a topological invariant — a surface cannot be bent (without stretching) so as to change the integral of its Gaussian curvature. It is a cornerstone of modern geometry and an ancestor of the Atiyah–Singer index theorem.
