An odeon (Greek: ᾠδεῖον, ōideion, "singing place") was a building in the ancient Greek and Roman world used for musical performances, singing contests, poetry readings and rehearsals. Odeons resembled small theatres, with tiered semicircular seating and a stage building, but were distinguished by being roofed — usually with a timber roof — which improved acoustics for music and made them usable in all weather.

Terminology

The word derives from Greek ōidē, "song" or "ode". In Latin usage odeum denoted the same class of building. Because they were roofed and relatively small — typically seating hundreds to a few thousand rather than the tens of thousands of a great theatre — odeons were also used for council meetings and lectures in some cities, overlapping in function with the bouleuterion.

History

The first famous odeon was the Odeon of Pericles in Athens (c. 435 BC), built beside the Theatre of Dionysus at the foot of the Acropolis; ancient sources say its pyramidal roof was modelled on the tent of the Persian king Xerxes. The type flourished in the Hellenistic and especially the Roman imperial periods, when wealthy patrons funded odeons across the Mediterranean: the Odeon of Herodes Atticus in Athens (AD 161, about 5,000 seats) and odeons at Pompeii, Ephesus, Aphrodisias, Lyon and elsewhere.

Notable odeons

  • Odeon of Pericles, Athens — the archetype, used for musical contests of the Panathenaea.
  • Odeon of Agrippa, Athens (c. 15 BC) — a grand 1,000-seat hall in the Agora whose roof later collapsed and was rebuilt.
  • Odeon of Herodes Atticus, Athens — still used for concerts today.
  • Odeon of Pompeii — the small roofed theatre (theatrum tectum) beside the large theatre.
  • Odeon of Domitian, Rome — built under Domitian for musical competitions of the Capitoline Games.

Later uses of the name

The word survived into modern languages as a name for concert halls and, famously, cinemas: the British Odeon cinema chain (founded 1930 by Oscar Deutsch) adopted the name for its picture palaces, and many modern theatres and cinemas worldwide are called Odeon or Odéon — including Paris's Odéon-Théâtre de l'Europe, a dramatic theatre rather than a cinema.
