Complete bipartite graph

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a complete bipartite graph or biclique is a special kind of bipartite graph where every vertex of the first set is connected to every vertex of the second set.

Graph theory itself is typically dated starting in 1736 with Leonhard Euler's paper on the Seven Bridges of Königsberg. However, drawings of complete bipartite graphs were already printed as early as 1669, in connection with an edition of the works of Ramon Llull edited by Athanasius Kircher.

Definition

A complete bipartite graph is a graph whose vertices can be partitioned into two subsets V1 and V2 such that no edge has both endpoints in the same subset, and every possible edge that could connect vertices in different subsets is part of the graph. That is, it is a bipartite graph (V1, V2, E) such that for every two vertices v1 in V1 and v2 in V2, v1v2 is an edge in E. A complete bipartite graph with partitions of size |V1| = m and |V2| = n is denoted Km,n; it has mn edges and m + n vertices.

Examples

  • K1,n graphs are star graphs.
  • K3,3, the utility graph, is one of the two minimal non-planar graphs in Kuratowski's theorem; it arises from the classic "three utilities problem" of connecting three houses to three utilities without crossings.
  • Every complete graph Kn contains complete bipartite subgraphs such as K⌊n/2⌋,⌈n/2⌉.

Properties

Given a bipartite graph, testing whether it contains a complete bipartite subgraph Ki,i for a parameter i is an NP-complete problem. A planar graph cannot contain K3,3 as a minor; bipartite planar graphs cannot contain K3,3 as a subgraph and have at most 2n − 4 edges (for n ≥ 3). Complete bipartite graphs have symmetry groups factorizable in terms of the partition sizes, and their adjacency matrices have a simple block structure. The number of spanning trees of Km,n is mn−1 nm−1.

Uses

Complete bipartite subgraphs (bicliques) model relationships in many applications, including formal concept analysis, biclustering of gene-expression data, and the detection of dense substructures in recommendation systems and social networks.
