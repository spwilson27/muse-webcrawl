The 1906 San Francisco earthquake struck the coast of Northern California at 5:12 a.m. on Wednesday, 18 April 1906. The quake, with an estimated moment magnitude of 7.9, ruptured the northern segment of the San Andreas Fault. Devastating fires soon broke out across San Francisco and raged for days, destroying over 80 percent of the city. Long officially put at 498, the death toll is now estimated at more than 3,000, making it one of the deadliest natural disasters in United States history.

Earthquake

A foreshock was followed about 20 to 25 seconds later by the main shock, which lasted roughly 42 seconds. The rupture extended about 296 miles (476 km), from northwest of San Juan Bautista to the triple junction at Cape Mendocino. Horizontal displacement of up to 20 feet (6 m) was observed along the fault. Shaking was felt from Oregon to Los Angeles and inland as far as central Nevada.

Fires

Broken gas mains and damaged chimneys ignited fires that spread rapidly; with most water mains broken, firefighters were largely helpless. Some fires were set deliberately by property owners whose insurance covered fire but not earthquake damage. The blazes burned for four days and destroyed more than 500 city blocks and some 28,000 buildings.

Aftermath and rebuilding

More than half of the city's roughly 400,000 residents were left homeless and camped in parks and military tent cities. Reconstruction began almost immediately; the city was substantially rebuilt by the time it hosted the Panama–Pacific International Exposition in 1915. The disaster spurred scientific study of earthquakes, including H. F. Reid's elastic-rebound theory, and led to changes in building practices and fire insurance law.
