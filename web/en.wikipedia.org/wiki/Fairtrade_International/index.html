Fairtrade International, known until 2011 as Fairtrade Labelling Organizations International (FLO), is a non-profit multi-stakeholder association that develops and reviews international Fairtrade standards and supports Fairtrade-certified producers. Founded in 1997 and based in Bonn, Germany, it coordinates 19 national Fairtrade organisations (including Fairtrade America and the Fairtrade Foundation in the UK) and three continental producer networks representing farmers and workers in Africa and the Middle East, Asia and the Pacific, and Latin America and the Caribbean.

System

Fairtrade International sets the standards that define Fairtrade certification, covering economic, social, and environmental criteria. Its two main economic instruments are the Fairtrade Minimum Price, a floor price intended to cover the average costs of sustainable production for products such as coffee, cocoa, and bananas, and the Fairtrade Premium, an additional sum paid to cooperatives or worker committees to invest in community, business, or environmental projects. Certification audits are carried out by FLOCERT, an independent certification body accredited to ISO/IEC 17065, which was spun off from FLO in 2003.

Products meeting the standards may carry the FAIRTRADE Mark, a blue-and-green circular label depicting a figure with a raised arm, introduced in 2002 to harmonise the earlier labels of national initiatives such as Max Havelaar (Netherlands, 1988), TransFair, and the Fairtrade Foundation.

History

The fair trade movement grew from post-war alternative trade organisations and solidarity campaigns. The first Fairtrade-labelled product, Max Havelaar coffee from Mexican cooperatives, reached Dutch supermarkets in 1988. National labelling initiatives proliferated in the early 1990s, prompting their unification under FLO in 1997. In 2011, Fair Trade USA resigned from the international system to pursue its own "Fair Trade for All" strategy, a split that drew criticism from other members. In the same year the organisation adopted the name Fairtrade International and a governance reform giving producer networks 50% of votes in its general assembly.

Scale and impact

The system covers products including coffee, cocoa, bananas, tea, sugar, cotton, flowers, and gold, produced by well over a million farmers and workers in more than 70 countries, with retail sales of Fairtrade products running to billions of euros annually. Research assessments of impact are mixed: studies find modest income and stability benefits and stronger cooperative organisation, while critics argue premiums are small relative to retail value, that certification costs burden smallholders, and that plantation wage workers benefit least.
