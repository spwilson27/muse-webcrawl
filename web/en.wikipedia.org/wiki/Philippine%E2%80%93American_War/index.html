The Philippine–American War was an armed conflict between the First Philippine Republic and the United States that lasted from February 1899 to July 1902. It began after the United States, having taken the Philippines from Spain under the Treaty of Paris (1898), refused to recognise the Philippine declaration of independence led by Emilio Aguinaldo.

Course of the war

Conventional fighting in 1899 favoured the better-armed American forces, and after the fall of the republic's capital the Filipino army shifted to guerrilla warfare. Aguinaldo was captured in March 1901 and soon called for an end to resistance. The war was marked by harsh counterinsurgency measures, including the use of concentration zones, and by atrocities on both sides that provoked a strong anti-imperialist movement in the United States.

Casualties and legacy

Estimates suggest around 4,200 American soldiers and about 20,000 Filipino combatants were killed, while civilian deaths from violence, famine, and disease numbered at least 200,000. President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war ended on 4 July 1902, though resistance, particularly in the Moro Province, continued for years. The conflict began nearly half a century of American colonial rule over the Philippines.
