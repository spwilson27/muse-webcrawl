Substance theory

Substance theory, or the substance–attribute theory, is an ontological theory in metaphysics holding that objects consist of a substance (or substratum) in which properties (attributes, accidents) inhere. The substance is the bearer of properties: it is what a thing fundamentally is, persisting through change while its properties may vary.

History

The theory originates with Aristotle, whose Categories distinguishes primary substances — individual things such as a particular horse or person — from the qualities, quantities, and relations predicated of them. Aristotle's related doctrine of hylomorphism analyzes substances as compounds of matter and form.

In early modern philosophy, Descartes distinguished thinking substance (res cogitans) from extended substance (res extensa), while Spinoza argued there is only one substance — God or Nature — of which minds and bodies are attributes. Locke described substance as an obscure "something, I know not what" that supports observable qualities. Critics such as Berkeley and Hume attacked the notion of an unknowable substratum, and bundle theory emerged as the chief rival: objects are nothing over and above bundles of co-present properties, with no underlying bearer.

Contemporary debates

Modern metaphysicians continue to debate whether properties require bearers. Defenders of substratum theories argue that bundle theories cannot adequately explain individuation — why two objects could not share all their properties — or the persistence of objects through change. Opponents reply that positing a featureless bearer explains nothing. Variants include "thin particulars," sortal essentialism, and neo-Aristotelian theories of substances as integrated wholes of matter and form.
