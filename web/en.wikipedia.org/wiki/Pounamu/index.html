Pounamu

Pounamu is a collective term for several types of hard and durable stone found in southern New Zealand. The principal varieties are nephrite (a form of jade), bowenite, and serpentinite. Pounamu — often called "greenstone" in New Zealand English — is of great cultural significance to the Māori people, who have used it for tools, weapons and ornaments for centuries. The Ngāi Tahu iwi are the legal guardians (kaitiaki) of pounamu resources.

Geology and sources

Pounamu occurs mainly on the west coast of the South Island, in the catchments of rivers such as the Arahura and Taramakau in Westland, and around Lake Wakatipu and Fiordland. It forms in metamorphic zones where ultramafic rocks have been altered, and is found as boulders and pebbles in riverbeds and beaches after erosion from its source outcrops.

Varieties

Māori classifications recognise several types by colour and pattern, including:

  • Inanga — pale, pearly grey-green
  • Kahurangi — rich green and highly translucent, the most prized
  • Kawakawa — deep green with dark flecks
  • Tangiwai — bowenite, translucent olive-green stone from Milford Sound
  • Kokopu — speckled, trout-like patterning

Cultural significance

Pounamu is a taonga (treasure) in Māori culture. Its toughness made it ideal for adzes (toki), chisels, and weapons such as the short flat club or mere. Carved ornaments include the hei tiki pendant, ear pendants (kuru and kapeu), and the curled koru and hook (hei matau) forms. Pounamu taonga are passed down through generations, accumulate personal history and mana, and are traditionally given as gifts rather than bought for oneself. Under the Ngāi Tahu (Pounamu Vesting) Act 1997, ownership of pounamu in the Ngāi Tahu tribal area was vested in the iwi.
