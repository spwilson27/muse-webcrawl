Dollar auction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The dollar auction is a two-player sequential game devised by economist Martin Shubik in 1971 to illustrate a paradox in game theory and escalation. An auctioneer offers a one-dollar bill for sale. The highest bidder wins the dollar, but the second-highest bidder must also pay the amount of their final bid while receiving nothing. This rule turns an apparently trivial auction into a trap: rational bidding can spiral far beyond the value of the prize.

Rules

Bidding begins at a low amount, such as one cent, and proceeds in small increments. The leading bidder stands to win the dollar for less than its face value, but whenever they are outbid, they face a loss equal to their last bid unless they bid again. Each participant therefore has an incentive to continue bidding to avoid being the second-highest bidder, even after the bids surpass one dollar.

Analysis

Shubik presented the game as a model of escalation of commitment: once resources have been sunk into a contest, it can seem individually rational at every step to continue, although all parties would be better off stopping. In classroom demonstrations, dollar bills have sold for several dollars, and reported auctions have reached tens of dollars. The game is related to the war of attrition and to all-pay auctions, in which every bidder pays.

Applications

The dollar auction is used to teach negotiation, military escalation, business competition, and the sunk cost fallacy. Analogies have been drawn to arms races, bidding wars for companies, and litigation, where parties continue spending to avoid losing what they have already spent.
