Retinal vein occlusion

Retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is a blockage of one of the veins that drain blood from the retina, causing blood and fluid to leak into the retinal tissue. It is the second most common retinal vascular disease after diabetic retinopathy and a significant cause of vision loss, particularly in older adults. Depending on the site of blockage it is classified as central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO), affecting the main vein at the optic nerve, or branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO), affecting one branch, typically where an artery crosses and compresses the vein.

Presentation and diagnosis

Patients typically notice painless blurring or loss of vision in one eye. Examination shows dilated, tortuous veins with flame-shaped and dot haemorrhages across the retina (in CRVO, the dramatic "blood and thunder" appearance), cotton wool spots and swelling of the macula (macular oedema), the commonest cause of visual loss. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) quantifies macular swelling and monitors treatment.

Complications and treatment

Severe, ischaemic occlusions can trigger growth of abnormal new vessels (neovascularisation), leading to vitreous haemorrhage or painful neovascular glaucoma. First-line treatment for macular oedema is repeated intravitreal injections of anti-VEGF agents such as ranibizumab and aflibercept, with corticosteroid implants as an alternative; laser is used for neovascular complications. Managing systemic risk factors — blood pressure, diabetes and lipids — reduces risk to the fellow eye.
