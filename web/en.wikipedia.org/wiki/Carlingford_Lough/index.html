Carlingford Lough (Irish: Loch Cairlinn) is a glacial fjord or sea inlet in northeastern Ireland, on the border between Northern Ireland to the north and the Republic of Ireland to the south. The lough forms the seaward end of the border, with County Down on its northern shore and the Cooley Peninsula in County Louth on its southern shore.

The lough opens into the Irish Sea and is fed principally by the Newry River (also known as the Clanrye River), which enters at its head near Warrenpoint and Newry. On the northern shore lie the towns of Warrenpoint and Rostrevor at the foot of the Mourne Mountains; on the southern shore are Omeath, Carlingford, and Greenore.

Geography

Carlingford Lough is a classic glacially scoured inlet flanked by mountains: the Mourne Mountains rise steeply to the north, while the Cooley Mountains — including Slieve Foy — overlook the southern shore. A narrow entrance, guarded by Haulbowline Lighthouse (built in 1824), leads from the open sea into the broader inner lough. The lough contains several small islands and extensive mudflats and saltmarsh at low tide.

History

The name derives from the Old Norse Kerlingfjǫrðr, meaning "the narrow sea-inlet of the hag" or "old woman", evidence of Viking settlement in the area. The Normans built Carlingford Castle (King John's Castle) at the mouth of the lough around the end of the 12th century. The lough's sheltered waters made its ports historically important for trade; Greenore, on the southern shore, was developed in the 19th century as a railway port with ferry links to Britain, and Warrenpoint remains a working commercial port.

Nature and designation

The lough is designated a Ramsar site and an Area of Special Scientific Interest, noted for its intertidal habitats and for wintering wildfowl, including internationally important numbers of pale-bellied brent geese. It is also known for oyster and mussel fisheries, and Carlingford oysters are a celebrated local product.
