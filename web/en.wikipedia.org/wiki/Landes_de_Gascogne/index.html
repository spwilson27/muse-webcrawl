The Landes de Gascogne (in Gascon, las Lanas de Gasconha) is a natural region of south-western France, occupying a vast sandy plain between the Gironde estuary, the Atlantic coast and the Adour river. Once a sparsely populated landscape of moorland, dunes, marshes and sheep pasture, it is today covered by the Landes forest — at around one million hectares the largest man-made maritime-pine forest in Western Europe.

History

Until the nineteenth century the inland Landes were wet heathlands grazed by large flocks of sheep, tended by shepherds who famously walked on stilts (échasses) to cross the sodden ground. Dunes advancing from the coast threatened settlements until they were fixed with pine plantings, notably by Nicolas Brémontier from the late eighteenth century.

Under the law of 19 June 1857, communes were required to drain and plant their moorlands with maritime pine. Drainage canals and mass planting transformed the region within a generation, creating an economy based on timber, resin tapping (gemmage), turpentine and paper pulp. The resin industry declined in the later twentieth century, and forestry reoriented toward timber and biomass; severe storms in 1999 (Martin) and 2009 (Klaus) each felled tens of millions of trees, and major wildfires struck the forest in the summer of 2022.

Geography

The region's flat, acidic sands rest over an impermeable clay layer, producing winter waterlogging and summer drought. The coastal strip is lined with dunes, including the Dune of Pilat, the tallest in Europe, and a chain of lakes such as Lacanau, Hourtin-Carcans and Biscarrosse.

Regional park

Part of the area is protected as the Landes de Gascogne Regional Natural Park, founded in 1970, which preserves remnants of the old agro-pastoral moorland, airials (oak-shaded farm clearings) and traditional timber-framed houses.
