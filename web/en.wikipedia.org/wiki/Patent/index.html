Patent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A patent is a type of intellectual property that gives its owner the legal right to exclude others from making, using or selling an invention for a limited period of time in exchange for publishing an enabling disclosure of the invention. The standard term under the TRIPS Agreement is 20 years from filing. Patents are granted by national or regional patent offices after examination (in most systems) for novelty, inventive step/non-obviousness and industrial applicability/utility, and they are territorial — a patent in one country confers no rights elsewhere.

Operation and debates

The patent bargain — disclosure for temporary exclusivity — is intended to spur innovation and knowledge diffusion. Litigation over infringement and validity is expensive and specialised; doctrines cover claim construction, equivalents, exhaustion and remedies (injunctions vs. royalties). Policy debates are intense: pharmaceutical patents vs. access to medicines (compulsory licensing, the 2001 Doha Declaration), "patent trolls" (non-practising entities), software and business-method eligibility, standard-essential patents in telecommunications, and whether patents in cumulative technologies hinder follow-on innovation. Historical systems ranged from Venice's 1474 statute to today's global filing regime under the Patent Cooperation Treaty.
