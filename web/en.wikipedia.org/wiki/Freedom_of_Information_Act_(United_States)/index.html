The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 U.S.C. § 552, is the United States federal law that grants the public the right to request access to records from any federal agency. It is often described as the law that keeps citizens in the know about their government. Federal agencies are required to disclose any information requested under the FOIA unless it falls under one of nine exemptions which protect interests such as personal privacy, national security, and law enforcement.

The Act applies only to executive-branch agencies; Congress, the courts and private companies are not covered. Any person — not only U.S. citizens — may request records, and agencies must generally respond within 20 business days, though backlogs of months or years are common for complex requests. Denials can be appealed administratively and then challenged in federal court, where the burden is on the agency to justify withholding.

History

FOIA replaced the vague disclosure provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act of 1946. Congressman John Moss championed the law for a decade before its passage in 1966; President Lyndon B. Johnson signed it, with reluctance, on 4 July 1966. The Act was significantly strengthened in 1974, in the wake of Watergate, over President Gerald Ford's veto, adding deadlines and sanctions. Later amendments include the Electronic FOIA Amendments of 1996 (covering electronic records), the OPEN Government Act of 2007 (creating the Office of Government Information Services ombudsman) and the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016, which codified a "presumption of openness" and the requirement that agencies show foreseeable harm before withholding.

Exemptions

The nine exemptions cover: (1) classified national-security material; (2) internal personnel rules; (3) information exempted by other statutes; (4) trade secrets and confidential commercial information; (5) deliberative inter-agency memoranda; (6) personal privacy; (7) law-enforcement records; (8) financial-institution supervision; and (9) geological data about wells. Released documents are often heavily redacted ("blacked out") under these provisions.

Use

Federal agencies receive over 800,000 FOIA requests a year. Journalists, researchers, businesses and citizens use FOIA to obtain everything from FBI files and expense records to scientific data. Landmark reporting based on FOIA material includes disclosures about surveillance programmes, government contracts and historical events.
