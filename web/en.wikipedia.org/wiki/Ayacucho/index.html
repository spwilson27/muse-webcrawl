Ayacucho (Quechua: Ayak'uchu) is a city in south-central Peru, capital of the Ayacucho Region and of Huamanga Province. It sits on a high plain of the eastern Andes at about 2,761 metres above sea level. The city is renowned for its 33 colonial churches — traditionally said to be one for each year of Jesus Christ's life — and for its central role in South American independence: the Battle of Ayacucho, fought on 9 December 1824 on the nearby Pampa de Quinua, sealed the defeat of Spanish royalist forces on the continent.

History

The area shows very ancient occupation: the Pikimachay cave near the city yielded artefacts dated to roughly 12,000 BC, among the earliest evidence of humans in South America. The region was later the heartland of the Wari (Huari) Empire (c. 500–1000 AD), the first pan-Andean state, whose capital lies about 25 km northeast. Ayacucho was incorporated into the Inca Empire in the 15th century.

Francisco Pizarro founded the Spanish city as San Juan de la Frontera de Huamanga in 1540. In 1677 the Universidad Nacional de San Cristóbal de Huamanga was founded, one of Peru's oldest universities. The city was renamed Ayacucho in 1825 by Simón Bolívar, commemorating the decisive battle; aya k'uchu is usually glossed as "corner of the dead" or "soul's corner" in Quechua, a reference to the battle's casualties.

The Shining Path era

Ayacucho was the birthplace of the Maoist insurgency of Sendero Luminoso (Shining Path), launched in 1980 by Abimael Guzmán, a philosophy professor at the city's university. The internal conflict devastated the region through the 1980s and early 1990s; Ayacucho was among the departments most affected by violence and displacement. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2003) documented extensive abuses by both insurgents and state forces.

Culture

The city is famous for Semana Santa (Holy Week) celebrations, among the most elaborate in Peru, and for its crafts: retablos (portable altar boxes), alabaster-like Huamanga stone carvings, pottery from Quinua, and textiles. Morochucos horsemen of the surrounding pampas are celebrated in local tradition.
