King Abdulaziz, founder and first king of Saudi Arabia.

Abdulaziz bin Abdul Rahman Al Saud (15 January 1875 – 9 November 1953), known in the West as Ibn Saud, was the founder and first king of Saudi Arabia, the third Saudi state. Over three decades of conquest and diplomacy he reunified most of the Arabian Peninsula under the rule of the House of Saud, transforming a patchwork of emirates and tribal territories into a single kingdom, proclaimed on 23 September 1932. Every subsequent Saudi monarch has been his son or grandson.

Early life and exile

Abdulaziz was born in Riyadh, then capital of the second Saudi state (Emirate of Nejd). In 1891 the rival Al Rashid of Ha'il defeated the Saudis, and the family went into exile, eventually settling in Kuwait under the protection of Sheikh Mubarak Al Sabah. Abdulaziz spent his youth in the desert and in Kuwaiti politics, learning warfare, negotiation, and the intricate tribal diplomacy of Arabia.

Reconquest of Arabia

In January 1902, with a small raiding party, Abdulaziz scaled the walls of Riyadh at night and killed the Rashidi governor — a legendary feat that restored Saudi rule in Nejd. There followed twenty years of campaigning, aided at times by the Ikhwan, zealous Bedouin religious warriors settled in agricultural colonies. He took al-Hasa from the Ottomans in 1913, defeated the Rashidis and annexed Jabal Shammar in 1921, and in 1924–25 conquered the Hejaz, ending Hashemite rule over Mecca and Medina. When the Ikhwan revolted against his attempts to restrain their raiding, he crushed them at the Battle of Sabilla in 1929. In 1932 the Kingdom of Hejaz and Nejd was renamed the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

Reign as king

As king, Abdulaziz centralised a poor desert state, governed through personal and tribal relationships, and kept Arabia neutral during the Second World War while cultivating the United States: in 1945 he met President Franklin D. Roosevelt aboard USS Quincy in the Great Bitter Lake, a meeting that founded the long US–Saudi partnership. Commercial oil was discovered in 1938 at Dammam No. 7, and oil revenues, which began flowing on a large scale after the war, started the transformation of the kingdom. Abdulaziz fathered dozens of sons — over 40 by most counts — whose descendants still form the core of the Saudi royal family. He died at Taif in 1953 and was succeeded by his eldest surviving son, Saud.
