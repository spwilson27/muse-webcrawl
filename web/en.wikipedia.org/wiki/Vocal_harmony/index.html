Vocal harmony

Vocal harmony is a style of vocal music in which a consonant note or notes are simultaneously sung as a main melody in a predominantly homophonic texture. Vocal harmonies are used in many subgenres of popular music, including doo-wop, barbershop music, folk, country, and rock, as well as in choral and sacred traditions.

Description

A harmony singer provides a part that complements the lead melody, typically a third or sixth above or below it, moving in parallel or oblique motion to form chords. Close harmony keeps the parts within a narrow range — a hallmark of barbershop quartets and groups such as the Andrews Sisters — while open harmony spreads the voices wider. Backing vocals may sing sustained "oohs" and "aahs," echo phrases in call and response, or join the lead in unison at climactic moments.

History

Polyphonic singing is ancient, from medieval organum and Renaissance motets to the shape-note hymnody and gospel quartets of the American South. In the 20th century, close-harmony groups carried the practice into popular music: the Mills Brothers, the Ink Spots, girl groups of the 1960s, and the lush multi-tracked harmonies of the Beach Boys and Queen. In recording studios, a single singer may overdub multiple harmony parts, as Freddie Mercury and Brian May did extensively, or a vocalist may harmonize with themselves live using harmonizer effects.

Notable practitioners

Groups celebrated for vocal harmony include the Everly Brothers, Simon & Garfunkel, Crosby, Stills & Nash, ABBA, the Bee Gees, Boyz II Men, and Fleet Foxes. Barbershop and a cappella traditions sustain competitive harmony singing worldwide.
