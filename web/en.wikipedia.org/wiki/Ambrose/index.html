Ambrose of Milan (Latin: Aurelius Ambrosius; c. 340 – 4 April 397), venerated as Saint Ambrose, was a bishop of Milan who became one of the most influential ecclesiastical figures of the 4th century. He was a statesman, a Doctor of the Church, and the mentor of Augustine of Hippo. He is counted among the four original Latin Doctors of the Church.

Life

Born into a Roman Christian family, Ambrose was serving as governor of Liguria and Emilia, headquartered in Milan, when in 374 he was unexpectedly acclaimed bishop by the people of Milan — though he was still an unbaptised catechumen. He was baptised, ordained, and consecrated within days.

As bishop, Ambrose defended Nicene orthodoxy against Arianism, resisted imperial interference in church affairs, and famously compelled Emperor Theodosius I to do public penance after the massacre at Thessalonica in 390.

Works and legacy

Ambrose wrote extensively on theology and ethics, including De officiis and De fide. He promoted congregational chant, giving his name to Ambrosian chant, and composed hymns, four of which are considered authentically his. His preaching and example were central to Augustine's conversion, and he baptised Augustine at Milan in 387.
