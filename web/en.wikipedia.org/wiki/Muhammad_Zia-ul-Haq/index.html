Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq (12 August 1924 – 17 August 1988) was a Pakistani four-star general who seized power in a military coup on 5 July 1977, deposing Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto, and ruled Pakistan for eleven years — as Chief Martial Law Administrator and, from 1978, as President — until his death in a plane crash. His tenure, the longest of any Pakistani head of state, was defined by martial law, an aggressive programme of Islamisation, and Pakistan's front-line role in supporting the Afghan mujahideen during the Soviet–Afghan War.

Rule

Zia had Bhutto tried and, in 1979, executed on a murder-conspiracy conviction that remains fiercely controversial in Pakistan. Under the slogan of Nizam-e-Mustafa ("the system of the Prophet"), he introduced Hudood ordinances prescribing Islamic punishments, zakat and ushr taxes, Islamic courts, and measures restricting the rights of women and of the Ahmadiyya community, reshaping Pakistani law and society for decades. Elections were repeatedly postponed; a non-party referendum in 1984 and non-party elections in 1985 produced a civilian government under Muhammad Khan Junejo, whom Zia dismissed in May 1988.

Abroad, Zia aligned Pakistan closely with the United States and Saudi Arabia after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979, channelling money and weapons through the ISI to the mujahideen, while millions of Afghan refugees settled in Pakistan. The war years also saw the growth of Pakistan's covert nuclear weapons programme and a "Kalashnikov culture" of arms and heroin at home.

Death

On 17 August 1988, Zia's C-130 transport crashed shortly after take-off from Bahawalpur, killing him, the US ambassador Arnold Raphel, senior officers and the crew of thirty in all. The cause was never conclusively established; sabotage has been widely suspected. He was buried at the Faisal Mosque in Islamabad, and his death opened the way to the restoration of parliamentary democracy under Benazir Bhutto.
