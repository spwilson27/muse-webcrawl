Nitrile

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The nitrile functional group, –C≡N

In organic chemistry, a nitrile is any compound that has a –C≡N functional group — a carbon atom triple-bonded to a nitrogen atom, with the carbon also bonded to the rest of the molecule (R–C≡N). Nitriles are named either as alkyl cyanides (e.g., methyl cyanide) or with the suffix -nitrile (e.g., acetonitrile, benzonitrile). The C≡N unit is linear, strongly polarized, and isoelectronic with carbon monoxide and dinitrogen.

Occurrence and production

Nitriles occur naturally in plant products, including cyanogenic glycosides and the kernels of stone fruits, and in some animal secretions. Industrially, the most important nitriles are acetonitrile (a widely used solvent, largely a by-product of acrylonitrile manufacture), acrylonitrile (made by ammoxidation of propene and used for acrylic fibres, ABS resin, and nitrile rubber), and adiponitrile (hydrogenated to hexamethylenediamine for nylon-6,6). Laboratory syntheses include dehydration of amides, the Sandmeyer and Kolbe nitrile reactions, and cyanide displacement of halides.

Reactions

The nitrile group is a versatile intermediate. Hydrolysis under acidic or basic conditions passes through the amide to give carboxylic acids; catalytic hydrogenation or hydride reduction yields primary amines; Grignard and organolithium reagents add to give ketones after workup. The α-hydrogens of nitriles are acidic enough to form carbanions used in C–C bond-forming reactions such as the Thorpe condensation.

Uses

Besides solvents and polymer feedstocks, nitrile groups appear in many pharmaceuticals (for example the aromatase inhibitor letrozole and the antidepressant citalopram) and agrochemicals. "Nitrile" also denotes nitrile rubber (NBR) and the disposable nitrile gloves made from it, valued for chemical resistance and as a latex alternative in medicine.
