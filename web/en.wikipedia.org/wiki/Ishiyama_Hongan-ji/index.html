Ishiyama Hongan-ji (石山本願寺) was the primary fortress of the Ikkō-ikki, leagues of warrior monks and commoners who opposed samurai rule during the Sengoku period of Japan. It was established by the Jōdo Shinshū leader Rennyo in 1496, on the site of the ancient Naniwa Imperial Palace, in what is now the city of Osaka.

History

Rennyo originally established a retreat temple beside the tomb of Prince Shōtoku at Kyōgoku. In 1496 he moved to Osaka and built a temple on easily defensible high ground where the Yodo River met Osaka Bay. The settlement that grew around it, called a jinaimachi (temple town), attracted merchants and craftsmen, and the complex was ringed by moats and earthworks, making it effectively a fortified city.

Under the leadership of the Hongan-ji abbots, the Ikkō-ikki of Osaka became the hub of a network of leagues across central Japan. The temple withstood attacks by the forces of Hosokawa and others, and in the 1570s it became the last major obstacle to Oda Nobunaga's unification of the country.

Siege by Oda Nobunaga

The siege of Ishiyama Hongan-ji, which began in 1570 and included the naval battles of Kizugawaguchi, was among the longest sieges in Japanese history, lasting eleven years. The temple was resupplied by sea by the Mōri clan and defended by thousands of believers and by mercenary groups such as the Saika-shū under Suzuki Magoichi. In 1580 the abbot Kennyo negotiated a surrender mediated by the Imperial Court; the fortress was evacuated and burned, and Nobunaga's successor, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, later built Osaka Castle on the site.

Legacy

The fall of Ishiyama Hongan-ji ended the era of the great militant temple leagues and was a decisive step in the unification of Japan. No trace of the temple survives above ground, though excavations in the Osaka Castle park have uncovered stone walls and other remains.
