Fortress Koblenz

Fortress Koblenz (German: Festung Koblenz) was a system of fortifications built around Koblenz in present-day Rhineland-Palatinate, Germany, after the Congress of Vienna assigned the Rhineland to Prussia. Constructed mainly between 1815 and the 1830s and repeatedly modernised, it was one of the largest fortress systems in 19th-century Europe, guarding the confluence of the Rhine and the Moselle. Its best-known surviving component is the Ehrenbreitstein Fortress on the east bank of the Rhine.

Components

The system combined a city enceinte with detached forts on the surrounding heights, so that an attacker could not bombard the city from commanding ground:

  • Ehrenbreitstein Fortress — rebuilt by Prussia between 1817 and 1828 on the rock 118 metres above the Rhine, opposite the Deutsches Eck; considered impregnable and never taken by assault.
  • Fort Großfürst Konstantin — on the Karthause heights south of the old town, built in the 1820s.
  • Feste Kaiser Franz — in Lützel, north across the Moselle, completed in the 1820s.
  • Feste Kaiser Alexander and other works on the Karthause and the left bank, with gates, redoubts, and the city wall ring.

History

Prussia built Koblenz into a first-class federal fortress of the German Confederation, garrisoned by thousands of troops. The defences were updated as artillery improved, with forts pushed further out in the 1860s–1880s. After the First World War, the Treaty of Versailles required demilitarisation of the Rhineland, and most of the Koblenz works were demolished in the 1920s under Allied supervision; Ehrenbreitstein survived in part because it was retained for occupation forces and later uses. Ehrenbreitstein today houses the Koblenz State Museum (Landesmuseum Koblenz), a youth hostel, and exhibition spaces, and is reached by a cable car over the Rhine built for the 2011 Federal Garden Show.
