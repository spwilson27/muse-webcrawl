Cell membrane

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The cell membrane (also plasma membrane) is the biological membrane that separates the interior of a cell from its environment. It consists mainly of a double layer of phospholipids with embedded proteins, and it controls the movement of substances in and out of the cell, receives signals, and helps cells adhere to one another and to their surroundings.

Structure

The prevailing description is the fluid mosaic model (Singer and Nicolson, 1972): a fluid phospholipid bilayer in which proteins float. Phospholipids are amphipathic — hydrophilic heads face the water on both sides, hydrophobic tails point inward. Cholesterol, present in animal membranes, modulates fluidity. Membrane proteins serve as channels, pumps, receptors, enzymes and adhesion molecules; carbohydrate chains on surface proteins and lipids form the glycocalyx used in cell recognition.

Transport

Small nonpolar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse across freely. Water crosses by osmosis, aided by aquaporin channels. Ions and polar molecules need channel or carrier proteins (facilitated diffusion), and pumping substances against gradients costs energy — active transport, powered by ATP, as in the sodium–potassium pump. Bulk material moves by endocytosis and exocytosis.

Functions

Beyond barrier and transport roles, the membrane houses receptor proteins for signalling, generates electrical potentials in neurons and muscle, and in bacteria carries out respiration and Photosynthesis chemistry that eukaryotes delegate to organelles.
