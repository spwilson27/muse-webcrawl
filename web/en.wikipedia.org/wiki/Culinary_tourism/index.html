Culinary tourism, also known as food tourism or gastronomic tourism, is travel undertaken wholly or partly for the purpose of experiencing the food and drink of a place. It includes activities such as visiting restaurants and street-food stalls, touring markets and farms, attending food festivals, taking cooking classes and following wine or beer trails.

Overview

Food has long been part of travel, but from the early 2000s it became a primary motivation for a growing segment of travellers, sometimes called "foodies". Industry groups such as the World Food Travel Association (founded 2003) promote the sector, and destinations increasingly market signature dishes and regional cuisines as part of their identity. Researchers note that food experiences are among the most memorable elements of a trip and a major share of tourist spending.

Forms

  • Restaurant tourism — travel centred on celebrated restaurants, including pilgrimages to Michelin-starred establishments.
  • Street food and markets — exploring hawker centres, night markets and farmers' markets, prominent in cities such as Bangkok, Singapore, Mexico City and Istanbul.
  • Cooking classes and food tours — guided experiences combining market visits with hands-on preparation of local dishes.
  • Drink tourism — wine tourism in regions such as Bordeaux, Napa Valley and Tuscany; whisky trails in Scotland; beer tourism in Belgium and Bavaria.
  • Festivals and events — harvest festivals, truffle fairs and events such as Oktoberfest.

Impact

Culinary tourism can support local producers, preserve traditional foodways and spread tourist spending beyond major attractions. Critics point to risks of gentrification of food districts, rising prices for residents, and the standardisation of "authentic" experiences for visitors. UNESCO's Creative Cities of Gastronomy network, launched in 2004, recognises cities for their culinary heritage.
