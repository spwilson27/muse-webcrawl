Tenor

A tenor is a type of classical male singing voice whose vocal range lies between the countertenor and the baritone. The tenor's vocal range extends up to C5, with the standard operatic tenor range generally considered to be from C3 to C5 (the "tenor C"). In choral music, the tenor is the highest of the four standard voice parts, situated above the alto and below the soprano in terms of the overall ensemble, and its range in four-part writing typically runs from B2 to A4.

Etymology

The word tenor comes from the Latin tenere, meaning "to hold". In medieval and Renaissance polyphony, the tenor voice carried the cantus firmus, the principal melody upon which the other voices were built, so the tenor literally "held" the tune.

History

In early polyphonic music, the tenor was the foundational voice. During the Baroque era, roles for high male voices were often taken by castrati, but the tenor gradually rose to prominence as the romantic hero of opera in the 19th century, in the works of composers such as Rossini, Bellini, Donizetti and Verdi. The development of a powerful high register, including the celebrated high C sung from the chest, was popularised by Gilbert Duprez in the 1830s.

Subtypes

Operatic tenors are classified into subtypes including the leggero (light, agile), lyric, spinto, dramatic and heldentenor (heroic) tenor, the latter associated with the heavy roles of Wagner. Famous tenors have included Enrico Caruso, Luciano Pavarotti, Plácido Domingo and Jussi Björling.
