Suramin is an antiparasitic drug used to treat African sleeping sickness (African trypanosomiasis) caused by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense and T. b. gambiense, and river blindness (onchocerciasis). Developed by Bayer scientists in Germany in 1916 and marketed from the early 1920s under the name Germanin, it is one of the oldest synthetic antimicrobial agents still in clinical use, and is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Chemical structure of suramin, a polysulfonated naphthylurea

Medical uses

Suramin is given by slow intravenous injection for the first (haemolymphatic) stage of sleeping sickness, since it does not cross the blood–brain barrier in useful amounts. Against Onchocerca volvulus it is macrofilaricidal — killing adult worms — but requires medical supervision because of reactions to dying parasites.

Adverse effects

Adverse reactions include nausea, vomiting, itching, urticaria, kidney damage, peripheral neuropathy and, rarely, severe reactions including collapse after the first dose. The drug binds strongly to plasma proteins and persists in the body for weeks.

Research

Suramin is a well-known antagonist of purinergic (P2) receptors and inhibits a wide range of enzymes and growth-factor signalling, which has led to its investigation in cancer and, controversially, in small trials for autism spectrum disorder — for which it is not an approved treatment.
