Markovnikov's rule

In organic chemistry, Markovnikov's rule (also spelled Markownikoff's rule) describes the regioselectivity of electrophilic addition reactions of protic acids (HX) to alkenes. Formulated by Russian chemist Vladimir Markovnikov in 1870, the rule states that the hydrogen atom of HX attaches to the carbon atom of the double bond that already has the greater number of hydrogen atoms, while the halogen (X) attaches to the carbon with fewer hydrogens — in the classic formulation, "the rich get richer".

Chemical basis

The modern explanation is mechanistic. In electrophilic addition, the proton first adds to the double bond to form a carbocation. The hydrogen adds to the carbon that produces the more stable carbocation — tertiary over secondary over primary — because alkyl groups stabilise carbocations through inductive effects and hyperconjugation. The nucleophile (X−) then attacks this carbocation, so the overall result places X on the more substituted carbon. Markovnikov formulated his rule empirically decades before the electronic theory of organic reactions; carbocation rearrangements (hydride or alkyl shifts) can precede nucleophile capture and complicate outcomes.

Anti-Markovnikov additions

Certain reactions give the opposite regiochemical outcome and are termed anti-Markovnikov. The classic case is the peroxide-initiated addition of HBr to alkenes (the peroxide effect, described by Kharasch in 1933), in which a radical chain mechanism places bromine on the less substituted carbon. Hydroboration–oxidation likewise yields the anti-Markovnikov alcohol, as does hydrosilylation under various conditions. These exceptions operate by mechanisms other than carbocation formation — radical addition or concerted processes governed by sterics and metal coordination.

Scope

The rule applies broadly to additions of protic acids and other electrophile–nucleophile pairs to asymmetrical alkenes and alkynes, including hydration (acid-catalysed, giving the Markovnikov alcohol) and addition of hydrogen halides. Related generalisations, such as Zaitsev's rule for elimination, describe analogous "more substituted product" preferences.
