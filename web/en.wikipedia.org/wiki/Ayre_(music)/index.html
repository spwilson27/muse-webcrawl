Ayre (music)

An ayre (also spelled air) is a form of solo song with lute accompaniment that flourished in England in the late Renaissance and early Baroque eras, roughly from 1597 to the 1620s. Typically strophic and written for a single voice with lute (often printed so it could alternatively be performed by a voice with viol consort or by four voices), the ayre combined refined poetry with music of either graceful simplicity or expressive intensity, and is closely related to the French air de cour.

History

The genre begins with John Dowland's First Booke of Songes or Ayres (1597), which became one of the best-selling music publications of the age and included the celebrated "Flow my tears" (Lachrimae). Over the following quarter-century more than thirty books of ayres were published by composers including Thomas Campion — who wrote both words and music and defended the form in theory — Philip Rosseter, John Danyel, Thomas Morley, Robert Jones, and Alfonso Ferrabosco the younger.

Published in table-book format, with the lute part printed so that performers seated around a table could each read their part, ayres were designed for domestic music-making in courtly and gentry households. Lighter ayres are dance-like and syllabic; the finest, such as Dowland's "In darkness let me dwell", are through-composed, chromatic, and intensely melancholic, reflecting the Elizabethan cult of melancholy. The fashion faded after the 1620s as the Italianate declamatory song and later the continuo song took over in the Caroline court.
