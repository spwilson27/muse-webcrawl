Fixed-wing aircraft

A fixed-wing aircraft is a heavier-than-air flying machine, such as an aeroplane (airplane), whose wings generate lift from the forward airspeed of the vehicle. The wings are fixed — permanently attached to the fuselage — distinguishing the type from rotary-wing aircraft (helicopters), whose lift surfaces rotate, and from ornithopters, whose wings flap. Forward motion, provided by an engine-driven propeller or jet, drives air over the wing; the wing's airfoil shape and angle of attack deflect the air downward and produce lower pressure above the wing, generating lift.

History

The scientific groundwork was laid by Sir George Cayley, who identified the four forces of flight — lift, weight, thrust and drag — and built the first successful model gliders in the early 19th century. Otto Lilienthal made over 2,000 controlled glider flights in the 1890s. On 17 December 1903, at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, the Wright brothers achieved the first sustained, controlled, powered fixed-wing flights in the Wright Flyer. Fixed-wing aircraft developed rapidly through two world wars and the jet age to become the dominant form of aviation.

Structure and control

A conventional fixed-wing aircraft consists of a fuselage, one or more wing pairs (monoplane, biplane, triplane), a tail assembly (empennage) with horizontal and vertical stabilisers, control surfaces — ailerons for roll, elevators for pitch, rudder for yaw — and landing gear. High-lift devices such as flaps and slats augment lift at low speeds for takeoff and landing. Wings may be cantilevered or braced, straight, swept, or delta-shaped, depending on the speed regime.

Propulsion and types

Propulsion ranges from piston engines driving propellers, through turboprops and turbofan jets, to rocket power in experimental types. Fixed-wing aircraft span unpowered gliders and microscopic drones to the largest airliners and military transports, and they are used for passenger and cargo transport, military operations, agriculture, surveying, sport and research. Because they require forward speed to stay airborne, most need runways, though seaplanes take off from water and some military types use catapults, arrested landings or vertical-lift hybrids.
