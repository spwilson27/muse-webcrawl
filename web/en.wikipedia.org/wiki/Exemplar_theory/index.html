Exemplar theory

Exemplar theory is a theory in cognitive psychology and categorisation research which holds that people categorise objects by comparing them to specific individual examples — exemplars — stored in memory, rather than to abstract summaries such as prototypes or rules. To decide whether a new item belongs to a category, a person (implicitly) computes its overall similarity to the remembered exemplars of that category and of competing categories, and assigns it to the category whose exemplars it most resembles.

Key models

The most influential formalisation is Robert Nosofsky's Generalized Context Model (GCM, 1986), which developed the context model of Medin and Schaffer (1978). In the GCM, similarity decays exponentially with psychological distance; attention weights stretch and shrink dimensions of the stimulus space, and the probability of assigning an item to a category is proportional to its summed similarity to that category's exemplars. Exemplar models can simulate not only typicality effects (items similar to many exemplars are categorised faster) but also sensitivity to specific learned instances, exceptions, and category variability.

Comparison with other theories

Prototype theory (associated with Eleanor Rosch) proposes that categories are represented by an abstract average or "best example". Exemplar theory differs in storing every encountered instance rather than a summary; proponents argue it explains why people remember and are influenced by individual exceptions (e.g. that penguins, though birds, do not fly), and why categorisation can shift with recently encountered examples. Rule-based theories propose that some categories — especially scientific and legal ones — are defined by explicit rules or theories, and many researchers now favour hybrid accounts combining exemplars, prototypes and rules.

Applications and evidence

Laboratory studies of category learning, in which participants learn to classify artificial stimuli, have repeatedly found exemplar models to fit human performance better than prototype models, particularly after extended training and when categories contain salient exceptions. Exemplar-based mechanisms have also been invoked in perception, speech sound categorisation (exemplar models of phonetics), social judgement and stereotyping, and medical diagnosis, where experienced clinicians may rely on remembered cases.

Criticism

Critics note the implausibility of storing literally every exemplar indefinitely, the computational cost of comparing a new item to all stored instances, and evidence that people do form abstractions. Defenders reply that memory for specific episodes is demonstrably vast, that summary representations can emerge from exemplar processes, and that modern variants allow decay, abstraction and selective attention.
