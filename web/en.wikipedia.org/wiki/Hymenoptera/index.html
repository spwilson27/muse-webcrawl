Hymenoptera

Hymenoptera is a large order of insects, comprising the sawflies, wasps, bees, and ants. Over 150,000 living species of hymenopterans have been described, in addition to over 2,000 extinct ones. Many species are parasitic. Females typically have a special ovipositor for inserting eggs into hosts or places that are otherwise inaccessible; in wasps, bees, and ants this organ is often modified into a sting. Young develop through holometabolism (complete metamorphosis): a worm-like larva, an inactive pupa, and the winged adult.

Description

Hymenopterans range in size from the fairy wasp Dicopomorpha echmepterygis, about 0.14 mm long, to large spider wasps several centimetres long. Adults have two pairs of membranous wings (the source of the name, from Greek hymen, "membrane") joined by a row of tiny hooks called hamuli, chewing or lapping mouthparts, and large compound eyes. Females have an ovipositor, which in the Aculeata (ants, bees, and stinging wasps) functions as a sting. Sex is determined by haplodiploidy: fertilized eggs become females, unfertilized eggs become males.

Classification and evolution

The order is traditionally divided into the mostly plant-feeding sawflies (Symphyta, a paraphyletic grade) and the wasp-waisted Apocrita, which includes parasitoid wasps, ants, and bees. Hymenoptera is one of the most species-rich insect orders, rivaled by beetles, flies, and moths, and the enormous diversity of parasitoid wasps suggests the true total may exceed a million species. The earliest hymenopteran fossils are sawflies from the Triassic, about 250 million years ago; social ants and bees radiated during the Cretaceous alongside flowering plants.

Ecology and importance

Hymenopterans are among the most important pollinators of wild and cultivated plants, and parasitoid wasps are major natural enemies of pest insects, widely used in biological control. Eusocial ants and bees form colonies of extraordinary complexity and dominate many terrestrial ecosystems in biomass. Honey bees provide honey and pollination services worth many billions of dollars annually, and declines in bee populations have become a significant conservation concern.
