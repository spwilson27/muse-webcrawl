Moral luck

Moral luck is a problem in moral philosophy arising when an agent is assessed for an action whose significant moral features depend on circumstances beyond the agent's control. The term was introduced into modern discussion by Bernard Williams and developed most influentially by Thomas Nagel in his 1979 essay "Moral Luck". The problem challenges what Nagel calls the Control Principle — the intuition that people are morally assessable only for what depends on factors under their control.

The problem

Two drivers set out equally carelessly; a child runs in front of one but not the other. The first kills the child, the second arrives home safely. Ordinary moral judgement — and the law — condemns the first driver far more severely, yet the only difference between them is luck. If we are responsible only for what we control, the difference in blame seems unjustifiable; yet abandoning it seems equally impossible.

Nagel's four kinds

Nagel distinguished four ways in which luck can bear on moral assessment:

  • Resultant luck — luck in how one's actions turn out (the two drivers above).
  • Circumstantial luck — luck in the kind of situations and moral tests one faces; for example, the ordinary citizen of Nazi Germany who collaborated would perhaps have lived a blameless life elsewhere.
  • Constitutive luck — luck in one's character, temperament, inclinations and capacities, which are partly innate and partly shaped by early environment.
  • Causal luck — luck in how one is determined by antecedent circumstances, raising the wider problem of free will and determinism.

Williams and moral luck

Bernard Williams used the (semi-fictional) example of the painter Gauguin, who abandons his family to pursue his art in Tahiti. Whether his decision can be justified — even to himself — depends, Williams argued, on whether he succeeds as an artist, a matter partly of luck. Williams used the example to attack the Kantian ideal of a morality insulated from fortune, in which the moral worth of the will is wholly independent of outcomes.

Responses

Responses divide between those who accept moral luck as a genuine feature of our practices (Williams, and in a different register Michael Walzer), those who attempt to dissolve the paradox by restricting moral assessment to the quality of the agent's will (many Kantians), and those who treat the phenomenon as evidence that moral judgement is systematically incoherent (Nagel himself leaned toward this paradoxical conclusion). The problem also informs debates in law — for example, whether attempts should be punished as severely as completed crimes, since the difference between them is often resultant luck alone — and has been taken up in discussions of responsibility for historical injustice and of "outcome bias" in psychology.
