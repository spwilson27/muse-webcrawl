Canadian whisky

Canadian whisky is a type of whisky produced in Canada. Most Canadian whiskies are blended multi-grain products, historically associated with rye grain, which gives rise to the common practice of referring to all Canadian whisky simply as "rye whisky" in Canada; by law, the terms "Canadian whisky," "Canadian rye whisky," and "rye whisky" are interchangeable in Canada and have the same legal meaning. Canadian whisky must be mashed, distilled, and aged in Canada, aged in wood barrels for at least three years, and possess the aroma, taste, and character generally attributed to Canadian whisky.

Characteristics

Unlike American straight whiskies, Canadian whiskies are typically blends of a lightly flavoured base whisky, usually distilled from corn in a column still to a high proof, with smaller proportions of flavouring whiskies made from rye, wheat, or barley, often distilled at lower proof to retain grain character. The blender's art is central to the style, which is generally lighter and smoother than many other whisky styles. Canadian law permits the addition of up to 9.09% of certain flavouring agents, including imported spirits such as sherry or fruit wines, a practice that distinguishes it from Scotch and American rules.

History

Whisky-making came to Canada with British and American settlers, and commercial distilling grew in the 19th century around mills and farms in Ontario and Quebec, with firms such as Gooderham & Worts, Hiram Walker (founder of Canadian Club), and Seagram becoming giants of the industry. During American Prohibition (1920–1933), enormous quantities of Canadian whisky were smuggled across the border by rum-runners, cementing the spirit's international reputation.

Well-known brands include Crown Royal, Canadian Club, Alberta Premium, Forty Creek, and Lot 40. In recent decades a craft distilling revival has produced highly regarded single-distillery rye whiskies.
