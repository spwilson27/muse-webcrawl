Blaxploitation is an ethnic subgenre of the exploitation film that emerged in the United States in the early 1970s. The films were made with Black audiences in mind, featured predominantly Black casts and funk and soul soundtracks, and were often set in urban neighbourhoods. They gave Black actors leading roles as assertive protagonists at a time when Hollywood offered few, but were also criticised — including by the NAACP, which coined the term — for perpetuating stereotypes.

History

The genre is usually dated from 1971, when Melvin Van Peebles's independently financed Sweet Sweetback's Baadasssss Song became a surprise hit, followed by MGM's Shaft, directed by Gordon Parks, whose Isaac Hayes soundtrack won an Academy Award. Super Fly (1972), with Curtis Mayfield's celebrated score, cemented the formula. Studios and independent producers quickly released dozens of titles across action, crime, horror (Blacula), martial arts, and western variants, starring performers such as Richard Roundtree, Pam Grier, Fred Williamson, Jim Brown, and Tamara Dobson.

Criticism and legacy

Black civic groups formed the Coalition Against Blaxploitation in 1972, arguing the films glamorised drugs and crime. By the late 1970s the cycle had faded as studios absorbed Black stars into mainstream productions. The genre's music, fashion, and imagery have remained influential, inspiring later tributes and parodies including I'm Gonna Git You Sucka (1988), Quentin Tarantino's Jackie Brown (1997), and Black Dynamite (2009).
