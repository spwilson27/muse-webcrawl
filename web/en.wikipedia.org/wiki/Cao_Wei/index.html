Cao Wei (Chinese: 曹魏; pinyin: Cáo Wèi; Wade–Giles: Ts'ao Wei), commonly known as Wei, was one of the three major states that competed for supremacy over China in the Three Kingdoms period (220–280 AD). With its capital initially in Xuchang and later in Luoyang, the state was established by Cao Pi in 220, based upon the foundations laid by his father, Cao Cao, towards the end of the Eastern Han dynasty.

The name "Wei" first became associated with Cao Cao when he was enfeoffed as the Duke of Wei in 213 by Emperor Xian of Han. In 216, Cao Cao was elevated to the status of vassal king under the title "King of Wei". When Cao Pi succeeded his father in 220, he forced Emperor Xian to abdicate the throne to him, ending the Han dynasty and founding Wei.

History

Origins

Towards the end of the Han dynasty, northern China came under the control of Cao Cao, the chancellor of the last Han ruler, Emperor Xian. Cao Cao laid the foundation for what was to become the state of Cao Wei. In 213, he was enfeoffed as the Duke of Wei and given ten cities under his domain, collectively named the "State of Wei". At that time, the southern part of China was already divided into two areas controlled by Sun Quan and Liu Bei. In 216, Cao Cao was promoted to King of Wei.

Reigns of Cao Pi and Cao Rui

Cao Cao died in 220 and was succeeded by his son Cao Pi in the titles of King of Wei and Imperial Chancellor. Later that year, Cao Pi forced Emperor Xian to abdicate in his favour, an event known as the Shanrang, and established the state of Cao Wei with himself as the new emperor. Liu Bei of Shu immediately contested Cao Pi's claim to the Han throne and declared himself emperor a year later; Sun Quan of Wu followed suit in 229. Cao Pi reigned for six years until his death in 226 and was succeeded by his son, Cao Rui, who reigned until 239.

Decline and fall

After Cao Rui's death, the regents Cao Shuang and Sima Yi shared power on behalf of the young emperor Cao Fang. In 249, Sima Yi seized power from Cao Shuang in the Incident at the Gaoping Tombs, marking the start of Sima clan dominance over Wei. Later Wei emperors were reduced to figureheads. Sima Yi's sons Sima Shi and Sima Zhao consolidated the family's grip; Sima Zhao was posthumously honoured as a founding figure of the Jin dynasty. In 263, Wei armies conquered Shu, ending the tripartite division for the first time. In February 266, Sima Zhao's son Sima Yan forced the last Wei emperor, Cao Huan, to abdicate and founded the Jin dynasty.

Government and society

Wei adopted the nine-rank system (jiupin zhongzheng zhi) for selecting officials, devised by the official Chen Qun, under which local arbiters graded candidates into nine ranks largely according to family background and reputation. The system tended to entrench aristocratic families in office. Wei also maintained the tuntian agricultural colony system, in which soldiers and civilians farmed state land and surrendered a share of the harvest, bolstering state revenues and military logistics along the northern frontier against the Xianbei and Wuhuan peoples.
