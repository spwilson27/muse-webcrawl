Pattern welding

Pattern welding is the practice of forming a blade from several pieces of steel of differing composition, forge-welded together and worked so that the finished blade displays a visible, often intricate pattern. The technique is famous from early medieval European swords — particularly Viking Age and Migration Period blades — and is often popularly confused with true Damascus steel, a crucible steel with a superficially similar watered pattern.

Twisted rods of alternating steel, welded and ground, produce the classic herringbone pattern.

Technique

The smith begins with rods or bars of iron and steels of differing carbon content, stacks or twists them together, and forge-welds the bundle at high heat. Repeated twisting, folding, and drawing-out manipulates the layers into designs: straight laminates, twists, chevrons, and the "herringbone" patterns of early medieval blades. After grinding and a light acid etch, the layers take on contrasting shades because they respond differently to etching and polishing.

History

Combining a hard, high-carbon edge with a tougher, more flexible core was originally a practical necessity: good steel was scarce and expensive, and composite construction made the most of it. Celtic and Roman blades used simple piled structures; by the 6th to 11th centuries, smiths in northern Europe produced highly decorative pattern-welded sword blades, some with inlaid maker marks such as +VLFBERH+T. As homogeneous crucible and blister steels became more available, the technique faded in Europe, though it persisted in Indonesian kris blades and in Japanese sword construction (which uses related folding and lamination methods). A modern revival among bladesmiths has made pattern-welded "Damascus" a mainstay of custom knives.
