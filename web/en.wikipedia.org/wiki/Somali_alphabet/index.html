Somali alphabet

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Somali Latin alphabet is the official writing system of the Somali language. Adopted by decree of the Somali government in 1972, it is a Latin alphabet of 26 letters with several digraphs, designed by the Somali linguist Shire Jama Ahmed. Somali had previously been written in adapted Arabic script (the Wadaad writing) and in indigenous scripts, most notably the Osmanya alphabet invented in the 1920s by Osman Yusuf Kenadid.

The alphabet

The letters are: B, T, J, X, KH, D, R, S, SH, DH, C, G, F, Q, K, L, M, N, W, H, Y, A, E, I, O, U. The letters P, V, and Z of the basic Latin alphabet are not used. Several values differ from English: C represents the voiced pharyngeal fricative /ʕ/ (Arabic ʿayn), X represents /ħ/, KH represents /x/, and J represents /tʃ/. Digraphs DH, SH, and KH stand for the retroflex stop /ɖ/, the fricative /ʃ/, and /x/ respectively. Long vowels are written double (aa, ee, ii, oo, uu), and vowel length is phonemic in Somali. Tone, which is grammatically significant, is not normally marked in ordinary writing.

History

For centuries Somali religious scholars wrote Somali in Arabic-based scripts. In the 20th century a dozen or more scripts were proposed, including the Osmanya, Borama (Gadabuursi), and Kaddare alphabets. A government language commission debated the question for years; the choice was politically sensitive because of associations between script, clan, and religion. In October 1972 the Supreme Revolutionary Council decreed the Latin alphabet official, and Somali became the language of administration and primary education. A mass literacy campaign in 1973–75 sent teachers and students into the countryside and dramatically raised literacy, making the alphabet a central symbol of Somali nation-building.
