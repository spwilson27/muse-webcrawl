Martin Elias Peter Seligman (born August 12, 1942) is an American psychologist, educator and author, widely regarded as the founder of positive psychology. A longtime professor at the University of Pennsylvania, he first became known for the theory of learned helplessness, and later for research on optimism, resilience and human flourishing. He served as president of the American Psychological Association in 1998, using the platform to launch positive psychology as an organised field.

Learned helplessness

In experiments at the University of Pennsylvania in the 1960s with Steven F. Maier, Seligman found that dogs exposed to unavoidable electric shocks later failed to escape from situations where escape was possible — they had "learned" to be helpless. Extended to humans, learned helplessness offered a laboratory model of depression and passivity. Seligman later developed, with colleagues including Lyn Abramson and John Teasdale, an attributional reformulation: people who habitually explain bad events by internal, stable and global causes are at greater risk of helplessness and depression, while an optimistic explanatory style is protective.

Positive psychology

As APA president in 1998, Seligman called for psychology to study not only mental illness but also the strengths and conditions that make life worth living. With Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi he introduced the field in a special issue of American Psychologist (2000). At Penn he founded the Positive Psychology Center and the Master of Applied Positive Psychology program. His later work proposed the PERMA model of well-being: Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning and Accomplishment.

Seligman also led the development of the Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program for the U.S. Army, teaching resilience skills to soldiers — an application that drew both wide adoption and scholarly debate.
