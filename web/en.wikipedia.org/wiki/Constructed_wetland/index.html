Constructed wetland

A constructed wetland is an artificial wetland engineered to treat wastewater, greywater, stormwater runoff or industrial effluents by mimicking the physical, chemical and biological processes of a natural wetland. Constructed wetlands are an example of nature-based solutions in sanitation engineering: they remove suspended solids, organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus, metals and pathogens through sedimentation, filtration, adsorption, plant uptake and microbial transformation, while requiring far less energy and mechanical equipment than conventional treatment plants.

Types

  • Free water surface wetlands consist of shallow basins with a free water layer above soil or gravel, densely planted with emergent macrophytes such as cattails (Typha), bulrushes (Schoenoplectus) and reeds (Phragmites). They resemble natural marshes and also provide wildlife habitat.
  • Subsurface flow wetlands keep the water level below a bed of gravel or sand through which wastewater passes. In horizontal-flow systems water moves laterally from inlet to outlet; in vertical-flow systems (also called reed bed filters or French systems) effluent is dosed intermittently onto the surface and percolates downward, improving oxygen transfer and nitrification.
  • Hybrid systems combine vertical and horizontal stages to achieve both nitrification and denitrification.

History

Scientific work on treating sewage with wetland plants began in Germany in the 1950s and 1960s with Käthe Seidel at the Max Planck Institute, who showed that bulrushes could remove phenols and reduce bacterial loads. Reinhold Kickuth developed the "root zone method" in the 1970s, and full-scale municipal systems spread through Europe and North America in the 1980s, supported by design work at the Tennessee Valley Authority and by researchers such as Robert Kadlec and Robert Knight, whose treatment-wetland databases standardised performance expectations.

Removal mechanisms and performance

Suspended solids are removed by sedimentation and filtration; biochemical oxygen demand is degraded by microbial biofilms on plant stems and media. Nitrogen is removed mainly by coupled nitrification–denitrification and plant uptake, while phosphorus is adsorbed to substrates and accreted in sediments, so its long-term removal is limited unless special media are used. Well-designed systems commonly achieve 80–95% removal of solids and BOD, substantial pathogen reduction, and moderate nutrient removal, with land requirements typically on the order of 1–5 m² per person equivalent for subsurface systems.

Applications

Constructed wetlands treat domestic sewage for small communities and individual households, polish effluent from conventional plants, buffer agricultural and urban stormwater, and treat mine drainage, landfill leachate and some industrial wastewaters. They are promoted in decentralised sanitation because of low operating costs, simple maintenance and co-benefits for biodiversity, carbon storage and landscape amenity, though they require more land than mechanised treatment and can emit methane and nitrous oxide.
