Computable number

In mathematics, particularly in computability theory, a computable number is a real number whose digits can be computed by a finite, terminating algorithm. Equivalently, it is a real number for which there exists a Turing machine that, given n, produces the first n digits of the number. The notion was introduced by Alan Turing in his 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers", with a correction published in 1937.

Properties

All rational numbers, all algebraic numbers, and many transcendental numbers such as π and e are computable. However, the computable numbers are countable, whereas the real numbers are uncountable, so almost all real numbers are not computable. The computable numbers form a real closed field, but equality of computable numbers is not decidable: there is no algorithm that can always determine whether two programs compute the same real.

Non-computable numbers

Famous examples of non-computable numbers include Chaitin's constant Ω, whose digits encode the halting probability of a universal Turing machine, and numbers defined using the halting problem or the busy beaver function. Computable numbers are closely related to the study of computable analysis, which develops analysis over this countable subfield.
