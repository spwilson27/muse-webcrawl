Rift valley
TypeLinear lowland between highlands or mountain ranges
Formed byExtensional tectonics (rifting)
Largest on landEast African (Great) Rift Valley
Related landformsGraben, horst, mid-ocean ridge

A rift valley is a linear-shaped lowland between several highlands or mountain ranges, created by the action of a geologic rift — the pulling apart of the Earth's lithosphere by extensional tectonic forces. Where a continental plate is being stretched, blocks of crust subside along normal faults to form a graben, the rift valley floor, flanked by uplifted shoulders and often accompanied by volcanism and earthquakes.

Examples

The greatest terrestrial example is the East African Rift system, extending some 6,000 km from the Afar Triple Junction through Ethiopia, Kenya and Tanzania toward Mozambique — where the African Plate is splitting into the Somali and Nubian plates and a new ocean may eventually form. Other notable rift valleys include the Rhine Rift Valley (Upper Rhine Graben) in Europe, the Baikal Rift Zone in Siberia (containing the world's deepest lake), the Rio Grande rift in New Mexico, the Basin and Range Province of the western United States, and the Dead Sea Transform valley. Rift valleys also run along the crests of mid-ocean ridges, where most of Earth's volcanism occurs, and have been identified on the Moon (as graben) and on Mars.

Evolution

If rifting continues, a rift valley can evolve into a narrow sea (as in the Red Sea, an incipient ocean) and ultimately a passive continental margin like those flanking the Atlantic. Rift basins accumulate thick sediments and are important sources of hydrocarbons, geothermal energy and fossil records — the East African rift valleys have yielded many of the key fossils documenting human origins.
