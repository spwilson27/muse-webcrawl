British New Wave

The British New Wave is a style of British films released in the late 1950s and 1960s, adapted from the "kitchen sink realism" plays and novels of the period. The films were frequently shot in black and white, often on location in the industrial towns of northern England, and dealt frankly with working-class life, sexuality, abortion, homosexuality and other social issues largely absent from earlier British cinema.

Characteristics

British New Wave films shared themes of class frustration and youthful rebellion embodied by the "angry young man" figure, and a documentary-influenced visual style. Many were directed by filmmakers who had emerged from the Free Cinema movement and documentary production, including Tony Richardson, Karel Reisz, Lindsay Anderson and John Schlesinger, and produced by companies such as Woodfall Film Productions.

Key films

  • Room at the Top (1959), directed by Jack Clayton.
  • Saturday Night and Sunday Morning (1960), directed by Karel Reisz, starring Albert Finney.
  • A Taste of Honey (1961), directed by Tony Richardson, from Shelagh Delaney's play.
  • A Kind of Loving (1962), directed by John Schlesinger.
  • The Loneliness of the Long Distance Runner (1962), directed by Tony Richardson.
  • This Sporting Life (1963), directed by Lindsay Anderson, starring Richard Harris.
  • Billy Liar (1963), directed by John Schlesinger.

Legacy

By the mid-1960s the movement gave way to more commercially varied British cinema, including the "Swinging London" films, but its realism influenced later British directors such as Ken Loach and Mike Leigh. The term "British New Wave" was borrowed from the French Nouvelle Vague, although the British films were generally more conventional in form and more socially focused in content.
