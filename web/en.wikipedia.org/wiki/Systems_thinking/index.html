Systems thinking

Systems thinking
Key conceptsFeedback loops, emergence, stocks and flows, leverage points
Key figuresLudwig von Bertalanffy, Jay Forrester, Donella Meadows, Peter Senge, Russell Ackoff
Related fieldsSystems theory, cybernetics, system dynamics, complexity science

Systems thinking is an approach to problem-solving and analysis that views problems as part of an overall system rather than as isolated events. Instead of breaking a phenomenon into parts and studying each separately (reductionism), systems thinking focuses on the interactions and interrelationships between parts, the feedback loops those interactions create, and the emergent behaviour of the whole.

Origins

The approach grew out of Ludwig von Bertalanffy's general systems theory (developed from the 1930s), Norbert Wiener's cybernetics, and Jay Forrester's system dynamics at MIT in the 1950s–60s, which introduced computer simulation of stocks, flows and feedback in industrial and urban systems. The 1972 report The Limits to Growth, produced with Donella Meadows and colleagues, applied system dynamics to global resource questions.

Core ideas

Key concepts include reinforcing and balancing feedback loops; delays between cause and effect; stocks (accumulations) and flows; emergence; and hierarchy and boundaries. Donella Meadows's influential essay on "leverage points" ranked places to intervene in a system—from parameters and buffers up to goals and paradigms—arguing that the most powerful interventions change a system's goals and mindset. Peter Senge popularised systems thinking in management with The Fifth Discipline (1990), framing it as the core discipline of the learning organisation.

Applications

Systems thinking is used in public health, environmental management, engineering, education, business and public policy, often supported by causal loop diagrams and simulation models. Critics note that systems models can oversimplify social realities or smuggle in the modeller's assumptions.
