Flat (music)

In music theory, flat (Italian: bemolle) means "lower in pitch by a chromatic semitone." In staff notation, the flat accidental is the symbol ♭ placed before a note. In musical notation, a B♭ note is played on a piano as the black key immediately to the left of the white key B. The opposite of a flat is a sharp (♯), which raises a note by a chromatic semitone.

Notation and use

A flat may appear in a key signature, where it affects every note on the corresponding line or space for the whole piece, or as an accidental before an individual note, where it applies for the remainder of the measure. A double flat (𝄫) lowers a note by two chromatic semitones; a note so lowered is enharmonically equivalent to a note two semitones below the natural. A natural sign (♮) cancels a previous flat or sharp.

In twelve-tone equal temperament, B♭ and A♯ are the same pitch (enharmonic equivalents), but they remain conceptually distinct and their use depends on harmonic context and the conventions of the key.

Order of flats

The flats in key signatures always appear in the same order: B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭, F♭. This order is itself the descending cycle of fifths, and each key signature adds one flat. The order is commonly memorized with mnemonics such as "Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father."

History

The flat symbol derives from a rounded lowercase "b" (b molle, "soft b") used in medieval chant notation to indicate the softened form of the note B, contrasted with the square "b" (b durum) that became the natural and sharp signs. The distinction was essential for avoiding the tritone in the Guidonian hexachord system.
