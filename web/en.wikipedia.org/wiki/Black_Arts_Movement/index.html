The Black Arts Movement (BAM) was an African American-led art movement, active during the 1960s and 1970s, often described as the cultural arm of the Black Power movement. Founded in Harlem by writer and activist Amiri Baraka in 1965, it promoted a distinctly Black aesthetic, political theatre, poetry and publishing.

History

Baraka founded the Black Arts Repertory Theatre/School in Harlem in the spring of 1965, shortly after the assassination of Malcolm X. Larry Neal's 1968 essay "The Black Arts Movement" became its manifesto, arguing that art should be "collectively produced" and serve the liberation of Black people. Institutions such as Chicago's OBAC writers' workshop, Detroit's Broadside Press and the journal Negro Digest/Black World spread the movement nationally.

Characteristics and figures

BAM writers — including Sonia Sanchez, Nikki Giovanni, Gwendolyn Brooks, Haki R. Madhubuti (Don L. Lee), Mari Evans and Etheridge Knight — rejected Eurocentric standards, drew on Black vernacular, jazz and African traditions, and addressed poetry directly to Black audiences. The movement developed Black studies programmes and independent Black publishing, and influenced later spoken-word and hip-hop traditions.

Criticism and legacy

Critics charged parts of the movement with essentialism, sexism and homophobia, debates carried on within Black feminist writing of the 1970s. Literary historians nonetheless credit BAM with transforming American letters, building durable institutions, and asserting Black cultural self-determination. See also the Harlem Renaissance, which it consciously succeeded.
