Lombards

A Lombard helmet plate depicting King Agilulf

The Lombards (Latin: Langobardi) were a Germanic people who conquered most of the Italian Peninsula between 568 and 774. Originating in northern Germany, the Lombards migrated southward through the Elbe valley and Pannonia before entering Italy under their king Alboin in 568, in the wake of the devastation of the Gothic War.

Kingdom in Italy

The Lombards established a kingdom in northern and central Italy, with its capital at Pavia — the area that gave its name to the modern region of Lombardy — while semi-independent Lombard duchies, notably Spoleto and Benevento, controlled parts of the centre and south. Initially largely Arian Christians or pagans, the Lombards gradually converted to Catholicism over the 7th century. King Rothari issued the Edictum Rothari in 643, the first written code of Lombard law.

Fall and legacy

The Lombard kingdom in the north was conquered by the Frankish king Charlemagne in 774, after a siege of Pavia. The southern Duchy of Benevento survived as an independent Lombard state into the 11th century. The Lombards left their name to Lombardy and contributed vocabulary to the Italian language; Lombard goldsmith work, such as the Iron Crown of Lombardy, is among the most celebrated art of the early Middle Ages. The principal narrative source for their history is Paul the Deacon's Historia Langobardorum, written in the late 8th century.
