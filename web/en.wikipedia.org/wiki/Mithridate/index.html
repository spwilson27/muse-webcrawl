Mithridate

Mithridate, also known as mithridatium, mithridatum, or mithridaticum, is a semi-mythical remedy said to have been created by Mithridates VI, king of Pontus (120–63 BC), as a universal antidote to poisoning. It reportedly contained as many as 65 ingredients, including opium, and was later elaborated into theriac.

History

Fearing assassination by poison, Mithridates is said to have taken small daily doses to build immunity — a practice now called mithridatism. After his defeat, his notebooks were translated by Pompey's freedman Lenaeus. Recipes attributed to Andromachus, Zopyrus, and Galen kept mithridate in pharmacopoeias into the 18th and 19th centuries.
