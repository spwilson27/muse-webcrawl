Puget Sound


Puget Sound near Seattle, with Mount Rainier in the distance.

Puget Sound is a complex estuarine system of interconnected marine waterways and basins in the U.S. state of Washington, part of the Salish Sea. It is a sound along the northwestern coast of the United States, an inlet of the Pacific Ocean, and part of the Salish Sea. The largest city on the sound is Seattle.

Geography

Puget Sound extends approximately 100 miles (160 km) from Deception Pass in the north to Olympia in the south. Its average depth is 450 feet (140 m) and its maximum depth, off Jefferson Point near Seattle, is 930 feet (280 m). The main basin stretches from Deception Pass south to the Tacoma Narrows, while branches such as Hood Canal, Possession Sound, and Saratoga Passage extend the system. Major islands include Whidbey Island—the largest—Bainbridge Island, Vashon Island, and the San Juan Islands at the northern entrance.

History

The Sound was named by George Vancouver for Peter Puget, a second lieutenant accompanying him on the Vancouver Expedition in 1792. Vancouver gave the name only to the waters south of the Tacoma Narrows, though it later came to refer to the entire system. Indigenous peoples, including the Coast Salish groups, have inhabited the region for thousands of years.

Ecology

Puget Sound is home to a rich ecosystem including salmon, orca (killer whales), harbor seals, bald eagles, and giant Pacific octopus. The Southern Resident orca population is listed as endangered, and the health of the Sound is a major regional environmental concern, leading to the creation of the Puget Sound Partnership in 2007 to coordinate restoration.

Transportation

The Washington State Ferries system, the largest ferry network in the United States, serves islands and peninsulas across the Sound. Ports at Seattle, Tacoma, and Everett make the region a major gateway for trans-Pacific trade.
