Red-eye effect

The red-eye effect in photography is the common appearance of red pupils in color photographs of the eyes of humans and several other animals. It occurs when using a photographic flash device very close to the camera lens in ambient low light, which prevents the subject's pupils from contracting.

Cause

Light from the flash passes through the pupil and reflects off the blood-rich retina at the back of the eye — in particular the choroid, which is full of blood vessels — and back out through the pupil to the camera. Because the flash and lens are nearly on the same axis, this reflected red light is captured strongly. The effect is more pronounced in people with light-colored eyes and in children, whose pupils dilate more in darkness.

Prevention and removal

The effect can be reduced by separating the flash from the lens axis, bouncing the flash off walls or ceilings, increasing ambient light so pupils contract, or using a pre-flash that makes the pupils constrict before the exposure. Many cameras offer a red-eye reduction mode, and modern photo-editing software and smartphones can detect and darken the affected pupils automatically.

A similar phenomenon in animals is eyeshine, caused by reflection from the tapetum lucidum, which usually appears green, yellow, or blue rather than red.
