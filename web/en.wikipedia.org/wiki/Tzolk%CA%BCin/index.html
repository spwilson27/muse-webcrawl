Tzolkʼin

The tzolkʼin (Mayan pronunciation: [t͡solˈkʼin], formerly also transliterated tzolkin) is the 260-day Mesoamerican calendar used by the Maya civilization of pre-Columbian Mesoamerica. It combines a cycle of twenty named days with another cycle of thirteen numbers, producing 260 unique day names. The tzolkʼin was used, together with the 365-day haabʼ, to form a synchronized cycle lasting for 52 haabʼ years, the so-called Calendar Round.

Days of the tzolkʼin

The twenty day names, in the order used in Yucatán at the time of the Spanish conquest, are: Imix, Ikʼ, Akʼbal, Kʼan, Chikchan, Kimi, Manikʼ, Lamat, Muluk, Ok, Chuwen, Eb, Ben, Ix, Men, Kib, Kaban, Etzʼnab, Kawak, and Ajaw. Each day name is paired with a number from 1 to 13, advancing together so that the full cycle repeats after 260 days.

Origins and use

The origins of the 260-day cycle are uncertain. Suggested explanations include the human gestation period of roughly nine months, the interval between zenith passages of the sun at the latitude of Izapa, the period of visibility of Venus, and agricultural cycles. The cycle was used to schedule religious ceremonies, divination, and naming, and it remains in use today among some Maya communities in the highlands of Guatemala.
