A sabre (also spelled saber in American English) is a type of backsword with a single-edged, moderately curved blade, typically associated with light cavalry. Derived from the curved swords of Turkic and Hungarian steppe warriors, the sabre spread through Eastern and Central Europe in the medieval period and became the standard cavalry sidearm of European and American armies from the Napoleonic era until the early twentieth century.

Characteristics

The classic military sabre has a curved blade optimised for slashing cuts from horseback, a single edge with a sharpened "false edge" near the point on some patterns, and a guard that protects the hand — ranging from a simple stirrup hilt to a bowl or three-bar guard. Officer and naval patterns were often lighter and more ornate than troopers' models. Blade lengths were commonly around 80–90 cm.

History

Curved swords of the sabre type entered Europe with Magyar and other steppe peoples in the 9th and 10th centuries and were developed in Hungary and Poland, where the szabla became a national symbol. By the late 18th century the sabre was carried by hussars and other light cavalry across Europe; famous patterns include the British 1796 light cavalry sabre and its controversially effective cutting blade, the French sabres of the Napoleonic Wars, and the American Model 1860 light cavalry saber of the Civil War era. As cavalry declined in the twentieth century, the sabre survived as a ceremonial weapon and in the sport of fencing, where sabre is one of the three weapons alongside foil and épée.

Fencing

In modern sport fencing, the sabre is a light cutting and thrusting weapon; the valid target area is everything above the waist including the head and hands, and bouts are fast and aggressive, governed by right-of-way rules similar to foil.
