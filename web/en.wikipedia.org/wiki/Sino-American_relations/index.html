Sino-American relations

Sino-American relations, also known as China–United States relations, are the diplomatic, economic and military relations between China and the United States of America. The relationship, often described as one of the most important bilateral relationships in the world, combines deep economic interdependence with strategic rivalry.

Early history

Trade between the two countries began shortly after American independence, when the ship Empress of China sailed to Canton in 1784. The Treaty of Wanghia (1844) established formal relations with the Qing Empire. Chinese labourers migrated to the United States in large numbers in the 19th century, prompting the discriminatory Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882.

Cold War and rapprochement

After the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949, the United States continued to recognise the Republic of China on Taiwan and the two countries fought on opposing sides in the Korean War. Relations began to thaw with President Richard Nixon's visit to Beijing in 1972 and the Shanghai Communiqué. Full diplomatic relations were established on 1 January 1979, with the United States recognising the PRC as the sole legal government of China while maintaining unofficial ties with Taiwan under the Taiwan Relations Act.

Engagement and trade

China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001 accelerated trade growth; China became one of America's largest trading partners and a major holder of US Treasury securities. Disputes persisted over trade imbalances, intellectual property, human rights, cyber-espionage and the South China Sea.

Strategic competition

From the late 2010s the relationship shifted toward open competition, marked by a trade war begun in 2018, technology restrictions affecting firms such as Huawei, and heightened tensions over Taiwan, Hong Kong and Xinjiang. Both governments describe the relationship as one of competition to be managed, alongside cooperation on issues such as climate change.
