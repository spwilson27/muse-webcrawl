Incumbent (ecclesiastical)


An Anglican parish church

In English ecclesiastical law, the incumbent is the holder of a parochial benefice in the Church of England — the cleric who holds the "cure of souls" of a parish and is entitled to its revenues. The term derives from the Latin incumbere, "to lie upon," reflecting the office-holder's duty to reside upon and serve the benefice.

Rectors and vicars

Incumbents are historically divided into rectors, who were entitled to the whole of the parish tithes, and vicars, who received only a portion (the "small tithes") because the rectory had been appropriated to a monastery or other body. A third category, perpetual curates, served parishes without tithe endowment. Modern legislation has largely leveled these distinctions: since the late twentieth century nearly all newly appointed incumbents in England are styled vicars, and the older categories survive chiefly as historical titles.

Appointment and tenure

Historically, the right to present a candidate to a benefice (the advowson) belonged to a patron, and the bishop instituted the presentee. Incumbents enjoyed the "parson's freehold," giving strong security of tenure. Reforms culminating in the Ecclesiastical Offices (Terms of Service) Measure 2009 moved most new appointments to "common tenure," a form of employment-like status with defined terms and capability procedures, while retaining elements of office-holder status.
