Harmonic mean

In mathematics, the harmonic mean is one of several kinds of average, and in particular, one of the Pythagorean means. It is sometimes appropriate for situations when the average rate is desired. The harmonic mean can be expressed as the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of the reciprocals of the given set of observations. As a simple example, the harmonic mean of 1, 4, and 4 is 2.

The harmonic mean is the smallest of the three Pythagorean means: for any set of positive numbers that are not all equal, the harmonic mean is less than the geometric mean, which is less than the arithmetic mean. It tends strongly toward the smallest elements of the set and, unlike the arithmetic mean, mitigates the influence of large outliers.

Definition

The harmonic mean H of the positive real numbers x1, x2, …, xn is defined to be the reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of their reciprocals. Equivalently, it is n divided by the sum of the reciprocals of the numbers. For two numbers a and b, the formula simplifies to H = 2ab / (a + b), which is twice their product divided by their sum.

The harmonic mean is also a Schur-concave function and is dominated by the minimum of its arguments, in the sense that it can never exceed the smallest value in the set. It is not defined (or is taken to be zero) if any of the values is zero, and it is generally inappropriate for data that may include negative values.

Relationship with other means

For a set of positive numbers, the three Pythagorean means satisfy the inequality H ≤ G ≤ A, with equality only when all the numbers are equal. The harmonic mean of two numbers is also related to the arithmetic and geometric means by the identity G² = H · A: the geometric mean is the geometric mean of the arithmetic and harmonic means. There is a corresponding construction in geometry: in a right triangle, the altitude drawn to the hypotenuse relates to the harmonic mean of the segments, and the harmonic mean appears in the theory of the harmonic series and in continued fractions.

A weighted harmonic mean can be defined analogously, replacing the count n by the sum of the weights and each reciprocal term by a weighted reciprocal. Weighted harmonic means are used, for example, in finance for averaging price–earnings ratios across a portfolio.

Applications

Average speed and rates

The classic application is averaging speeds over a fixed distance. If a vehicle travels a certain distance at speed x and then returns the same distance at speed y, its average speed for the round trip is the harmonic mean of x and y, not their arithmetic mean. For example, travelling out at 60 km/h and back at 40 km/h gives an average speed of 48 km/h. The same logic applies to any rate averaged over a fixed amount of the denominator quantity — for instance, average fuel economy in litres per 100 km computed over fixed distances, or the average price paid for a security bought in fixed currency amounts at different prices (as in dollar-cost averaging).

Physics and engineering

In physics, the harmonic mean arises when combining quantities that add reciprocally. Resistors connected in parallel, or capacitors in series, combine by the sum of reciprocals, so the equivalent value is proportional to a harmonic mean. The reduced mass of a two-body system and the thin-lens equation involve the same structure. In hydrology and fluid mechanics, harmonic means are used for averaging hydraulic conductivity across layered media perpendicular to flow. The F1 score used in information retrieval and machine learning is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, chosen because it penalises large imbalances between the two.

Other uses

In finance, the harmonic mean is recommended for averaging multiples such as the price–earnings ratio, because it gives equal weight to each data point in a way the arithmetic mean does not. In population genetics and in the computation of average population sizes fluctuating over generations, the harmonic mean captures the disproportionate effect of small (bottleneck) values. In geometry, the harmonic mean appears in the properties of harmonic quadrilaterals and in Apollonian circle packings.

History

The harmonic mean was known to the Pythagoreans, who studied the three "proportions" — arithmetic, geometric, and harmonic (originally called the "subcontrary" mean) — in connection with music theory. The ratios of the harmonic mean relate to the intervals of the octave, fifth, and fourth: for strings of lengths 6 and 12, the harmonic mean is 8, producing the characteristic consonances of Greek music. The mean was transmitted through Boethius and medieval works on arithmetic and music, and it acquired its present name from this musical association with harmonics.
