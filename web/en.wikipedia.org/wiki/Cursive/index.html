Cursive

Cursive (also known as script, longhand or joined-up writing) is any style of penmanship in which characters are written joined in a flowing manner, generally for the purpose of making writing faster, in contrast to block letters. It varies in its formal standards and can be highly stylized, as in calligraphy, or casual to the point of illegibility.

Cursive handwriting developed historically from the need to write quickly and legibly. The word "cursive" derives from the Latin cursivus, meaning "running," from currere, "to run."

History

Cursive forms of writing appeared in ancient Egypt (hieratic and demotic scripts), ancient Rome (Roman cursive), and in Chinese calligraphy. Medieval European scribes used cursive hands to copy manuscripts efficiently. In the 19th and 20th centuries, standardized cursive systems were taught in schools in many countries, including the Palmer Method and the D'Nealian script in the United States and Spencerian script before them.

Styles

Formal cursive is generally joined, while casual cursive mixes joins and lifts of the pen, sometimes combining print and cursive forms. Some cursive scripts, such as those used in German schools historically (Kurrent and Sütterlin), differ markedly from Latin-based forms. Other languages have their own cursive traditions, including Cyrillic handwriting and Arabic script, which is inherently cursive.

In education

Cursive instruction declined in many school systems in the early 21st century as keyboards and digital devices became widespread; the Common Core standards in the United States did not require cursive. However, several U.S. states and other jurisdictions have since reintroduced requirements to teach cursive, citing benefits for motor skills, literacy, and the ability to read historical documents.
