Interstitial cystitis (IC), also called bladder pain syndrome, is a chronic condition characterised by pelvic pain, pressure or discomfort perceived to be related to the urinary bladder, accompanied by urinary urgency and frequency, in the absence of infection or other identifiable causes. It affects women considerably more often than men.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically include a persistent urge to urinate, frequent urination in small amounts day and night (nocturia), and pain that worsens as the bladder fills and eases after voiding. Pain may be felt in the pelvis, urethra, vulva or perineum, and during sexual intercourse. Symptoms characteristically wax and wane, with flares triggered by stress, certain foods and drinks (such as coffee, alcohol, citrus and spicy foods), and hormonal changes.

Causes and diagnosis

The cause is unknown. Proposed mechanisms include a defect in the protective glycosaminoglycan layer of the bladder lining, mast cell activation, autoimmune processes and neurogenic inflammation. Some patients have Hunner lesions — inflamed areas of bladder wall visible on cystoscopy — which define a subtype with distinct features. Diagnosis is clinical and one of exclusion, after ruling out urinary tract infection, bladder cancer, endometriosis and other conditions; questionnaires and symptom scales are used to support assessment.

Treatment

There is no cure, and treatment is aimed at symptom relief, usually in a stepwise fashion: dietary modification, stress management, pelvic floor physiotherapy and behavioural therapy first, then oral medicines such as amitriptyline or pentosan polysulfate, bladder instillations, and for refractory cases procedures such as hydrodistension, fulguration of Hunner lesions, botulinum toxin injections or, rarely, major surgery. The condition is associated with other chronic pain syndromes, including irritable bowel syndrome and fibromyalgia, and can substantially impair quality of life.
