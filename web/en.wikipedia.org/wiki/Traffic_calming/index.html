Traffic calming
Purpose: Reduce vehicle speed and through-traffic
Measures: Speed humps, chicanes, curb extensions, woonerf
Origins: Netherlands, 1970s (Delft)

Traffic calming uses physical design and other measures to improve safety for motorists, pedestrians and cyclists, by slowing traffic and reducing its volume, especially in residential streets.

Measures

Vertical deflections include speed bumps, speed humps (gentler), speed cushions and raised crosswalks. Horizontal deflections include chicanes, curb extensions (bulb-outs), traffic circles and medians. Narrowing lanes, street trees, and shared-space designs — such as the Dutch woonerf, pioneered in Delft in the 1970s — encourage driver caution psychologically.

Effects and debate

Studies associate traffic calming with fewer and less severe collisions and better conditions for walking and cycling. Critics raise concerns about emergency-vehicle delay, traffic displacement to other streets, noise, and effects on buses. It is a component of Vision Zero road-safety strategies.
