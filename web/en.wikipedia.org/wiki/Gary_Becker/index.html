Gary Stanley Becker (December 2, 1930 – May 3, 2014) was an American economist who received the 1992 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. He was a professor of economics and sociology at the University of Chicago and one of the most influential social scientists of the past century.

Becker pioneered the application of economic analysis to areas of human behaviour traditionally studied by sociologists and demographers: racial discrimination, crime and punishment, the allocation of time, marriage, divorce and family life, and drug addiction.

Work

His first book, The Economics of Discrimination (1957), modelled prejudice as a "taste" for which employers, employees and customers pay a price. Human Capital (1964) analysed education and training as investments and transformed education policy and labour economics. A Treatise on the Family (1981) applied maximising behaviour and markets to marriage, fertility and household production. His 1968 paper "Crime and Punishment: An Economic Approach" founded the economic analysis of crime.

Recognition

Becker won the John Bates Clark Medal in 1967 and the Nobel Memorial Prize in 1992 "for having extended the domain of microeconomic analysis to a wide range of human behaviour and interaction, including nonmarket behaviour". He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2007.
