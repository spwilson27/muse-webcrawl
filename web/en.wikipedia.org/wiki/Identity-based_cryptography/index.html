Identity-based cryptography

Identity-based cryptography is a type of public-key cryptography in which a user's public key is derived directly from a unique identity string — such as an email address, a telephone number, or a name — rather than from a randomly generated key published in a digital certificate. A trusted third party called the Private Key Generator (PKG) holds a master secret and issues each user the private key corresponding to their identity. This eliminates the need for certificate directories and lets senders encrypt a message for a recipient before that recipient has even obtained their private key.

History

The concept was proposed by Adi Shamir in 1984, who constructed an identity-based signature scheme but was unable to realize identity-based encryption. Practical identity-based encryption (IBE) was achieved only in 2001, when Dan Boneh and Matthew Franklin built a secure scheme from the Weil pairing on elliptic curves, and Clifford Cocks independently developed a scheme based on quadratic residues that had been devised earlier at GCHQ. Boneh and Franklin's work sparked extensive research into pairing-based cryptography.

How it works

A typical IBE system has four algorithms: Setup, in which the PKG generates public parameters and a master secret; Extract, in which the PKG computes a user's private key from their identity string; Encrypt, which uses the identity string itself as the public key; and Decrypt, which uses the issued private key. Identity-based signature (IBS) schemes follow the same pattern for signing.

Advantages and drawbacks

Because public keys are self-describing, identity-based systems simplify key management in closed environments such as enterprises, and naturally support features like key expiry by embedding dates in identity strings (e.g., "alice@example.com|2026"). However, the PKG can compute every user's private key, an inherent key escrow problem that makes the PKG a high-value target and removes non-repudiation for signatures. Revocation is also awkward: a compromised identity generally cannot be revoked without changing the identity string itself. Techniques such as hierarchical IBE and distributed PKGs mitigate but do not eliminate these issues.

Applications

Identity-based encryption has been deployed in enterprise email encryption products and standardized in IEEE 1363.3 and in IETF RFCs 5091 and 5408–5410. Related primitives include certificateless cryptography and attribute-based encryption, which generalize the notion of identity to sets of attributes.
