Inflaton

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The inflaton field would have driven the exponential expansion of cosmic inflation.

The inflaton is a hypothetical scalar field that may be responsible for cosmic inflation in the very early universe. During the inflationary epoch, thought to have occurred around 10−36 to 10−32 seconds after the Big Bang, the potential energy of the inflaton field dominated the cosmos and drove a period of near-exponential expansion, enlarging the universe by a factor of at least 1026.

Role in inflation

As the inflaton slowly rolls down its potential-energy curve ("slow-roll" inflation), its nearly constant energy density acts like a cosmological constant, accelerating expansion. Quantum fluctuations in the field are stretched to cosmic scales and become the seeds of galaxies, producing the nearly scale-invariant spectrum of temperature fluctuations measured in the cosmic microwave background by COBE, WMAP, and Planck.

Reheating

When the field reaches the bottom of its potential it oscillates and decays into ordinary particles in a process called reheating, converting vacuum energy into the hot plasma of the conventional Big Bang model. The inflaton's identity is unknown: candidates range from the Higgs boson (in Higgs inflation) to fields arising in string theory and grand unified theories.

Observational tests

Measurements of the scalar spectral index (ns ≈ 0.965) and the absence so far of primordial gravitational waves (tensor-to-scalar ratio r < 0.036 from BICEP/Keck) already rule out the simplest potentials, favouring plateau-like models such as Starobinsky's R² inflation.
