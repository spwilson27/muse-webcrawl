Multiregional origin of modern humans

The multiregional hypothesis is a scientific model that provides an alternative explanation to the more widely accepted "recent African origin" model (also called the "Out of Africa" model) for the pattern of human evolution. The multiregional hypothesis holds that the evolution of anatomically modern humans has been occurring within a continuous human range since the dispersal of early Homo populations out of Africa roughly two million years ago.

Overview

Proposed in its modern form by Milford H. Wolpoff, Alan Thorne, and others in the 1980s, the model argues that regional populations of archaic humans in Africa, Europe, and Asia evolved in parallel into modern humans while exchanging genes through continuous gene flow, so that the species remained unified while retaining regional skeletal features. Supporters point to apparent morphological continuity in fossil sequences in regions such as East Asia and Australasia.

Genetic evidence

The majority of genetic evidence — including mitochondrial DNA, Y-chromosome studies, and whole-genome analyses — supports a primarily recent African origin for modern humans, with limited admixture from archaic populations such as Neanderthals and Denisovans. This has led many researchers to favor an intermediate position sometimes called "assimilation" or the "mostly out of Africa" model, in which a largely African origin is combined with modest regional interbreeding.

History of the model

Ideas of regional continuity trace back to Franz Weidenreich's work in the 1940s and Carleton Coon's "candelabra" model of the 1960s, though the modern multiregional hypothesis explicitly rejects independent, separate evolution in favor of a single interconnected species.
