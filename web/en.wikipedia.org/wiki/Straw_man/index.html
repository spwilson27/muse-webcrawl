Straw man

A straw man fallacy (sometimes written as strawman) is the informal fallacy of refuting an argument different from the one actually under discussion, while not recognising or acknowledging the distinction. One who engages in this fallacy is said to be "attacking a straw man".

The typical straw man argument creates the illusion of having refuted or defeated an opponent's proposition through the covert replacement of it with a different proposition (i.e., "stand up a straw man") and the subsequent refutation of that false argument ("knock down a straw man") instead of the opponent's proposition. Straw man arguments have been used throughout history in polemical debate, particularly regarding highly charged emotional subjects.

Structure

The straw man fallacy generally has the following structure:

  1. Person 1 asserts proposition X.
  2. Person 2 argues against a superficially similar proposition Y, as if an argument against Y were an argument against X.

This reasoning is fallacious because arguing against Y is not the same as arguing against X.

History

The phrase may originate from the practice of using human-shaped straw dummies in military training, or from scarecrows. The metaphor entered English-language discussion of logical fallacies by the late 19th and early 20th centuries. A related technique is the "steel man" argument, in which one instead presents the strongest version of an opponent's position before responding to it.
