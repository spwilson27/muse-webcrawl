The Winter War was a military conflict between the Soviet Union and Finland. It began with a Soviet invasion of Finland on 30 November 1939, three months after the outbreak of the Second World War, and ended three and a half months later with the Moscow Peace Treaty on 13 March 1940. The League of Nations deemed the attack illegal and expelled the Soviet Union, its last significant act.

Despite vastly superior numbers and materiel, the Red Army suffered heavy losses — by some estimates over 300,000 casualties against some 70,000 Finnish — against defenders employing motti tactics, ski mobility and intimate knowledge of the terrain in temperatures as low as −43 °C. The humiliating Soviet performance encouraged Hitler's belief that the USSR could be quickly defeated. Finland ceded about 9 percent of its territory, including Karelia and its second city, Viipuri, but preserved its independence and democracy.

Background

Under the secret protocol of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, Finland fell within the Soviet sphere. Moscow demanded territory on the Karelian Isthmus, islands in the Gulf of Finland and a base at Hanko to protect Leningrad. Finland refused the full demands; the Soviets staged the shelling of Mainila as a pretext and invaded without a declaration of war.

Course of the war

Soviet offensives against the Mannerheim Line stalled in December 1939 amid bungled logistics and purges-weakened command. Finnish counterattacks at Tolvajärvi, Suomussalmi and the Raate Road destroyed entire Soviet divisions. In February 1940 Timoshenko's reorganised offensive, using massed artillery, finally broke the Mannerheim Line at Summa, forcing Finland to negotiate before its reserves were exhausted.

Motti tactics and notable actions

Motti (from a Finnish word for a pile of firewood) described cutting road-bound Soviet columns into isolated pockets and destroying them piecemeal. The sniper Simo Häyhä, "the White Death", was credited with over 500 kills. Foreign volunteers arrived from Sweden and elsewhere, but Allied intervention plans came too late.

Peace and consequences

The Moscow Peace Treaty ceded Finnish Karelia, Salla, part of the Rybachy Peninsula and the Hanko lease; over 400,000 Karelians were evacuated and resettled. The war fed Finnish alignment with Germany in the Continuation War of 1941–44, and Stalin's experience prompted sweeping Red Army reforms.
