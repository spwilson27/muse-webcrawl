Nonsense literature


Labelled placeholder: Wonderland illustration

Nonsense literature is a broad categorisation of literature that uses sensical and nonsensical elements to defy language conventions or logical coherence, and that exploits the playfulness of language. The effect of nonsense is often caused by an excess of meaning, rather than a lack of it. Nonsense literature is well established in literature, most notable being those of Lewis Carroll and Edward Lear in the 19th century, but its roots lie in folklore, nursery rhymes and older literary traditions.

Nonsense is a form of literary play, involving the balancing of sense and nonsense in order to tease and delight the reader. It should be distinguished from mere gibberish or absurdity: nonsense literature typically maintains strict formal structures — rhyme, metre, syntax and narrative logic — while subverting semantics and reference.

History

Elements of nonsense appear in Aesop's fables, medieval riddles, nursery rhymes ("Hey Diddle Diddle"), and the works of François Rabelais. The genre crystallised in Victorian England with Edward Lear's A Book of Nonsense (1846), popularising the limerick, and Lewis Carroll's Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865), Through the Looking-Glass (1871) and the poem "Jabberwocky."

Twentieth century and after

Nonsense elements run through the works of Edward Gorey, Spike Milligan, Dr. Seuss, Mervyn Peake, Flannery O'Connor's lighter pieces, the Dada movement, and the theatre of the absurd. Scholars such as Elizabeth Sewell (The Field of Nonsense, 1952) and Susan Stewart (Nonsense, 1979) have analysed its logic and cultural role; Hugh Haughton's anthology The Chatto Book of Nonsense Poetry (1988) surveys the field.

Characteristics

Common devices include invented words (portmanteaus and nonce words), impossible creatures and places, logical puzzles, lists, wordplay, parody of scholarly apparatus, and illustrations that heighten the absurdity.
