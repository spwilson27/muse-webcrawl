Oviraptorosauria

Oviraptorosauria ("egg thief lizards") is a group of feathered maniraptoran theropod dinosaurs from the Cretaceous period of Asia and North America. They are characterized by short, deep skulls — often with prominent crests — and toothless, beak-like jaws (except in the earliest forms), parrot-like heads, and evidence of brooding behaviour and extensive feather coverings.

Name and history

The name derives from Oviraptor philoceratops, described in 1924 from a specimen found atop a nest of eggs in Mongolia's Gobi Desert and presumed to have been stealing them. Decades later, similar nests were found to belong to oviraptorosaurs themselves, and specimens of Citipati were discovered fossilized in a bird-like brooding posture over their own eggs — the supposed thief was a parent. The name, however, remains.

Groups and diversity

Major subgroups include the early, toothed Incisivosaurus and Caudipteridae; the mostly Asian Caenagnathoidea; and the Oviraptoridae proper, such as Oviraptor, Citipati, Khaan and the huge Gigantoraptor (about 8 metres long). North American caenagnathids include Anzu wyliei and Chirostenotes. Their powerful beaks are thought to have been used for a varied diet — plants, molluscs or small prey — debated from stomach contents including gastroliths and lizard remains.

Biology

Oviraptorosaur eggs are elongated (Elongatoolithidae) and laid in paired arcs in ring-shaped nests; their blue-green pigmented eggshells are among the few known examples of dinosaur egg colour. Some specimens preserve pygostyles and fossil feathers suggesting display fans on the tail.
