Scale invariance

In physics, mathematics, statistics and economics, scale invariance is a feature of objects or laws that does not change if scales of length, energy, or other variables are multiplied by a common factor. A system is scale-invariant when its statistical or dynamical properties look the same at different magnifications; the associated transformations are called dilations or scaling transformations. Scale invariance is closely connected with power laws, fractals and self-similarity.

Self-similar structures such as fractals display the same pattern at every magnification — the hallmark of scale invariance.

In physics

Scale invariance emerges at second-order phase transitions, where the correlation length diverges and the system becomes critical: fluctuations occur on all length scales, described by the renormalisation group and universal critical exponents. In field theory, a theory with no intrinsic mass scale — such as classical electromagnetism — is scale invariant, though quantum effects can break the symmetry through the trace anomaly. Conformal field theories extend scale invariance to full conformal symmetry and underlie much of modern theoretical physics.

In other fields

In statistics, scale-invariant distributions include heavy-tailed power laws: city sizes, incomes (the Pareto distribution), earthquake energies and word frequencies show similar statistics at different scales. Fractal geometry, pioneered by Benoit Mandelbrot, formalises self-similar scale-free structures in coastlines, clouds, and financial time series. Scale invariance also appears in cosmology (a nearly scale-invariant primordial spectrum of density fluctuations, as observed in the cosmic microwave background) and in biology's allometric scaling laws.
