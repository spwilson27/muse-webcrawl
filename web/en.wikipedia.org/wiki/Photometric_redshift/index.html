Photometric redshift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Photometric vs. spectroscopic redshift comparison.

A photometric redshift is an estimate of the redshift (and hence the distance) of an astronomical object such as a galaxy, obtained from its brightness measured through a set of broadband filters — its photometry — rather than from a full spectrum. Because imaging a field through a few filters is far faster than taking spectra of every object, photometric redshifts make it possible to estimate distances for hundreds of millions of galaxies in large surveys.

Methods

The technique exploits broad features in galaxy spectra, most importantly the 4000 Å break and the Lyman break, which shift predictably through the filter set as redshift increases. Two families of methods are used:

  • Template fitting — observed fluxes are compared with a library of galaxy spectral templates redshifted over a grid of values; the best-fitting combination gives the redshift and galaxy type.
  • Empirical/machine-learning methods — algorithms are trained on galaxies with known spectroscopic redshifts to map colours to redshift.

Typical accuracies for bright galaxies are a few percent in (1+z), with a minority of "catastrophic outliers" where breaks are confused. Precise photometric redshifts are a key requirement for weak-lensing cosmology surveys such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST and ESA's Euclid mission.
