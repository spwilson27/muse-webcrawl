Mozarabic Rite

Mozarabic Rite
Also calledHispanic Rite, Visigothic Rite, Rite of Toledo
OriginVisigothic Spain, by the 6th–7th centuries
LanguageLatin (with Mozarabic chant)
Present useToledo Cathedral (Mozarabic Chapel) and a small number of parishes

The Mozarabic Rite, also called the Hispanic or Visigothic Rite, is the liturgy of the Catholic Church that developed in the Iberian Peninsula under the Visigoths and was preserved by the Mozarabs — Christians living under Muslim rule in al-Andalus. It is one of the historic Latin liturgical rites distinct from the Roman Rite.

History

Standardised by councils of Toledo and figures such as Isidore of Seville, Leander and Ildefonsus, the rite has a distinctive calendar, its own chant tradition, and characteristic prayers (such as the illatio, corresponding to the Roman preface). After the Christian reconquest of much of Spain and pressure for Roman uniformity, the Roman Rite progressively replaced it from the eleventh century: legend holds that the rival missals were submitted to trial by fire and combat. When Toledo was recaptured in 1085, six parishes were permitted to keep the old rite.

Survival and revival

Around 1500 Cardinal Cisneros rescued the rite from oblivion, founding the Mozarabic Chapel in Toledo Cathedral, having surviving manuscripts edited and printed (the Missale Mixtum of 1500 and Breviary of 1502), and endowing chaplains to celebrate it daily — a practice that continues. Following the Second Vatican Council the rite was revised and promulgated anew (Missale Hispano-Mozarabicum, 1991), and its use was extended in the Archdiocese of Toledo; Pope John Paul II celebrated it in Rome in 1992, the first pope to do so.
