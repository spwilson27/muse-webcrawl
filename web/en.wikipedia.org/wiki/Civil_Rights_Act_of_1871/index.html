Civil Rights Act of 1871

Enacted: 20 April 1871
Other short title: Ku Klux Klan Act; Third Enforcement Act
Key surviving provision: 42 U.S.C. § 1983

The Civil Rights Act of 1871 (17 Stat. 13), also known as the Ku Klux Klan Act or Third Enforcement Act, is an Act of the United States Congress which empowered the President to suspend the writ of habeas corpus to combat the Ku Klux Klan and other terrorist organisations during Reconstruction. The act was requested by President Ulysses S. Grant and was passed within three months of its introduction.

Provisions

The act authorised the federal government to prosecute conspiracies to deprive citizens of their constitutional rights, and permitted the President to use the army and suspend habeas corpus in districts declared to be in insurrection. Grant used these powers in nine South Carolina counties in late 1871, and federal prosecutions broke the first Klan's power in the South.

Its first section, codified today at 42 U.S.C. § 1983, creates a private right of action against any person who, acting “under color of” state law, deprives another of rights secured by the Constitution or federal law. Dormant for nearly a century, § 1983 was revived by the Supreme Court in Monroe v. Pape (1961) and became the principal vehicle for civil rights litigation against state and local officials, including police officers — subject to doctrines such as qualified immunity. Another section survives as 42 U.S.C. § 1985, concerning conspiracies to interfere with civil rights.

History and legacy

The act was the third of the Enforcement Acts passed to protect African Americans' rights under the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments against Klan violence. While its criminal provisions fell into disuse after Reconstruction, § 1983 remains one of the most litigated statutes in American law.
