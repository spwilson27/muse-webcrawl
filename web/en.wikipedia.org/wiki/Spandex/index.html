Spandex

Spandex, Lycra, or elastane is a synthetic fiber known for its exceptional elasticity. It is a polyether-polyurea copolymer that was invented in 1958 by chemist Joseph Shivers at DuPont's Benger Laboratory in Waynesboro, Virginia, and introduced commercially in 1962 under the trade name Lycra. The name "spandex" is an anagram of the word "expands".

Properties

Spandex fibers can be stretched to more than five times their length and recover their original shape without significant loss of strength. The fiber is composed of long polymer chains containing at least 85% segmented polyurethane, with alternating rigid and flexible segments: the soft segments allow stretching while the hard segments provide strength and recovery. Spandex is stronger and more durable than natural rubber, resists body oils, perspiration, and many chemicals, but can be damaged by chlorine and heat.

Uses

Spandex is almost never used alone; small percentages blended with cotton, wool, nylon, or polyester give garments stretch and shape retention. Applications include athletic wear, swimwear, cycling shorts, leggings, skinny jeans, underwear, hosiery, compression garments, and foundation garments. Its adoption transformed the apparel industry beginning in the 1960s and accelerated with the aerobics and fitness boom of the 1980s.

Production

The fiber is produced mainly by dry spinning: a prepolymer formed from a macroglycol and a diisocyanate is chain-extended with a diamine, then extruded through spinnerets and dried as the solvent evaporates. Major producers include Inviya, Hyosung (Creora), and The Lycra Company.
