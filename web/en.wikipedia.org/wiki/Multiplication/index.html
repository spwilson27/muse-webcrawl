Multiplication

Multiplication (often denoted by the cross symbol ×, the mid-line dot operator ·, juxtaposition, or, on computers, an asterisk *) is one of the four elementary mathematical operations of arithmetic, the others being addition, subtraction, and division. The result of a multiplication operation is called a product.

Definition

The multiplication of whole numbers may be thought of as repeated addition; that is, the multiplication of two numbers is equivalent to adding as many copies of one of them, the multiplicand, as the quantity of the other one, the multiplier; both numbers together are called factors. For example, 4 × 3 = 12 can be seen as 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 or as 4 + 4 + 4. The operation extends naturally to rational numbers, real numbers, complex numbers, and to abstract structures such as matrices, vectors, polynomials, and elements of rings and fields, where the definition is no longer literally repeated addition.

Properties

  • Commutative: a × b = b × a.
  • Associative: (a × b) × c = a × (b × c).
  • Distributive over addition: a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c.
  • Identity element: 1, since a × 1 = a; multiplication by 0 always gives 0.

Computation

Historically, multiplication was performed with tables, abaci, Napier's bones, slide rules, and methods such as lattice multiplication and Egyptian doubling. In schools, long multiplication based on the distributive law and memorized times tables remains standard. Modern computers implement multiplication in hardware, and a long line of research on multiplication algorithms — from Karatsuba's algorithm to FFT-based methods — concerns multiplying very large integers efficiently.

Notation and terminology

The cross symbol × was introduced by William Oughtred in the 17th century; the dot operator is common in higher mathematics, while juxtaposition of variables (ab) is standard in algebra. In computing, the asterisk * is used in most programming languages. The terms multiplicand and multiplier are little used today because multiplication is commutative for numbers, and both operands are usually called factors.
