Western Australia (WA) is a state occupying the western third of the landmass of Australia. It is the second-largest country subdivision in the world, larger than most nations, yet home to fewer than three million people, over three-quarters of them in the capital, Perth — one of the most isolated major cities on Earth, closer to Jakarta than to Sydney. The state's size spans tropical Kimberley in the north, vast deserts (Great Victoria, Gibson, Great Sandy), and the Mediterranean-climate southwest, a global biodiversity hotspot.

History and economy

Aboriginal peoples have lived in WA for at least 45,000 years (the Kimberley has some of the world's oldest rock art). Dutch navigators charted the coast in the 1600s — Dirk Hartog landed in 1616, and wrecks like the Batavia (1629) littered the "South Land" coast — but Britain claimed the Swan River Colony only in 1829. Gold rushes at Kalgoorlie and Coolgardie in the 1890s transformed the colony, which joined the Commonwealth of Australia in 1901 somewhat reluctantly. The modern economy rides on mining: the Pilbara produces roughly half the world's seaborne iron ore; WA is also a top gold and LNG producer, and its mining royalties underwrite Australia's trade balance. Perth, founded 1829 and booming with each resources cycle, sits on the Swan River (Derbarl Yerrigan) between the Indian Ocean and the Darling Range, near the Great Barrier Reef's western counterpart ecosystems of Ningaloo Reef.
