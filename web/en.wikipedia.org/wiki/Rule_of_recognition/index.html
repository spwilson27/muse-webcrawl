Rule of recognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In H. L. A. Hart's jurisprudence, the rule of recognition is the master secondary rule of a legal system: the social rule, practised by officials, that specifies the criteria a norm must satisfy to count as law in that system. In the United Kingdom it is (roughly) that what the King in Parliament enacts is law; in the United States, that what accords with the Constitution's procedures is law. Unlike other rules, the rule of recognition is neither valid nor invalid — it is accepted, existing as a matter of convergent official practice viewed from the internal point of view (Hart's distinction between merely habitual conformity and rule-following acceptance).

The concept does the key work in The Concept of Law (1961): it grounds legal validity without recourse to morality or to Kelsen's presupposed Grundnorm, explains the continuity of legal systems through changes of rulers, and marks the transition from a “pre-legal” regime of primary rules to a legal system proper. Critics (Dworkin above all) objected that it cannot account for principles whose authority is not pedigree-based, and that in hard cases officials disagree about the criteria themselves — the debate that organised Anglophone jurisprudence for a generation.
