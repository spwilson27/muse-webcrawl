War on terror

The war on terror, officially the Global War on Terrorism (GWOT), is a global military campaign launched by the United States and its allies following the September 11 attacks in 2001. The phrase was first used by U.S. President George W. Bush on 16 September 2001 and formally elaborated in a speech to Congress on 20 September, in which he declared that the war "will not end until every terrorist group of global reach has been found, stopped and defeated."

The war in Afghanistan (2001–2021) was the longest-running component of the war on terror.

Background

On 11 September 2001, al-Qaeda hijackers carried out coordinated attacks on the World Trade Center in New York City and the Pentagon, killing 2,977 people. The U.S. Congress passed the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) on 18 September 2001, authorising the president to use force against those responsible and associated forces — a legal basis invoked for operations in numerous countries for decades afterwards.

Major campaigns

The first campaign was the invasion of Afghanistan in October 2001 (Operation Enduring Freedom), which overthrew the Taliban regime that had sheltered al-Qaeda; the ensuing war lasted until the Taliban returned to power following the U.S. withdrawal in August 2021. In March 2003 the United States led an invasion of Iraq, justified in part by alleged links between Saddam Hussein's regime and terrorism and by claims of weapons of mass destruction that proved unfounded; the Iraq War formally ended for U.S. combat forces in 2011, but U.S. forces returned from 2014 to fight the Islamic State (ISIS), which had seized large parts of Iraq and Syria. Other components included operations in Pakistan's tribal areas (including extensive drone strikes), the Philippines, the Horn of Africa, the Trans-Sahara, and Yemen. On 2 May 2011, U.S. Navy SEALs killed al-Qaeda leader Osama bin Laden in Abbottabad, Pakistan.

Domestic and legal measures

The war on terror brought sweeping domestic changes in the United States, including the USA PATRIOT Act, the creation of the Department of Homeland Security, expanded surveillance programmes, the detention camp at Guantanamo Bay, "enhanced interrogation" techniques widely characterised as torture, and military commissions for trying captured suspects.

Costs and criticism

The Costs of War Project at Brown University has estimated the post-9/11 wars' cost at roughly $8 trillion and the direct and indirect death toll in the major war zones in the millions, with tens of millions displaced. Critics argue the campaign's broad framing, civilian casualties, torture and indefinite detention damaged U.S. standing and in some cases fuelled further extremism, while supporters credit it with dismantling al-Qaeda's core and preventing another attack on the scale of 9/11. The Obama administration retired the phrase "war on terror" in favour of "overseas contingency operations," but the underlying military and legal framework largely persisted.
