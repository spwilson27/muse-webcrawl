Iceland poppy

The Iceland poppy (Papaver nudicaule, syn. Oreomecon nudicaulis) is a boreal flowering plant in the poppy family, Papaveraceae. Native to subpolar regions of Asia and North America and to the mountains of Central Asia, it is widely grown as an ornamental for its delicate, papery, cup-shaped flowers.

Description

It is a hardy, short-lived perennial, often grown as a biennial or annual in gardens. The plant forms a basal rosette of bluish-green, hairy, pinnately lobed leaves, from which rise slender, leafless, hairy stems bearing solitary flowers. The flowers, about 5–7 cm across, come in shades of white, yellow, orange, salmon, and pink, with a boss of yellow stamens at the center. The species name nudicaule refers to its naked (leafless) flower stems.

Cultivation

Iceland poppies prefer cool climates, full sun, and sharply drained soil, and they often fail in hot, humid summers. Numerous cultivars have been selected, including the Champagne Bubbles series and the large-flowered 'Matador'. The flowers are popular in bouquets, though the stems are usually seared or scalded to prolong vase life. Despite the common name, wild Iceland poppies do not actually grow in Iceland; the name reflects their far-northern distribution generally.

Chemistry and taxonomy

Like other poppies, the plant contains alkaloids, in its case chiefly nudicaulins — unusual pigments responsible for the yellow-orange flower colors. Recent phylogenetic work has led some botanists to segregate the Arctic-alpine poppies into the genus Oreomecon.
