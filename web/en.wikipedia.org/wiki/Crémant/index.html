Crémant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crémant is a French sparkling wine made by the traditional method outside Champagne.

Crémant is a designation for French sparkling wines made by the traditional method (the same bottle-fermentation method used for Champagne) but produced in regions other than Champagne. The name originally described a softer, creamier style of Champagne with lower pressure; when the European Union restricted the use of the term méthode champenoise to Champagne itself, "Crémant" was adopted as the official appellation term for these regional sparkling wines.

Appellations

There are several Crémant appellations d'origine contrôlée (AOCs), each using its region's characteristic grape varieties:

  • Crémant d'Alsace — the largest by production, typically from Pinot Blanc, Riesling, Pinot Gris, Auxerrois, and Chardonnay.
  • Crémant de Bourgogne — from Burgundy, mainly Chardonnay and Pinot Noir.
  • Crémant de Loire — from the Loire Valley, often Chenin Blanc based.
  • Crémant de Bordeaux, Crémant du Jura, Crémant de Limoux, Crémant de Die, and Crémant de Savoie.

Outside France, the term is also used under EU rules in Luxembourg (Crémant de Luxembourg) and in parts of Belgium and Germany.

Production

Crémant wines must be made by the traditional method: a still base wine undergoes a second fermentation in the bottle, followed by ageing on the lees (a minimum of nine months under French AOC rules) and riddling and disgorgement. Grapes must be hand-harvested and pressed gently, and the finished wine has a slightly lower pressure (typically around 3.5–5 atmospheres) than Champagne, giving a softer mousse. Most Crémant is white; rosé versions, coloured by brief skin contact or blending, are also produced, especially in Alsace and Burgundy.

Character

Because they use regional grapes and are generally produced in a fresher, fruit-driven style, Crémants are often regarded as good-value alternatives to Champagne. They range from bone dry (brut nature and extra brut) to off-dry (demi-sec), and are served as aperitifs and with seafood, charcuterie, and celebrations.
