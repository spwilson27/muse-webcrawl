Negative income tax

A negative income tax (NIT) is a welfare system in which people earning below a certain threshold receive supplementary payments from the government instead of paying taxes. The idea was popularised by economist Milton Friedman in his 1962 book Capitalism and Freedom, though a similar proposal had been made earlier by Juliet Rhys-Williams in the 1940s. Supporters argue that an NIT would replace the patchwork of welfare programmes with a single, simple, automatically administered income floor while preserving incentives to work.

How it works

The system specifies a guaranteed minimum income, a tax rate, and a break-even income at which no tax is paid and no supplement received. A household with no earnings receives the full guarantee; as earnings rise, the supplement is reduced by a fraction of each additional unit earned (the taper rate), so total income always increases with work. Above the break-even point, the household pays ordinary positive income tax. For example, with a guarantee of $10,000 and a 50% taper, a person earning $8,000 would receive a supplement of $6,000, for a total of $14,000.

History and experiments

President Richard Nixon's Family Assistance Plan (1969) incorporated NIT principles but failed in the Senate. Between 1968 and 1982 the United States and Canada ran large-scale negative income tax experiments — in New Jersey, Gary (Indiana), Seattle and Denver, and Manitoba (Mincome) — to measure effects on work effort, health, and family stability. The experiments found modest reductions in labour supply, concentrated among secondary earners and young people staying in school longer, and in the Manitoba site, improvements in health outcomes including reduced hospitalisations. The results informed later policy but no full national NIT was adopted; the US Earned Income Tax Credit (1975) is a related wage-subsidy scheme restricted to workers.

Arguments

Advocates, from Friedman on the free-market right to many on the left, praise the NIT for transparency, low administrative cost, absence of stigma, and avoidance of the "welfare cliff" in which earning more can leave a household worse off. Critics question the fiscal cost of a guarantee high enough to live on, the work-disincentive of high taper rates (which act as a high marginal tax rate on the poor), and whether cash alone substitutes for services such as housing and healthcare. The NIT is a close relative of universal basic income; the two converge in net effect when a UBI is financed through the tax system.
