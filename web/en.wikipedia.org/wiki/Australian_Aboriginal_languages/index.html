The Australian Aboriginal languages consist of around 290–363 languages belonging to an estimated 19 language families, spoken by Aboriginal Australians. At the time of European settlement in 1788 there were roughly 250 distinct languages with hundreds of dialects. The vast majority belong to the Pama–Nyungan family, which covers some seven-eighths of the continent; the remaining families, concentrated in the far north (Arnhem Land and the Kimberley), are grouped as non-Pama–Nyungan.

Characteristics

Australian languages share many phonological traits — typically a single series of stops, several nasal and lateral phonemes, three- or four-vowel systems, and an absence of fricatives — and grammatical features including ergative case marking in many languages, complex kinship vocabularies and, in some, avoidance registers ("mother-in-law language"). Whether all Australian families descend from a single proto-language remains unresolved.

Decline and revitalisation

Colonisation, dispossession and policies of assimilation and child removal devastated Aboriginal languages. Today most surviving languages are endangered, and only a minority — among them Warlpiri, Pitjantjatjara, Yolngu Matha and Arrernte — are still being acquired by children as a first language. Revitalisation efforts include language centres, bilingual school programmes, dictionaries and apps, and the reclamation of sleeping languages such as Kaurna from written records.
