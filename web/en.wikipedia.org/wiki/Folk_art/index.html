Folk art

FieldVisual art / material culture
CharacteristicsMade by self-taught or traditionally trained makers, often within a cultural community

Folk art covers objects made in a traditional style, usually by makers who learned through apprenticeship, family practice or community custom rather than academic training. It expresses the cultural identity of a group and is often both decorative and functional.

Characteristics

Folk art encompasses a wide range of media: painting, quilting and other textiles, woodcarving, pottery, metalwork, tole painting, weather vanes, trade signs and embroidered samplers. Unlike fine art produced for an art market, folk objects commonly serve everyday or ritual purposes — decorated furniture, devotional images such as Mexican ex-votos, toys, and festival objects — and designs are often passed down through generations.

Study and collecting

Antiquarian and modernist interest in folk art grew in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, when collectors valued its directness and abstraction. Institutions such as the American Folk Art Museum in New York preserve large collections. The category overlaps with, but is distinct from, "outsider art" and "naïve art", which typically describe individual self-taught artists working outside a shared tradition.
