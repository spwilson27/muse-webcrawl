Ragù

Tagliatelle al ragù, the classic Bolognese serving.

In Italian cuisine, ragù (from French ragoût) is a meat-based sauce that is commonly served with pasta. It is typically made by slowly simmering minced meat with a soffritto of onion, celery, and carrot, wine, and a small amount of tomato, for several hours until rich and thick.

Varieties

The best known is ragù alla bolognese from Bologna, whose traditional recipe — registered with the Bologna Chamber of Commerce in 1982 — uses beef (and sometimes pork), pancetta, milk, white wine, and little tomato; it is properly served with tagliatelle or used in lasagne, rather than with spaghetti. Ragù alla napoletana from Naples instead uses large pieces of meat simmered whole in a tomato-rich sauce, which are then removed and served as a second course. Other regional variants use lamb, duck, or wild boar.

Terminology

The word entered Italian from French in the 18th century, when French culinary influence was strong at Italian courts. Outside Italy, "spaghetti bolognese" has become a ubiquitous adaptation quite different from the Bolognese original. See also Bolognese sauce and Italian cuisine.
