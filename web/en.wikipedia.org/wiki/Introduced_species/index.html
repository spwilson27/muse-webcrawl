Introduced species

An introduced species — also called an alien, exotic or non-native species — is an organism that is not native to a given location but has been brought there by human activity, deliberately or accidentally, and has become established. Introduced species that spread aggressively and cause ecological or economic harm are termed invasive species, though most introductions remain localised and benign.

Pathways and examples

Humans have moved species for agriculture, horticulture, forestry, the pet trade, hunting and biological control, and accidentally via ballast water, hull fouling, packing materials and escaped livestock. Famous cases include rabbits and cane toads in Australia, kudzu and zebra mussels in North America, brown tree snakes on Guam, and rats, cats and stoats in New Zealand, where introduced predators devastated flightless bird faunas that had evolved without mammals.

Effects

Introduced species can prey upon, outcompete, hybridise with or transmit disease to native species, and can restructure food webs and ecosystem processes such as fire regimes and nutrient cycling. Invasions are considered a leading driver of modern extinctions, especially on islands, and impose major costs on agriculture, fisheries and infrastructure. Control methods range from biosecurity and quarantine to eradication campaigns, targeted trapping and controversial classical biological control.

Debates

Some ecologists argue that the blanket negative framing of non-native species is overstated, noting that many introductions integrate without catastrophe and may even provide ecosystem services; others caution that impacts can appear only after long lag phases. The "tens rule" roughly holds that only a small fraction of introduced species establish, and a small fraction of those become pests.
