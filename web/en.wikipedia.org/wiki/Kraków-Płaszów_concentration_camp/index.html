The Kraków-Płaszów concentration camp (German: Konzentrationslager Plaszow) was a Nazi concentration camp operated by the SS in Płaszów, a southern suburb of Kraków, in the General Government region of German-occupied Poland. Most of the prisoners were Polish Jews.

Construction of the camp began in the summer of 1942 on the site of two former Jewish cemeteries, using Jewish forced labor. It was originally established as a slave-labour camp of the armaments industry, and was formally converted into a concentration camp (Konzentrationslager) in January 1944. The camp supplied several arms factories with Jewish slave workers, and its best-known prisoners were the Jews employed at Oskar Schindler's factory, who were later immortalized in the film Schindler's List (1993).

History

Establishment

The camp opened in October 1942 as a forced labour camp (Zwangsarbeitslager) for Jews. It was situated on ground that had contained two Jewish cemeteries, and the tombstones were smashed and used as paving stones for the camp roads. Prisoners were housed in barracks surrounded by barbed wire, with guard towers manned by SS and auxiliary units.

Amon Göth

From February 1943 the camp was commanded by SS-Hauptsturmführer Amon Göth, a figure notorious for his brutality. Göth lived in a villa overlooking the camp and was known to shoot prisoners from his balcony. Following the liquidation of the Kraków Ghetto on 13–14 March 1943, many of its surviving inhabitants were transferred to Płaszów. Göth was arrested in September 1944 on charges of corruption and prisoner abuse, and was succeeded by Arnold Büscher. After the war Göth was tried in Kraków and hanged in September 1946 near the site of the camp.

Liquidation

As the Red Army advanced in the autumn of 1944, the camp was progressively evacuated. Bodies of prisoners who had been murdered in mass executions were exhumed and burned to conceal the crimes. The last transport of prisoners left in January 1945, days before the camp was liberated by Soviet forces on 20 January 1945.

Memorial

Today the camp site is a protected memorial area containing several monuments, including the Monument of Torn-Out Hearts (1964), and the remains of the "Grey House", used by the SS for torture. The area of the camp, including the sites of the former cemeteries, is largely overgrown open ground.
