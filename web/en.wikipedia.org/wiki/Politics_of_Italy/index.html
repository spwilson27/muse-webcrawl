Politics of Italy

The politics of Italy take place within the framework of a unitary parliamentary republic, established by the Constitution of 1948. The President of the Republic is the head of state, while the President of the Council of Ministers (prime minister) is the head of government and holds executive power. Legislative power is vested in a bicameral Parliament composed of the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate of the Republic.

Constitutional framework

The Italian Constitution, which entered into force on 1 January 1948, was drafted by a Constituent Assembly elected after the 1946 institutional referendum that abolished the monarchy. It establishes Italy as a democratic republic founded on labour, guarantees fundamental rights, and divides the state into regions, provinces and municipalities. A Constitutional Court reviews the constitutionality of laws. The Constitution's first twelve articles set out the fundamental principles of the Republic, including the inviolability of human rights, equality before the law, and the separation of church and state.

Executive branch

The President of the Republic is elected by Parliament in joint session, together with regional delegates, for a seven-year term. The President appoints the prime minister, promulgates laws, commands the armed forces, and may dissolve Parliament. The Council of Ministers, led by the President of the Council, directs the government. Since 1946 Italy has had frequent changes of government, though the broader party coalitions have often persisted across cabinets.

Legislative branch

Italy has a system of perfect bicameralism: the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate have equal powers, and both must approve legislation in identical form. Following a constitutional reform approved by referendum in 2020, the Chamber has 400 deputies and the Senate 200 elected senators, plus a small number of life senators. Members are elected for five-year terms under a mixed electoral system combining proportional representation with single-member districts.

Judiciary

The judiciary is independent of the executive and legislative branches. The highest court for most matters is the Supreme Court of Cassation. A self-governing body, the Superior Council of the Judiciary, oversees the appointment and discipline of judges and prosecutors.

Political parties and elections

For most of the post-war period (the so-called First Republic), politics was dominated by the Christian Democracy party, opposed by the Italian Communist Party, the largest communist party in Western Europe. The corruption investigations of the early 1990s (Tangentopoli) dissolved the old party system, giving way to the "Second Republic" of centre-right and centre-left coalitions. Prominent parties of the recent era include the Democratic Party, Forza Italia, the League, the Five Star Movement, and Brothers of Italy. Giorgia Meloni, leader of Brothers of Italy, became prime minister in October 2022, the first woman to hold the office.

Administrative divisions

Italy is divided into 20 regions, five of which enjoy special autonomous status: Sicily, Sardinia, Trentino-Alto Adige/Südtirol, Aosta Valley and Friuli-Venezia Giulia. Regions have their own statutes, councils and presidents, and exercise legislative power over matters not reserved to the state.

Foreign relations

Italy is a founding member of the European Union, NATO, and the G7. Rome hosts the headquarters of several United Nations agencies, including the Food and Agriculture Organization. See also foreign relations of Italy and elections in Italy.
