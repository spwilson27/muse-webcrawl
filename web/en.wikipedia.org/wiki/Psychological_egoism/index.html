Psychological egoism

Psychological egoism
FieldPhilosophy, psychology
TypeDescriptive thesis about motivation
Associated thinkersThomas Hobbes, Bernard Mandeville, Jeremy Bentham
Contrasted withPsychological altruism

Psychological egoism is the descriptive thesis that human beings are, as a matter of psychological fact, always motivated by self-interest — that every voluntary action is ultimately aimed at some benefit to the agent, such as pleasure, the avoidance of pain, or personal satisfaction. It is distinct from ethical egoism, which claims people ought to pursue their own interest: psychological egoism claims they cannot do otherwise.

History

Versions of the thesis appear in Thomas Hobbes's account of motivation in Leviathan (1651), in Bernard Mandeville's The Fable of the Bees (1714), which derived public benefits from private vices, and in the psychological hedonism of Jeremy Bentham, who held nature to have placed mankind under the governance of "two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure". The thesis gained renewed attention in the 20th century through debates in moral philosophy and, later, through its echo in the "selfish gene" framework in evolutionary biology.

Arguments and criticisms

Defenders appeal to intuition pumps — the rescuer's warm glow, the donor's satisfaction — arguing that every apparently altruistic act yields some self-regarding payoff. Critics, beginning with Bishop Joseph Butler's sermons (1726), reply that this confuses the object of a desire with the satisfaction that follows from fulfilling it: satisfaction presupposes a desire for something other than satisfaction itself, otherwise there would be nothing to satisfy. Philosophers such as Joel Feinberg have argued that in its strongest form the theory is unfalsifiable — any counterexample can be redescribed as self-interested — and therefore empirically empty. Empirical psychologists, notably C. Daniel Batson, designed experiments on empathy-induced helping intended to test whether genuinely other-regarding motivation exists, with results widely read as evidence for psychological altruism.

Relation to other theories

Psychological egoism, if true, would undermine ethical theories demanding self-sacrifice, since "ought implies can"; ethicists dispute both the premise and the inference. It is also a foil for rational-choice modelling in economics, which typically assumes self-interested agents as a methodological convenience rather than a universal law of motivation.
