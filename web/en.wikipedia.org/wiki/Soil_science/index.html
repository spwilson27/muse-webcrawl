Soil science

Soil science is the study of soil as a natural resource on the surface of the Earth, including soil formation, classification, and mapping; the physical, chemical, biological, and fertility properties of soils; and these properties in relation to the use and management of soils. It has two main branches: pedology, which studies soil in its natural setting, and edaphology, which studies soil in relation to plant growth and land use.

History

The Russian geologist Vasily Dokuchaev (1846–1903) is regarded as the founder of modern soil science. Studying the chernozem soils of the Russian steppe, he recognised soil as an independent natural body formed by the interaction of climate, organisms, parent material, relief, and time — the five soil-forming factors later formalised by Hans Jenny in his 1941 book Factors of Soil Formation. Dokuchaev's work led to soil classification systems based on soil genesis rather than geology alone.

Soil profile and horizons

A vertical cross-section of soil, the soil profile, reveals distinct layers called horizons: the organic O horizon; the topsoil A horizon; the leached E horizon; the subsoil B horizon, where materials accumulate; the parent material C horizon; and bedrock, the R horizon. The arrangement and properties of these horizons reflect the soil-forming processes at a site.

Soil properties

Soil scientists study texture (the proportions of sand, silt, and clay), structure, colour, soil pH, cation exchange capacity, organic matter content, moisture relations, and the soil biota — bacteria, fungi, earthworms, and other organisms that drive nutrient cycling.

Classification and survey

Soils are classified using systems such as USDA Soil Taxonomy, which recognises twelve soil orders, and the international World Reference Base for Soil Resources. National soil surveys map soil types and their suitability for agriculture, forestry, and construction, providing a foundation for land-use planning and soil conservation.

Applications

Soil science underpins agriculture and food security through fertiliser recommendations and soil health management; informs environmental protection, including carbon sequestration, erosion control, and remediation of contaminated land; and contributes to understanding climate change, since soils store more carbon than the atmosphere and vegetation combined.
