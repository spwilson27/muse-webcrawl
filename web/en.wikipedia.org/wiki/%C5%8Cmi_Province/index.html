Ōmi Province (近江国, Ōmi no kuni) was an old province of Japan, which today comprises Shiga Prefecture. It was one of the provinces of the Tōsandō circuit and was sometimes called Gōshū (江州). Ōmi bordered Wakasa and Echizen Provinces to the north, Mino and Ise to the east, Iga and Yamato to the south, and Yamashiro and Tanba to the west.

History

The province occupied the basin of Lake Biwa, Japan's largest lake, and its position between the capital region and eastern Japan made it a strategic corridor throughout Japanese history. Hōjō Tokimasa was the first shugo (military governor) of the province, appointed in 1192 at the founding of the Kamakura shogunate. During the Sengoku period, Ōmi was contested by the Rokkaku and Azai clans; Azai Nagamasa, brother-in-law of Oda Nobunaga, held Odani Castle in the north of the province until his defeat in 1573. Toyotomi Hideyoshi's nephew Toyotomi Hidetsugu built Hachimanyama Castle, and the province later contained the Hikone Domain of the Ii family, among the most trusted retainers of the Tokugawa shogunate.

The ancient provincial capital was located near modern Ōtsu, the site of the short-lived imperial court of Emperor Tenji in the 7th century. The province was also home to Enryaku-ji on Mount Hiei, the head temple of Tendai Buddhism, which wielded major political power in the Heian and medieval periods.

Ōmi merchants

Ōmi gave its name to the Ōmi shōnin, itinerant merchants from the province who became among the most successful traders of the Edo period. Renowned for the principle of sanpō-yoshi — "good for the seller, good for the buyer, good for society" — Ōmi merchant houses founded businesses that survive today, including ancestors of firms such as Takashimaya, Itochu and Marubeni.

Districts and legacy

Ōmi Province consisted of twelve districts, including Echi, Gamō, Inukami, Kanzaki, Kōga, Kurita, Sakata, Shiga, Takashima, Yasu and Azai. The province was abolished in the Meiji reorganisation of 1872 and its territory became Shiga Prefecture. Place-names such as Ōmihachiman and Lake Biwa's traditional epithet "Ōmi's lake" preserve its memory.
