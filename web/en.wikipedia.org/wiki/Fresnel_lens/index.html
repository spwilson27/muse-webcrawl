Fresnel lens

A Fresnel lens (pronounced "fray-NEL") is a type of composite compact lens developed by the French physicist Augustin-Jean Fresnel for use in lighthouses. The design allows the construction of lenses of large aperture and short focal length without the mass and volume of material that would be required by a conventional lens. A Fresnel lens divides the surface of a conventional lens into a set of concentric annular sections, each with the curvature needed to refract light toward the focus, dramatically reducing thickness.

History

The concept had antecedents in work by Buffon and the Marquis de Condorcet, but Fresnel, working for the French Lighthouse Commission from 1819, developed the practical design: a central bull's-eye lens surrounded by concentric rings of prisms that refract — and in the upper and lower sections, reflect — light into a horizontal beam. The first Fresnel lens was installed in the Cordouan lighthouse at the mouth of the Gironde in 1823. The design transformed lighthouse optics, greatly increasing the range at which a light could be seen.

Orders of lens

Fresnel lighthouse lenses are classified by "order", according to their focal length and size. First-order lenses, the largest, have a focal length of about 920 mm and stand several metres tall; orders descend through second, third and so on to sixth-order lenses used for harbour lights, with intermediate sizes such as the third-and-a-half order. Larger lenses could project a beam visible for 20 nautical miles or more.

Modern uses

Fresnel lenses are now widely produced in moulded plastic for applications where bulk and weight matter more than imaging quality: overhead projectors, magnifying sheets, traffic signals, car headlamps, solar concentrators, and the condenser lenses of stage and film lighting. In lighthouses, most original glass Fresnel lenses have been retired in favour of modern rotating beacons or LED optics, though many are preserved in museums.
