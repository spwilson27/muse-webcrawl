Dvorak technique

The Dvorak technique (developed between 1969 and 1984 by Vernon Dvorak) is a widely used system to estimate tropical cyclone intensity based solely on visible and infrared satellite images. The technique assigns a T-number (Tropical number) to a storm by assessing features such as the organisation of its cloud bands, the temperature and shape of its eye, and the coldness of its surrounding cloud tops. The T-number is then converted to an estimate of maximum sustained winds and minimum central pressure.

Development

Vernon Dvorak, working at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, developed the method through the 1970s using polar-orbiting and later geostationary satellite imagery. Because reconnaissance aircraft were unavailable in most ocean basins, the technique became, and remains, the primary method of estimating tropical cyclone strength worldwide outside the North Atlantic and north-east Pacific.

Method

The analyst identifies the storm's cloud pattern type — curved band, shear, central dense overcast, central cold region, embedded centre or eye pattern — and measures quantities such as the length of banding wrapping around the centre, the width and temperature of the eye, and the temperature of the coldest surrounding cloud ring. Constraint rules limit how quickly the T-number can change, reflecting the fact that tropical cyclones strengthen and weaken gradually.

Variants

Later refinements include the Advanced Dvorak Technique (ADT), which automates much of the analysis, and objective methods based on microwave imagery and machine learning. Despite its subjectivity, the original technique remains an operational benchmark at tropical cyclone warning centres.
