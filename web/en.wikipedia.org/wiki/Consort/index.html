Consort

A consort is the spouse of a reigning monarch. Terms such as queen consort, king consort, and prince consort distinguish a monarch's spouse from a monarch who reigns in their own right (a regnant). A consort's status, title, and role are usually defined by constitutional practice, law, and court custom rather than by sovereign power, although many consorts have exercised considerable influence.

Titles and roles

The wife of a king is conventionally styled a queen consort and is usually crowned or anointed alongside her husband, as with the queens of the United Kingdom. The husband of a reigning queen has more often been styled a prince consort — a title formally granted, for example, to Prince Albert, husband of Queen Victoria, in 1857 — though some husbands of queens regnant have been made kings consort, sharing the royal title without the sovereign's constitutional powers. In constitutional monarchies the consort's duties are largely ceremonial and charitable: accompanying the sovereign, patronising charities and cultural institutions, and representing the state.

Influence

Historically, consorts could shape policy through access to the monarch and through dynastic connections to foreign courts; queens consort such as Eleanor of Aquitaine (as wife of Henry II of England) and Catherine de' Medici in France wielded substantial political power, the latter as regent. Conversely, a consort was frequently blamed for unpopular policies or foreign influence. When a monarch dies, the surviving consort typically becomes a queen dowager (or dowager king/prince) and may act as regent for a minor heir.
