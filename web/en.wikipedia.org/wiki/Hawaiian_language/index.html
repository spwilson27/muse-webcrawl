Hawaiian language

ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi
Family: Austronesian, Polynesian
Region: Hawaiʻi, especially Niʻihau
Speakers: ~24,000 (estimates vary)
Status: Official language of Hawaiʻi; revitalised

The Hawaiian language (ʻōlelo Hawaiʻi) is a Polynesian language of the Austronesian family, native to the Hawaiian Islands and, with English, an official language of the US state of Hawaiʻi.

Phonology and writing

Hawaiian is noted for a small sound inventory: eight consonants including the ʻokina (glottal stop) and five vowels with long counterparts marked by a kahakō (macron). Its written form was developed by missionaries in the 1820s, and Hawaiians achieved high literacy, publishing many newspapers.

Decline and revitalisation

After the 1893 overthrow of the monarchy, Hawaiian was banned as a medium of school instruction in 1896 and speakers collapsed to a few thousand by the 1980s. Revitalisation through Pūnana Leo preschools (from 1983), immersion schools and the University of Hawaiʻi has grown a new generation of speakers.
