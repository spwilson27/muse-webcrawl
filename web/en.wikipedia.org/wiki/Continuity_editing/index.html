Continuity editing

Continuity editing is the process, in film editing, of maintaining consistent detail in the various elements of a scene or story over time, so that the audience perceives separate shots as a single continuous action. It is the dominant style of editing in narrative cinema, sometimes called the "invisible" style because its techniques are designed to be imperceptible.

The 180-degree rule keeps cameras on one side of the axis of action so screen direction stays consistent.

Techniques

Continuity editing relies on a set of conventions developed in early Hollywood studio filmmaking:

  • The 180-degree rule — the camera stays on one side of an imaginary "axis of action" between two characters, preserving consistent left–right orientation on screen.
  • Match on action — a cut is made in the middle of a movement, so the action continues seamlessly in the next shot.
  • Eyeline match — a character looks off screen, and the following shot shows what they are looking at, from a matching angle.
  • Shot/reverse shot — alternating shots of two people in conversation, typically over each other's shoulder.
  • The 30-degree rule — successive shots of the same subject should differ in camera angle by at least 30 degrees to avoid a jarring jump cut effect.
  • Establishing shot — an opening wide shot that sets the spatial layout of a scene.

Continuity of detail

Beyond camera placement, continuity editing requires consistency of props, costumes, lighting, and actor position between shots that may be filmed days apart. On professional productions a script supervisor (historically the "continuity girl") records these details. Errors — a glass that refills itself, a costume that changes — are known as continuity errors and are catalogued by attentive viewers.

Alternatives

Deliberate violations of continuity include the jump cut, associated with Jean-Luc Godard's Breathless (1960), and the montage theories of Soviet filmmakers such as Sergei Eisenstein, who treated editing as collision rather than seamless linkage. Non-continuity styles are common in avant-garde and music video work.
