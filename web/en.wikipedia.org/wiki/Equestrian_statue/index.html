Equestrian statue

An equestrian statue is a statue of a rider mounted on a horse, typically cast in bronze and of monumental scale. From the earliest surviving examples in ancient Rome, equestrian monuments have been among the most prestigious and technically demanding commissions in sculpture, reserved overwhelmingly for rulers, generals and national heroes. The tradition was revived on a grand scale in the Italian Renaissance and continued into the modern era.

Antiquity

The most famous surviving ancient example is the Equestrian Statue of Marcus Aurelius in Rome (c. 175 AD), which escaped being melted down in the Middle Ages because it was mistakenly believed to depict Constantine, the first Christian emperor. It now stands in the Capitoline Museums, with a copy in the Piazza del Campidoglio.

Renaissance revival

The type was revived in 15th-century Italy with Donatello's statue of the condottiere Gattamelata in Padua (completed 1453) and Verrocchio's monument to Bartolomeo Colleoni in Venice. Leonardo da Vinci's colossal horse for Milan was never cast; its full-scale realisation, the "American Horse", was finally completed in 1999.

Technical challenges

A rearing or striding horse presents a severe engineering problem: a multi-tonne bronze must be balanced on two or three slender legs. Sculptors often used the horse's tail, a support figure, or a third element touching the ground to stabilise the composition, and later works relied on internal iron armatures and improved casting.

Folklore

A widespread modern myth claims that the position of the horse's legs encodes how the rider died — both forelegs raised for death in battle, one raised for death from wounds, all four on the ground for natural death. The convention does not hold up against the actual statues and is generally regarded as folklore.
