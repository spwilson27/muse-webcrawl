Motorway

A motorway is a high-capacity road designed for fast motor traffic, with controlled access, divided carriageways and grade-separated junctions. The term is used in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand and several other countries for roads equivalent to the American freeway or interstate, the German Autobahn, the French autoroute and the Italian autostrada.

Characteristics

Motorways prohibit slow or vulnerable road users such as pedestrians, cyclists and horse riders, and typically feature hard shoulders, emergency telephones or refuge areas, central reservations and slip roads (on- and off-ramps). Speed limits are generally higher than on other roads — 70 mph (113 km/h) in the UK — and stopping is forbidden except in emergencies.

History

The first purpose-built motor roads appeared in the 1920s, including Italy's Autostrada dei Laghi (1924) and Germany's early Autobahnen. Britain's first motorway, the Preston Bypass, opened in 1958, followed by the M1 in 1959. Motorway networks now form the backbone of long-distance road transport in most developed countries.

Signage and numbering

In Britain motorway signs are blue with white text, and routes carry the prefix "M" (for example, M6). "Smart motorways" use variable speed limits and, controversially, sometimes convert the hard shoulder into a running lane.
