A smart city is an urban area that uses digital technology, data and connectivity — especially networks of sensors and the Internet of things — to collect information and manage assets, resources and services more efficiently. Definitions vary widely, but most encompass data-driven governance, intelligent transport and utilities, environmental monitoring and digital citizen services. The concept overlaps with earlier ideas of the "digital city," "intelligent city" and "ubiquitous city."

Components

  • Smart mobility: adaptive traffic signals, real-time transit information, congestion pricing, bike- and car-sharing, integrated ticketing.
  • Smart grid and environment: smart meters, demand response, air- and water-quality sensor networks, leak detection.
  • Smart governance: open data portals, online services, participatory budgeting platforms.
  • Smart infrastructure: connected streetlights, waste bins signalling fill levels, predictive maintenance.

Examples

Barcelona is frequently cited for its open-source Sentilo sensor platform, superblocks (superilles) and later emphasis on "technological sovereignty." Singapore's Smart Nation programme integrates nationwide sensors and digital identity. Songdo in South Korea was built from scratch as a ubiquitous city, though critics found it sterile. Amsterdam Smart City (2009) pioneered public-private-citizen collaboration, and India's Smart Cities Mission (2015) targeted 100 cities. The high-profile Sidewalk Labs project in Toronto was cancelled in 2020 amid privacy controversy and economic headwinds.

Criticism

Scholars such as Adam Greenfield (Against the Smart City, 2013) and Evgeny Morozov have criticised the concept as a top-down, vendor-driven vision that privileges corporate technology sales, enables pervasive surveillance, and sidelines democratic politics and social inequality. Data breaches, algorithmic bias and the digital divide recur in the debate; newer frameworks emphasise open standards, data trusts and citizen control of data.
