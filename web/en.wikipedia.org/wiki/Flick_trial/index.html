Flick trial

The Flick trial (officially The United States of America vs. Friedrich Flick, et al.) was the fifth of twelve trials for war crimes conducted by the United States in its occupation zone in Germany at Nuremberg after the end of World War II. It was the first of three trials of leading German industrialists.

Case

The principal defendant was Friedrich Flick, head of the Flick conglomerate, one of the wealthiest and most powerful industrial concerns in Nazi Germany. Flick and five co-defendants, including his eldest son Otto-Ernst Flick and his nephew Bernhard Weiss, were charged with war crimes and crimes against humanity, including the use of slave labour in Flick mines and factories, the plunder of occupied territories, and "Aryanization" of Jewish property, as well as support of the SS through donations — the so-called Circle of Friends of Himmler.

Judgment

The trial ran from April to December 1947. Flick was convicted on counts including slave labour and sentenced to seven years' imprisonment; he was released in 1950 under a general clemency. Two co-defendants were convicted and three acquitted.
