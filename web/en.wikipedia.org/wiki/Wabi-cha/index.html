Wabi-cha

Wabi-cha (侘茶), is a style of Japanese tea ceremony particularly associated with Sen no Rikyū, Takeno Jōō and their followers. It emphasises simplicity, humility and restraint, in contrast to the lavish display of Chinese luxury goods that characterised earlier tea practice under the Ashikaga shogunate.

History

The aesthetic roots of wabi-cha lie with Murata Jukō (1423–1502), a Zen monk regarded as the founder of the tea ceremony, who favoured simple Japanese utensils alongside Chinese pieces and served tea in modest surroundings. Takeno Jōō (1502–1555), a wealthy Sakai merchant and Rikyū's teacher, further developed the wabi style, reducing the size of the tea room and promoting unadorned native ceramics.

Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591), tea master to Toyotomi Hideyoshi, carried wabi-cha to its fullest expression. He favoured small, rustic tea houses of only two tatami mats, with a low crawling-in entrance (nijiriguchi) that forced all guests, regardless of rank, to bow humbly; rough, dark Raku tea bowls made by the potter Chōjirō; bamboo flower vases; and a garden path (roji) modelled on a mountain trail. Under Rikyū, tea became a spiritual discipline embodying the principles of harmony (wa), respect (kei), purity (sei) and tranquility (jaku).

Aesthetics

The term wabi derives from a word suggesting poverty, insufficiency and loneliness, transformed in tea culture into an appreciation of the austere, the imperfect and the transient. Wabi-cha interiors are dim and undecorated, utensils are plain and often irregular, and the gathering is small and intimate. The style profoundly influenced Japanese arts and architecture far beyond the tea room.
