Ebony is a dense black or dark brown hardwood obtained from several species of the genus Diospyros, which also includes the persimmons. The finest ebony is almost pure black, fine-grained and heavy enough to sink in water. It has been prized since antiquity for ornamental objects, furniture inlay and musical instrument parts.

History and uses

Ebony was traded in ancient Egypt and mentioned in classical sources as a luxury material from India and Africa. From the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, European cabinet-makers used it for veneers and inlay in high-status furniture — the French term for a cabinet-maker, ébéniste, derives from it. Today the wood is used in small, high-value items: fingerboards, pegs and tailpieces of stringed instruments, piano and harpsichord keys, bagpipe parts, knife handles, chess pieces and turned ornaments. Ceylon ebony (Diospyros ebenum) and Gabon ebony (D. crassiflora) are the classic black species, while Macassar ebony from Sulawesi is valued for its dramatic brown-and-black striping.

Conservation

Centuries of exploitation have left many ebony species depleted; several are listed as endangered or vulnerable, and most are now covered by CITES trade controls. High demand and very slow growth make genuine ebony increasingly scarce and expensive, and substitutes such as stained pearwood or synthetic materials are widely used.
