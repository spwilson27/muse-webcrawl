Insect repellent

An insect repellent is a substance applied to skin, clothing, or surfaces that discourages insects — particularly mosquitoes, ticks, flies, and fleas — from landing on or approaching the treated area. Repellents reduce bites and thereby help prevent vector-borne diseases such as malaria, dengue, Lyme disease, and Zika fever. They work mainly by interfering with insects' olfactory receptors, masking the human odours and carbon dioxide that attract them.

Common active ingredients

The best-known synthetic repellent is DEET (N,N-diethyl-meta-toluamide), developed by the United States Army in 1946 and still regarded as a benchmark for duration and breadth of protection; higher concentrations extend protection time rather than increasing efficacy per se. Picaridin (icaridin) is a widely used odourless alternative with comparable performance, and other options include IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (whose active component is PMD), and permethrin, which is an insecticide used on clothing and gear rather than skin. Plant-based products such as citronella provide shorter-lived protection.

Safety and use

Health agencies such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention consider registered repellents, used as directed, safe for adults and (with concentration and age guidance) for children, and recommend them for travellers to regions with mosquito-borne disease. Effectiveness varies with concentration, sweating, swimming, and the target insect species.
