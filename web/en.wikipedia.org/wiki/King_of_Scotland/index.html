King of Scotland

The King of Scotland (Scottish Gaelic: Rìgh Alba) was the head of state of the Kingdom of Scotland until the union with England in 1707. Notably, Scottish monarchs were styled "King of Scots" (Latin: Rex Scottorum) — king of the people rather than of the land — although "King of Scotland" was also used in some periods and became common in popular usage.

History

Tradition credits Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpin), king of the Picts from 843, with founding the kingdom by uniting the Picts and the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata. Over the following centuries the kingdom of Alba expanded to encompass Lothian, Strathclyde and Moray. Famous holders of the title include Macbeth, David I, Robert the Bruce, who secured Scotland's independence in the Wars of Scottish Independence, and Mary, Queen of Scots.

Union of the Crowns

In 1603 James VI of Scotland inherited the English throne as James I, uniting the crowns in his person while the kingdoms remained separate states. The separate title lapsed with the Acts of Union 1707, which created the Kingdom of Great Britain under Queen Anne, the last monarch to reign as Queen of Scotland.

Coronation

Scottish kings were traditionally crowned at Scone Abbey, seated upon the Stone of Scone (Stone of Destiny), which was seized by Edward I of England in 1296 and kept at Westminster; it was returned to Scotland in 1996 and used again in the coronation of Charles III in 2023.
