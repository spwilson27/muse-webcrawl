Unclaimed property

Unclaimed property (also called abandoned property) refers to financial assets and other property held by a business or institution for an owner who has lost contact with the holder or failed to claim the property within a statutory period. Under the unclaimed property laws of most US states, such property must eventually be reported and remitted ("escheated") to the state, which holds it until the owner or heirs claim it.

Types

Common categories include dormant bank and brokerage accounts, uncashed payroll and dividend cheques, forgotten security deposits and utility refunds, insurance policy proceeds, gift cards, safe deposit box contents, and unclaimed tax refunds. Property generally becomes reportable after a dormancy period, typically three to five years depending on the type and jurisdiction, during which the holder must attempt to contact the owner.

Escheat and claiming

Although the process is called escheat, modern state laws are custodial: the state takes custody, not permanent title, and owners may generally reclaim their property indefinitely. US states collectively hold tens of billions of dollars in unclaimed property and return billions each year. Claimants can search official state databases, aggregated by the National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators' endorsed sites MissingMoney.com and Unclaimed.org. US Supreme Court decisions, notably Texas v. New Jersey (1965), established priority rules: property goes first to the state of the owner's last known address, and if that is unknown, to the holder's state of incorporation — a rule that has made Delaware, where many corporations are incorporated, a particularly large recipient.

Other jurisdictions

Analogous schemes exist elsewhere: the United Kingdom operates a Dormant Assets Scheme for bank accounts unused for 15 years, with reclaim rights preserved, and Canada requires federally regulated banks to transfer unclaimed balances to the Bank of Canada. Finder services that charge large fees to locate property have attracted regulatory criticism, since claims can be made directly for free.
