Viktor Emil Frankl (26 March 1905 – 2 September 1997) was an Austrian neurologist, psychiatrist, and Holocaust survivor. He founded logotherapy, a school of psychotherapy centered on the search for meaning in life, sometimes called the "Third Viennese School of Psychotherapy" after Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology. His book Man's Search for Meaning (1946), recounting his experiences in Nazi concentration camps and presenting his theory, has sold tens of millions of copies and is regarded as one of the most influential books of the twentieth century.

Life

Frankl studied medicine at the University of Vienna, specializing in neurology and psychiatry, and worked on suicide prevention among students and at the Vienna General Hospital. After the Nazi annexation of Austria, he was deported in 1942 to Theresienstadt and later to Auschwitz and Dachau subcamps; his wife, parents, and brother perished. Liberated in 1945, he returned to Vienna, rebuilt his career, and published his camp memoir within a year. He later held professorships in Vienna and visiting posts in the United States, lecturing worldwide and receiving numerous honors.

Logotherapy

Logotherapy holds that the primary human motivation is not pleasure (Freud) or power (Adler) but the will to meaning. Meaning can be found through creative work, through experience and love, and—even in unavoidable suffering—through the attitude one chooses. Frankl famously wrote that "everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances." Techniques include paradoxical intention and dereflection. The theory's account of the "existential vacuum" influenced existential and humanistic psychology and later positive psychology.
