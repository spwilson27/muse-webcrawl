Robust statistics

Key figuresPeter J. Huber, Frank Hampel, John Tukey, Peter Rousseeuw
Core conceptsBreakdown point, influence function, M-estimators
Example methodsMedian, MAD, Huber loss, trimmed mean, RANSAC

Robust statistics are statistical methods designed to remain reliable when the data deviate from the assumptions of classical models — especially the assumption of normality — for example when the data contain outliers or come from heavy-tailed distributions. A robust procedure performs well under a range of distributions, sacrificing only a little efficiency at the ideal model in exchange for insensitivity to contamination.

Motivation

Classical estimators such as the sample mean and standard deviation are highly sensitive to a single extreme observation: one outlier can move the mean arbitrarily far. The sample median, by contrast, resists up to 50% contamination. Real data sets routinely contain errors and anomalous points, motivating estimators whose behavior changes only modestly under small departures from model assumptions.

Key concepts

  • Breakdown point: the smallest fraction of contaminated observations that can drive an estimator to arbitrarily bad values. The mean has breakdown point 0; the median, 50%, the maximum possible.
  • Influence function: measures the effect of an infinitesimal contamination at a point on the estimator; bounded influence functions characterize robust procedures (Hampel).
  • M-estimators: a general class defined by minimizing a sum of a loss function of residuals; least squares is a (non-robust) special case, while Huber's loss and Tukey's biweight bound the effect of large residuals.

Common robust methods

Robust measures of location include the median, trimmed and Winsorized means, and the Hodges–Lehmann estimator; robust scale measures include the median absolute deviation (MAD) and Rousseeuw's Sn and Qn estimators. Robust regression methods include least absolute deviations (L1), Huber regression, least median of squares, least trimmed squares, and MM-estimators; RANSAC is a widely used robust fitting scheme in computer vision. Robust analogues of covariance estimation, such as the Minimum Covariance Determinant, support robust principal component analysis and outlier detection.

History

Although robust ideas appear in earlier work (e.g., by Eddington and Daniell), the modern theory was founded by Peter J. Huber's 1964 paper on robust estimation of a location parameter and developed by Frank Hampel, who introduced the influence function and qualitative robustness in his 1968 dissertation. Peter Rousseeuw introduced high-breakdown regression and scale estimators in the 1980s.
