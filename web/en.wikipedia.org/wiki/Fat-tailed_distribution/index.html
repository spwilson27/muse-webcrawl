Fat-tailed distribution

A fat-tailed distribution is a probability distribution that exhibits a large skewness or kurtosis — that is, a greater probability of extreme outcomes — in comparison with the normal distribution. Fat-tailed distributions have a "tail" that decays more slowly than the exponential decay of the Gaussian. In the strongest cases the tails follow a power law, and the distribution may have infinite variance or even an undefined mean.

Definition and measurement

There is no single universally accepted definition, but a common criterion uses the fourth moment: a distribution with positive excess kurtosis (greater than that of the normal distribution, i.e. leptokurtic) is described as fat-tailed. A stricter and more informative approach characterises the rate at which the tail probability P(X > x) decays as x grows. Power-law (Pareto-type) tails decay polynomially, like x−α; subexponential distributions, including the log-normal and heavy-tailed Weibull, decay faster than a power law but slower than the exponential family. Examples of fat-tailed distributions include the Cauchy distribution, Student's t-distribution with few degrees of freedom, and Lévy stable distributions.

Importance

Fat tails occur in many natural and social phenomena: earthquake magnitudes, flood levels, city sizes, income and wealth distributions, and financial asset returns. Their practical importance lies in risk estimation. Models that assume normally distributed returns — as much of classical portfolio theory and pre-2008 risk management did — severely underestimate the frequency of extreme price movements, a criticism developed prominently by Benoit Mandelbrot, who showed that cotton prices and later stock returns vary far more wildly than the Gaussian model allows, and by Nassim Nicholas Taleb in his work on "black swan" events. In fat-tailed settings, a small number of extreme observations can dominate the sample mean and variance, so that statistics computed from short samples are unstable.
