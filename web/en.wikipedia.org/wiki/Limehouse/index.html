Limehouse is a district in the London Borough of Tower Hamlets in East London, England, on the north bank of the River Thames opposite Rotherhithe. Its name comes from the lime kilns (lime oasts) that operated here from the medieval period, supplying lime for London's building trade.

History

Limehouse grew up as a maritime community serving the Port of London. From the 17th century it was a centre for shipbuilding, rope-making and chandlery, and its pubs and lodging houses served sailors from around the world. In the 19th century it contained London's original Chinatown, settled by Chinese seamen around Limehouse Causeway and Pennyfields; the community largely dispersed after wartime bombing, and London's Chinatown later re-established itself in Soho. The area was a byword for opium dens in Victorian literature — it appears in the works of Charles Dickens, who had a godfather in the parish, and in Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray.

The Limehouse Basin (originally the Regent's Canal Dock) opened in 1820, linking the Thames with the canal network, and the Regent's Canal and Limehouse Cut made the district a hub of inland waterways. The Hawksmoor church of St Anne's, Limehouse (1714–1730) is a local landmark. After the decline of the docks, the area was redeveloped from the 1980s onward as part of the London Docklands, and it is served by Limehouse station on the Docklands Light Railway and c2c lines. The district also gave its name to the Limehouse Declaration of 1981, which launched the Social Democratic Party (SDP), issued from the nearby home of David Owen.
