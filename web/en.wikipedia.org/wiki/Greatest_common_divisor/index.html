Greatest common divisor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two or more integers, which are not all zero, is the largest positive integer that divides each of the integers. For two integers x and y, the greatest common divisor is written gcd(x, y). For example, gcd(8, 12) = 4.

The concept can be extended to polynomials (see polynomial greatest common divisor) and to other commutative rings. Two numbers are called coprime if their greatest common divisor equals 1.

Computation

The GCD can be found by prime factorisation, but for large numbers the Euclidean algorithm is far more efficient: repeatedly replace the larger number by the remainder of dividing the two, until the remainder is zero. The extended Euclidean algorithm also yields Bézout coefficients expressing the GCD as a linear combination of the inputs.

Properties

  • gcd(a, b) divides every common divisor of a and b.
  • gcd(a, b) · lcm(a, b) = a · b for positive a and b.
  • gcd(a, 0) = |a|, and the GCD is commutative and associative.

Applications

GCDs are used to reduce fractions to lowest terms, to solve linear Diophantine equations, and in cryptography, for example in the RSA algorithm, where key generation relies on coprimality tests.
