Wreck diving

Wreck diving is a type of recreational and technical diving in which divers explore the wreckage of ships, aircraft and other artificial structures sunk underwater. Wrecks may have been lost by accident or deliberately sunk as artificial reefs or dive attractions. Many wrecks develop into rich marine habitats, attracting fish and other life, and wreck sites can also be of historical and archaeological interest.

Types

  • Non-penetration diving – swimming around the exterior of the wreck, within sight of open water.
  • Limited penetration – entering lighted areas of the wreck within the light zone, close to an exit.
  • Full penetration – travelling deep inside the structure, beyond sight of the entrance, an advanced technical activity comparable to cave diving.

Hazards

Wrecks present hazards including entanglement in nets, lines and debris; sharp, rusting metal; silt that can reduce visibility to zero; collapsing structures; and the risk of becoming lost inside a hull. Decompression obligations and overhead environments that prevent direct ascent to the surface add to the risk. Divers use guideline reels, redundant lights, cutting tools and specialised training in buoyancy and finning techniques to manage these dangers.

Training

Most training agencies, including PADI, SSI and NAUI, offer wreck diving speciality courses covering mapping, penetration planning, reel use and emergency procedures. Advanced penetration diving is taught by technical diving organisations.

Notable wreck sites

Famous diveable wrecks include the SS Thistlegorm in the Red Sea, the Zenobia off Cyprus, the wrecks of Truk Lagoon in Micronesia, the President Coolidge in Vanuatu, and the USS Oriskany, sunk off Florida as an artificial reef. Many wartime wrecks are protected as war graves, and divers are expected to look but not touch.
