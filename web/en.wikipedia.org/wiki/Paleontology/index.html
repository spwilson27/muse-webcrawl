Paleontology is the scientific study of life that existed prior to, and sometimes including, the start of the Holocene epoch (roughly 11,700 years before present). It includes the study of fossils to classify organisms and examine their interactions with each other and their environments — their paleoecology. Paleontological observations have been documented as far back as the 5th century BCE, but the science became established in the 18th century, notably as a result of Georges Cuvier's comparative anatomy and William Smith's stratigraphy, and developed rapidly in the 19th century.

History

In 1796 Cuvier demonstrated that fossil mammoths and mastodons were extinct species unlike any living animal, establishing extinction as a fact. William Smith's geological maps showed that fossil assemblages characterise rock layers in a consistent order, founding biostratigraphy. Mary Anning's discoveries of ichthyosaurs and plesiosaurs on the Jurassic Coast of England revealed marine reptiles to science. The 19th century brought the description of dinosaurs (Richard Owen coined the term "Dinosauria" in 1842), the "Bone Wars" between American fossil hunters Edward Drinker Cope and Othniel Charles Marsh, and the integration of paleontology with Darwin's theory of evolution after 1859.

Methods

Paleontologists excavate fossils from sedimentary rock, prepare them mechanically or chemically, and describe them using comparative anatomy and, increasingly, computed tomography, isotopic geochemistry, and molecular techniques where ancient biomolecules survive. Phylogenetic analysis reconstructs evolutionary relationships; radiometric dating of associated igneous rocks or fossils themselves assigns ages; and taphonomy — the study of how organisms decay and fossilise — controls for the biases of the fossil record, which famously under-represents soft-bodied organisms and upland environments.

Major contributions

  • Deep time: the fossil record revealed Earth's history spans billions of years.
  • Mass extinctions: the end-Permian and end-Cretaceous events were recognised through fossil turnover.
  • Evolutionary transitions: fossils document landmark events such as the origin of tetrapods, the evolution of birds from dinosaurs, and human evolution.
  • Past climates: fossil assemblages and isotopes reconstruct ancient environments.
