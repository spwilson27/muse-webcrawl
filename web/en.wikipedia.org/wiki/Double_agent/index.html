Double agent

In the field of counterintelligence, a double agent is an employee of a secret intelligence service whose primary purpose is to spy on a target organisation, but who in fact is a member of that target organisation, or is controlled by it. Double agents may be agents of the target service who are "turned" after capture, volunteers who offer themselves to both sides, or "re-doubled" agents sent back against their original service. Because both sides must trust the agent while knowing the agent is trusted by the enemy, double agents are among the most difficult assets to run in espionage.

Britain's Double-Cross System in the Second World War turned captured German agents and used them to feed deception to Berlin.

The Double-Cross System

The most celebrated use of double agents was the British Double-Cross System of the Second World War, run by MI5's B1a section under the Twenty Committee (XX, hence "double cross"). Britain captured or received nearly every German agent sent to the country and offered many of them the choice of working for Britain or facing execution. Controlled agents such as Juan Pujol García ("Garbo"), Duško Popov ("Tricycle"), and Eddie Chapman ("Zigzag") transmitted a carefully managed mixture of true but harmless information and false reports, culminating in Operation Fortitude, the deception that convinced Germany the D-Day landings would fall at Calais rather than Normandy.

Related terms

  • Triple agent — an agent who pretends to be a double agent for one side while remaining loyal to the other, or who switches allegiance three times.
  • Mole — a long-term penetration agent recruited before gaining access to secret material.
  • Agent provocateur — an agent who incites others to commit incriminating acts.

Famous Cold War double agents and moles include Oleg Penkovsky, who passed Soviet secrets to Britain and the United States, and Aldrich Ames, the CIA officer who spied for the Soviet Union.
