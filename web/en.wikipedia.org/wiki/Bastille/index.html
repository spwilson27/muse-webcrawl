The Bastille (formally the Bastille Saint-Antoine) was a fortress in Paris, built during the Hundred Years' War to defend the eastern approach to the city at the Porte Saint-Antoine. From the 17th century it served as a state prison, and it became a powerful symbol of royal tyranny.

History

Construction began in 1370 under Charles V; the fortress had eight towers and thick walls surrounded by a moat. Under Cardinal Richelieu and Louis XIV it held prisoners detained by lettres de cachet — arbitrary royal warrants — including the writer Voltaire and the mysterious Man in the Iron Mask. By 1789 it held only seven prisoners, but it remained the emblem of absolutist oppression.

Storming and legacy

On 14 July 1789 a revolutionary crowd stormed the Bastille to seize gunpowder, freed the prisoners and killed the governor, the Marquis de Launay. The event, commemorated annually as Bastille Day, France's national day, marked the beginning of the French Revolution. The fortress was demolished stone by stone; the site is now the Place de la Bastille, marked by the July Column.
