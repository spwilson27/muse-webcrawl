The Abortion Act 1967 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that legalised abortion on certain grounds by registered practitioners in Great Britain (England, Scotland and Wales). It did not extend to Northern Ireland, where abortion remained largely criminalised until the law was changed in 2019–2020.

Background

Before the Act, abortion was an offence under the Offences against the Person Act 1861, subject to a common-law exception recognised in the 1938 case of R v Bourne. The Medical Termination of Pregnancy Bill was introduced as a private member's bill by the Liberal MP David Steel, and was supported by the Labour government of Harold Wilson, which gave it parliamentary time.

Provisions

The Act made abortion legal where two registered medical practitioners agree in good faith that one of the statutory grounds is met, including that continuance of the pregnancy would involve risk to the life of the pregnant woman, or risk of injury to her physical or mental health, greater than termination; or that there is a substantial risk the child would be seriously handicapped. In practice, the Act provided a statutory defence for doctors rather than a right to abortion on request.

Amendments

The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 reduced the general time limit from 28 weeks to 24 weeks and amended the grounds. During the COVID-19 pandemic, temporary provisions permitted early medical abortion at home following a teleconsultation, later made permanent in England and Wales in 2022.
