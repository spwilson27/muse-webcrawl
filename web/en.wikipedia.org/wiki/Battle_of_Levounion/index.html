Battle of Levounion

The Battle of Levounion was fought on 29 April 1091 between the Byzantine Empire under Emperor Alexios I Komnenos, allied with Cuman forces, and the Pechenegs, at Levounion near the Maritsa (Evros) River in Thrace. The Pechenegs were virtually annihilated, ending their threat to Byzantium once and for all and securing Alexios's throne at the moment of the Empire's deepest crisis.

Background

The Pechenegs, a Turkic nomad people, had crossed the Danube and ravaged the Balkans for years; in 1090–91 they advanced to the approaches of Constantinople itself, in contact with the Seljuk emir Tzachas. Alexios I, whose treasury and army were exhausted, gambled on an alliance with the Pechenegs' own enemies, the Cumans, whose khans Togortok (Tugorkhan) and Boniak arrived with a great host in April 1091. Alexios feasted and showered them with gifts, overcoming mutual distrust, and agreed to strike the Pecheneg camp before the Cumans could be bought off or depart.

The battle

On 29 April 1091 the combined army attacked the Pecheneg wagon laager at Levounion at dawn, achieving complete surprise — the Pechenegs had expected the customary day of preparation before battle. The fighting dissolved into a massacre: the Cumans and Byzantines cut down warriors and families alike around the wagons. Anna Komnene wrote in the Alexiad that "an entire people, numbering myriads, was exterminated on that day" — the Byzantines even composed a rueful song that the Pechenegs, by one day, missed the feast of St. George turned slaughter. So few Pechenegs survived that their people ceased to exist as an independent force; the remnant was settled as Byzantine military colonists (the Moglena Pechenegs).

Aftermath

Levounion rescued the Byzantine state at its nadir, secured the European provinces and freed Alexios to campaign in Anatolia and to deal with Tzachas. It also taught the emperor caution toward his Cuman allies, whom he hurried out of the empire loaded with gifts. The victory formed the backdrop to Alexios's appeal to the West for soldiers — an appeal answered, on a scale he never intended, by the First Crusade of 1096.
