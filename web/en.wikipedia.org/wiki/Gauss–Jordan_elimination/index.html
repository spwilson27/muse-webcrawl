Gauss–Jordan elimination

In mathematics, Gauss–Jordan elimination is an algorithm for solving systems of linear equations. It is usually understood as a sequence of operations performed on the corresponding matrix of coefficients. This method can also be used to compute the rank of a matrix, the determinant of a square matrix, and the inverse of an invertible matrix.

The method is named after Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855) and Wilhelm Jordan (1842–1899). It is a variation of Gaussian elimination, in which elementary row operations transform the matrix into reduced row echelon form rather than merely row echelon form.

Overview

Gauss–Jordan elimination proceeds by applying elementary row operations — swapping two rows, multiplying a row by a nonzero scalar, and adding a multiple of one row to another — until the coefficient matrix is in reduced row echelon form: each pivot is 1, each pivot is the only nonzero entry in its column, and the pivots descend in a staircase pattern.

Applications

Solving linear systems

A system of linear equations can be represented as an augmented matrix. Applying Gauss–Jordan elimination to the augmented matrix yields the solution directly, since the reduced matrix expresses each pivot variable in terms of the free variables (or constants, when the system has a unique solution).

Computing inverses

To compute the inverse of a square matrix A, one forms the augmented matrix [A | I] and applies Gauss–Jordan elimination; if A is invertible, the result is [I | A⁻¹].

Rank and null space

The number of nonzero rows in the reduced form gives the rank of the matrix, and the reduced form makes the null space easy to read off.

History

Methods equivalent to Gaussian elimination appear in the ancient Chinese text The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art. Gauss used the method in his work on least squares and orbit determination. Wilhelm Jordan described the full reduction variant in his 1888 handbook of geodesy.

Computational complexity

Gauss–Jordan elimination requires roughly n³/3 arithmetic operations for an n × n system in its basic form for solving, and about n³ operations for inversion, comparable to Gaussian elimination with back-substitution.
