IMSI-catcher

An IMSI-catcher is a telephone eavesdropping device used for intercepting mobile phone traffic and tracking the movement of mobile phone users. Essentially a "fake" mobile tower acting between the target mobile phone and the service provider's real towers, it is considered a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack. The 3G wireless standard offers some risk mitigation due to mutual authentication required from both the handset and the network. However, sophisticated attacks may be able to downgrade 3G and LTE to non-LTE network services which do not require mutual authentication.

Overview

An IMSI-catcher exploits weaknesses in the GSM protocol. Within the 2G GSM standard, there is no authentication of the tower to the phone. This flaw is used by an IMSI catcher to masquerade as a legitimate base station and log the IMSI numbers (International Mobile Subscriber Identity) of all the mobile phones in the area, as they attempt to connect to the IMSI catcher. It allows forcing the cell phone connected to it to use no call encryption (A5/0 mode) or to use easily breakable encryption (A5/1 or A5/2 mode), making the recorded calls easy to intercept and convert into audio.

Types

IMSI-catchers range from small handheld units to larger vehicle-mounted systems. Devices commonly known by the name Stingray, manufactured by the Harris Corporation (now L3Harris), are among the best known in the United States, where their use by law enforcement has been controversial. Other commercial products include the Triggerfish and devices sold by firms such as Rohde & Schwarz and Gamma Group.

Functions

IMSI-catchers can be used to identify and track phones by their IMSI or IMEI numbers, intercept calls and text messages, and in some configurations inject false messages or perform denial-of-service attacks. They collect metadata on all phones within range, not only the target device, which has raised significant privacy concerns.

Countermeasures

Newer network standards such as 4G LTE and 5G include stronger mutual authentication and encrypted identifiers (the 5G SUCI replaces the plaintext IMSI), making IMSI catching more difficult, though downgrade attacks remain possible where legacy networks are available. Detection apps for rooted Android devices, such as SnoopSnitch, attempt to identify suspicious base station behaviour.
