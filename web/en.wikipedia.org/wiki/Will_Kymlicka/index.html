Will Kymlicka (born 1962) is a Canadian political philosopher best known for his work on multiculturalism and minority rights. He is a professor and Canada Research Chair in Political Philosophy at Queen's University at Kingston, and his defence of group-differentiated rights for cultural minorities within liberal theory has made him one of the most influential contemporary theorists of multicultural citizenship.

Work

In Liberalism, Community and Culture (1989) and Multicultural Citizenship: A Liberal Theory of Minority Rights (1995), Kymlicka argued that individual autonomy — the liberal value par excellence — depends on access to a secure cultural structure, a "societal culture" that provides meaningful options. From this he derived a taxonomy of group-differentiated rights: self-government rights for national minorities (such as Indigenous peoples and the Québécois), polyethnic rights for immigrant groups (such as exemptions and public support that ease integration without forced assimilation), and special representation rights for disadvantaged groups. He distinguishes "external protections" of a group against the larger society, which liberalism can endorse, from "internal restrictions" on a group's own members, which it cannot.

Kymlicka's textbook Contemporary Political Philosophy: An Introduction (1990; 2nd ed. 2002) became a standard survey of theories of justice. His later work includes Finding Our Way (1998) on Canadian debates, Multicultural Odysseys (2007) on the international spread of multiculturalism, and, with Sue Donaldson, Zoopolis (2011), extending political theory to animal rights and arguing for forms of citizenship and sovereignty for domesticated and wild animals.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether cultures are as bounded as the theory requires, whether group rights entrench conservative elites within minorities, and whether the national-minority/immigrant distinction fits cases such as African Americans. Kymlicka's framework remains the benchmark against which theories of cultural pluralism are usually measured.
