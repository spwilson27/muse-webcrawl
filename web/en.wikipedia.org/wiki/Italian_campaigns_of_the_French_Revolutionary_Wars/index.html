Italian campaigns of the French Revolutionary Wars

The Italian campaigns of the French Revolutionary Wars (1792–1802) were a series of conflicts fought principally in northern Italy between French revolutionary armies and the forces of Austria, Piedmont-Sardinia, and their allies during the Wars of the First and Second Coalitions.

First campaign, 1792–1797

French forces occupied Savoy and Nice in 1792, but Italy remained a secondary theatre until 1796, when the young general Napoleon Bonaparte took command of the Army of Italy. In a rapid series of operations — Montenotte, Millesimo, Mondovì — he knocked Piedmont out of the war, then defeated the Austrians at Lodi, Castiglione, Bassano, Arcole, and Rivoli, and captured Mantua. Austria sued for peace in the Treaty of Campo-Formio (October 1797), ceding Lombardy to France and recognising the French-sponsored Cisalpine Republic, in exchange for Venice.

Second campaign, 1799–1801

In 1799 Austro-Russian forces under Alexander Suvorov drove the French from most of Italy. Returning from Egypt, Bonaparte crossed the Great St Bernard Pass in May 1800 and narrowly defeated the Austrians at Marengo. Further French victories, including Hohenlinden in Germany, led to the Treaty of Lunéville (1801), restoring French dominance in Italy.

Significance

The campaigns destroyed the old political order of the Italian peninsula, spread revolutionary institutions and legal reforms, and made Napoleon's reputation, paving his way to power in France.
