Ashdown Forest is an ancient area of open heathland and woodland occupying the highest sandy ridge of the High Weald in East Sussex, England. Though called a forest, most of its 2,630 hectares (6,500 acres) of common land is open heath—one of the largest areas of lowland heath in south-east England—dotted with pine clumps and birch and oak woods. It is protected as part of the High Weald Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty and for its rare heathland wildlife, including Dartford warblers and nightjars.

History

The name derives from the Old English Æscende or similar forms, meaning ash-tree hill. The wider forest was a Norman royal hunting forest, enclosed with a pale (fence) in the thirteenth century under Henry III, and for centuries it supported deer for the chase. From the Tudor period the forest's timber, coppice, and charcoal fuelled the Wealden iron industry, whose hammer ponds survive in the valleys. The landscape of open heath was maintained for centuries by commoners grazing livestock and cutting bracken and gorse; when grazing declined in the twentieth century, birch and pine began to invade, and conservation grazing and cutting have since been used to restore the heath.

Rights over the common land belong to commoners of the surrounding parishes, and its management—formalised by the Ashdown Forest Act 1885 and later legislation—is in the hands of a Board of Conservators. A landmark case in the 1880s, in which the commoner Bernard Hale successfully resisted Lord De La Warr's enclosures, helped secure the forest's public future.

Winnie-the-Pooh

A. A. Milne bought Cotchford Farm at Hartfield on the forest's edge in 1925, and the forest provided the setting for his Winnie-the-Pooh stories; the fictional Hundred Acre Wood corresponds to the real Five Hundred Acre Wood nearby. Sites familiar from the books and E. H. Shepard's illustrations include Gills Lap (the "Enchanted Place"), the Lone Pine, and Poohsticks Bridge, where visitors still play the game of racing sticks under the bridge. The Ashdown Forest Centre at Wych Cross provides information on the forest's history and wildlife.

Geology and landscape

The forest sits on the Hastings Beds of the Wealden Group—sandstones and clays of early Cretaceous age—rising to 223 metres (732 ft) at Camp Hill, among the highest points in East Sussex, with sweeping views to the North and South Downs. Deep stream valleys (ghylls) cut the plateau, sheltering ancient woodland, while exposed ridges carry the heath of heather, gorse, and bracken.
