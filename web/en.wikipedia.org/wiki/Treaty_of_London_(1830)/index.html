The Treaty of London of 1830 — usually called the London Protocol — was an agreement of 3 February 1830 between the three great powers Britain, France and Russia by which Greece was recognised as a fully independent, sovereign state, the first such settlement arising from the Greek War of Independence against the Ottoman Empire.

Background and terms

The protocol followed the allied victory at the Battle of Navarino (1827), the Russo-Turkish War of 1828–29 and the Treaty of Adrianople (1829), by which the Ottoman Empire accepted the powers' mediation. Whereas earlier arrangements had envisaged an autonomous Greek principality under Ottoman suzerainty, the 1830 protocol declared Greece entirely independent, under a hereditary monarch to be chosen from a European royal house (but not from the ruling families of the three guarantors). Its borders were drawn at the Arta–Volos line, giving the new state the Peloponnese, Attica and central Greece north to that line, plus Euboea and the Cyclades — considerably less than Greek maximal claims.

Aftermath

After the assassination of Governor Ioannis Kapodistrias in 1831 threw Greece into disorder, the powers in 1832 imposed a monarchy: the Bavarian prince Otto became the first king of Greece under the Convention of London (1832), with the powers guaranteeing a loan. The Ottoman Empire recognised the settlement the same year. The 1830 protocol is the founding international act of the modern Greek state.
