Origins

Jacobitism (from Jacobus, Latin for James) was the political movement dedicated to restoring the exiled House of Stuart to the thrones of England, Scotland, and Ireland after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 deposed James II and VII. Jacobites held that James's son, James Francis Edward Stuart ("the Old Pretender"), remained the legitimate heir by divine right, and that the Hanoverian succession was usurpation. The movement drew on High Church Anglican nonjurors, Catholics, Highland clans, Irish nationalists, and Tories alienated by the Whig establishment.

The risings

Jacobitism expressed itself in four major armed risings: the 1689 rising in Scotland (crushed at Killiecrankie though its leader Dundee was killed); the 1715 rising ("the Fifteen"), led by the Earl of Mar after George I's accession, defeated at Sheriffmuir and Preston; the small 1719 rising, a Spanish-backed fiasco at Glenshiel; and the 1745 rising ("the Forty-Five") of Charles Edward Stuart, ended at Culloden in 1746.

Supporters and culture

Beyond arms, Jacobitism sustained a rich underground culture: coded toasts ("the little gentleman in velvet," i.e. the mole whose hill tripped William III's horse), Jacobite poetry, secret societies, and an extensive network of agents across Britain, Ireland, France, and Rome, where the exiled court resided from 1719. Support was strongest in the Scottish Highlands, Catholic Ireland, and northern England.

Decline

After Culloden, repressive legislation and the Highlands' economic transformation drained the movement of force. The death of Charles Edward Stuart in 1788, followed by that of his brother Henry Benedict Stuart in 1807, ended the direct Stuart line. Romantic writers, above all Sir Walter Scott, transformed Jacobitism from a political threat into the sentimental mythology of modern Scotland.
