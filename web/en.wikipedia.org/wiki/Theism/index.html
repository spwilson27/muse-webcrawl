Theism

Theism is broadly defined as the belief in the existence of a god or deities. In common parlance, it typically refers to belief in a personal god who created the universe and remains actively involved in it, as in the Abrahamic religions.

Terminology

The term comes from the Greek theos (god). It was used by Ralph Cudworth (1678) in contrast to atheism; in the 17th and 18th centuries it was sometimes near-synonymous with deism, but modern usage distinguishes them: deists hold that God created the world but does not intervene in it, whereas theists generally affirm continuing divine action, revelation and providence.

Forms of theism

  • Monotheism — belief in a single God (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism).
  • Polytheism — belief in many gods (historical Greek, Norse and Egyptian religions; many forms of Hinduism).
  • Pantheism — God is identical with the universe.
  • Panentheism — the universe is in God, but God is more than the universe.
  • Henotheism — devotion to one god while accepting others.
  • Deism — a non-interventionist creator, often classed separately.

Arguments

Classical arguments for God's existence include the cosmological argument, the teleological (design) argument, the ontological argument of Anselm, and arguments from morality, consciousness and religious experience. Counter-arguments include the problem of evil, Occam's razor and the burden-of-proof challenge pressed by atheists and agnostics. The philosophy of religion continues to debate these claims, alongside questions about divine attributes — omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence and eternity — studied in philosophical theology.
