Finite geometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fano plane, the finite projective plane of order 2.

A finite geometry is any of a number of systems of geometry with only a finite number of points. The familiar Euclidean geometry is not finite, because a Euclidean line contains infinitely many points. A geometry based on the graphics displayed on a computer screen, where the pixels are considered to be the points, would be a finite geometry.

Finite planes

The simplest finite geometries are the finite affine and projective planes. A finite projective plane of order n has n2 + n + 1 points, with the same number of lines, each line containing n + 1 points. The smallest example is the Fano plane of order 2, with 7 points and 7 lines. Finite planes are known to exist for every order that is a prime power; whether planes exist for other orders is a famous open problem — the Bruck–Ryser–Chowla theorem rules out some orders, and order 10 was excluded by a massive computer search.

Finite spaces

More generally, finite geometries can be constructed from finite fields: the projective space PG(n, q) over the Galois field GF(q) gives a finite geometry whose points, lines, and planes satisfy incidence axioms analogous to those of ordinary projective space.

Applications

Finite geometries are closely connected with combinatorics, coding theory, and the design of experiments (block designs).
