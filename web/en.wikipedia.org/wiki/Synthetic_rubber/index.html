Synthetic rubber

A synthetic rubber is an artificially made elastomer — a polymer with rubber-like elasticity — manufactured mainly from petroleum-derived monomers. Synthetic rubbers now account for the majority of world rubber consumption, especially in tyres, where they are blended with natural rubber. Major types include styrene-butadiene rubber (SBR, the highest-volume synthetic rubber), polybutadiene rubber (BR), nitrile rubber (NBR, resistant to oils), neoprene (polychloroprene, CR), butyl rubber (IIR, used for inner tubes and liners), ethylene propylene diene monomer rubber (EPDM), and silicone rubbers for high-temperature and medical uses.

History

Early synthetic rubbers were developed in Germany before and during the First World War (Bayer's methyl rubber) and in the 1920s–30s with Buna rubbers (butadiene polymerised with sodium, Natrium) and Buna-S (styrene-butadiene). In the United States, DuPont commercialised neoprene in 1931. The decisive expansion came during the Second World War: cut off from Southeast Asian natural rubber plantations after Japan's conquests, the United States mounted a crash government-industry programme that built dozens of plants and raised output of GR-S (government rubber–styrene) from a few thousand to over 700,000 tonnes per year by 1945, supplying the Allied tyre and equipment effort. Cold War and post-war advances in emulsion and solution polymerisation, and later in catalysts, greatly improved properties and consistency.

Production and uses

Synthetic rubbers are produced by emulsion or solution polymerisation of monomers such as butadiene, styrene, isoprene, chloroprene, acrylonitrile and isobutylene, then coagulated, dried and baled. They are compounded with fillers (carbon black, silica), oils, vulcanising agents and antioxidants before shaping. Applications include tyres (treads, sidewalls), hoses, belts, seals and gaskets, footwear soles, adhesives, gloves, and specialised aerospace and medical products. Compared with natural rubber, synthetics offer tailored resistance to heat, oil, ozone and abrasion, though natural rubber retains advantages in resilience and tear strength, so the two are often blended.
