Homestake experiment

The Homestake experiment (sometimes called the Davis experiment) was an astrophysics experiment led by Raymond Davis Jr. at the Homestake Gold Mine in Lead, South Dakota, to detect neutrinos emitted by nuclear fusion in the Sun. Running from the late 1960s until 1994, it was the first experiment to measure the flux of solar neutrinos reaching the Earth — and it found far fewer than theory predicted, creating the "solar neutrino problem" that took three decades to resolve.

Design

The detector sat about 1,478 metres (4,850 feet) underground to shield it from cosmic rays. It consisted of a tank containing roughly 380,000 litres (100,000 gallons) of perchloroethylene, a chlorine-rich dry-cleaning fluid. Occasionally a solar neutrino would convert a chlorine-37 nucleus into a radioactive argon-37 nucleus; every few months helium gas was bubbled through the tank to extract the handful of argon atoms produced, which were then counted by their radioactive decay in miniature proportional counters.

Results and the solar neutrino problem

Davis's team detected only about one-third of the neutrino flux predicted by John Bahcall's standard solar model. The discrepancy persisted through years of checking and became one of the outstanding puzzles of twentieth-century physics. It was ultimately explained by neutrino oscillation — neutrinos change flavour on their journey from the Sun, and the chlorine detector was sensitive to only one flavour — confirmed by the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in 2001–2002.

Legacy

Davis shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics for pioneering contributions to the detection of cosmic neutrinos. The Homestake Mine later became the Sanford Underground Research Facility, which now hosts experiments including the LUX-ZEPLIN dark matter search.
