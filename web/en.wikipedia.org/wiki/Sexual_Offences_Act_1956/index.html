Sexual Offences Act 1956

The Sexual Offences Act 1956 (4 & 5 Eliz. 2. c. 69) was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that consolidated the English law of sexual offences, largely restating provisions from earlier statutes including the Offences against the Person Act 1861 and the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885. It governed sexual offences in England and Wales from 1957 until it was largely repealed and replaced by the Sexual Offences Act 2003.

Provisions

The Act created or restated offences including rape (section 1, then defined as unlawful sexual intercourse with a woman who did not consent), intercourse with girls under 13 and under 16, incest, buggery, gross indecency between men, indecent assault, abduction, and offences connected with prostitution — including living on the earnings of prostitution and controlling a prostitute. Its provisions on homosexual acts were amended by the Sexual Offences Act 1967, which partially decriminalised homosexual acts in private between men over 21 in England and Wales.

Reform

Over the decades the Act was repeatedly amended: the marital rape exemption implied by its wording was abolished by the House of Lords in R v R (1991) and by statute in 1994, and male rape was recognised in 1994. Following a government review, the Sexual Offences Act 2003 repealed almost all of the 1956 Act and recast the law with a modern definition of consent, effective 1 May 2004.
