Chromaticism

The twelve pitches of the chromatic scale

Chromaticism is a compositional technique interspersing the primary diatonic pitches and chords with other pitches of the chromatic scale. Chromaticism is the product of the union of the modal and tonal practices of western music, and in contradistinction to the diatonic scale and major/minor tonality. Compositions which incorporate chromaticism are often called chromatic compositions.

The chromatic scale encompasses all twelve of the available pitches in an octave, in contrast to the diatonic scale, a regular seven-note scale comprising five whole steps and two half steps. In chromatic music, half steps are used as structural pitches rather than as passing ornamentation, and notes outside the prevailing key are introduced systematically.

History

Chromatic writing appeared in the Renaissance in the works of madrigalists such as Carlo Gesualdo and Luca Marenzio, whose extreme chromatic passages anticipated later developments. In the Baroque era, chromaticism was frequently used to express grief, in figures such as the descending chromatic fourth known as the passus duriusculus.

During the nineteenth century, composers including Richard Wagner, Franz Liszt, and Frédéric Chopin expanded chromatic harmony dramatically. The prelude to Wagner's Tristan und Isolde is often cited as a landmark in the dissolution of functional tonality and a precursor of atonality in the twentieth century.

Types of chromaticism

Chromaticism may be melodic, involving the successive use of chromatic pitches, or harmonic, involving chords containing notes foreign to the key, such as secondary dominants, augmented sixth chords, and the Neapolitan chord. Modal mixture, borrowing chords from the parallel major or minor key, is another related technique.
