Julian day

Julian day
EpochNoon, 24 November 4714 BC (proleptic Julian calendar) = 1 January 4713 BC (proleptic Gregorian)
Introduced byJoseph Justus Scaliger (1583); adopted for astronomy by John Herschel
UnitDays (with fractional part for time of day)

The Julian day is the continuous count of days since the beginning of the Julian period, and is used primarily by astronomers, and in software for easily calculating elapsed days between two events (e.g. food production date and sell-by date).

The Julian period is a chronological interval of 7,980 years; year 1 of the Julian period was 4713 BC. The Julian calendar year 2026 is year 6739 of the current Julian period. The next Julian period begins in the year AD 3268. Historians used the period to identify Julian calendar years within which an event occurred when no such year was given in the historical record, or when the year given by previous historians was incorrect.

The Julian day number (JDN) is the integer assigned to a whole solar day in the Julian day count starting from noon Greenwich Mean Time, with Julian day number 0 assigned to the day starting at noon on Monday, 24 November 4714 BC in the proleptic Julian calendar (1 January 4713 BC in the proleptic Gregorian calendar). For example, the Julian day number for 1 January 2000 was 2451545.

Variants

Because the starting point is so long ago, several reduced variants are in use:

  • Modified Julian day (MJD) = JD − 2400000.5, starting at midnight on 17 November 1858; used by NASA and others.
  • Truncated Julian day (TJD), used by NASA for space missions.
  • Lilian day number, counting from 15 October 1582, the start of the Gregorian calendar.
  • Rata Die number, counting from 1 January AD 1 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.

History

The Julian period was proposed by Joseph Justus Scaliger in 1583 in his work De emendatione temporum. It combines three cycles: the 28-year solar cycle, the 19-year Metonic cycle, and the 15-year indiction cycle; their product is 7,980 years. The astronomer John Herschel popularised the use of Julian day numbers in astronomy in his Outlines of Astronomy (1849).

Conversion

Algorithms convert between Julian day numbers and Gregorian calendar dates by arithmetic on integer counts of days, accounting for leap years in the Gregorian rule (years divisible by 4, except centuries not divisible by 400). The fractional part of a Julian date gives the time of day as a fraction of a day since noon.
