Beer–Lambert law

The Beer–Lambert law, also known as the Beer–Lambert–Bouguer law, relates the attenuation of light to the properties of the material through which the light is travelling. It states that the absorbance of a solution is directly proportional to the concentration of the absorbing species and to the path length of the light through the sample. The law is fundamental to spectrophotometry and analytical chemistry.


Light passing through a sample of path length l

Formulation

The law is commonly expressed as:

A = ε · l · c

where A is the absorbance (dimensionless), ε is the molar attenuation (extinction) coefficient, l is the optical path length, and c is the concentration of the absorbing species. Absorbance is also defined in terms of incident and transmitted light intensities, A = log10(I0/I).

History

The law combines the work of Pierre Bouguer (1729), who found that light intensity decreases exponentially through a medium; Johann Heinrich Lambert (1760), who formalised the dependence on path length; and August Beer (1852), who established the proportionality to concentration.

Applications and limitations

The Beer–Lambert law underpins quantitative UV–visible spectroscopy, colorimetry, and measurements such as blood oximetry and atmospheric gas monitoring. It assumes monochromatic, parallel light; a homogeneous, non-scattering medium; and no chemical changes in the absorber. Deviations occur at high concentrations, in the presence of scattering or fluorescence, and with polychromatic radiation.
