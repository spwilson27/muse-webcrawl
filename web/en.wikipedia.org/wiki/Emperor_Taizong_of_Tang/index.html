Emperor Taizong of Tang (28 January 598 – 10 July 649), personal name Li Shimin, was the second emperor of the Tang dynasty of China, reigning from 626 to 649. Traditionally regarded as a model ruler and the co-founder of the dynasty, he is celebrated for the prosperous and well-governed Zhenguan era (627–649).

Rise to power

Li Shimin was the second son of Li Yuan, who founded the Tang dynasty in 618 as Emperor Gaozu amid the collapse of the Sui. A brilliant cavalry commander while still in his teens and twenties, Li Shimin won the decisive campaigns that unified China, including the defeat of rival warlords at Hulao in 621. His military fame eclipsed that of his elder brother, Crown Prince Li Jiancheng, and the rivalry ended in bloodshed: on 2 July 626, in the Xuanwu Gate Incident, Li Shimin ambushed and killed both Jiancheng and his brother Li Yuanji, then compelled his father to name him crown prince. Gaozu abdicated weeks later.

Reign

As emperor, Taizong staffed his court with capable officials — Fang Xuanling, Du Ruhui, and the outspoken remonstrant Wei Zheng among them — and was famed for tolerating frank criticism. He lightened taxes and corvée, reformed the law code, expanded the examination system, and patronised scholarship, compiling standard editions of the Confucian classics. His armies destroyed the Eastern Turkic Khaganate in 630, capturing Jieli Khan, and extended Tang influence deep into Central Asia; neighbouring peoples acclaimed him Tian Kehan ("Heavenly Khan"). Later campaigns against Tuyuhun, the Tarim Basin city-states, and Goguryeo (645, ultimately unsuccessful) pushed Tang power to its early peak.

Legacy

Taizong died in 649, possibly hastened by elixirs taken for longevity, and was buried in the Zhaoling mausoleum, famous for its stone reliefs of his six favourite chargers. The Zhenguan era became the Confucian benchmark against which later rulers were judged, and the recorded dialogues between Taizong and his ministers, the Zhenguan Zhengyao, served for centuries as a handbook of statecraft across East Asia.
