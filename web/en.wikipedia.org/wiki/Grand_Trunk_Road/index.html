The Grand Trunk Road (formerly known as Uttarapatha, Sadak-e-Azam, and the "River of Life") is one of Asia's oldest and longest major roads, running roughly 2,400 kilometres (1,500 mi) across the northern part of the Indian subcontinent from Chittagong and Kolkata in the east to Kabul in the west, passing through Delhi, Amritsar, Lahore and Peshawar. For centuries it has linked the eastern and western regions of South Asia, carrying traders, armies, pilgrims and migrants.

History

The route follows the ancient Uttarapatha, the "northern road" of early Indian texts, developed under the Maurya Empire in the 3rd century BCE, when it ran from the Ganges delta to Taxila and beyond. The Pashtun emperor Sher Shah Suri rebuilt and extended it in the 16th century — planting trees, and building caravanserais, wells and mosques along it — and the Mughals maintained it as the Badshahi Sadak ("Imperial Road"). Under British rule it was metalled and renamed the Grand Trunk Road, celebrated by Rudyard Kipling in his novel Kim (1901) as a "broad, smiling river of life."

The road today

The historic alignment is now covered by modern highways: in India largely by National Highway 19 and National Highway 44, in Pakistan by the N-5, and in Afghanistan by the Kabul–Jalalabad road. It remains one of the most heavily used transport corridors in the region.
