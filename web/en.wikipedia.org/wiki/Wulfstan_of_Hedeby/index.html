Wulfstan of Hedeby

Wulfstan of Hedeby (Latin: Wulfstanus; fl. c. 880) was a Danish merchant and traveller of the late 9th century. His account of a voyage from Hedeby (in modern Schleswig-Holstein, Germany) eastward along the southern Baltic coast to the trading port of Truso in Prussian territory is preserved, together with the voyage of Ohthere of Hålogaland, in the Old English translation of Orosius's Histories against the Pagans, produced in the circle of King Alfred the Great. Wulfstan's report is one of the earliest written sources for the peoples and customs of the southern Baltic.

The voyage

Wulfstan related that he sailed from Hedeby to Truso in seven days and nights, keeping Wendland (the Slavic coast) to starboard and the Danish islands of Langeland, Lolland, Falster and Scania to port, lands he noted belonged to Denmark. Beyond Bornholm he passed Blekinge, Möre, Öland and Gotland, which belonged to the Swedes (Sweon). Truso stood on the shore of the Vistula Lagoon near the mouth of the river Ilfing (the Elbląg), in the land of the Este — the Old Prussian people he calls the Eastas.

Account of the Prussians

The most detailed part of Wulfstan's report describes the customs of the Este, especially their funerary practices. He reports that they could produce artificial cold to preserve the bodies of the dead uncorrupted for long periods — up to six months — while drinking and games continued, after which the corpse was cremated. The deceased's property was divided into portions laid out along a course, and horsemen raced for them, the fastest rider winning the largest share. He also describes horse racing, mead drinking, and notes that the poor drank mare's milk while the rich drank mead.

Significance

Wulfstan's account, alongside Ohthere's, is a key source for Viking Age geography and trade, the geography of the Baltic peoples before their Christianisation, and the history of the port of Truso, whose site near modern Elbląg, Poland, was rediscovered by archaeologists in the late 20th century.
