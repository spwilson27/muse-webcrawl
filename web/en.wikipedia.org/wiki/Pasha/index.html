Pasha

Pasha (Turkish: paşa) was a high rank in the Ottoman political and military system, typically granted to governors, generals and dignitaries. It was the highest title of official honor in the Ottoman Empire, and was also used in the Kingdom of Egypt and in Morocco.

History

The word is derived from the Persian pādshāh ("master king") and entered Turkish usage as a title of honor. In the Ottoman Empire, the title was conferred by the sultan upon notable officials. Pashas were further distinguished by the number of horsetails (tugh) that served as their insignia of rank: one, two, or three. Three-horsetail pashas were generally viziers and governors of major provinces.

The title often formed part of a proper name, as in Enver Pasha, Mustafa Kemal Pasha (later Atatürk), and Muhammad Ali Pasha of Egypt.

Usage elsewhere

In Egypt, the title continued to be used during the periods of the Khedivate and the Kingdom, ranking above bey. The Republic of Egypt abolished titles of nobility in 1952. In Morocco, "pacha" remains a title given to certain senior officials and urban administrators.

The title is sometimes rendered as basha in older English sources.
