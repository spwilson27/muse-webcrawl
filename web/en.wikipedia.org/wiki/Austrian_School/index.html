Austrian School

The Austrian School is a heterodox school of economic thought that emphasizes methodological individualism, marginalism, and the importance of subjective value, entrepreneurship, and spontaneous order. Originating in late-19th-century Vienna, it is associated with Carl Menger, Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, Ludwig von Mises, and Friedrich Hayek.

History

Carl Menger's Principles of Economics (1871) founded the school and helped launch the marginal revolution alongside Jevons and Walras. Böhm-Bawerk developed capital and interest theory and critiqued Marxism. In the 20th century Mises systematized the school in Human Action (1949) and developed Austrian business cycle theory; Hayek extended work on prices as information, spontaneous order, and the knowledge problem, winning the Nobel Memorial Prize in 1974.

Methodology

Austrians stress praxeology — deductive reasoning from the axiom of human action — in Mises's version, and subjectivism: value is not intrinsic but assigned by individuals. They emphasize uncertainty, dispersed knowledge, and market processes over equilibrium models.

Major contributions

  • Subjective theory of value and marginal utility resolving the diamond–water paradox
  • Opportunity cost and imputation theory
  • Austrian business cycle theory: credit expansion distorts interest rates and causes malinvestment
  • Economic calculation problem in socialism (Mises, Hayek)
  • Spontaneous order and catallaxy

Criticism

Mainstream economists criticize Austrian rejection of econometrics and mathematical modelling, and its business cycle theory. Others value its contributions to price theory, entrepreneurship (Israel Kirzner), and institutional economics.
