Ku Klux Klan

The Ku Klux Klan (/ˌkuː klʌks ˈklæn/), commonly shortened to the KKK or the Klan, is the name of several distinct American white supremacist, far-right terrorist organisations, the first of which was founded in 1865. The Klan has existed in three separate eras, each promoting extremist reactionary positions such as white nationalism, anti-immigration and — especially in its later manifestations — Nordicism, antisemitism, anti-Catholicism, Prohibition, right-wing populism, homophobia, Islamophobia and anti-communism.


A Ku Klux Klan parade in Washington, D.C., in the 1920s, during the second Klan's peak.

First Klan (1865–1872)

The first Klan was founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, in late 1865 or early 1866 by six former Confederate officers. It rapidly became a vehicle for violent resistance to Reconstruction in the South, murdering and intimidating freedmen and their white allies. Under its first Grand Wizard, former Confederate general Nathan Bedford Forrest, the organisation was nominally disbanded in 1869, and federal enforcement — including the Enforcement Acts of 1870–71 and President Grant's suspension of habeas corpus in parts of South Carolina — effectively destroyed it by 1872.

Second Klan (1915–1944)

In 1915 William J. Simmons revived the Klan on Stone Mountain, Georgia, inspired by D. W. Griffith's film The Birth of a Nation and the lynching of Leo Frank. Professional promoters turned it into a mass movement: by the mid-1920s membership numbered in the millions, with strongholds in the Midwest as well as the South. The second Klan targeted Black Americans, Jews, Catholics and immigrants, and wielded considerable political influence before scandals — notably the 1925 conviction of Indiana Grand Dragon D. C. Stephenson for rape and murder — and internal feuding caused membership to collapse. It was formally dissolved in 1944.

Third Klan (1946–present)

After the Second World War, fragmented and decentralised Klan groups resurged in opposition to the civil rights movement, committing murders including those of activists in Mississippi (the 1964 "Mississippi Burning" killings) and the 1963 Birmingham church bombing. Federal prosecutions, civil lawsuits (notably the 1987 judgment that bankrupted the United Klans of America) and infiltration reduced the movement to a set of small, unconnected groups, estimated by the Southern Poverty Law Center to number only a few thousand members in recent decades.

Terminology and symbolism

Klan ranks use elaborate titles (Grand Wizard, Grand Dragon, Exalted Cyclops), and its rituals and symbols — white robes and hoods, burning crosses ("cross lighting") and the "Mystic Insignia of the Klansman" — were largely codified by the second Klan, drawing on the romanticised depiction in Thomas Dixon's novels and Griffith's film.
