Hemianopsia

Hemianopsia (or hemianopia) is the loss of one half of the visual field in one or both eyes. It results from damage to the visual pathways behind the optic chiasm — such as the optic tract, optic radiations or occipital cortex — most commonly from stroke, but also from tumours, trauma or other lesions. The pattern of field loss helps localise the lesion.

Types

Homonymous hemianopsia is loss of the same side of the visual field in both eyes (for example, the right half in each eye) and indicates a lesion behind the chiasm on the opposite side. Bitemporal hemianopsia is loss of the outer (temporal) halves of both fields, classically caused by a pituitary tumour pressing on the optic chiasm. Binasal hemianopsia, loss of the inner halves, is rare. Partial defects include quadrantanopia, affecting one quarter of the field.

Symptoms and effects

People with hemianopsia may bump into objects on the affected side, lose their place while reading, and have difficulty driving or navigating. Because the defect affects corresponding halves of both eyes, patients sometimes misinterpret it as blindness in one eye. The condition is diagnosed by visual field testing (perimetry) and imaging of the brain to find the cause.

Management

Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible. Rehabilitation includes visual scanning training to compensate with eye movements, occupational therapy, and in some cases prism lenses that shift images from the blind field into the seeing field. Some spontaneous recovery can occur in the first months after a stroke.
