Bell state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of two qubits linked as a Bell pair, with correlated measurement arrows]
A Bell pair: two qubits in a maximally entangled state.

Bell states (or EPR pairs) are the four specific maximally entangled quantum states of two qubits, named for John Stewart Bell. They form the canonical basis of two-qubit entanglement: for example the singlet-like state (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2 and the triplet-like (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, in which neither qubit has a definite state yet measurements on the pair are perfectly correlated (or anticorrelated) along any axis.

Bell states are the working currency of quantum information: Quantum teleportation consumes one Bell pair plus two classical bits to transfer a qubit; superdense coding uses one pair to send two bits with one qubit; entanglement swapping chains Bell pairs to extend quantum networks; and Bell tests measure correlations between just such states. Bell pairs are generated optically (parametric down-conversion), in trapped ions, superconducting circuits and solid-state defects, and their fidelity is the benchmark figure of merit for quantum hardware.

The four states

The four Bell states are related by applying a Pauli operation (X, Y, Z or identity) to one qubit of any one of them — the fact exploited by teleportation's correction step and by dense coding's encoding. They are maximally entangled: each qubit's reduced state is the completely mixed state, carrying no information alone — all information lives in the correlations, the essence of Quantum entanglement.
