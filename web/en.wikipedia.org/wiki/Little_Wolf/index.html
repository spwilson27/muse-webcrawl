Little Wolf (c. 1820–1904), Sweet Medicine Chief of the Northern Cheyenne.

Little Wolf (Cheyenne: Ó'kôhómôxháahketa, literally "Little Coyote"; c. 1820–1904) was a Northern Cheyenne chief and Sweet Medicine Chief, one of the four principal chiefs of the Cheyenne Council of Forty-four. He is best known, together with Dull Knife, for leading the Northern Cheyenne Exodus of 1878–79, a desperate journey from Indian Territory back to the tribe's Montana homeland.

Life

Little Wolf distinguished himself as a warrior and leader in the conflicts on the northern Plains, including the wars following the Sand Creek massacre of 1864. He was a signatory to the Treaty of Fort Laramie in 1868 and fought alongside the Lakota and other Cheyenne in the Great Sioux War of 1876, though accounts differ on his precise role at the Battle of the Little Bighorn. In 1877 the Northern Cheyenne were forcibly removed to Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), where disease and starvation devastated them.

The Northern Cheyenne Exodus

In September 1878 Little Wolf and Dull Knife led some 350 people north. Their band split: Dull Knife's followers were captured and imprisoned at Fort Robinson, Nebraska, where many were killed in the Fort Robinson breakout of January 1879. Little Wolf's band wintered in Nebraska's Sandhills, evading pursuit, and reached Montana in the spring of 1879, surrendering to troops under Lt. William P. Clark. Little Wolf later served as an army scout. His advocacy helped secure the establishment of the Northern Cheyenne Indian Reservation in 1884.

Later years and legacy

Little Wolf remained a respected elder, though his standing was damaged late in life when, in 1890, he killed a fellow tribesman in a dispute and went into exile from the tribal council. He died in 1904. His life story was recounted by the anthropologist George Bird Grinnell and forms part of Mari Sandoz's Cheyenne Autumn (1953), later filmed by John Ford.
