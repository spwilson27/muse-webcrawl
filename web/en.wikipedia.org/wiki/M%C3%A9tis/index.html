Métis

Métis
Regions: Canada (Prairies, Northwest), northern US
Languages: English, French, Michif
Recognition: Section 35, Constitution Act, 1982
National body: Métis National Council (founded 1983)

The Métis are an Indigenous people of Canada (and parts of the northern United States) descended from unions between First Nations women and European — mainly French and Scottish — fur traders, who developed a distinct culture, language, and identity, particularly in the Red River region of present-day Manitoba.

Culture and language

Métis culture blends Indigenous and European elements: the Michif language combines French nouns with Cree verbs, and traditions include fiddle music, jigging, intricate beadwork, and the infinity-flag symbol. Historically, Métis communities were central to the fur trade and the buffalo hunt on the northern plains.

Resistance and recognition

Under Louis Riel, the Métis led the Red River Resistance of 1869–70, which brought Manitoba into Confederation, and the North-West Resistance of 1885, after which Riel was executed. The Métis were recognised as one of Canada's Aboriginal peoples in the Constitution Act, 1982, and landmark court decisions, including Powley (2003) and Daniels (2016), have affirmed Métis rights and federal responsibility.

Today

Hundreds of thousands of Canadians identify as Métis. The Métis Nation is represented by the Métis National Council and provincial affiliates, and in 2019 Canada signed a self-government agreement with the Métis Nation of Alberta, with further agreements following.
