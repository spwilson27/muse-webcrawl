Hopi pottery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hopi pottery is the ceramic tradition of the Hopi people of northeastern Arizona, whose ancestors (the Ancestral Puebloans) have made pottery in the region for more than a thousand years. It is renowned for its smooth, matte-painted surfaces in shades of cream, yellow, orange and red-brown, decorated with bold geometric and stylised designs, including motifs of birds, feathers and migration.

Technique

Hopi pottery is hand-built by the coil method — no potter's wheel is used — then scraped, sanded and stone-polished. After painting with mineral and vegetal pigments applied with a yucca-leaf brush, vessels are fired outdoors in a shallow pit, traditionally using sheep dung as fuel; the firing atmosphere produces the characteristic warm yellow-to-orange "blush" colours on the white-firing clay. Shapes include seed jars (nearly closed forms with a tiny opening), jars, bowls, canteens and wedding vases.

History and revival

The tradition descends through Pueblo pottery types such as Jeddito and Sikyatki polychromes of the 14th–17th centuries. By the late 19th century, Hopi pottery had declined under competition from metal and enamelware. A revival began at First Mesa in the 1890s, led by the Tewa potter Nampeyo (c. 1859–1942) of Hano, who studied ancient Sikyatki sherds excavated nearby and recreated and reinterpreted their designs; her work, encouraged by traders and the developing tourist and collector market, re-established Hopi pottery as a fine art and a livelihood.

Modern potters

Nampeyo's descendants — including Fannie Nampeyo and, in later generations, potters such as Dextra Quotskuyva — together with other First Mesa families, continue the tradition; distinguished Hopi potters from other mesas include Helen Naha (Feather Woman) of Santa Clara–Hopi background and the makers of finely carved and incised wares. Hopi pottery is held in major museum collections and commands high prices, while also remaining in ceremonial and domestic use.
