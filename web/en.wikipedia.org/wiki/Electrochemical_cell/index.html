Electrochemical cell

An electrochemical cell is a device that generates electrical energy from chemical reactions or uses electrical energy to facilitate chemical reactions. Electrochemical cells which generate a current from an energy-producing chemical reaction are called voltaic cells or galvanic cells, while those that generate chemical reactions via electrolysis are called electrolytic cells.

Structure

A typical cell consists of two half-cells, each containing an electrode immersed in an electrolyte. Oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode. The half-cells are connected by a salt bridge or porous membrane that allows ions to flow while preventing the electrolytes from mixing, completing the electrical circuit.

Galvanic and electrolytic cells

In a galvanic cell, such as the Daniell cell (zinc and copper electrodes in their sulfate solutions), a spontaneous redox reaction produces a voltage, about 1.10 V for the Daniell cell. In an electrolytic cell, an external power source drives a non-spontaneous reaction — for example the electrolysis of water or the industrial production of aluminium and chlorine.

Applications

Electrochemical cells underlie batteries (primary and rechargeable), fuel cells, electroplating, corrosion and its prevention, and electrochemical sensors. The theoretical voltage of a cell is given by the difference of the standard electrode potentials of its half-reactions, and its behaviour under load by the Nernst equation.
