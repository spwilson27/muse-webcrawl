TECO (Text Editor & Corrector, originally Tape Editor and Corrector) is a text editor developed at MIT in the early 1960s by Dan Murphy for the PDP-1, and later ported across DEC's PDP and VAX systems. It is one of the ancestors of modern interactive text editing and is historically significant as the editor out of which Emacs grew.

Character

TECO is character-string oriented: every character typed is potentially a command, and its cryptic one- and two-letter command strings were famously compared to line noise—giving rise to the saying that "any character string is a valid TECO program, though not necessarily the one you intended". TECO includes loops, conditionals and arbitrary computation through its macro and Q-register facilities, making it effectively a programming language. Richard Stallman's Emacs began (1976) as a set of "Editor MACroS" for TECO on the PDP-10 ITS system before being rewritten as a Lisp-based editor.

Legacy

TECO influenced later editors and string-processing tools, and the folklore of hacker culture (recorded in the Jargon File) preserves many TECO anecdotes. Implementations and emulations of TECO still run on modern systems under hobbyist and retro-computing auspices.
