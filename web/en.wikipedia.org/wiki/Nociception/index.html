Nociception

Nociception (from Latin nocere, "to harm") is the sensory nervous system's detection of, and response to, potentially or actually tissue-damaging stimuli. Specialised sensory neurons called nociceptors transduce mechanical, thermal, and chemical noxious stimuli into electrical signals that travel via the spinal cord to the brain. Nociception is the physiological process underlying the experience of pain, but the two are not identical: pain also includes emotional and cognitive processing, and nociception can occur without conscious pain, as in spinal reflexes.

Mechanism

Nociceptors are free nerve endings found in skin, joints, muscle, and viscera. Fast, sharp "first pain" is carried by thinly myelinated Aδ fibres, while slow, dull, burning "second pain" travels in unmyelinated C fibres. Key transduction molecules include the TRPV1 channel (activated by heat above about 43 °C, acid, and capsaicin), TRPA1 (reactive chemicals), and acid-sensing ion channels. Signals enter the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and ascend mainly in the spinothalamic tract to the thalamus and somatosensory cortex, while other pathways reach the limbic system, contributing pain's affective dimension.

Modulation and sensitisation

Nociceptive transmission is modulated by descending pathways from the brainstem and by local "gate control" interactions in the dorsal horn. After tissue injury, inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins, bradykinin, and nerve growth factor lower nociceptor thresholds (peripheral sensitisation), producing hyperalgesia and allodynia; sustained input can induce central sensitisation, implicated in chronic and neuropathic pain. Nociception also triggers protective reflexes — such as the withdrawal reflex — and autonomic responses.

Clinical relevance

Understanding nociception underlies most analgesic therapy, from NSAIDs (which reduce peripheral sensitisation) to opioids (which act on central modulation) and local anaesthetics (which block conduction). Rare genetic disorders of nociceptive channels, such as congenital insensitivity to pain from mutations in SCN9A, show how essential the system is for survival.
