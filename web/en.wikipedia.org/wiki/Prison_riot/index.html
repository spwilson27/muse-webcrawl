Prison riot

Prison riots are acts of concerted defiance by groups of prisoners against prison authorities.

A prison riot is an act of concerted defiance or disorder by a group of prisoners against the administrators of a prison, or against another group of prisoners. Prison riots are often preceded by grievances about conditions such as overcrowding, poor food, inadequate medical care or perceived abuses by staff, though they can also arise from conflicts between rival groups of inmates.

Causes

Sociologists and criminologists have identified a number of recurring contributing factors, including:

  • Overcrowding and understaffing, which strain facilities and raise tensions.
  • Poor living conditions, including sanitation, food quality and lack of programmes.
  • Changes in administration or routine that prisoners perceive as losses of privileges.
  • Gang rivalries and competition for control of illicit markets inside the prison.
  • Flashpoints such as a violent incident between guards and prisoners.

Notable examples

Famous prison riots include the 1971 Attica Prison riot in New York, in which 43 people died; the 1980 New Mexico State Penitentiary riot, one of the most violent in United States history; the 1990 Strangeways Prison riot in Manchester, England, which lasted 25 days; and the 1992 Carandiru massacre in Brazil, in which military police killed 111 prisoners while suppressing an uprising.

Response and prevention

Authorities typically respond by isolating the affected areas, negotiating with prisoner representatives, and, where negotiation fails, retaking control by force — a course of action that can produce heavy casualties and subsequent legal and political fallout. Long-term prevention strategies focus on reducing overcrowding, improving conditions, grievance procedures, intelligence-gathering, and staff training in de-escalation.
