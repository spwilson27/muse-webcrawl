8-bit (art)

8-bit art
MediumDigital art, video games, animation
OriginsGraphics of third- and fourth-generation game consoles and home computers of the 1980s–1990s
RelatedPixel art, chiptune, demoscene

8-bit art is a style of digital art modelled on the graphics of 8-bit computers and video game consoles of the 1980s and early 1990s — machines such as the Nintendo Entertainment System, Commodore 64 and ZX Spectrum. Characterised by visibly blocky pixels, small canvases and severely limited colour palettes, the style arose from hardware constraints but is now chosen deliberately as an aesthetic in games, animation, music videos and graphic design.

Origins

Early video game hardware could display only a handful of colours per sprite and resolutions as low as 256×240 pixels. Artists working within these limits developed techniques such as dithering — using patterns of two colours to suggest a third — and careful anti-aliasing by hand. As hardware moved to 16-bit and then 3D graphics, the 8-bit look became a marker of the medium's early history.

Revival

From the 2000s onward, independent game developers revived the style both as nostalgia and as a practical production method: titles such as Cave Story, Fez and Shovel Knight used pixel graphics to critical acclaim. The aesthetic also spread to animated series, advertising and the related musical style of chiptune, in which music is composed for vintage sound chips. Modern "8-bit" works often cheat the original constraints, using larger palettes and resolutions than period hardware allowed.
