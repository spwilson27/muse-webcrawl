The western grey kangaroo (Macropus fuliginosus), also referred to as the western grey giant kangaroo, black-faced kangaroo, mallee kangaroo, and (on Kangaroo Island) the sooty kangaroo, is a large and very common kangaroo found across the southern part of Australia, from just south of Shark Bay through coastal Western Australia and much of South Australia, into western Victoria and the Murray–Darling Basin in New South Wales.

Description

It is one of the largest macropods in Australia. Adults weigh 28 to 54 kilograms (62 to 120 lb) and stand 0.84 to 1.1 metres (2 ft 9 in to 3 ft 7 in) tall, with a long muscular tail providing balance. The fur is thick and coarse, ranging from pale greyish-brown to chocolate brown; the throat, chest and belly are paler. It is very similar in appearance to its close relative, the eastern grey kangaroo, and the two were long considered the same species. The western grey has a distinctive musky odour, leading to the nickname "stinker" for mature males in some districts.

Behaviour and ecology

The western grey kangaroo is mainly nocturnal and crepuscular, resting in the shade during the heat of the day and grazing at night on grasses and shrubs. It lives in groups called mobs of up to about fifteen animals. During the breeding season males compete for females, including through "boxing" contests. Females give birth to a single tiny joey, which develops in the pouch for roughly nine months and continues to suckle after emerging for several more months. The species is abundant and is harvested commercially under quota in some states for meat and hides.

Subspecies

Two subspecies are commonly recognised: M. f. fuliginosus, endemic to Kangaroo Island, and M. f. melanops of the mainland. The island form is smaller and darker, hence the name "sooty kangaroo".
