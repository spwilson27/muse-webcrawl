Corn syrup

Corn syrup is a food syrup which is made from the starch of corn (called maize in many countries) and contains varying amounts of sugars: glucose, maltose and higher oligosaccharides, depending on the grade. Corn syrup is used in foods to soften texture, add volume, prevent crystallization of sugar, and enhance flavor. It can be made into high-fructose corn syrup (HFCS) by processing with the enzyme D-xylose isomerase to convert some of its glucose into fructose.

Production

Historically, corn syrup was produced by combining corn starch with dilute hydrochloric acid and then heating under pressure. The process was invented by the German chemist Gottlieb Kirchhoff in 1811. Today, corn syrup is obtained from the wet milling of corn; the starch slurry is treated with the enzyme alpha-amylase to shorten the starch chains, and then with glucoamylase to break the shortened chains down to glucose. The resulting syrup is filtered and refined.

Uses

Major uses of corn syrup in commercially prepared foods are as a thickener, a sweetener, and a humectant — an ingredient that retains moisture and thus maintains a food's freshness. In the United States, corn syrup was formerly a major sweetener because cane and beet sugar were generally more expensive; high-fructose corn syrup became widely used in soft drinks and processed foods from the 1970s onward. Light corn syrup is clear and moderately sweet, while dark corn syrup, a combination of corn syrup, molasses, caramel color and flavor, is much sweeter and more strongly flavored.
