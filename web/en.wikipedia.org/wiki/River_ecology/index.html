River ecology

River ecology is the study of the ecological interactions between organisms and their environment within rivers and streams. Rivers are flowing bodies of water that move continuously in one direction, shaping the landscapes they pass through and sustaining distinctive communities of plants, animals, fungi, and microorganisms adapted to moving water.

Characteristics of river ecosystems

Unlike lakes, rivers are open systems with a strong directional flow of water, nutrients, sediment, and energy from upstream to downstream. Key physical factors include current velocity, discharge, temperature, dissolved oxygen, turbidity, and the composition of the riverbed substrate, which ranges from bedrock and boulders in headwaters to sand and silt in lowland reaches.

The River Continuum Concept, proposed by Vannote and colleagues in 1980, describes how biological communities change predictably along a river's length. Headwater streams, often shaded by riparian vegetation, depend heavily on leaf litter and other organic matter washed in from the surrounding land. Shredding invertebrates such as stonefly and caddisfly larvae break this material down. In mid-sized rivers, sunlight reaches the water more readily, algae and aquatic plants become important producers, and grazers become more abundant. In large lowland rivers, fine particles of organic matter drifting from upstream support collector organisms such as mussels and filter-feeding insect larvae.

Habitats within rivers

Rivers contain a mosaic of habitats. Riffles are shallow, fast-flowing reaches with coarse substrate and high oxygen levels, home to many mayfly, stonefly, and caddisfly larvae. Pools are deeper, slower areas where fish rest and feed. The hyporheic zone is the saturated sediment beneath and beside the channel where surface water and groundwater mix, supporting specialised invertebrates and serving as a refuge during floods and droughts. Floodplains, connected to the channel during high flows, provide spawning and nursery habitat for fish and rich feeding grounds for birds.

Biodiversity

Rivers support a disproportionately large share of Earth's biodiversity relative to their surface area. Freshwater ecosystems in general harbour roughly ten percent of all known species. Riverine organisms include fish, amphibians, freshwater molluscs, crustaceans, aquatic insects, and riparian plants. Many species are migratory: salmon, eels, and shad move between rivers and the sea, while other fish migrate long distances within river systems such as the Amazon and Mekong.

Threats

River ecosystems are among the most threatened on Earth. Dams fragment habitats and block migrations; water abstraction lowers flows; pollution from agriculture, industry, and sewage degrades water quality; and channelisation destroys habitat complexity. Invasive species and climate change add further pressure. Freshwater species populations have declined more steeply on average than those in terrestrial or marine systems, making river conservation a major focus of environmental science and policy.
