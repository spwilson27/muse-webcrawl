Couronian colonization of the Americas

Couronian colonization of the Americas refers to the overseas ventures of the Duchy of Courland and Semigallia, a small Baltic duchy (in present-day Latvia) that was a vassal of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Despite its modest size, Courland under Duke Jacob Kettler established colonies in West Africa and the Caribbean in the mid-seventeenth century, most notably on the island of Tobago.

Duke Jacob's ambitions

Duke Jacob Kettler (reigned 1642–1682) pursued an ambitious programme of shipbuilding, trade, and manufacture intended to make Courland a maritime power. Couronian ships traded widely, and the duke sought colonial possessions that could supply tobacco, sugar, and other tropical products while providing markets for Couronian goods.

New Courland on Tobago

In 1654 a Couronian expedition under the command of Willem Mollens arrived at Tobago aboard the ship Das Wappen der Herzogin von Kurland, carrying settlers, soldiers, and officials. The colonists founded a settlement on the north-eastern side of the island around Great Courland Bay and named the colony New Courland (German: Neu-Kurland). A fort, Fort Jacobus, was built, and plantations were laid out. The Dutch, however, had established their own settlement, Nieuw-Vlissingen, on the opposite side of the island, and rivalry between the two colonies was intense.

Decline and loss

The colony's fortunes collapsed when Sweden invaded Courland during the Second Northern War in 1658 and Duke Jacob was taken prisoner; deprived of support from home, the Couronian garrison on Tobago surrendered to the Dutch in 1659. Tobago changed hands repeatedly among the Dutch, French, and English in the following decades. Courland made attempts to recover the island, and settlers were sent again in the 1680s, but the ventures failed, and by the end of the seventeenth century the Couronian colonial enterprise in the Americas had effectively ended.

Legacy

Great Courland Bay and other Tobago place names preserve the memory of the Couronian presence. The episode is notable as the colonial undertaking of one of the smallest European states to participate in the colonisation of the Americas, and it remains a point of interest in Latvian historiography.
