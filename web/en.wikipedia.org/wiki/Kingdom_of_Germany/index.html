Kingdom of Germany

The Kingdom of Germany (Latin: Regnum Teutonicum, "Teutonic Kingdom") was the eastern part of the Frankish empire created by the Treaty of Verdun (843), which developed into the core realm of the Holy Roman Empire. At its greatest extent it comprised modern Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Bohemia, Slovenia, the Low Countries and parts of France, Italy and Poland.

Formation

Under Louis the German and his successors the East Frankish kingdom acquired a distinct identity. Following the end of the East Frankish Carolingians, the dukes elected Conrad I (911) and then Henry I of the Saxon Ottonian dynasty (919). Henry's son Otto I was crowned king at Aachen in 936 and emperor in Rome in 962, binding the German kingship to the imperial title.

Structure

The kingdom was organised around great stem duchies — Saxony, Franconia, Bavaria, Swabia and Lorraine — and governed through an itinerant court, royal monasteries and bishoprics (the Imperial Church system). Kings were formally elected by the leading princes; the Golden Bull of 1356 fixed seven prince-electors. Election of the "King of the Romans" usually preceded imperial coronation by the Pope.

Development

From the Investiture Controversy of the 11th–12th centuries, princely power grew at the expense of the crown. Under Frederick II, statutes in favour of the ecclesiastical and secular princes (1220, 1232) entrenched territorial rule. The kingdom's identity became subsumed within the Holy Roman Empire, formally the "Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation" by the late 15th century, until the empire's dissolution in 1806.
