Hazard analysis and critical control points, or HACCP (/ˈhæsʌp/), is a systematic preventive approach to food safety from biological, chemical, and physical hazards in production processes. Rather than relying on inspection of finished products, HACCP identifies where hazards are most likely to occur and controls them at those points. It is used throughout the food chain, from primary production to final consumption, and is mandatory in many jurisdictions for meat, seafood, juice and dairy processing.

History

HACCP was developed in the 1960s by the Pillsbury Company in collaboration with NASA and the United States Army Natick Laboratories, to produce food for crewed space flights with near-absolute assurance of safety. The approach drew on the engineering concept of failure mode and effects analysis. Pillsbury presented the system publicly at a food-safety conference in 1971. In the 1990s, following outbreaks of foodborne illness such as the 1993 Jack in the Box E. coli incident, regulators in the United States and the European Union progressively made HACCP-based plans compulsory for many food businesses. The Codex Alimentarius Commission publishes the international reference text, and the principles underpin the ISO 22000 food safety standard.

The seven principles

  1. Conduct a hazard analysis. Identify potential biological, chemical and physical hazards at each step.
  2. Determine the critical control points (CCPs). Points at which control is essential to prevent, eliminate or reduce a hazard to an acceptable level.
  3. Establish critical limits for each CCP, such as minimum cooking temperature or maximum storage time.
  4. Establish monitoring procedures to check that each CCP is under control.
  5. Establish corrective actions to be taken when monitoring shows a CCP is not under control.
  6. Establish verification procedures to confirm the HACCP system is working effectively.
  7. Establish documentation and record-keeping procedures.

Implementation

Businesses assemble a multidisciplinary HACCP team, describe the product and its intended use, construct a flow diagram of the process, and confirm it on site. Prerequisite programmes such as good manufacturing practice, sanitation standard operating procedures and pest control underpin the plan. Critical limits commonly monitored include cooking temperatures, refrigeration temperatures, water activity, pH, and chlorine levels in wash water.
