Net art, also known as net.art or internet art, is a form of art that uses the Internet as its primary medium and distribution platform. It emerged in the mid-1990s as artists began to treat the World Wide Web — its hypertext structure, browsers, protocols and networked social space — as artistic material. The term originated, according to legend, from a garbled email fragment received by Slovenian artist Vuk Ćosić in 1995.

History

Early net art communities formed around mailing lists such as Nettime and Rhizome (founded 1996), and listservs like 7-11. Artists such as the duo Jodi disrupted the browser interface with raw HTML and code; Olia Lialina's My Boyfriend Came Back from the War (1996) became a canonical hypertext narrative; Alexei Shulgin organized the field with manifestos and competitions. In 1997 documenta X included net art works, and the Whitney Museum's BitStreams (2001) and its artport portal signalled institutional recognition.

Characteristics

Net art is typically interactive, participatory and networked, existing only in the distributed conditions of the online environment: it cannot simply be printed or hung on a wall without losing essential aspects. Recurring practices include browser art, software art, ASCII art, works using search engines and databases, and "internet-aware" art that reflects on surveillance, identity and the emerging platform economy. Preservation is a central problem, as link rot, obsolete plug-ins and changing standards decay works quickly; organizations like Rhizome maintain emulation-based conservation.
