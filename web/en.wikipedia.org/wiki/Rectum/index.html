Rectum

The rectum (pl.: rectums or recta) is the final straight portion of the large intestine in humans and some other mammals, and the gut in others. The adult human rectum is about 12 centimetres (4.7 in) long, and begins at the rectosigmoid junction (the end of the sigmoid colon) at the level of the third sacral vertebra, ending at the anal canal.

Structure

The rectum follows the curve of the sacrum and features transverse folds (the valves of Houston). Its lower portion, the rectal ampulla, dilates to store feces before defecation. Unlike other parts of the colon, it lacks taeniae coli, and it derives blood supply from the superior, middle, and inferior rectal arteries.

Function

The rectum acts as a temporary storage place for feces. As its walls distend with filling, stretch receptors signal the nervous system, producing the urge to defecate; the internal and external anal sphincters control expulsion.

Clinical significance

Conditions of the rectum include rectal prolapse, proctitis, hemorrhoids, and rectal cancer. The rectum is examined by digital rectal examination and endoscopy, and is a route for administering some medications.
