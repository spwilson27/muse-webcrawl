Satya

Satya (Sanskrit: सत्य, satya) is a Sanskrit word usually translated as "truth" or "essence", and is a central concept and virtue in Indian religions including Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Derived from sat, "that which is, being, reality", satya denotes not only factual truthfulness in speech but fidelity to reality in thought, word, and deed.

In Hinduism

Satya appears among the oldest concepts of the Vedas, where it is paired with ṛta, the cosmic order. In the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali, satya is the second of the five yamas (restraints), requiring abstention from falsehood — with the qualification, developed in the epics and commentaries, that truth should be spoken in a way that is beneficial and not needlessly harmful. The Mundaka Upanishad's phrase satyameva jayate — "truth alone triumphs" — became the national motto of India and appears on its state emblem.

In Jainism and Buddhism

In Jainism, satya is one of the five great vows (mahavratas) undertaken by monks and, in limited form, by laypeople; because speech can cause harm, Jains couple it with the doctrine of anekantavada, the many-sidedness of truth. In Buddhism, truthful speech (sammā-vācā) is an element of the Noble Eightfold Path, and the Four Noble Truths themselves are called arya-satya.

Gandhi

Mohandas Gandhi built his philosophy on satya, holding that "Truth is God" and naming his method of nonviolent resistance satyagraha — "holding firmly to truth" or "truth-force". For Gandhi, satya was inseparable from ahimsa (nonviolence): since no person possesses the whole truth, no one is justified in using violence to impose it.
