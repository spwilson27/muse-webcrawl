Band gap

Band gap
DisciplineSolid-state physics
SymbolEg
Typical unitElectronvolt (eV)

In solid-state physics and solid-state chemistry, a band gap, also called a bandgap or energy gap, is an energy range in a solid where no electronic states exist. In graphs of the electronic band structure of solids, the band gap refers to the energy difference (often expressed in electronvolts) between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band in insulators and semiconductors. It is the energy required to promote an electron from the valence band to the conduction band.

Classification of materials

Materials are broadly classified by the size of their band gap. In conductors the valence and conduction bands overlap, so there is effectively no gap. Semiconductors have a small gap, typically below about 3 eV, allowing thermal excitation of some electrons across it. Insulators have a large gap, commonly 5 eV or more, so that conduction is negligible at ordinary temperatures.

Examples

Silicon has an indirect band gap of about 1.12 eV at room temperature, gallium arsenide a direct gap of about 1.42 eV, and diamond a gap of about 5.5 eV. The magnitude and type (direct or indirect) of the gap determine a material's optical absorption and emission properties, and hence its usefulness in devices such as light-emitting diodes, lasers and solar cells.

Temperature dependence

Band gaps generally shrink as temperature increases, an effect often described by the empirical Varshni relation. Pressure and alloying also shift the gap, which is exploited in band-gap engineering.
