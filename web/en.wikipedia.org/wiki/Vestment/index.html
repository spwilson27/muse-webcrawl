Vestment

Vestments are the liturgical garments and articles associated primarily with the Christian religion, especially by Eastern Churches, Catholics (of all rites), Lutherans, and Anglicans. Many other groups also make use of liturgical garments; among the Reformed (Calvinist) churches a minister will often wear the Geneva gown. Vestments worn by clergy in worship descend largely from the ordinary dress of the Greco-Roman world of late antiquity, preserved and elaborated by the Church after secular fashions changed.

Western vestments

The principal vestments of the Western Church include the alb, a long white tunic; the stole, a band of cloth worn around the neck by deacons, priests, and bishops in different arrangements; the chasuble, the outermost garment of the celebrant at Mass or Eucharist; the dalmatic worn by deacons; and the cope, a long processional cloak. Colours vary with the liturgical season: white for feasts of Christ and saints who were not martyrs, red for martyrs and the Holy Spirit, green for Ordinary Time, and violet for Advent and Lent.

Eastern vestments

In the Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches, corresponding garments include the sticharion, epitrachelion, zone (belt), epimanikia (cuffs), and the phelonion. Vestments are often richly embroidered with crosses, icons, and liturgical inscriptions.

History

The use of distinctive liturgical dress developed gradually. By the fourth century, clergy began retaining older, more formal styles of dress for worship as everyday clothing changed. Through the Middle Ages vestments became increasingly ornate, with heavy embroidery and precious fabrics. The Second Vatican Council encouraged noble simplicity in liturgical dress, while traditional forms continue in widespread use.
