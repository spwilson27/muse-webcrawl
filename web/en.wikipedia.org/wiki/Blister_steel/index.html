Blister steel

Blister steel is steel produced by the cementation process, in which bars of wrought iron were packed with powdered charcoal in sealed stone or clay chests and heated for days at high temperature. Carbon diffused into the surface of the iron, converting it to steel, and the reaction of carbon with slag inclusions within the iron produced bubbles of carbon monoxide that raised characteristic blisters on the surface of the bars — hence the name.

Process

In a cementation furnace, iron bars (often high-quality Swedish bar iron) were layered with charcoal in refractory chests and heated to around 1,100 °C for between seven and eleven days, then allowed to cool slowly. The resulting bars had a high carbon content at the surface grading to low carbon in the core. The quality of blister steel varied; bars were graded and sometimes broken and re-forged ("shear steel") or melted in crucibles to produce homogeneous crucible steel, the process developed by Benjamin Huntsman in Sheffield around 1740.

History

The cementation process was practised in continental Europe from the early 17th century and in England at Newcastle and in the Sheffield region. Blister steel was the principal form of steel produced in Britain until the mid-19th century, used for edge tools, springs, files and cutlery, before being superseded by bulk steelmaking processes such as the Bessemer process.
