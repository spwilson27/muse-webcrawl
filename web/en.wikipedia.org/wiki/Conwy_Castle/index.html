Conwy Castle (Welsh: Castell Conwy) is a fortification in Conwy, located in North Wales. It was built by Edward I during his conquest of Wales, between 1283 and 1289. Constructed as part of a wider project to create the walled town of Conwy, the combined defences cost around £15,000, a huge sum for the period.

History

Edward I built Conwy as one of the castles of his "iron ring" designed to encircle and subdue the Welsh principality of Gwynedd. The master mason was James of St George, a Savoyard architect responsible for many of Edward's Welsh castles. The castle and town walls were built in an astonishingly short time, largely between 1283 and 1287.

During the rising of Madog ap Llywelyn in 1294–95, Edward was besieged at Conwy and supplied by sea until relief arrived. In 1401, during the revolt of Owain Glyndŵr, the castle was briefly seized by Welsh forces. It later saw service in the English Civil War, when it was held for the Royalists before falling to Parliament in 1646, after which it was slighted.

Architecture

The castle occupies a rocky outcrop overlooking the River Conwy estuary. It has a rectangular plan divided into an outer and an inner ward, defended by eight massive round towers and two barbicans. The inner ward contained the royal apartments, including a great hall, chambers and a chapel, arranged around a small courtyard. The curtain walls are unusually well preserved, and the castle retains its characteristic machicolations and a complete circuit of wall walks.

World Heritage status

In 1986 Conwy Castle was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the "Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd" site, together with Harlech, Caernarfon and Beaumaris. UNESCO considers the castles outstanding examples of late 13th-century military architecture in Europe. The castle is managed by Cadw and is a major tourist attraction.
