The Story of My Life is an autobiography by Helen Keller, published in 1903 when Keller was 22 years old and a student at Radcliffe College. It recounts her early life, including her childhood illness that left her deaf and blind, her education under teacher Anne Sullivan, and her experiences up to her college years.

Contents

The book describes Keller's birth in Tuscumbia, Alabama, in 1880, the fever at 19 months that took her sight and hearing, and her frustrated early childhood. It gives a vivid account of the arrival of Anne Sullivan in March 1887 and the famous breakthrough at the water pump, when Keller grasped that the finger-spelled word "w-a-t-e-r" named the cool liquid flowing over her hand. The narrative continues through her education at the Perkins Institution for the Blind, her speech lessons, and her studies at the Cambridge School for Young Ladies and Radcliffe College. The published volume combines Keller's autobiography with letters and a supplementary account of her education written by her editor, John Albert Macy.

Composition and publication

Portions of the work were first serialized in the Ladies' Home Journal in 1902. Keller wrote the autobiography during her junior year at Radcliffe, with Sullivan and Macy assisting in the editing. Keller dedicated the book to Alexander Graham Bell, a longtime supporter of deaf education who had first directed the Keller family to the Perkins Institution.

Reception and legacy

The book was an immediate success and has remained in print for over a century, translated into numerous languages (including some 50 by many accounts). It is a staple of school curricula and, together with William Gibson's play and film The Miracle Worker, shaped the public image of Keller and Sullivan. Keller later expanded her life story in the books The World I Live In (1908) and Midstream: My Later Life (1929).
