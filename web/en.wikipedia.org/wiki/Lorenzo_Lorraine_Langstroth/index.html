Lorenzo Lorraine Langstroth (25 December 1810 – 6 October 1895) was an American clergyman, teacher and beekeeper who is credited with inventing the modern movable-frame beehive, the Langstroth hive, which remains the standard hive design used throughout the world. He is often called the "Father of American Beekeeping".

Early life and ministry

Langstroth was born in Philadelphia and graduated from Yale University in 1831. He served as a tutor at Yale while studying theology, and subsequently held Congregational pastorates in Massachusetts, including at Andover and Greenfield. Periodic bouts of depression and ill health forced him to give up regular preaching, and he worked as a school principal and teacher while pursuing his interest in insects — an enthusiasm dating from childhood.

Bee space and the movable-frame hive

Keeping bees in the popular Huber leaf hive, Langstroth observed in 1851 that bees leave a consistent gap of roughly three-eighths of an inch (about 6–9 mm) in the hive: spaces smaller than this they fill with propolis, and spaces larger they fill with comb. This "bee space" gave him the key to a practical hive. On 5 October 1851 he conceived a hive in which frames hang from a rabbet at the top of the box, separated from each other and from the hive walls by exactly one bee space, so that each frame can be lifted out for inspection without crushing bees or breaking comb. He received a United States patent for his hive in October 1852.

In 1853 he published Langstroth on the Hive and the Honey-Bee, a practical manual that went through many editions and, in later revisions with Charles Dadant, became the standard work of modern apiculture. Langstroth also introduced the Italian honey bee to the United States on a significant scale, importing queens from Italy in 1859–1860.

Later life and legacy

Langstroth never grew wealthy from his invention, and his patent was widely infringed; he spent much of his later life in Oxford, Ohio, and finally in Dayton, where he died in 1895 while preaching a sermon. He is buried in Woodland Cemetery, Dayton. The Langstroth hive — with its standardised boxes, frames and bee space — enabled the industrialisation of honey production and remains the most common hive pattern in North America and much of the world.
