Romanticism in France

French Romanticism
Periodc. 1800 – 1850
Key figuresVictor Hugo, Madame de Staël, Chateaubriand, Lamartine, Delacroix, Géricault, Berlioz
LandmarksHernani (1830); The Raft of the Medusa (1819); Symphonie fantastique (1830)

French Romanticism was the Romantic movement in French literature, art and music of the first half of the nineteenth century. Reacting against the rules of Neoclassicism and the rationalism of the Enlightenment, French Romantics exalted emotion, imagination, nature, the medieval past and the individual genius, and they fought celebrated public battles to free French verse and drama from classical unities and the alexandrine's strictures.

Origins

Precursors include Jean-Jacques Rousseau's celebration of feeling and the pre-Romantic Sturm und Drang influence from Germany, mediated by Madame de Staël's De l'Allemagne (1810/1813), which introduced German Romantic thought to France. François-René de Chateaubriand's René (1802) and Génie du christianisme (1802) established the melancholy, world-weary hero — the mal du siècle — while translations of Shakespeare, Byron and Scott loosened classical models.

Literature and drama

Alphonse de Lamartine's Méditations poétiques (1820) launched Romantic poetry. Victor Hugo became the movement's leader: his preface to Cromwell (1827) served as its manifesto, and the premiere of his verse drama Hernani on 25 February 1830 provoked the famous "Battle of Hernani" between Romantic and classical partisans in the audience of the Comédie-Française. Hugo's novels Notre-Dame de Paris (1831) and later Les Misérables (1862) carried Romanticism's medievalism and social sympathy to a mass readership; Alfred de Musset, Alfred de Vigny, George Sand and Théophile Gautier were central figures, and Stendhal and Balzac engaged critically with the movement.

Painting and music

In painting, Théodore Géricault's The Raft of the Medusa (1818–19) and The Charging Chasseur announced Romantic energy and contemporary subject matter; Eugène Delacroix — The Massacre at Chios (1824), The Death of Sardanapalus (1827), Liberty Leading the People (1830) — championed colour and movement against the linear classicism of Ingres. In music, Hector Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique (1830) exemplified programmatic Romanticism, and Paris hosted the expatriate Romantics Chopin and Liszt. By mid-century, Realism — Courbet in painting, Flaubert in prose — was displacing Romanticism as the avant-garde.
