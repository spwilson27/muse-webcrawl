Communist Party of the Soviet Union

The Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) was the founding and ruling political party of the Soviet Union. Founded in 1912 as the Bolshevik faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party, it ruled the USSR as a one-party state from 1922 until its dissolution in 1991.

History

Led by Vladimir Lenin, the Bolsheviks seized power in the October Revolution of 1917, renamed themselves the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks) in 1918, and in 1952 adopted the name CPSU. Under Joseph Stalin the party oversaw forced industrialisation, collectivisation, and purges. After Stalin's death in 1953, Nikita Khrushchev's de-Stalinisation, Leonid Brezhnev's era of stability/stagnation, and Mikhail Gorbachev's glasnost and perestroika followed.

Organisation

The party was organised on democratic centralism. Its highest body in theory was the Congress; in practice power lay with the Politburo, Secretariat, and Central Committee. The General Secretary became effectively head of state and government. Membership reached about 19 million by the late 1980s.

Role in the state

Article 6 of the 1977 Soviet Constitution enshrined the CPSU's leading role. The party controlled appointments through the nomenklatura system across government, military, and economy, with parallel party committees at every level.

Dissolution

After the failed August 1991 coup, Gorbachev resigned as General Secretary, Boris Yeltsin suspended and then banned the party in Russia, and the USSR dissolved in December 1991. Successor parties include the Communist Party of the Russian Federation.
