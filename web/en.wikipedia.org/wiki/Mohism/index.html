Mohism

Mohism (Chinese: 墨家; pinyin: Mòjiā; lit. 'School of Mo') was an ancient Chinese philosophy of logic, rational thought and science developed by the academic scholars who studied under the ancient Chinese philosopher Mozi (c. 470 BC – c. 391 BC), embodied in an eponymous book: the Mozi. Mohism flourished during the Warring States period as one of the Hundred Schools of Thought and was a major rival to Confucianism.

Doctrine

The core Mohist doctrine is impartial care (jian ai), the principle that people should care equally for all others regardless of their relationship, in contrast to the Confucian emphasis on graded love and family obligation. Mohists held that morality consists in promoting the benefit of all and eliminating harm, a stance often described as a form of consequentialism or state utilitarianism. Other central tenets included opposition to aggressive war (fei gong), frugality and opposition to lavish funerals and music, meritocracy in government, belief in the will of Heaven (Tian zhi) and in ghosts and spirits who reward the good and punish the wicked, and the "three tests" for evaluating doctrines: their basis in antiquity, their origin in the people's experience, and their benefit when applied.

Logic and science

The later Mohist Canons (Mojing) developed sophisticated work in logic, definitions, disputation, geometry, optics and mechanics. Mohist texts contain early descriptions of the pinhole camera (camera obscura), the lever, and principles of motion. Mohists were also renowned as experts in defensive siege warfare, travelling to defend cities under attack.

Decline

Mohism declined after the Qin unification and was marginalised under the Han dynasty in favour of Confucianism; the Mohist school disappeared by the middle of the Han period, though its texts were preserved and its ideas influenced Chinese thought. Interest revived in the late Qing and Republican eras, when scholars rediscovered Mohist logic and compared its ethics with Western utilitarianism.
