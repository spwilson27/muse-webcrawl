Bus deregulation in Great Britain

Bus deregulation in Great Britain refers to the removal of quantity controls on local bus services in England, Scotland, and Wales under the Transport Act 1985, effective from 26 October 1986 ("Deregulation Day"). Operators could run any route on a commercial basis by registering it, competing "on the road," while public authorities tendered only socially necessary services that the market did not provide. London was excluded and retained a franchised system.

Background

Since the Road Traffic Act 1930, bus services had been licensed by Traffic Commissioners, with territorial monopolies split between municipal operators and the state-owned National Bus Company. The Thatcher government argued that regulation had produced inefficiency and declining ridership, and abolished route licensing and split up and privatized the National Bus Company.

Effects

Initial effects included "bus wars" in some cities, the growth of minibus services, and sharp falls in public subsidy costs. Over the longer term, however, patronage outside London continued to decline, fares generally rose, and the industry consolidated into a few large groups (Stagecoach, FirstGroup, Arriva, Go-Ahead). Critics contrast Britain's deregulated regions with London, where franchised services saw sustained ridership growth.

Later reforms

The Bus Services Act 2017 allowed mayoral combined authorities to introduce franchising; Greater Manchester became the first to do so, launching its franchised "Bee Network" from 2023. Subsequent governments have moved to extend franchising powers more widely, effectively ending the deregulation experiment in several city regions.
