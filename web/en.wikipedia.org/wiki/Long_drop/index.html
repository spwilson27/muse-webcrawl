Long drop

A gallows arranged for the measured long drop

The long drop is a method of judicial hanging in which the condemned prisoner falls a measured distance calculated according to body weight, so that the fall is arrested by the rope with enough force to fracture the upper cervical spine and cause rapid death, rather than slow strangulation.

History

Earlier hanging practice in Britain used a short drop, under which the prisoner died by strangulation over several minutes. The long drop was introduced in the 19th century as a more humane alternative. It is generally credited to the Irish scientist Samuel Haughton, who published calculations in 1866, and to the English executioner William Marwood, who put it into practice from the 1870s and argued that the length of drop should be proportioned to the prisoner's weight.

The Home Office later issued official "Tables of Drops" (1888, revised 1913) that executioners such as John Ellis and Albert Pierrepoint used as a starting point, adjusting for the prisoner's physique and neck strength. Too short a drop risked slow strangulation; too long a drop risked decapitation, which occurred in a small number of botched cases.

Mechanism

The method aims to produce a fracture-dislocation of the second and third cervical vertebrae (the classic "hangman's fracture"), severing or irreparably damaging the spinal cord and causing immediate unconsciousness and rapid death. The noose is positioned with the knot under the left side of the jaw so that the head is thrown back sharply when the rope tightens.
