Military dictatorship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A military dictatorship is a form of government in which the military controls political power, whether through a junta of officers, a single officer-dictator, or a façade of civilian institutions subordinated to the armed forces. Military regimes were the most common type of authoritarian government in the mid-twentieth century, especially in Latin America, Africa, the Middle East and parts of Asia, but have become rarer since the Cold War's end.

They typically arise by coup d'état, justified by claims of civilian corruption, chaos or subversion. Barbara Geddes's typology distinguishes military regimes from personalist and single-party regimes, finding military regimes the shortest-lived: officers often prefer to return to the barracks once their corporate aims are met, and the institution's own hierarchy provides a mechanism for removing a failed ruler. Outcomes for human rights vary: some juntas (Chile 1973–90, Argentina 1976–83) ran campaigns of disappearance and torture; others held limited, scheduled returns to civilian rule.

Transitions

Military regimes usually end by negotiated transition, electoral defeat in a plebiscite, economic collapse or defeat in war (the Greek junta after Cyprus 1974, Argentina after the Falklands 1982). Pacts and reserved domains — amnesties, military budgets, senators-for-life — often mark the price of withdrawal, leaving “tutelary” powers that constrain the successor democracy.
