Weather vane

A weather vane, also called a wind vane or weathercock, is an instrument used for showing the direction of the wind. It is typically used as an architectural ornament on the highest point of a building. A weather vane consists of a rotating pointer — often an arrow or a decorative figure such as a rooster — mounted above fixed directionals indicating the cardinal points. The pointer's tail catches the wind so that the head points in the direction from which the wind is blowing.

History

Among the oldest known weather vanes was the bronze Triton atop the Tower of the Winds in Athens, built in the first century BC. Pope Gregory I is said to have declared the rooster a symbol of St. Peter, and in the ninth century Pope Nicholas I reportedly ordered a rooster placed on every church; rooster vanes (weathercocks) became a fixture of European churches. In early America, hand-forged vanes decorated barns and public buildings, and colonial craftsmen such as Shem Drowne created famous examples, including the grasshopper vane on Boston's Faneuil Hall (1742).

Design and use

A functional vane must be balanced on its axis and have enough surface area on its tail to respond to light winds. While modern meteorology measures wind direction with electronic sensors and wind socks, weather vanes remain common as decoration and folk-art objects; antique American vanes are highly collectible. The largest weather vane in the world, in Montague, Michigan, stands 48 feet tall and depicts a sailing ship.
