Compact space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, and more specifically in general topology, compactness is a property that seeks to capture, in abstract form, the idea of a space being "small" or "finitely describable". A topological space is called compact if every open cover of it has a finite subcover — that is, any collection of open sets whose union contains the space already contains it in finitely many of them.

Definition and examples

Formally, a space X is compact if for every collection {Ui} of open sets with X ⊆ ∪Ui, there is a finite subcollection whose union still contains X. Examples include any finite space, a closed bounded interval [a, b] of real numbers, the unit sphere, and the Cantor set. Non-examples include the open interval (0, 1), the whole real line ℝ, and infinite discrete spaces.

Heine–Borel and metric spaces

The Heine–Borel theorem states that a subset of Euclidean space ℝn is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded. In general metric spaces, compactness is equivalent to the space being complete and totally bounded, and also to sequential compactness: every sequence has a convergent subsequence whose limit lies in the space.

Properties and importance

Continuous functions on compact spaces attain their maximum and minimum values (the extreme value theorem) and are uniformly continuous; the continuous image of a compact space is compact; closed subsets of compact spaces are compact; and products of compact spaces are compact (Tychonoff's theorem). These facts make compactness one of the most useful notions in analysis, geometry, and mathematical physics — a finitary substitute for finiteness itself.
