Trilateration

Trilateration is the use of distances (or "ranges") to determine an unknown position, based on the geometry of circles, spheres, or triangles. In contrast to triangulation, which measures angles, trilateration measures distances from known reference points.

Derivation

In two dimensions, the position of an unknown point can be determined if its distances to three known, non-collinear points are known: each distance defines a circle on which the point must lie, and the intersection of the three circles fixes the location. In three dimensions, spheres replace circles, and four reference points generally suffice to resolve the position uniquely.

Applications

Trilateration is the fundamental principle behind satellite navigation systems such as the Global Positioning System (GPS), in which a receiver computes its position from the distances to several satellites, inferred from the travel times of radio signals. It is also used in surveying, robotics, seismology (locating earthquake epicentres from arrival times), and indoor positioning systems using Wi-Fi or ultra-wideband beacons.

Errors and geometry

The accuracy of a trilaterated position depends on the geometry of the reference points relative to the target. When reference points are nearly collinear, small measurement errors in distance produce large errors in position — an effect quantified in GPS as dilution of precision (DOP).
