Orthostatic hypotension

Orthostatic hypotension, also known as postural hypotension, is a medical condition wherein a person's blood pressure drops when they stand up or sit down. Primary orthostatic hypotension is often caused by autonomic nervous system disorders, while secondary causes include dehydration, blood loss, and medications. The drop in blood pressure may be sudden (vasovagal), within three minutes (classic), or gradual (delayed).

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms occur on standing and resolve on lying down: lightheadedness, dimming of vision, tinnitus, weakness, and in severe cases syncope (fainting), which can cause falls and fractures, particularly in older people.

Mechanism

On standing, gravity shifts 500–1000 mL of blood to the veins of the legs and abdomen. Normally baroreceptors trigger sympathetic vasoconstriction and an increase in heart rate, maintaining cerebral perfusion. Failure of this autonomic reflex — from neurodegenerative disease (such as Parkinson's disease or multiple system atrophy), diabetes, volume depletion, or drugs — produces the characteristic drop.

Diagnosis

Blood pressure and pulse are measured after five minutes lying, then one and three minutes after standing. A sustained fall of at least 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic confirms the diagnosis; a tilt table test may be used in unclear cases.

Management

Management addresses the underlying cause, reviews medications, and advises slow postural changes, adequate hydration and salt, physical counter-manoeuvres, and abdominal or leg compression. Persistent cases may be treated with midodrine, droxidopa, or fludrocortisone.
