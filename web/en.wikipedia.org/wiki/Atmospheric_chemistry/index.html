Atmospheric chemistry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atmospheric chemistry is a branch of atmospheric science in which the chemistry of the Earth's atmosphere and that of other planets is studied. It is a multidisciplinary approach of research and draws on environmental chemistry, physics, meteorology, computer modeling, oceanography, geology and volcanology and other disciplines.

Composition

The bulk composition of dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and 0.9% argon, but trace gases such as carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and ozone — present at parts per million or less — exert an outsized influence on climate and air quality. Water vapour varies from near zero to about 4% by volume.

Chemical processes

Solar ultraviolet radiation drives photochemistry in the upper atmosphere. The hydroxyl radical (OH) acts as the atmosphere's main oxidising agent, removing most reduced trace gases. In the stratosphere, the Chapman cycle describes the formation and destruction of ozone, while catalytic cycles involving chlorine and bromine — released from chlorofluorocarbons — cause ozone depletion, including the Antarctic ozone hole.

Tropospheric chemistry and pollution

In the troposphere, emissions of nitrogen oxides (NOx) and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight to produce ground-level ozone and photochemical smog. Oxidation of sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides yields sulphuric and nitric acids, the principal components of acid rain. Aerosol particles formed by gas-to-particle conversion affect cloud formation and scatter or absorb radiation.

Methods

Atmospheric chemists combine laboratory kinetics studies, field measurements from aircraft, balloons and ground stations, satellite remote sensing, and numerical models ranging from box models to global chemical transport models such as GEOS-Chem.
