Esker

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An esker, eskar, eschar, or os (sometimes called an asar, osar, or serpent kame) is a long, winding ridge of stratified sand and gravel, examples of which occur in glaciated and formerly glaciated regions of Europe and North America. Eskers are frequently several kilometres long and, because of their peculiar uniform shape, look like railway embankments.

Formation

Most eskers are argued to have formed within ice-walled tunnels by streams that flowed either under or within a glacier. Meltwater flowing in a subglacial or englacial tunnel deposits its load of sand and gravel; when the ice melts away, the sediment remains as a sinuous ridge standing in relief on the land surface — an example of inverted topography. Some eskers formed in open meltwater channels on the ice surface or at the glacier margin, and short, fan-shaped "beaded" eskers record successive positions of a retreating ice front.

Distribution and examples

Eskers are characteristic of the deglaciated landscapes of Ireland (the word derives from Irish eiscir, "a ridge"), Scotland, Scandinavia, Finland — where they are called harju — Canada, and the northern United States. Among the longest is the Thelon esker in Canada's Northwest Territories and Nunavut, extending some 800 km. The Esker Riada system in Ireland historically formed a routeway across the midlands bogs between Dublin and Galway. In Maine, the Katahdin Woods and Waters region and other parts of the state host extensive esker systems.

Importance

Because they are made of sorted sand and gravel, eskers are widely quarried for aggregate. They often underlie roads, since their dry, elevated crests make good routes through poorly drained terrain. Eskers also host important aquifers in Finland and elsewhere, and their distribution helps geologists reconstruct the drainage and retreat pattern of past ice sheets. Similar ridges identified on Mars have been interpreted as eskers, suggesting past glacial conditions there.
