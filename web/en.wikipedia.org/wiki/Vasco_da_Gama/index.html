Vasco da Gama

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Vasco da Gama's flagship São Gabriel, a nau of the India run]

Vasco da Gama (c. 1460 – 24 December 1524) was a Portuguese explorer who commanded the first ships to sail directly from Europe to India, rounding the Cape of Good Hope and opening the sea route that transformed world trade. His first voyage (1497–1499) reached Calicut on India's Malabar Coast in May 1498 after a crossing of the Indian Ocean guided from Malindi by a local pilot. Da Gama's voyages — including a brutal second expedition (1502) — founded Portugal's seaborne empire in Asia; he died in 1524 at Kochi, months after arriving as viceroy of Portuguese India.

The first voyage

Da Gama sailed from Lisbon in July 1497 with four ships, swung wide into the Atlantic to catch the winds, rounded the Cape (22 November 1497), and worked up the East African coast — hostile Mozambique, friendly Malindi — before the 23-day open-ocean crossing to Calicut (20 May 1498). Trade talks foundered on the modesty of his gifts against the Zamorin's expectations; the monsoon-timed return crossing took three months and killed much of his crew by scurvy — of about 170 men, only some 55 returned in 1499. The cargo of spices that did arrive repaid the voyage many times over.

Later career

His second voyage (1502), with twenty ships, combined trade with terror: he bombarded Calicut, extorted Kilwa, and infamously burned the pilgrim ship Miri with hundreds aboard. Portugal's India enterprise passed to others (Almeida, Albuquerque) who built the Estado da Índia; da Gama, ennobled as Count of Vidigueira, was sent out as viceroy in 1524 to curb corruption, and died at Kochi that Christmas Eve. Camões' epic Os Lusíadas (1572) made him Portugal's national hero; modern accounts weigh his seamanship against his cruelty.
