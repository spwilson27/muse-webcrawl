Geiger–Marsden experiment

Other namesGold foil experiment, Rutherford experiment
ExperimentersHans Geiger, Ernest Marsden
SupervisorErnest Rutherford
Date1908–1913
ResultDiscovery of the atomic nucleus

The Geiger–Marsden experiment (also called the gold foil experiment or the Rutherford experiment) was a landmark experiment by which scientists learned that every atom has a nucleus where all of its positive charge and most of its mass are concentrated. They deduced this by measuring how an alpha particle beam is scattered when it struck a thin metal foil. The experiments were performed between 1908 and 1913 by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden under the direction of Ernest Rutherford at the University of Manchester.

Summary

The prevailing theory of atomic structure at the time was the "plum pudding model", developed by Lord Kelvin and further developed by J. J. Thomson, in which an atom's positive charge was spread throughout its volume. If this model were correct, alpha particles would pass through the foil with only minor deflections. Instead, while most alpha particles did pass straight through, about 1 in 8,000 was deflected by more than 90 degrees, some bouncing almost directly back toward the source.

Rutherford later remarked that it was "as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it rebounded and hit you." The only plausible explanation was that the positive charge was concentrated in a tiny central mass, the nucleus, small enough that only the rare alpha particle passing very close to it would be strongly repelled.

Conclusions

Rutherford's 1911 analysis of the data led to the Rutherford model of the atom, in which an atom has a very small, very dense central nucleus of positive charge surrounded by mostly empty space and orbiting electrons. The experiment is considered a founding result of nuclear physics.
