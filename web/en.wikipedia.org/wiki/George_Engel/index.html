George Engel (15 April 1836 – 11 November 1887) was a German-American anarchist and labour activist. He was one of the eight defendants tried for conspiracy in the aftermath of the 1886 Haymarket bombing in Chicago, and one of the four who were hanged.

Life

Born in Kassel, Engel was orphaned young and apprenticed as a shoemaker. He emigrated to the United States in 1872, working in Philadelphia and then Chicago, where he opened a toy shop. Radicalised by unemployment during the depression of the 1870s and by the violent suppression of strikes, he joined the Socialist Labor Party and then the anarchist International Working People's Association, founding the journal Der Anarchist. He was known as an advocate of "propaganda of the deed" within Chicago's German anarchist movement.

Haymarket trial

Engel was not present at the Haymarket meeting of 4 May 1886 — he said he was at home playing cards — but prosecutors charged the defendants with conspiracy, arguing that their speeches and writings had incited the unknown bomb-thrower. Engel was convicted along with seven others and sentenced to death. In his address to the court he defended his anarchist convictions and declared he despised the government trying him.

Execution and legacy

Engel was hanged on 11 November 1887 together with August Spies, Albert Parsons and Adolph Fischer. In 1893 Illinois governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the surviving defendants and condemned the trial as a miscarriage of justice. Engel is buried at the Haymarket Martyrs' Monument in Forest Park, Illinois, a site of pilgrimage for the international labour movement; the executions are commemorated worldwide in connection with May Day.
