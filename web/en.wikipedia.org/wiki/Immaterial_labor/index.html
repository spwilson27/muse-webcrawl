Immaterial labor

Immaterial labor is a concept in Marxist and post-Marxist theory, developed principally by Italian autonomist thinkers such as Maurizio Lazzarato, Antonio Negri and Michael Hardt, describing labor that produces immaterial goods — information, communication, relationships, knowledge and emotional responses — rather than physical commodities.

Definition

Lazzarato, who introduced the term in the 1990s, defined immaterial labor as the labor that produces the informational and cultural content of commodities. It includes the work of computer programmers, designers, advertisers, service workers and caregivers. Hardt and Negri broaden the idea to "affective labor" — work that produces social networks, community and feelings of well-being.

Role in post-Fordism

Theorists argue that immaterial labor has become hegemonic in post-Fordist capitalism, much as industrial labor was under Fordism: even material production is increasingly organised around information, communication and cooperation. Because its products are social relations themselves, immaterial labor is said to blur the boundary between work and life and between production and consumption.

Criticism

Critics contend that the concept exaggerates the novelty and prevalence of such work, neglects the persistence of material and industrial labor globally, and can obscure class relations. Others question whether immaterial products are truly measurable within a labour theory of value.
