Braemar Castle is a five-storey L-plan castle in Aberdeenshire, Scotland, standing on a mound above the River Dee near the village of Braemar. It was built in 1628 by John Erskine, Earl of Mar, as a hunting lodge and stronghold. Burned during the Jacobite rising of 1689, it was later leased to the government as a garrison for Hanoverian troops and given its distinctive star-shaped curtain wall. Since the 18th century it has been associated with the Farquharson family of Invercauld, and it is now operated by the local community as a visitor attraction.

History

The castle was erected in 1628 by the Earl of Mar to consolidate the Erskine presence in the upper Dee valley and to overawe the unruly clans of the district. In 1689 it was set on fire by John Farquharson of Inverey, the "Black Colonel", acting for the Jacobite cause, to prevent its use by government forces. The ruin was acquired by the Farquharsons of Invercauld, and after the Jacobite rising of 1715—which was in fact launched at Braemar, where the Earl of Mar raised the standard of James Francis Edward Stuart—the castle was leased to the government in 1748 and rebuilt as a military garrison. It was during this period that the unusual curtain wall, shaped like a star or cogged ring and pierced by gun loops, was added, together with the cap-house roofs on the turrets.

The military garrison left in 1797, and the castle reverted to the Farquharsons, who refitted it as a family residence in the 19th century. It was used as a home by the Farquharson lairds and hosted Queen Victoria, who visited Deeside frequently from nearby Balmoral. In 2007 the castle was leased to Braemar Community Limited, a local charitable trust, which undertook fundraising and restoration and opened the castle and its twelve furnished rooms—covering some 400 years of Jacobite and family history—to the public.

Architecture

The castle is a compact five-storey tower house on an L-plan, harled and limewashed, with round corner turrets and small conical roofs. Its most striking feature is the 18th-century curtain wall, built for the garrison in an unusual star-shaped (cogged) plan adapted to musket defence. Inside, the great hall, drawing room, and bedrooms are furnished in styles ranging from the 17th to the 20th century, and the collections include Jacobite relics, family portraits, and items connected with the Farquharsons and the Braemar Gathering, the famous Highland games held in the village since the early 19th century.
