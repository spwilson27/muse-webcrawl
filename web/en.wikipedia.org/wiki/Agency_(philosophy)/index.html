Agency (philosophy)

Agency
FieldAction theory, metaphysics, ethics
Key topicsIntention, free will, moral responsibility, reasons for action

In philosophy, agency is the capacity of an agent to act in a world. It is normally opposed to natural forces, which are caused to act in a certain way by causation rather than by deliberation and choice. The philosophy of agency is a subdiscipline of action theory, concerned with topics including the nature of action, the difference between actions and mere events, intentional action, and the explanation of action by reasons, beliefs, and desires.

Concepts of agency

In the standard theory associated with Donald Davidson, an action is behaviour that is intentional under some description and caused, in the right way, by the agent's beliefs and pro-attitudes. Other accounts emphasize guidance and control by the agent, the role of plans and policies (as in Michael Bratman's planning theory), or the agent's identification with her motives (as in Harry Frankfurt's hierarchy of desires). Philosophers distinguish agency from mere behaviour by reference to intentionality, reasons-responsiveness, and the agent's ability to have done otherwise — a central issue in debates over free will and moral responsibility.

Related debates

Questions of agency extend beyond individual human action: philosophers debate group or corporate agency (whether firms, states, or other collectives can act and bear responsibility), shared agency in joint action, and the conditions under which children or non-human animals count as agents. In social theory, "agency" is also contrasted with "structure" in explaining social life, a usage developed by theorists such as Anthony Giddens.
