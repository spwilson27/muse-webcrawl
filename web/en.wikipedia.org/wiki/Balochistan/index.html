Balochistan is a historical region in southwestern Asia, home to the Baloch people, spanning southeastern Iran, southwestern Pakistan, and a small part of southern Afghanistan. The largest share forms Pakistan's province of Balochistan — the country's largest province by area but its least populous — while the Iranian portion forms most of the province of Sistan and Baluchestan.

Geography

The region is dominated by arid mountains and plateaus, including the Sulaiman and Kirthar ranges in the east and the Makran coastal range along the Arabian Sea. Rivers are largely seasonal; the Hingol is the largest river of the Pakistani province. The climate is harsh, with very hot summers in the lowlands, cold winters in the highlands, and scarce rainfall. The deep-water port of Gwadar, on the Makran coast, is a strategic hub of the China–Pakistan Economic Corridor.

History

Balochistan hosts some of South Asia's earliest farming settlements, notably Mehrgarh (c. 7000 BCE), a precursor of the Indus Valley Civilization. In the 19th century the region was divided among Britain, Persia, and Afghanistan: the Khanate of Kalat and neighbouring princely states came under British paramountcy, and the Durand Line (1893) and Goldsmid Line fixed modern borders. At the partition of India in 1947, Kalat's accession to Pakistan was contested, seeding a long-running Baloch nationalist movement and periodic insurgency in the Pakistani province. The Iranian portion has likewise experienced unrest and smuggling across the porous borders.

Economy and culture

The region is rich in natural gas (the Sui field), coal, copper, and gold — including the Reko Diq deposit — yet remains among the least developed parts of both countries, with livelihoods based on pastoralism, fishing, dates, and trade. Balochi is the principal language; Brahui, Pashto, and Persian are also widely spoken. Baloch culture is noted for its tribal codes of honour, embroidery, and oral epic poetry.
