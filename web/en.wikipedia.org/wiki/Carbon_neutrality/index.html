Carbon neutrality

Carbon neutrality is a state of net-zero carbon dioxide emissions, achieved by balancing emissions of carbon dioxide with their removal (often through carbon offsetting) or by eliminating emissions altogether. It is used in the context of carbon-dioxide-releasing processes associated with transport, energy production, agriculture and industry. Under the Paris Agreement, countries aim to reach global peaking of greenhouse gas emissions as soon as possible and to undertake rapid reductions so as to achieve a balance between sources and sinks in the second half of the 21st century.

Methods

Carbon-neutral status can be reached by reducing emissions (energy efficiency, renewable energy, electrification), by removing carbon through reforestation, soil carbon sequestration or carbon capture and storage, and by purchasing carbon offsets or credits. Standards such as PAS 2060 and the GHG Protocol govern measurement and claims, though offsetting has been criticised where credits do not represent real, additional reductions.

Commitments

Many countries, cities and companies have announced carbon-neutrality targets, commonly for 2050. The European Union's European Green Deal aims for climate neutrality by 2050, and China has pledged carbon neutrality before 2060. Costa Rica and Bhutan are frequently cited as countries that have achieved or approached carbon-neutral status.

Related concepts

The broader term net zero extends the balance to all greenhouse gases, expressed in carbon-dioxide equivalents, whereas carbon neutrality in its narrow sense concerns CO2 alone.
