Iraq War

The Iraq War was an armed conflict in Iraq that began with the invasion of the country on 20 March 2003 by a coalition led by the United States and the United Kingdom, which overthrew the Ba'athist government of Saddam Hussein within three weeks. The invasion was justified by its authors principally on the claim that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and had links to al-Qaeda; no stockpiles of WMD were found, and the 9/11 Commission found no operational relationship with al-Qaeda — a failure of intelligence and of public presentation that made the war intensely controversial in the US, Britain and worldwide, where it had proceeded without an explicit UN Security Council authorisation and in the face of the largest anti-war protests in history. The swift conventional victory dissolved into a long occupation and insurgency: looting and state collapse, the Coalition Provisional Authority's decisions to disband the Iraqi army and purge Ba'athists, sectarian civil war between Sunni and Shia militias (2006–08) that killed tens of thousands, the Abu Ghraib prisoner-abuse scandal, Iranian-backed militias and the rise of al-Qaeda in Iraq — the ancestor of the Islamic State. An American "surge" and the Sunni "Awakening" reduced violence from 2007, and US combat forces withdrew by December 2011. Estimates of Iraqi deaths vary from roughly 150,000 to several times that figure; American military deaths numbered about 4,500, coalition and Iraqi security forces and contractors many more. The war's consequences — a sectarianised Iraq within Iran's orbit, the emergence of ISIS and the 2014–17 war that followed, regional destabilisation, and a lasting crisis of trust in Western intelligence and decision-making — are still unfolding.

Invasion (2003)

After months of crisis at the UN — Resolution 1441's "final opportunity", the weapons inspections of Hans Blix's UNMOVIC, Colin Powell's presentation to the Security Council in February 2003 and the failure of a second resolution — the coalition invaded without a further vote. Air strikes opened on 19–20 March with an attempted decapitation strike on Saddam; ground forces drove from Kuwait to Baghdad in three weeks of rapid manoeuvre (with the war's heaviest fighting at Nasiriyah, Najaf and the airport), while special forces and Kurdish peshmerga, with US airpower, fixed Iraqi forces in the north. Baghdad fell on 9 April — the toppling of Saddam's statue in Firdos Square becoming the war's iconic image — and on 1 May President George W. Bush declared the end of major combat operations aboard USS Abraham Lincoln beneath a "Mission Accomplished" banner. Saddam was captured near Tikrit in December 2003, tried by an Iraqi tribunal, and hanged on 30 December 2006.

Occupation and insurgency

The occupation, run first by ORHA and then by L. Paul Bremer's Coalition Provisional Authority, made decisions whose consequences dominated the decade: de-Ba'athification and the disbanding of the army threw hundreds of thousands of armed, humiliated Sunni men into opposition just as looting and the collapse of services destroyed the state's legitimacy. An insurgency coalesced through 2003–04 — ex-Ba'athists, Sunni nationalists, foreign jihadists of Abu Musab al-Zarqawi's al-Qaeda in Iraq, and the Shia Mahdi Army of Muqtada al-Sadr — while photographs of prisoner abuse at Abu Ghraib (revealed April 2004) devastated the coalition's standing. Elections in 2005 brought a Shia-led government and a permanent constitution, but the bombing of the al-Askari shrine in Samarra in February 2006 ignited open sectarian civil war: death squads, neighbourhood cleansing in Baghdad, and monthly civilian deaths in the thousands. In 2007 the United States "surged" some 30,000 additional troops and, crucially, allied with Sunni tribal "Awakening" movements against al-Qaeda; violence fell sharply. A Status of Forces Agreement (2008) set the timetable by which American forces left the cities in 2009 and Iraq altogether in December 2011.

Consequences and assessments

The war's accounting remains contested in every dimension: the missing WMD (the Iraq Survey Group found degraded pre-1991 remnants and programmes in abeyance, not stockpiles); the legality and wisdom of the invasion (the British Chilcot Report of 2016 criticised the legal basis, the intelligence and the post-war planning in devastating terms); the human cost and the refugee crisis of some four million displaced; the empowerment of Iran, which gained in Baghdad the influence its eight-year war with Saddam (the Iran–Iraq War) had failed to win; and the incubation, in the war's prisons and insurgent networks, of the Islamic State, whose seizure of Mosul in 2014 required a second Western intervention. In American and British politics the war shadowed a generation of leaders and debates about intervention that continue to the present.
