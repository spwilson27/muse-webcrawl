Minoru Yamasaki (December 1, 1912 – February 6, 1986) was an American architect best known for designing the original World Trade Center in New York City and several other large-scale projects. A leading figure of New Formalism, he reacted against the starkness of the International Style by incorporating ornament, screens, arches, and delicate detailing into modernist structures.

Early life

Yamasaki was born in Seattle to Japanese immigrant (Issei) parents and grew up in poverty; he worked in Alaskan salmon canneries to pay for his education. He earned a bachelor's degree in architecture from the University of Washington in 1934 and a master's from New York University, then worked for firms including Harrison & Abramovitz, designers of Rockefeller Center projects. During the Second World War he avoided internment, unlike many Japanese Americans on the West Coast, because he was living in New York. By 1949 he had co-founded his own practice in the Detroit area.

Major works

Yamasaki's terminal building for Lambert–St. Louis International Airport (1956), with its intersecting concrete shell vaults, brought him national attention. He designed the United States pavilion and Pacific Science Center for the 1962 Seattle World's Fair, and campus buildings for Wayne State University and other universities.

Two projects defined his reputation in opposite ways. The Pruitt–Igoe public housing complex in St. Louis (completed 1955) was initially praised but deteriorated amid underfunding and social failure; its televised demolition beginning in 1972 became a symbol of the failure of modernist housing. The World Trade Center (completed 1973), with its twin 110-storey towers faced in aluminium alloy and narrow windows reflecting Yamasaki's fear of heights, made him world-famous; the towers were destroyed in the September 11 attacks of 2001, three decades after his death. His Rainier Bank Tower in Seattle, tapering from a narrow pedestal, is another well-known work.

Style

Yamasaki sought what he called "serenity, surprise, and delight" in architecture. His New Formalist buildings combined modern engineering with classical proportions, pointed and Tudor-like arches, colonnades, and reflective pools, drawing on influences from Gothic, Islamic, and Japanese architecture.
