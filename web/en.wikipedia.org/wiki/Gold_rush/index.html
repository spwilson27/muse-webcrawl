A gold rush or gold fever is a migration of large numbers of people to an area where gold has been discovered. Gold rushes were characteristic of the 19th century, when improved transport and communication spread news of discoveries rapidly and drew prospectors from around the world, transforming the regions involved economically, demographically and politically.

Characteristics

Gold rushes typically began with the discovery of placer gold in streams, which individual miners could work with simple tools such as pans and sluice boxes. As surface deposits were exhausted, mining became capital-intensive, shifting to hydraulic and underground hard-rock operations dominated by companies. Boomtowns sprang up near the diggings, often lawless at first, and merchants supplying miners frequently grew wealthier than the prospectors themselves — a pattern summarized in the phrase "selling shovels."

Notable gold rushes

The California gold rush (1848–1855), triggered by James W. Marshall's discovery at Sutter's Mill, drew some 300,000 people and accelerated California's admission to the United States. In Australia, the Victorian gold rushes beginning in 1851 made Melbourne one of the world's richest cities. South Africa's Witwatersrand rush (1886) founded Johannesburg and eventually produced a large share of all gold ever mined. The Klondike gold rush (1896–1899) drew about 100,000 prospectors toward the Yukon, of whom only a fraction arrived and fewer still prospered.

Legacy

Gold rushes drove infrastructure — railways, ports, telegraphs — and reshaped the settlement of North America, Australia and southern Africa, often with devastating consequences for Indigenous peoples and the environment. The term is now applied figuratively to any frenzied migration toward a newly valuable resource or market.
