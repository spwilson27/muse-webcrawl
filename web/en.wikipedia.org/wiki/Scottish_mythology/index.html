Scottish mythology

Scottish mythology is the body of myths that have emerged over time in Scotland, some of which have been elaborated upon by successive generations, and at other times rejected and replaced by other explanatory narratives. It draws on a mixture of Celtic, Norse and Pictish traditions, reflecting the successive peoples who inhabited the region.

Origins

The roots of Scottish mythology lie in the traditions of the early peoples of Scotland: the Picts, the Gaels who arrived from Ireland, the Britons of the south, and the Norse settlers of the north and west. Gaelic tradition, preserved in Ireland and Scotland through the medieval period, provides much of the surviving material, including tales of heroes, otherworldly beings and supernatural creatures of the land and sea.

Nature myths

The ancient Scots, like many early peoples, revered nature and attributed spirits to rivers, lochs and mountains. Beings such as the bean nighe (washerwoman), a spirit seen washing the clothes of those about to die, and the each-uisge, a dangerous water horse said to inhabit lochs, belong to this stratum of belief. Storms on the west coast were attributed to the Cailleach, a divine hag associated with winter.

Creatures and beings

Scottish folklore includes kelpies, shape-shifting water spirits; selkies, seal-folk able to shed their skins and take human form; the wulver, a benevolent wolf-headed man; brownies, household spirits that perform chores at night; and the cat sìth and cù sìth, fairy animals of the Highlands. Figures from the Fenian and Ulster cycles of Irish mythology, such as Fionn mac Cumhaill and Cú Chulainn, also appear in Scottish Gaelic tradition.

Survival

Many elements of Scottish mythology survived in folk tales collected in the 19th century, notably in John Francis Campbell's Popular Tales of the West Highlands (1860–62), and continue to shape Scottish literature and popular culture.
