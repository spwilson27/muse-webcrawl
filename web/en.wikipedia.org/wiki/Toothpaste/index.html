Toothpaste

Toothpaste is a paste or gel dentifrice used with a toothbrush to clean and maintain the aesthetics and health of teeth. It aids removal of dental plaque and food debris, suppresses halitosis, and delivers active agents — above all fluoride — that prevent dental caries and gum disease.

Composition

Typical toothpaste contains abrasives (20–50%; hydrated silica, calcium carbonate), humectants such as sorbitol and glycerol to prevent drying, water, thickeners, detergents (commonly sodium lauryl sulfate) for foaming, flavourings, and therapeutic agents. Fluoride, usually sodium fluoride or stannous fluoride at 1,000–1,500 ppm in adult pastes, promotes remineralisation of enamel. Specialised formulations address sensitivity (potassium nitrate, stannous fluoride), tartar control, or whitening.

History

Dentifrices date to ancient Egypt and Rome, using crushed shells, pumice and ash. Tooth powders dominated until the late 19th century, when toothpaste in collapsible tubes — modelled on artists' paint tubes — was marketed by Washington Sheffield and by Colgate (1896). Fluoride toothpastes were introduced in the 1950s following trials of stannous fluoride (Crest, 1955), and are credited with much of the subsequent decline in tooth decay in developed countries.

Safety

Excessive fluoride ingestion by young children can cause dental fluorosis, so pea-sized amounts and supervision are advised. Toothpaste should not be swallowed in quantity.
