Louis VI (Latin: Ludovicus VI; 1 December 1081 – 1 August 1137), called the Fat (French: le Gros) or the Fighter (French: le Batailleur), was King of the Franks from 1108 to 1137. He was the first Capetian king to bring real power to the monarchy within the royal domain, spending much of his twenty-nine-year reign campaigning against the "robber barons" of the Île-de-France and against the Anglo-Norman kings of England.

Reign

Louis was crowned during his father's lifetime and succeeded him in 1108. Vigorous and warlike in youth — hence "the Fighter" — he became grossly overweight in middle age, hence "the Fat". His reign was a long struggle to impose royal authority on the castles around Paris held by predatory lords such as Hugh of Le Puiset and Thomas of Marle. In these wars he was supported by the Church and by the towns, whose communal charters he frequently confirmed.

Wars with England

Louis fought Henry I of England over Normandy and the Vexin, supporting the claim of William Clito, Henry's nephew. In 1119 Louis was defeated at the Battle of Brémule. In 1124, when the Emperor Henry V threatened invasion, Louis rallied the French nobility under the oriflamme at Reims — an early stirring of national unity — and the invasion collapsed.

Legacy

Aided by his adviser Abbot Suger of Saint-Denis, Louis strengthened the administration of the royal domain and made the crown the protector of the weak against disorderly vassals. On his deathbed he arranged the marriage of his son Louis VII to Eleanor of Aquitaine, temporarily uniting the crown with the great southern duchy. His biography by Suger is a key source for the period.
