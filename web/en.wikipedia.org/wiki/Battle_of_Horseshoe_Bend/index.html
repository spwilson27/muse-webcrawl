The Battle of Horseshoe Bend, fought on 27 March 1814, was the decisive battle of the Creek War in Alabama. Forces under Major General Andrew Jackson — US regulars, Tennessee militia, and allied Cherokee and Lower Creek warriors — overwhelmed the Red Stick faction of the Creek Nation at their fortified village of Tohopeka on a bend of the Tallapoosa River.

Background

The Creek War began as a civil war within the Creek Nation, inflamed by the prophetic movement associated with Tecumseh's confederacy. After the Red Stick attack on Fort Mims in August 1813, US forces invaded Creek country. Jackson's campaigns through the autumn and winter of 1813–14, including the battles of Tallushatchee, Talladega, Emuckfaw, and Enotachopo Creek, drove the Red Sticks to their stronghold at Tohopeka ("Horseshoe Bend"), where about 1,000 warriors fortified the neck of a 100-acre peninsula with a stout log barricade.

The battle

Jackson deployed roughly 2,000 infantry and 700 allied warriors. His artillery bombarded the barricade without effect for hours, but his Cherokee allies swam the river, seized the Red Stick canoes, and attacked the village from the rear, setting it ablaze. Jackson then ordered a frontal assault: the 39th US Infantry under Colonel John Williams, with the young ensign Sam Houston among the first over the breastwork, stormed the barricade in hand-to-hand fighting. By nightfall some 800 or more Red Stick warriors — the great majority of those engaged — were dead, the highest loss of life for Native American forces in any single battle with the United States. American losses were about 49 killed and 154 wounded; allied Creek and Cherokee losses were also significant.

Aftermath

The Red Stick leader Menawa, badly wounded, escaped, but organized resistance collapsed. The Treaty of Fort Jackson of August 1814 forced the Creek Nation — Jackson's allies as well as his enemies — to cede some 23 million acres, roughly half of present-day Alabama and part of Georgia, to the United States. The victory made Jackson a national hero and opened the Southeast to American settlement and, eventually, Indian removal. The battlefield is preserved as Horseshoe Bend National Military Park.
