Deep Blue (chess computer)

DeveloperIBM
PredecessorDeep Thought
Known forFirst computer to beat a reigning world chess champion in match play
StatusRetired; on display at the Smithsonian

Deep Blue was a chess-playing expert system run on a unique purpose-built IBM supercomputer. It was the first computer to win a game, and the first to win a match, against a reigning world chess champion under regular time controls. Developed at IBM by a team including Feng-hsiung Hsu, Murray Campbell, and Joe Hoane, Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov in a six-game rematch in May 1997 by a score of 3½–2½, after losing the first match in 1996 by 4–2.

Design

Deep Blue was a massively parallel system based on IBM RS/6000 SP workstations augmented with custom VLSI chess accelerator chips. The 1997 version used 30 nodes with 480 custom chess chips, evaluating roughly 200 million positions per second. Its evaluation function, tuned with the help of grandmasters including Joel Benjamin, combined brute-force search with handcrafted knowledge — thousands of features evaluating material, king safety, pawn structure, and piece placement. The machine derived from the earlier Deep Thought program built at Carnegie Mellon University.

The matches

In the 1996 match in Philadelphia, Deep Blue won the first game — the first victory by a computer over a reigning world champion under classical time controls — but Kasparov recovered to win 4–2. In the 1997 rematch in New York, Deep Blue won game two in a game whose subtle moves unsettled Kasparov, and won the deciding sixth game quickly after Kasparov blundered in the opening. Kasparov accused IBM of cheating (a charge never substantiated) and demanded a rematch, but IBM retired the machine. One of its two racks is displayed at the National Museum of American History.

Legacy

Deep Blue became a cultural landmark for machine intelligence, though its methods relied on specialized hardware and brute-force search rather than learning. Its name inspired IBM's later "DeepQA" project behind Watson, which won at Jeopardy! in 2011. Modern neural-network engines such as AlphaZero later surpassed it with self-learned evaluation.
