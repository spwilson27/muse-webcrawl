Theremin

The theremin (originally known as the etherophone, thereminophone or termenvox) is an electronic musical instrument invented by the Russian physicist Léon Theremin (Lev Semyonovich Termen) in 1920. It is the only musical instrument played without physical contact: the performer stands before the instrument and moves the hands in the vicinity of two metal antennas — a vertical rod controlling pitch and a horizontal loop controlling volume — whose proximity alters the capacitance of high-frequency oscillators. The resulting heterodyned signal is amplified and sent to a loudspeaker, producing the theremin's characteristic eerie, singing, portamento tone.

Operating principles

The theremin contains two high-frequency oscillators, one fixed and one variable. The performer's hand near the pitch antenna adds capacitance that detunes the variable oscillator; the difference (beat) frequency between the two oscillators falls in the audio range and forms the pitch. Moving the other hand toward or away from the volume loop correspondingly changes the amplitude. Because there are no frets, keys or fixed reference points, playing in tune demands exceptional aural precision; virtuosi such as Clara Rockmore developed an "aerial fingering" technique of discrete hand positions.

History

Theremin demonstrated his invention in Paris in 1927 to sensational acclaim, then toured the United States, where RCA marketed the instrument from 1929. The theremin found a niche in film scores — notably Miklós Rózsa's Spellbound (1945) and The Lost Weekend, and Bernard Herrmann's The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951) — and in popular music, from the Beach Boys' "Good Vibrations" (which used the related electro-theremin) to Led Zeppelin and beyond. Robert Moog built theremins from the 1950s and his company remains a leading manufacturer. Clara Rockmore, Lydia Kavina (Theremin's great-niece and pupil) and Pamelia Stickney are among the instrument's noted performers. Its sound, produced without touch, presaged later electronic and gestural controllers.
