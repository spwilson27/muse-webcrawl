Progressive house is a subgenre of house music that emerged in the early 1990s in the United Kingdom. The style is characterised by long, gradually developing tracks — often lasting eight minutes or more — built from layered synthesiser textures, subtle melodic motifs and extended tension-and-release structures rather than the song-like verse–chorus forms of mainstream dance music. The term "progressive" was borrowed from progressive rock, signalling music that progresses and evolves over the course of a track or a DJ set.

History

The term was popularised around 1992, when the British dance magazine Mixmag used it to describe a new wave of UK house productions influenced by Italian house, trance and the Balearic sound of Ibiza. Early exemplars included Leftfield, Underworld, Spooky and William Orbit's Guerilla Records label. The duo Sasha and John Digweed became the genre's most prominent DJs, and their 1994 mix album Renaissance: The Mix Collection is widely regarded as a landmark release that defined the sound: long, seamless blends of tracks by artists such as Leftfield, Bedrock and Underworld.

During the late 1990s and early 2000s, progressive house merged extensively with trance, and labels such as Hooj Choons, Bedrock Records and Global Underground carried the sound worldwide. In the 2010s the label "progressive house" was widely applied — controversially among purists — to the big-room, festival-oriented EDM of artists such as Swedish House Mafia, Avicii and Alesso, whose short, anthemic tracks bore little resemblance to the long-form original style. Meanwhile, producers such as Eric Prydz, Deadmau5, Jeremy Olander and the Anjunadeep roster kept a melodic, gradually building variant of the sound alive, often distinguished as "melodic house" or "true progressive".

Characteristics

Progressive house tracks typically run at 122–130 beats per minute in 4/4 time. Hallmarks include:

  • Long intros and outros designed for DJ mixing
  • Gradual addition and removal of layers over many bars
  • Extended breakdowns in which melodic and atmospheric elements dominate
  • Subtle, evolving synthesiser arpeggios and pads
  • An emphasis on mood and "journey" over immediate hooks
