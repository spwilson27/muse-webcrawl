Military revolution

Pike-and-shot formations typified the tactical transformation of early modern warfare.

The Military Revolution is a theory in military history, first proposed by Michael Roberts in 1955 and expanded by Geoffrey Parker, holding that a series of radical changes in military strategy and tactics between roughly 1560 and 1660 transformed European warfare, vastly increased the size of armies, and contributed to the rise of the modern state.

The thesis

Roberts identified four linked changes: a tactical revolution based on drilled linear infantry formations and volley fire, pioneered by Maurice of Nassau and Gustavus Adolphus; a great increase in army size; broader and more ambitious strategies; and a profound impact on society and state administration, since larger armies demanded new systems of taxation, supply, and bureaucracy. Parker emphasized the trace italienne (star fort) fortifications that made siege warfare dominant and further swelled military establishments, and argued the revolution helps explain Europe's subsequent global military ascendancy.

Criticism and debate

Historians including Jeremy Black have questioned the concept, arguing that change was more gradual, evolutionary, and regionally varied, and that decisive developments also occurred in other periods — such as the later "revolution in military affairs" associated with industrialization, and the 20th-century revolutions of mechanization and nuclear weapons. The term is also used more loosely for any rapid, fundamental transformation of warfare.
