Environmental remediation

Environmental remediation is the removal of pollution or contaminants from environmental media such as soil, groundwater, sediment or surface water, in order to protect human health and restore ecosystems. Remediation is typically driven by regulation — in the United States most prominently by the Superfund law (CERCLA) and state brownfield programmes — and by the requirements of land redevelopment.

Approaches

Ex situ methods excavate contaminated soil or pump groundwater to the surface for treatment: soil washing, thermal desorption, stabilisation and landfarming are common. In situ methods treat contamination in place, including soil vapour extraction, chemical oxidation or reduction, thermal treatment, permeable reactive barriers and air sparging. Bioremediation uses micro-organisms — sometimes stimulated with nutrients or oxygen, sometimes engineered — to degrade organic pollutants, while phytoremediation uses plants to extract or stabilise metals and organics.

Process

A typical project proceeds from site investigation and risk assessment, through remedy selection balancing protectiveness, cost and time, to construction, long-term monitoring and institutional controls where residual contamination is left managed in place. Monitored natural attenuation is accepted at some sites where plumes are stable and shrinking.

Challenges

Legacy contamination such as chlorinated solvents, heavy metals, petroleum hydrocarbons and emerging contaminants like PFAS presents technical difficulties — back-diffusion from low-permeability zones can sustain plumes for decades — and cleanup standards and liability allocation remain politically contested.
