Stop and search

Stop and search is the power of police officers in the United Kingdom to stop and search a person or vehicle where they have reasonable grounds to suspect the person is carrying stolen or prohibited items, such as weapons or drugs. The principal power is under section 1 of the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE); other powers arise under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 and the Firearms Act 1968.

No-suspicion powers

Two exceptional powers allow searches without individual suspicion: section 60 of the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994, authorisable in anticipation of serious violence, and formerly sections 44–45 of the Terrorism Act 2000, the latter ruled incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights in Gillan and Quinton v United Kingdom (2010) and replaced by a more limited power under section 47A.

Safeguards and controversy

Officers must give their name, station and the grounds for the search (the "GOWISELY" procedure), and the person searched is entitled to a record. Use of stop and search is persistently controversial because Black people in England and Wales are searched at several times the rate of white people, and only a minority of searches lead to arrest. Successive reviews—from the Macpherson Report (1999) onwards—have debated its balance of crime prevention against community confidence.
