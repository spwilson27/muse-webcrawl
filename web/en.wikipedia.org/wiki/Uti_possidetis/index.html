Uti possidetis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Uti possidetis is a principle in international law that serves to preserve the boundaries of colonies emerging as states: newly independent states inherit the administrative borders they had as colonial (or internal) units, unless they agree otherwise. Originally a Roman-law possessory interdict (retaining possession of what one possesses), the doctrine was applied in Spanish America's independence (freezing the boundaries of the viceroyalties and captaincies-general, uti possidetis juris of 1810), adopted by the Organisation of African Unity in 1964 (the Cairo resolution on colonial borders), and generalised by the International Court of Justice in the Frontier Dispute case (Burkina Faso/Mali, 1986) as a principle of general application to decolonization.

Rationale and costs

The doctrine trades justice for stability: it prevents the border wars that any open-season redrawing would invite, giving new states an instant, defensible title to territory — at the cost of sanctifying borders drawn by colonial administrators through ethnic and geographic nonsense, a grievance underlying many post-colonial conflicts (Somalia's claims, the Kashmir and Durand Line disputes in their several logics). Europe borrowed the principle for Yugoslavia's dissolution (the Badinter Committee applied it to the republics' internal boundaries in 1991–92), extending uti possidetis beyond decolonization into state breakup — a move repeated in the post-Soviet space (the Alma-Ata declarations) and invoked in arguments over Ukraine's 1991 borders.
