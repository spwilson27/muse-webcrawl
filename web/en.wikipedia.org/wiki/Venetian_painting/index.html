Venetian painting
Colour and light characterise the Venetian school

Venetian painting was a major force in Italian Renaissance and later painting. Beginning with the Bellini family and their workshops, and including masters such as Giorgione, Titian, Tintoretto, Veronese and the Bassano family, Venetian artists developed a distinctive approach characterised by the primacy of colour (colorito) over line and design (disegno), in contrast to the Florentine school.

Characteristics

Venetian painters worked extensively in oil on canvas — a medium suited to the humid lagoon climate, which damaged fresco, and to Venice's trade in pigments, which gave artists access to rich ultramarine, vermilion and other colours. Their works are noted for luminous, atmospheric effects of light, sensuous renderings of flesh and fabric, and compositions set in contemporary Venetian dress and architecture. The theoretical opposition between Venetian colorito and Florentine disegno was a central debate of 16th-century art writing, notably in Vasari's Lives.

Development

Venetian painting emerged from Byzantine traditions — the mosaics of St Mark's Basilica — and from early Renaissance figures such as Giovanni Bellini, who transformed devotional painting with serene Madonnas in glowing landscapes. Giorgione introduced the enigmatic pastoral mood of works like The Tempest. Titian, the dominant figure of the High Renaissance in Venice, painted for the Habsburg emperors and set the standard for portraiture and mythological painting across Europe. The later 16th century belonged to Tintoretto, whose vast, dramatic canvases fill the Scuola Grande di San Rocco, and Veronese, famed for huge banquet scenes.

Later centuries

After a quieter 17th century, Venetian painting revived in the 18th with Tiepolo's luminous frescoes, Canaletto's precise city views (vedute), and the genre scenes of Pietro Longhi — the last flowering of the Venetian school before the fall of the Republic in 1797.
