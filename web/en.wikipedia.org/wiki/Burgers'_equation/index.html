Burgers' equation

Solution of Burgers' equation steepening into a shock

Burgers' equation is a fundamental partial differential equation occurring in fluid mechanics, nonlinear acoustics, gas dynamics and traffic flow. It was introduced by Harry Bateman in 1915 and later studied by Johannes Burgers in 1948 as a simplified model of turbulence. In one space dimension the viscous form is:

∂u/∂t + u ∂u/∂x = ν ∂²u/∂x²

where u is a velocity field and ν is the viscosity. The equation combines nonlinear advection (the u ∂u/∂x term, which makes waves steepen) with diffusion (the right-hand term, which smooths them).

Behaviour and shocks

Without viscosity (ν = 0) the inviscid Burgers' equation develops discontinuous shock waves in finite time from smooth initial data, because faster-moving parts of the wave overtake slower ones. With small positive viscosity the shocks are smoothed into narrow layers, which is why the equation is a standard test bed for numerical methods for conservation laws: too little numerical diffusion makes solutions oscillate, too much smears the shock.

The Cole–Hopf transformation

Remarkably, the viscous Burgers' equation can be solved exactly. The Cole–Hopf transformation, found independently by Julian Cole and Eberhard Hopf around 1950, substitutes u = −2ν φx/φ and converts the nonlinear equation into the linear heat equation for φ, which can be solved in closed form and transformed back. This makes Burgers' equation one of the few nonlinear PDEs with an explicit general solution, and a classic teaching example.

Applications

Besides modelling traffic flow and shock formation, Burgers' equation appears in the study of interface growth, acoustics, and as a one-dimensional analogue of the Navier–Stokes equations. The stochastic version, forced Burgers turbulence ("Burgulence"), is related to the KPZ equation of interface growth.
