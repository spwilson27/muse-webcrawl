Lilith

Lilith
OriginMesopotamian and Jewish mythology
NamesHebrew: לִילִית (Lilit); Akkadian: lilītu
RoleNight demon; in later folklore, first wife of Adam

Lilith (Hebrew: לִילִית, romanized: Līlīṯ) is a figure in Mesopotamian and Jewish mythology, first attested in Sumerian texts as a class of storm and night demons (lilītu), and later depicted as a dangerous female demon of the night who steals children and seduces men.

The name appears once in the Hebrew Bible, at Isaiah 34:14, among the wild creatures inhabiting the ruins of Edom, and in several Dead Sea Scrolls and Babylonian Talmud passages. In the medieval satirical work the Alphabet of Ben Sira (c. 8th–10th century CE), Lilith was given her best-known role: the first wife of Adam, created from the earth at the same time as him, who refused to lie beneath him, spoke the Ineffable Name of God, and fled the Garden of Eden.

Mesopotamian origins

Sumerian and Akkadian texts describe the lilû (male) and lilītu and ardat lilî (female) demons, associated with wind, storms and the night, believed to prey on women in childbirth and on infants. The famous Burney Relief (the "Queen of the Night") has sometimes been identified as Lilith, though this is disputed.

In Jewish tradition

In the Talmud, Lilith is described as a hairy female demon; rabbinic literature blames her for nocturnal emissions and endangering newborns. Amulets inscribed with the names of the angels Senoy, Sansenoy and Semangelof were placed over infants to protect them from her. In Kabbalah she became the consort of Samael and a central figure of the realm of evil, the qlippoth.

The Alphabet of Ben Sira

The Alphabet of Ben Sira tells how Lilith, created equal to Adam, refused to take the inferior position, saying "Why should I lie beneath you? We are both equal." When Adam insisted, she uttered the Divine Name and flew away. Angels sent to retrieve her failed, and she agreed only to spare infants protected by amulets bearing the angels' names, and to lose a hundred of her own demon children daily. The tale, originally satirical, was taken up by later folklore and occultism as canonical.

Later reception

Lilith was revived by the Romantics — Goethe put her in Faust, and Dante Gabriel Rossetti and John Collier painted her as a beautiful, dangerous femme fatale. In the 20th century she was adopted by feminists as a symbol of female independence and refusal of subordination; the first independent Jewish women's magazine took her name. She remains a popular figure in modern fantasy, occultism and popular culture.
