Compound bow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia. Not to be confused with composite bow.

A compound bow is a modern bow that uses a levering system of cables and pulleys (cams) to bend the limbs. The cams give the bow its characteristic draw-force curve: the draw weight peaks and then "lets off" by 65–90%, so the archer holds only a fraction of the peak weight at full draw, allowing steadier aim. Compound bows are stiffer, more energy-efficient, and less sensitive to temperature and humidity than traditional bows.

Eccentric cams at the limb tips produce the compound bow's let-off.

History

The compound bow was invented by Holless Wilbur Allen in Missouri in the 1960s (US patent granted 1969) and commercialised with Tom Jennings in the 1970s. It spread rapidly in bowhunting and then target archery; compound events were added to the World Archery Championships in 1995 and to the World Games, though as of the mid-2020s the compound bow is not part of the Olympic Games, which use the recurve bow.

Design

Modern compounds have a machined aluminium or carbon riser, short stiff limbs of laminated fibreglass composites, and one, two, or hybrid cam systems (single cam, binary cam, cam-and-a-half). Typical hunting bows have draw weights of 40–80 lb and shoot arrows at 300 feet per second or more. Accessories include pin or scope sights, stabilisers, arrow rests such as drop-away rests, peep sights, and mechanical release aids, which together give accuracy approaching that of firearms at short range. Crossbows use a similar limb-and-cam arrangement mounted on a stock.

Use

The compound bow dominates North American bowhunting and is used in target, field, and 3D archery worldwide. Because of let-off, archers can hold at full draw while waiting for game, a decisive advantage for hunting.
