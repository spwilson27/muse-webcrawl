Pasko

Pasko is the Filipino celebration of Christmas. The word comes from the Spanish Pascua, a term introduced during the Spanish colonial period and applied to the Nativity feast. The Philippines, one of only two predominantly Catholic countries in Asia, is famous for having one of the longest Christmas seasons in the world: decorations and carols appear as early as September, at the start of the "-ber months," and festivities continue into January.

Central to Pasko is Simbang Gabi ("night Mass"), a novena of nine Masses held at dawn (or, in recent practice, in the evening) from 16 to 24 December, culminating in the Misa de Gallo on Christmas Eve. After the final Mass, families gather for Noche Buena, a midnight feast typically featuring lechon (roast pig), ham, queso de bola (Edam cheese), bibingka and puto bumbong rice cakes, and hot chocolate.

Other customs include the parol, a star-shaped lantern symbolising the Star of Bethlehem — the city of San Fernando, Pampanga, is known as the "Christmas Capital of the Philippines" for its giant lantern festival — panunuluyan pageants re-enacting Mary and Joseph's search for lodging, carolling by children, visits to godparents on Christmas morning to receive aguinaldo (gifts, often crisp peso bills), and the monito-monita exchange of gifts. The season formally closes with the Feast of the Three Kings (Epiphany) in early January, and in some places extends to the Feast of the Santo Niño later that month.

Significance

Pasko is above all a season of homecoming: millions of overseas Filipino workers return or send remittances and balikbayan boxes, and family reunions anchor the holiday. Its blend of Spanish Catholic ritual, local custom, and modern commercial celebration makes it the most important festival in Filipino cultural life.
