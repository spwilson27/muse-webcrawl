Giardiasis

SpecialtyInfectious disease; gastroenterology
CauseGiardia duodenalis (also called G. lamblia)
TransmissionFaecal–oral; contaminated water and food
TreatmentMetronidazole, tinidazole, or nitazoxanide

Giardiasis, popularly known as beaver fever, is a parasitic disease caused by the flagellated protozoan Giardia duodenalis (syn. G. lamblia, G. intestinalis). The parasite colonises the small intestine and is one of the most common intestinal parasites worldwide, spread mainly through water contaminated with its hardy cysts.

Signs and symptoms

Many infections are asymptomatic, but symptomatic giardiasis causes watery, foul-smelling diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, bloating, flatulence, nausea and fatigue, typically beginning one to two weeks after exposure. Fat malabsorption can lead to greasy stools and weight loss, and temporary lactose intolerance is common. Symptoms usually last two to six weeks; chronic infection occurs in a minority.

Transmission and life cycle

Infection follows ingestion of Giardia cysts from contaminated water, food, surfaces or person-to-person contact; outbreaks are associated with untreated drinking water, swimming venues, childcare settings and wilderness water sources (hence "beaver fever"). In the small intestine each cyst releases trophozoites that attach to the intestinal wall; as they pass toward the colon they re-encyst and are excreted.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by stool antigen tests or microscopic identification of cysts or trophozoites. The nitroimidazole antibiotics metronidazole and tinidazole are first-line treatments, with nitazoxanide and other agents as alternatives. Prevention centres on water treatment, filtration or boiling of backcountry water, and hand hygiene.
