Nalanda (Sanskrit: नालन्दा) was a renowned Buddhist mahavihara — a great monastery and university — in the ancient kingdom of Magadha, in modern Bihar, India. Flourishing from the 5th to the 12th century CE under the patronage of the Gupta emperors and later dynasties, it was among the world's earliest residential universities, drawing students and scholars from across Asia, including China, Korea, Japan, Tibet, Mongolia, Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. Its curriculum ranged across Buddhist philosophy, logic, grammar, medicine, mathematics, astronomy and the Vedas. The ruins of Nalanda were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016, and a modern Nalanda University was established nearby in 2010.

History

Tradition associates the site with the Buddha and with his disciple Sariputra, who was born and died there. The mahavihara as an institution dates to the Gupta period: Kumaragupta I (r. 414–455) is credited with its founding endowment, and successors including Harsha of Kannauj added monasteries, temples and revenue grants from some 200 villages. The Chinese pilgrims Xuanzang, who studied at Nalanda in the 630s under the abbot Shilabhadra, and Yijing, who spent a decade there, left detailed accounts of its rigorous admissions — only a minority of applicants passed the gatekeeper debates — its thousands of resident monks, and its great library, the Dharmaganja, housed in three buildings, one nine storeys high.

Scholarship and decline

Nalanda's masters — Nagarjuna, Asanga and Vasubandhu by tradition, and historically Dharmakirti, Shantarakshita, Atisha and Naropa — shaped Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism across Asia; the university was a conduit of Indian thought to Tibet and China. Under the Pala dynasty (8th–12th centuries) it shared prominence with Vikramashila and Odantapuri. Around 1193–1200 the forces of the Turkic invader Bakhtiyar Khalji sacked Nalanda; Tibetan sources describe the burning of its libraries and the slaughter or flight of its monks, and teaching dwindled to a remnant before ceasing in the 13th century.

Archaeology

The excavated site, about 1.6 by 0.8 km, exposes rows of brick monasteries (viharas) with monks' cells around courtyards, temples (chaityas), the great Sariputta stupa, votive stupas, sculptures and coins, identified in the 19th century by Alexander Cunningham using Xuanzang's itinerary. A museum houses the finds. In 2006–2010 India and partner Asian countries revived the name with a new international Nalanda University at Rajgir, which began teaching in 2014.
