Verse–chorus form

Verse–chorus form is a musical structure in which a song alternates between a repeating verse, whose lyrics change each time over similar music, and a repeating chorus (or refrain), whose music and lyrics stay largely the same and which usually contains the song's title and emotional climax. It is the dominant form in popular music from the mid-twentieth century onward.

Structure

A typical verse–chorus song may open with an intro, then verse–chorus–verse–chorus, often with a contrasting bridge before a final chorus, and an outro. The chorus is designed to be the most memorable part — the "hook" — while verses advance the story or argument. A shorter pre-chorus may build tension between verse and chorus.

Contrast with other forms

Verse–chorus differs from the older AABA (thirty-two-bar) form, in which the refrain itself contains contrasting verses, and from simple verse form, which repeats verses without a distinct chorus. The form crystallised in rock and roll, rhythm and blues and country music of the 1950s and 1960s and remains the default architecture of pop songwriting.
