Atlantic Coastal Plain

Atlantic Coastal Plain
LocationEastern United States
ExtentMassachusetts to Florida and the Gulf Coast
ElevationMostly below 300 ft (90 m)

The Atlantic Coastal Plain is a physiographic region of low-lying, flat land along the East Coast of the United States. It extends roughly 2,200 miles (3,500 km) from Cape Cod, Massachusetts, southward through the Mid-Atlantic and Southeast, around Florida, and westward along the Gulf Coast toward Texas, where it merges with the Gulf Coastal Plain. It generally lies below 300 feet (91 m) in elevation and was formed by marine sediments deposited as sea levels rose and fell over millions of years.

Geology

The plain consists of layered sedimentary rocks — sands, silts, clays, marls and limestones — deposited since the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. Its inland boundary is the fall line, where harder Piedmont rocks give way to softer coastal sediments; many major cities (Richmond, Columbia, Raleigh) grew at fall-line sites where rivers dropped and water power was available.

Features

  • Tidewater region – estuaries and tidal rivers, including Chesapeake Bay.
  • Barrier islands and the Outer Banks of North Carolina.
  • Swamps and wetlands such as the Great Dismal Swamp and the Okefenokee.
  • Florida peninsula – built largely of limestone, with the Everglades at its south.

Ecology

The region historically supported longleaf pine savannas, bottomland hardwood forests and vast wetlands. Its warm, humid climate and flat terrain have made it important for agriculture (cotton, peanuts, tobacco, citrus) and forestry, while its low elevation makes it vulnerable to hurricane storm surge and sea-level rise.
