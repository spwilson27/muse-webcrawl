Eureka effect

The eureka effect (also known as the aha! moment or eureka moment) refers to the common human experience of suddenly understanding a previously incomprehensible problem or concept. Such insights are typically abrupt, accompanied by a feeling of certainty and pleasure, and often arrive when the thinker is not consciously working on the problem.

Origin of the term

The name comes from the anecdote, retold by Vitruvius, that the ancient Greek scholar Archimedes cried "Eureka!" ("I have found it!") when, stepping into a bath, he realised that the volume of water displaced could measure the volume — and hence the purity — of Hiero's golden crown.

Research

Psychologists distinguish insight problem-solving from analytic, stepwise reasoning. Experiments using puzzles, remote-associates tests and riddles find that solutions reached by insight are remembered better and are more often correct, and that a period of incubation — setting the problem aside — can help. Neuroimaging studies associate the aha! moment with a burst of high-frequency activity in the right anterior temporal lobe and with changes in alpha rhythms over visual cortex just before insight, as if the brain briefly shutters input to let a weak solution surface. The effect also has a dark side: the confidence of a eureka moment can attach to false insights, and experimental subjects sometimes experience aha! feelings for incorrect solutions.
