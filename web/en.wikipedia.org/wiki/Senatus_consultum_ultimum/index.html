Senatus consultum ultimum

The senatus consultum ultimum ("final decree of the Senate"), often abbreviated SCU, was a Roman emergency measure of the late Republic by which the Senate urged the magistrates—usually the consuls—to take whatever steps were necessary to protect the state, conventionally expressed in the formula that the consuls should "see to it that the republic suffer no harm" (videant consules ne quid res publica detrimenti capiat).

Use

The decree was first passed in 121 BC against Gaius Gracchus and Marcus Fulvius Flaccus, who were killed along with thousands of supporters. It was subsequently invoked at moments of crisis: against Saturninus in 100 BC, against Lepidus in 77 BC, against Catiline in 63 BC—when Cicero used it to justify the execution of the conspirators—and in 49 BC against Julius Caesar, an act that helped precipitate the civil war when Caesar crossed the Rubicon.

Legality

Its legal force was fiercely contested. The decree had no basis in statute and did not formally suspend citizens' rights such as provocatio (appeal to the people); opponents argued that executions carried out under it remained illegal, as in the exile of Cicero in 58 BC for executing the Catilinarians without trial. Modern scholars debate whether the SCU was a constitutional emergency power or merely a statement of senatorial opinion lending political cover to magistrates.
