Giovanni Domenico Cassini (8 June 1625 – 14 September 1712), also known as Jean-Dominique Cassini, was an Italian (later French) astronomer, engineer and mathematician. He discovered four moons of Saturn, the gap in Saturn's rings that bears his name (the Cassini Division), and, with Jean Richer, produced the first reasonably accurate determination of the scale of the Solar System. He was the first director of the Paris Observatory and founded a dynasty of astronomers spanning four generations.

Life and career

Born in Perinaldo, Cassini was educated by Jesuit scholars and in 1644 became assistant at the observatory of the Marquis Cornelio Malvasia near Bologna. In 1650 he was appointed professor of astronomy at the University of Bologna. His early work included constructing a famous meridian line (1655) in the Basilica of San Petronio, with which he refined measurements of the Sun's apparent motion and the obliquity of the ecliptic. He also worked as a hydraulic engineer for Pope Clement IX, managing rivers and fortifications.

In 1669 Cassini moved to France at the invitation of Louis XIV to help establish the Paris Observatory, becoming its first director. He took French citizenship in 1673 and spent the rest of his life in Paris, going blind in about 1710.

Discoveries

Using the excellent refracting telescopes made by Giuseppe Campani, Cassini made a series of landmark observations:

  • He measured the rotation periods of Jupiter and Mars, and in 1665 was among the first to observe Jupiter's Great Red Spot.
  • He discovered four moons of Saturn: Iapetus (1671), Rhea (1672), and Tethys and Dione (1684).
  • In 1675 he observed the division in Saturn's rings — the Cassini Division — and correctly suggested the rings were composed of countless small particles.
  • In 1672, coordinating observations of Mars from Paris and Cayenne (by Jean Richer), he obtained a value for the solar parallax that gave the Earth–Sun distance to within about 7% of the modern figure.
  • He studied comets, the zodiacal light, and lunar libration (Cassini's laws, describing the Moon's rotation, were published posthumously).

Legacy

The NASA/ESA/ASI Cassini–Huygens mission to Saturn (1997–2017) was named in his honour, as are craters on the Moon and Mars and the asteroid 24101 Cassini. His son Jacques, grandson César-François (Cassini de Thury) and great-grandson Jean-Dominique succeeded him at the Paris Observatory and produced the first modern topographic map of France, the Carte de Cassini.
