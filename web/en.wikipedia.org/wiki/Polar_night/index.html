The polar night is a phenomenon in the northernmost and southernmost regions of Earth where night lasts for more than 24 hours. This occurs only inside the polar circles. The opposite phenomenon, the polar day or midnight sun, occurs when the Sun stays above the horizon for more than 24 hours.

Midday twilight during the polar night in Troms og Finnmark, Norway.

Types

Because twilight persists even when the Sun is below the horizon, several grades are distinguished: civil polar night (the Sun is less than 6° below the horizon and civil twilight occurs at noon), nautical polar night (up to 12° below), and astronomical polar night (up to 18° below, with true darkness around the clock). The last occurs only within about 5.5° of the poles, including at Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station, which experiences roughly six months of continuous night.

Effects

Prolonged darkness affects circadian rhythms, mood, and vitamin D levels in Arctic populations; settlements such as Utqiaġvik (Barrow), Alaska, see about two months without sunrise. Many communities hold festivals welcoming the Sun's return. The polar night also shapes ecosystems, pausing photosynthesis and driving animal adaptations such as hibernation and migration.
