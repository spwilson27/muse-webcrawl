Kidney

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The kidneys are two bean-shaped organs, each about the size of a fist, located at the back of the abdomen. They filter the blood — some 180 litres of filtrate per day — removing waste products and excess water as urine, while regulating blood pressure, electrolyte balance, acid–base balance and red-cell production (via the hormone erythropoietin). Each kidney contains about a million filtering units called nephrons.

Urine travels via the ureters to the bladder. The kidneys' endocrine roles include activating vitamin D and releasing renin, the first step of the renin–angiotensin system that controls blood pressure and fluid volume.

Disease and replacement

Chronic kidney disease, most often from diabetes and hypertension, progressively destroys nephrons; diabetes and kidney failure are tightly linked globally. End-stage failure is treated by dialysis — artificial filtering, performed several times a week — or by kidney transplantation, historically the first successful organ transplant (1954, Joseph Murray).

In other animals

Kidney form tracks habitat: desert mammals have elongated loops of Henle to concentrate urine, while freshwater fish excrete copious dilute urine. Birds and reptiles excrete nitrogen mainly as uric acid paste to save water.
