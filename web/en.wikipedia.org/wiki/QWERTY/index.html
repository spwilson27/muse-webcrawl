QWERTY

QWERTY (/ˈkwɜːrti/) is a keyboard layout for Latin-script alphabets. The name comes from the order of the first six keys on the top letter row of the keyboard (Q W E R T Y). The design is based on a layout created by Christopher Latham Sholes and Carlos Glidden, and sold to E. Remington and Sons in 1873.

History

Sholes developed the layout during the 1860s and 1870s for his typewriter. Popular accounts claim it separated frequently used letter pairs to prevent the typebars of early machines from jamming, though modern scholarship suggests telegraph operators' needs and frequent digraphs alike influenced the arrangement. The layout became dominant with the success of the Remington No. 2 of 1878, which introduced the shift key.

Variants and alternatives

National variants include French AZERTY and German QWERTZ. Alternative English layouts, most notably the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard (1936) and Colemak, claim greater typing efficiency, but QWERTY's installed base and network effects — an oft-cited example of path dependence — have preserved its dominance on computers and mobile devices.
