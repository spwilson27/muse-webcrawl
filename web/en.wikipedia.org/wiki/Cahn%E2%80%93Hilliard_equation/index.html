Cahn–Hilliard equation

FieldMathematical physics / materials science
Named afterJohn W. Cahn and John E. Hilliard
TypePartial differential equation

The Cahn–Hilliard equation is a partial differential equation describing spinodal decomposition — the process by which a mixture of two components spontaneously separates into distinct regions rich in each component. It was proposed by John W. Cahn and John E. Hilliard in 1958.

The equation is a fourth-order diffusion-type equation for a conserved order parameter, derived from a free-energy functional that penalises both bulk separation and interfacial gradients.

Formulation

The equation evolves a concentration field u according to ∂u/∂t = Δ(ε²Δu − f(u)), where f is typically the derivative of a double-well potential such as u³ − u. The parameter ε relates to the width of interfaces between phases.

Applications

Beyond alloy and polymer phase separation, Cahn–Hilliard models are used in image inpainting, tumor-growth modelling, thin-film dynamics, and multiphase fluid flow coupled with the Navier–Stokes equations.

Analysis

The equation is the H⁻¹ gradient flow of the associated free energy, which decreases over time. Mathematical study concerns well-posedness, sharp-interface limits recovering surface-tension-driven motion, and efficient numerical schemes such as convex splitting.
