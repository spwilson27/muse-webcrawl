Merchant navy

Merchant fleets carry the overwhelming majority of world trade by volume.

A merchant navy or merchant marine is the fleet of a nation's merchant ships, along with the officers and crews who sail them. The term distinguishes civilian commercial shipping — cargo ships, tankers, container ships, bulk carriers, ferries and passenger vessels — from the military navy. Together, merchant fleets carry roughly 90 percent of world trade by volume, making them the backbone of the global economy.

National merchant fleets

Countries maintain merchant fleets in various ways. Britain's Merchant Navy — a title formally granted by King George V in recognition of merchant seamen's service in the First World War — historically ranked among the world's largest; its officers are trained at colleges such as Warsash and its seafarers commemorated on Merchant Navy Day (3 September). The United States maintains the United States Merchant Marine, with its federal academy at Kings Point, and reserves coastal trade (cabotage) to US-built, US-crewed ships under the Jones Act of 1920.

Flags of convenience

Many ships are registered not in their owners' home country but under "flags of convenience" — open registries such as Panama, Liberia and the Marshall Islands, which between them account for a large share of world tonnage. Open registration offers tax and regulatory advantages but has drawn criticism over safety standards, labour conditions and opacity of ownership. Organisations such as the International Transport Workers' Federation campaign for seafarers' conditions aboard such vessels.

Life at sea

Merchant ships are crewed by deck, engineering and catering departments under a master (captain). Modern crews are strikingly multinational, with large numbers of seafarers from the Philippines, India, China and Eastern Europe. International regulation is set by the International Maritime Organization (IMO), notably the SOLAS and STCW conventions, and by the Maritime Labour Convention of 2006.
