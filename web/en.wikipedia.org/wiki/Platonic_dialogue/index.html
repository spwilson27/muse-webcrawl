Platonic dialogue

A medieval manuscript of Plato

The Platonic dialogue is the literary and philosophical form used by the ancient Greek philosopher Plato in his writings: conversations, usually led by his teacher Socrates, in which philosophical questions are examined through question and answer.

Form and characters

Most dialogues are narrated conversations set in Athens among Socrates and his contemporaries – statesmen, sophists, generals, and young aristocrats. Socrates typically professes ignorance and, through a series of short questions (the elenchus), shows that his interlocutor's confident beliefs are inconsistent, ending in aporia (puzzlement) – the characteristic conclusion of the early dialogues such as the Euthyphro and Laches. In the middle dialogues, including the Phaedo, Symposium, and Republic, Socrates expounds doctrines associated with Plato himself, notably the theory of Forms.

Chronology and corpus

Thirty-five dialogues and a set of letters were traditionally attributed to Plato. Scholars conventionally divide the dialogues into early, middle, and late groups on stylistic and doctrinal grounds; the sprawling, technical Laws, in which Socrates does not appear, is generally regarded as his last work. The authenticity of some dialogues (for example the Alcibiades) and of the letters remains debated.

Influence

Plato effectively invented the philosophical dialogue as a genre; it was imitated by Cicero, Plutarch, Lucian, Galileo (in his Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems), Berkeley, and Hume. Because Plato never speaks in his own voice, interpreting the dialogues has raised the enduring question of how far the character "Socrates" represents Plato's own views.
