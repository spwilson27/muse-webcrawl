Alan Cobham is an American mathematician and logician known for foundational work in computational complexity theory and combinatorics on words.

Cobham's thesis

In his 1965 paper "The intrinsic computational difficulty of functions", Cobham argued that the problems feasible in practice are exactly those solvable in polynomial time, thereby characterising the complexity class P as the class of tractable problems. This position, paralleled in the work of Jack Edmonds and now known as the Cobham–Edmonds thesis, remains a guiding principle of complexity theory, even though the strong form of the thesis is debated in the age of quantum computing.

Cobham's theorem

In combinatorics on words, Cobham's theorem (1969) states that if a set of natural numbers is recognisable by finite automata in two multiplicatively independent bases, then it is ultimately periodic. The result, a landmark of automata theory, links numeral systems with formal language theory.

Other work

Cobham also worked on the theory of automatic sequences and on definability in arithmetic, spending much of his career at IBM's research laboratories.
