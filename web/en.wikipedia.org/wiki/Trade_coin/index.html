Trade coin

A trade coin is a coin minted by one country or authority for use in trade with another region, rather than for circulation in the issuing country itself. Trade coins were typically made of precious metal — usually silver, sometimes gold — with a stable weight and fineness, so that merchants who did not trust or recognise foreign currency would accept them by weight and reputation. Several became de facto international currencies over wide regions and long periods.

Classic silver trade coins: the Spanish dollar and the Maria Theresa thaler.

Famous trade coins

  • Spanish dollar (piece of eight) — minted from the silver of Potosí and Mexican mines, it circulated worldwide from the 16th to the 19th century and was legal tender in the United States until 1857.
  • Maria Theresa thaler — first struck in 1741 and issued with the same design and frozen date of 1780 for centuries; it became the standard trade coin of the Red Sea, Arabia, and East Africa well into the 20th century.
  • Trade dollar — the United States minted a slightly heavier silver dollar in 1873–1885 specifically to compete in the China trade; Britain, France, and Japan issued analogous trade dollars and piastre coinage for Asian markets.
  • Venetian ducat and sequin — gold trade coins trusted across the Mediterranean and Levant for centuries.

Characteristics

Because recipients valued trade coins by their metal content, issuers maintained consistent weights and purity, and successful designs changed little: familiarity itself was a brand. In destination markets, coins were often countermarked or "chopped" by local merchants and money-changers attesting to their quality, as with Chinese chop marks on Spanish and American dollars. Trade coins frequently continued to be struck after the issuing authority disappeared, or were restruck by other mints, because demand for the trusted type persisted.

Decline

The spread of modern national currencies, colonial coinages, paper money, and eventually the collapse of silver-based monetary systems in the 20th century ended the era of the trade coin, though the Maria Theresa thaler continued to be struck as a bullion and commemorative piece.
