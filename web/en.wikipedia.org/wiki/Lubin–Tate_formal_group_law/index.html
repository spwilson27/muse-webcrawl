Lubin–Tate formal group law

In mathematics, the Lubin–Tate formal group law is a formal group law constructed for a given local field, introduced by Jonathan Lubin and John Tate in 1965. It plays a central role in local class field theory, because the torsion points of the Lubin–Tate formal group provide an explicit construction of the totally ramified abelian extensions of the local field.

Definition

Let K be a local field with ring of integers O, residue field of characteristic p, and uniformiser π. A Lubin–Tate formal group law is obtained from a power series f(T) in O[[T]] satisfying f(T) ≡ πT (mod T2) and f(T) ≡ Tq (mod π), where q is the cardinality of the residue field. For such a series f there exists a unique formal group law F over O that commutes with f, in the sense that f is an endomorphism of F. The resulting formal group is a formal O-module of height 1 in the unramified sense.

Torsion points and class field theory

The πn-torsion points of the formal group — the roots of the iterated series f∘n — generate a tower of totally ramified Galois extensions of K. Adjoining all these torsion points gives the Lubin–Tate extension, whose Galois group is isomorphic to the unit group O×. Together with the unramified extensions, this yields an explicit description of the maximal abelian extension of K, a key step in the local Kronecker–Weber type theorem proved by Lubin and Tate.

Applications

Lubin–Tate theory underlies explicit local class field theory and feeds into the construction of the local Langlands correspondence. The associated formal groups are also examples of formal groups of height equal to the degree of the field extension, and their deformation theory connects to the Lubin–Tate spectrum in stable homotopy theory and chromatic homotopy theory.
