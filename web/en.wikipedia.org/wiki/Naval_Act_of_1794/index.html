Naval Act of 1794

The Naval Act of 1794, formally "An Act to provide a Naval Armament", was a law of the United States passed on 27 March 1794 that authorised the construction of six frigates — the first ships of the United States Navy. It marked the creation of a permanent American naval force after the Continental Navy of the Revolutionary War had been disbanded.

Background

After independence, the United States sold off its last warship in 1785, leaving American merchant shipping exposed. When war between Britain and revolutionary France disrupted Atlantic trade, and Barbary pirates from Algiers captured American ships and crews in 1793, pressure grew for a navy. Debate in Congress was fierce: opponents feared cost and a standing military force, while supporters argued commerce could not be protected without one. The Act passed the House of Representatives by a narrow margin and included a clause suspending construction if peace were concluded with Algiers.

The six frigates

The Act authorised four 44-gun and two 36-gun frigates, designed by Joshua Humphreys to be stronger and faster than European counterparts. The ships were United States, Constellation, Constitution, Chesapeake, Congress and President. A 1796 peace with Algiers triggered the suspension clause, and President Washington had to persuade Congress to fund completion of three; the remaining three were authorised in 1798 amid the Quasi-War with France. Constitution, launched in 1797, survives today as the world's oldest commissioned warship still afloat.

Significance

The Act laid the foundation of the US Navy, led to the creation of the Department of the Navy in 1798, and set precedents for American naval shipbuilding at six federal navy yards.
