Konnyaku

Blocks of konnyaku, traditionally flecked with hijiki seaweed

Konnyaku (Japanese: 蒟蒻 or コンニャク) is a traditional Japanese food made from the corm of the konjac plant (Amorphophallus konjac, also called devil's tongue or voodoo lily). The corm is dried and ground into a flour rich in glucomannan, a soluble dietary fibre; mixed with water and an alkaline solution (traditionally limewater or wood-ash lye), the flour sets into a firm, rubbery, translucent jelly that has been eaten in Japan for well over a thousand years.

Forms and uses

Konnyaku is sold as grey or white blocks (ita-konnyaku), as noodles called shirataki, and formed into balls, slices, and novelty shapes. It has almost no flavour of its own and virtually no digestible calories — it is essentially all water and fibre — so it is prized both as a texture in simmered dishes such as oden and sukiyaki and as a diet food. Because glucomannan absorbs water and swells, konnyaku is also manufactured into fruit jellies, dietary supplements, and even cosmetic sponges (konjac sponges).

History

Konjac cultivation and cooking came to Japan from China, where the plant was used medicinally; by the Edo period konnyaku was a common food and a standard shōjin (Buddhist vegetarian) ingredient. Japan, particularly Gunma Prefecture, remains the centre of konjac farming: the plant takes two to three years to produce a usable corm and the raw corm is toxic (rich in calcium oxalate), requiring careful processing.

Safety

The firm mini-cup konnyaku jellies became notorious as a choking hazard, especially for children and the elderly, and were banned or reformulated in the European Union, the United States, and elsewhere after a series of fatal incidents. Block and noodle forms remain widely sold.
