String theory

In string theory, particles are vibrations of tiny strings.

In physics, string theory is a theoretical framework in which the point-like particles of particle physics are replaced by one-dimensional objects called strings. String theory describes how strings propagate through space and interact with each other; on distance scales larger than the string scale, a string looks just like an ordinary particle, with its mass, charge and other properties determined by the vibrational state of the string.

One vibrational state of the string corresponds to the graviton, the hypothetical quantum of gravity, so string theory is a candidate theory of quantum gravity that aims to unify all fundamental forces. The framework requires extra spatial dimensions (ten spacetime dimensions in superstring theories), compactified on tiny geometries such as Calabi–Yau manifolds. Despite its mathematical depth and influence — and successes such as the AdS/CFT correspondence (Maldacena, 1997), a duality relating gravity in anti-de Sitter space to a quantum field theory on its boundary — string theory has produced no confirmed experimental prediction, and its 'landscape' of ~10⁵⁰⁰ possible vacua raises questions about its testability.

History

String theory began in 1968 as a model of the strong interaction (Veneziano's amplitude; Susskind, Nambu and Nielsen's string interpretation) and was recast as a theory of gravity by Scherk and Schwarz in 1974 after the rise of Quantum chromodynamics. The 'first superstring revolution' (1984) — Green and Schwarz's anomaly cancellation in ten dimensions — triggered an explosion of work, followed by the 'second revolution' (1995) in which Edward Witten unified the five superstring theories as limits of an eleven-dimensional 'M-theory', with D-branes (Polchinski) as essential objects.

Status

String theory dominates academic work on quantum gravity and has fed mathematics (mirror symmetry, enumerative geometry) and, through holography, even nuclear and condensed-matter physics. Its critics (e.g. Lee Smolin, Peter Woit) argue it has drifted from empirical science; defenders reply that it remains the only known consistent framework joining gravity to quantum mechanics. Rivals include Loop quantum gravity and other approaches to quantum gravity.
