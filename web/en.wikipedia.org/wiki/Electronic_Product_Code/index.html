Electronic Product Code

The Electronic Product Code (EPC) is designed as a universal identifier (using an idiosyncratic numerical code system) for every physical object in the world. EPCs are a family of coding schemes created as an eventual successor to the printed barcode, most prominently the Universal Product Code (UPC). EPC identifiers are intended to be stored on radio-frequency identification (RFID) tags attached to individual items, cases, or pallets, allowing each distinct object—not merely each product class—to be uniquely identified and tracked through supply chains.

Structure

An EPC is typically a 96-bit identifier (other lengths such as 64-bit and 128/198-bit variants exist) divided into fields: a header identifying the encoding scheme, a manager number identifying the organization responsible, an object class identifying the type of product, and a serial number unique to the specific instance. The most widely used scheme, SGTIN-96, encodes a Global Trade Item Number together with a serial number.

History

The EPC system was developed at the Auto-ID Center at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, founded in 1999 under Kevin Ashton, who is credited with coining the term "Internet of Things". In 2003, stewardship of the standards passed to EPCglobal, a joint venture of GS1 and GS1 US, which ratified and maintains the EPC standards, including the EPCglobal Network architecture and the EPCIS (EPC Information Services) specification for sharing event data.

Applications

EPC-based RFID is used in retail inventory management, logistics, and manufacturing. Large retailers, notably Walmart, promoted EPC adoption in the mid-2000s by requiring suppliers to tag pallets and cases. Item-level tagging later spread in apparel retail, where it enables rapid stock counts and omnichannel fulfilment.
