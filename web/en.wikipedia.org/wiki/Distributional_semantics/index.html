Distributional semantics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A schematic of a semantic vector space

Distributional semantics is a research area that develops and studies theories and methods for quantifying and categorizing semantic similarities between linguistic items based on their distributional properties in large samples of language data. The basic idea of distributional semantics can be summed up in the so-called distributional hypothesis: linguistic items with similar distributions have similar meanings.

The distributional hypothesis is usually credited to Zellig Harris (1954) and is encapsulated in J. R. Firth's famous dictum that "a word is characterized by the company it keeps".

Distributional semantic models

Distributional semantic models represent words as vectors in a high-dimensional space built from co-occurrence statistics in text corpora. Similarity between words is measured by geometric distance, typically cosine similarity, between their vectors. Classical approaches include latent semantic analysis (LSA), while modern neural approaches such as word2vec and GloVe learn dense embeddings that capture rich semantic and syntactic regularities, including analogies such as "king is to queen as man is to woman".

Applications

Distributional models are used in information retrieval, word-sense disambiguation, semantic similarity and relatedness judgements, automatic thesaurus construction, and as input features to downstream natural language processing systems. Contextualized models such as BERT and other transformer-based language models extend the idea by making a word's representation depend on its surrounding context.
