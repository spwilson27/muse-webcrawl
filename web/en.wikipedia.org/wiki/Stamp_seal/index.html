Stamp seal

A stamp seal is a small object with a design carved or molded into one face, used by pressing or stamping it into a soft material — typically clay, wax, or ink — to leave an identifying impression. Stamp seals were one of the earliest administrative technologies of the ancient world, used to mark ownership, seal containers and doors against tampering, authenticate documents, and serve as amulets. They contrast with cylinder seals, which were rolled across wet clay.

Origins and early history

Stamp seals appeared in the Neolithic Near East; simple patterned stamps, including the objects called pintaderas, are known from sites such as Çatalhöyük. By the 6th–5th millennia BCE, seal use spread across Mesopotamia and Iran. Early impressions sealed the clay lumps placed over jar stoppers, knots, and door pegs, controlling access to stored goods. The Halaf and Ubaid cultures produced seals with geometric and animal designs. In the Indus Valley civilization (c. 2600–1900 BCE), steatite stamp seals bearing animal motifs and signs of the undeciphered Indus script became a hallmark artifact, used apparently in trade and administration.

Materials and forms

Seals were carved from stone (steatite, hematite, carnelian, rock crystal), faience, metal, bone, ivory, and wood, and ranged from simple flat stamps with a perforated lug handle to Egyptian scarabs and, later, signet rings. In Mesopotamia stamp seals were gradually displaced by cylinder seals during the Uruk period, but they remained dominant in many other regions — Anatolia, the Aegean (Minoan and Mycenaean seals are celebrated works of miniature art), Egypt, Iran, and South and East Asia, where personal seal stamps (such as the Chinese yìnzhāng, Japanese hanko, and Korean dojang) remain in daily legal and artistic use.

Function and study

For archaeologists, seal impressions on bullae and jar handles are evidence for ancient bureaucracies, trade networks, and personal identity; whole hoards of clay sealings survive from palace archives. Because seal designs often incorporate early writing, glyptic studies contribute to the history of scripts as well as art. In the Hebrew Bible and the ancient Near East more broadly, the signet functioned as a symbol of delegated authority, as when Pharaoh gives Joseph his signet ring in Genesis.
