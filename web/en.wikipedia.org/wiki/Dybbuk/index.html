Dybbuk

Dybbuk דיבוק
Origin: Jewish folklore
Type: Possessing spirit of the dead

In Jewish mythology, a dybbuk (Yiddish: דיבוק, from the Hebrew word דָּבַק davak, meaning "adhere" or "cling") is a malicious possessing spirit believed to be the dislocated soul of a dead person that has escaped from Gehenna. It supposedly leaves the host body once it has accomplished its goal, sometimes after being exorcised by a rabbi.

History

Accounts of possession by dybbuks flourished in Eastern European Jewish communities from the 16th century onward, particularly in the milieu of Lurianic Kabbalah, which developed doctrines of transmigration of souls (gilgul). The dybbuk was said to enter a living person — often someone who had committed a secret sin — and speak through them, until expelled by ritual exorcism involving prayer, the shofar, and adjuration.

In culture

The concept became famous through S. Ansky's Yiddish play The Dybbuk (1914, premiered 1920), subtitled "Between Two Worlds", in which the soul of a dead bridegroom possesses his beloved. The play inspired ballets, operas, and films, and the figure recurs in modern horror, including the film The Unborn (2009) and the viral legend of the "dybbuk box".
