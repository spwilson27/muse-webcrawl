Nizari Isma'ilism (Arabic: النزارية, romanized: al-Nizāriyya) is the largest branch of Isma'ilism, itself a branch of Shia Islam. The Nizaris are the followers of Nizar ibn al-Mustansir, whom they recognize as the nineteenth Imam and the rightful successor to the Fatimid caliph-imam al-Mustansir Billah (died 1094). Today the Nizari community, often known simply as the Isma'ili Muslims or Khojas in parts of South Asia, is led by the Aga Khan, whom Nizaris believe to be the living, hereditary Imam of the Time. The community is estimated to number between 12 and 15 million adherents spread across more than 25 countries in Central and South Asia, East Africa, the Middle East, Europe, and North America.

History

Succession dispute of 1094

Nizari Isma'ilism originated in the succession crisis that followed the death of the Fatimid caliph-imam al-Mustansir Billah in 1094. The powerful vizier al-Afdal Shahanshah installed al-Mustansir's younger son, al-Musta'li, on the throne in Cairo, bypassing the elder son and designated heir, Nizar. The Isma'ili da'wa (mission) in Persia and Syria, led by the charismatic missionary Hassan-i Sabbah, refused to recognize al-Musta'li and upheld the imamate of Nizar and his descendants. Nizar himself was defeated in a revolt at Alexandria and died in prison in 1095, but his followers maintained that his line of imams continued, initially in concealment. The schism permanently divided the Isma'ili world into Nizari and Musta'li branches.

The Alamut state

From his mountain fortress of Alamut in the Alborz range south of the Caspian Sea, Hassan-i Sabbah built a network of strongholds across Persia and Syria that coalesced into a de facto Nizari state. The Nizaris of this period became famous — and infamous in hostile sources — for the targeted killing of political and religious opponents by devotees known in Western legend as the "Assassins," a name derived from the Arabic hashishiyya, a derogatory label applied by their enemies. In 1164, the fourth lord of Alamut, Hasan II, proclaimed the Qiyama (Resurrection), a radical reinterpretation of the faith that abrogated the literal observance of the sharia in favour of its spiritual meaning, and declared himself the manifest Imam of the line of Nizar. The Alamut state endured until 1256, when the fortress capitulated to the Mongol forces of Hulagu Khan during the Mongol invasions, and the Nizari communities of Persia were subjected to massacre and dispersal.

Post-Alamut period and the Anjudan revival

After the fall of Alamut, the Nizari imams lived for two centuries in concealment (satr), emerging in the fifteenth century at the village of Anjudan in central Persia, from which they directed a revival of the da'wa. Missionaries carried the faith to India, where converts — many of them Hindu merchants of Gujarat — became known as Khojas and developed a distinctive devotional literature of hymns called ginans. In the early nineteenth century the imam Hasan Ali Shah received the title Aga Khan from the Qajar shah of Persia; after a falling-out with the court he emigrated to India, where the headquarters of the imamate have effectively remained connected ever since.

Beliefs and practices

Nizari theology emphasizes the distinction between the exoteric (zahir) and esoteric (batin) dimensions of revelation. The Quran and the sharia possess inner meanings that can only be unlocked through ta'wil (spiritual hermeneutics) taught by the Imam of the Time, who is believed to be the sole authoritative interpreter of the faith in each age. This doctrine of ta'lim — the necessity of authoritative teaching — was classically articulated in response to critics such as al-Ghazali. Nizari ethics stress pluralism, generosity, intellectual inquiry, and service to humanity; religious practice centres on the jamatkhana (house of congregation), where communal prayers (du'a) are offered three times daily. Unlike most other Muslim communities, Nizaris do not observe the five daily salat prayers in the conventional form, and religious authority flows directly from the Imam's guidance (farmans) rather than from a clerical hierarchy.

The Imamate

The cornerstone of Nizari faith is the unbroken line of hereditary imams descended from Ali ibn Abi Talib and Fatima, the daughter of the Prophet Muhammad, through the designated succession (nass) of each imam by his predecessor. Prince Shah Karim al-Hussaini, Aga Khan IV, succeeded his grandfather Aga Khan III in 1957 as the 49th Imam, at the age of twenty, while a student at Harvard University. His grandfather's will bypassed his own sons in favour of the young prince, citing the changed conditions of the modern world. The Aga Khan IV led the community for more than six decades until his death in February 2025, whereupon his son Rahim al-Hussaini succeeded as the 50th Imam, Aga Khan V, in accordance with the designated succession announced in his father's will.

Community institutions

Under the Aga Khan IV, the Nizari community developed an unusually elaborate institutional structure. The Aga Khan Development Network (AKDN) coordinates agencies working in health, education, rural development, culture, and economic development across the developing world, serving Muslim and non-Muslim populations alike; its agencies include the Aga Khan Foundation, the Aga Khan University, and the Aga Khan Fund for Economic Development. Nizaris in each country of residence are governed by councils operating under constitutions promulgated by the Imam; a single constitution was granted to the worldwide community in 1986. The community's settlement in the West expanded greatly after the expulsion of Asians from Uganda in 1972, when thousands of Nizari families resettled in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States.
