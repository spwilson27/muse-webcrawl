Gender schema theory

Gender schema theory is a cognitive theory of gender development proposed by psychologist Sandra Bem in 1981. It holds that children learn about male and female roles from the culture in which they live, and that they organize incoming information into schemas — mental structures — about what is appropriate for their own gender, which then guide their behaviour and self-concept.

Overview

According to the theory, children adjust their behaviour to align with the gender norms of their culture from an early age. A child develops a gender schema by observing and interacting with the social environment: the gender-linked associations that pervade a culture are internalized, and the child comes to evaluate their own adequacy as a person in terms of the gender schema.

Bem argued that gender-schematic processing leads individuals to sort attributes and behaviours into masculine and feminine categories, even when a trait has little to do with sex itself, and that some individuals are more gender-schematic than others (measured partly with the Bem Sex-Role Inventory, which treats masculinity and femininity as independent dimensions).

Relation to other theories

Gender schema theory is related to the cognitive-developmental accounts of Lawrence Kohlberg and to social learning theory, combining elements of both: like social learning theory it emphasizes environmental input, and like cognitive theories it emphasizes the child's active construction of knowledge. Carol Martin and Charles Halverson developed a related schema model of gender development in the same period.
