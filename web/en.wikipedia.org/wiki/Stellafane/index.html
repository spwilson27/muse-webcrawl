Stellafane (a contraction of the Latin stella, star, and fanum, shrine — "shrine of the stars") is the historic meeting place of the Springfield Telescope Makers, an amateur astronomy club in Springfield, Vermont, United States. It is regarded as the birthplace of amateur telescope making in America, and its annual convention is among the oldest gatherings of amateur astronomers in the world.

History

The Springfield Telescope Makers were founded in 1920 by Russell W. Porter, an Arctic explorer, artist, and optician who had worked on the 200-inch Hale Telescope project. In 1923 the club acquired land on Breezy Hill outside Springfield, and members built the distinctive pink stucco clubhouse in 1924. The Porter Turret Telescope, an innovative equatorial turret design, was completed in 1930 and remains in use. The site was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1989 in recognition of its role in the amateur astronomy movement.

The convention

The Stellafane Convention, first held in 1926, draws amateur astronomers each summer for telescope-making competitions, optical judging, talks, swap tables, and observing under Vermont skies. Amateur telescope making competitions at Stellafane established standards — optical quality, mechanical design, and craftsmanship — emulated by star parties worldwide. Events are traditionally held around a new moon near the Perseid meteor shower in late July or August.

Influence

Porter and the Springfield club popularized the building of reflecting telescopes by amateurs through articles in Scientific American, whose "Amateur Scientist" column grew out of the Stellafane circle. Club member Albert G. Ingalls edited the influential Amateur Telescope Making book series beginning in 1926, spreading the hobby globally.

Facilities

In addition to the original clubhouse and Porter Turret Telescope, the site includes the McGregor Observatory, housing a large Schupmann medial telescope, and the Flanders Pavilion. An annex site, Stellafane East, hosts camping and convention activities.
