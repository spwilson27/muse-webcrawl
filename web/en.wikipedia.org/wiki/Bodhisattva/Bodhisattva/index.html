Bodhisattva

In Buddhism, a bodhisattva (Sanskrit: बोधिसत्त्व, bodhisattva; Pali: bodhisatta) is a person who is on the path towards Buddhahood. In Mahāyāna Buddhism, a bodhisattva is any person who has generated bodhicitta, a spontaneous wish and compassionate mind to attain Buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings. Mahāyāna bodhisattvas are spiritually heroic persons who work to attain complete enlightenment while delaying their own final liberation in order to save others.

Etymology

The Sanskrit term bodhisattva is compounded of bodhi ("awakening", "enlightenment") and sattva ("being", "essence"). It is often translated as "enlightenment-being" or "one whose essence is awakening". In early Buddhist texts, the term "bodhisatta" is used primarily to refer to the Buddha Gautama in his previous lives and in his final life before awakening, as depicted in the Jātaka tales.

In early Buddhism and Theravāda

In early Buddhism and in modern Theravāda, a bodhisatta is someone on the path to full Buddhahood. The Theravāda tradition recognizes the bodhisattva path as an option for rare beings who aspire to become sammāsambuddhas (fully self-awakened Buddhas) over countless lifetimes, practicing the ten perfections (pāramī). Most practitioners, however, aim for arahantship as disciples of a Buddha.

In Mahāyāna Buddhism

Mahāyāna Buddhism elevated the bodhisattva ideal to the central path for all practitioners, in contrast to the goal of the arhat, which Mahāyāna texts sometimes criticized as self-centered. The Mahāyāna bodhisattva takes vows (praṇidhāna) to work for the liberation of all beings, develops the six perfections (pāramitās) — generosity, morality, patience, effort, meditation, and wisdom — and progresses through the bodhisattva stages (bhūmis), usually enumerated as ten.

Celestial bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteśvara (compassion), Mañjuśrī (wisdom), Samantabhadra, Kṣitigarbha, and Maitreya (the future Buddha) became objects of devotion throughout East and Central Asia. Texts such as the Lotus Sūtra, the Bodhicaryāvatāra of Śāntideva, and the Mahāyāna sūtras on the bodhisattva stages elaborate the doctrine extensively.

The bodhisattva path

According to Mahāyāna doctrine, the path begins with the arising of bodhicitta and the taking of the bodhisattva vows before a teacher. The practitioner then traverses the five paths and ten bhūmis, gradually removing the afflictive and cognitive obscurations. Grounds include Joyous, Stainless, Luminous, Radiant, Difficult to Train, Manifest, Gone Afar, Immovable, Good Intelligence, and Cloud of Dharma. A fully accomplished bodhisattva of the higher grounds possesses miraculous powers, emanates forms in many worlds, and teaches innumerable beings.
