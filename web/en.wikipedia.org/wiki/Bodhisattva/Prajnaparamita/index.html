Prajñāpāramitā

Prajñāpāramitā (Sanskrit: प्रज्ञापारमिता) means "the Perfection of (Transcendent) Wisdom" in Mahayana Buddhism. Prajñāpāramitā refers to a perfected way of seeing the nature of reality, as well as to a particular body of Mahayana sutras and to the female deity who personifies the concept. The central teaching of these texts is the emptiness (śūnyatā) of all phenomena: nothing possesses independent, intrinsic existence. The most famous short texts are the Heart Sutra and the Diamond Sutra.

The literature

The Prajñāpāramitā corpus developed over several centuries from about the 1st century BCE. The Aṣṭasāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 Lines") is among the earliest Mahayana sutras. The corpus grew into versions of 25,000 and even 100,000 lines, and was also condensed into extremely short works: the Heart Sutra compresses the doctrine into a few hundred characters, culminating in the mantra gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā; the Diamond Sutra presents the teaching as a dialogue on the illusory nature of all marks and concepts.

Doctrine

The texts teach that all dharmas — including the aggregates, the senses, and even nirvana and the path itself — are empty of inherent existence, existing only dependently. The Heart Sutra's famous formula, "form is emptiness, emptiness is form", summarises this non-dual view. A bodhisattva who relies on the Perfection of Wisdom, such as Avalokiteśvara, perceives the five aggregates as empty and thereby overcomes suffering. Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka philosophy later systematised the Prajñāpāramitā teaching, and his works became foundational for Mahayana thought across Asia.

Personification

From the Gupta period onward, Prajñāpāramitā was worshipped as a goddess, "Mother of all Buddhas", depicted in sculpture across India, Nepal, Tibet and Southeast Asia, notably in 13th-century East Javanese art.
