The Heart Sutra (Sanskrit: Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya, "The Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom") is a popular sutra in Mahayana Buddhism, and one of the best known of all Buddhist texts. In a few hundred characters it distils the Perfection of Wisdom teaching of emptiness (śūnyatā).

Content

The bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, practising the perfection of wisdom, perceives that the five aggregates (skandhas) are empty of inherent existence, and declares: "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form." The sutra negates the standard categories of Buddhist doctrine in turn — no eye, no ear, no suffering, no path, no attainment — and concludes with the mantra gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā ("gone, gone, gone beyond, gone altogether beyond — awakening, hail!").

Origins

The sutra survives in a short and a long (framed) version. Scholarship associated with Jan Nattier argues that its core was composed in China, drawing on material from Kumārajīva's translation of the Large Perfection of Wisdom Sutra, and was translated into Sanskrit; the famous Chinese version is attributed to Xuanzang (7th century CE).

Practice

The Heart Sutra is chanted daily in Zen, Tendai and other East Asian schools, and frequently in Tibetan Buddhism; it is also a classic subject of East Asian calligraphy.
