Zen

Zen (Japanese pronunciation: [dzeꜜɴ, dzeɴ]; from Chinese: 禪, Chán; Korean: 선, Seon; Vietnamese: Thiền) is a school of Mahayana Buddhism that originated in China during the Tang dynasty, known as the Chan School, and later developed into various subschools and branches in Japan, Korea and Vietnam. By the mid-20th century Zen had become influential in the West through teachers and writers such as D. T. Suzuki, Alan Watts and Shunryu Suzuki.

Zen emphasises direct experience of one's true nature (kenshō, satori) through meditation — seated meditation (zazen) in the Sōtō school, and kōan introspection in the Rinzai school — rather than through scriptural study or ritual. Its traditional self-description, attributed to Bodhidharma, the legendary Indian monk who brought Chan to China, speaks of "a special transmission outside the scriptures, not founded upon words and letters, pointing directly at the human mind, seeing one's nature and becoming Buddha."

History

Chan developed in China from the 6th century onwards through the interaction of Mahayana Buddhism with Daoist thought, flourishing in the Tang and Song dynasties under masters such as Huineng, Linji Yixuan and Hongzhi Zhengjue. It was transmitted to Japan in the Kamakura period by Eisai (Rinzai) and Dōgen (Sōtō). In the 20th century, Japanese and later Western teachers popularised Zen meditation worldwide, and Korean Seon and Vietnamese Thiền masters such as Thích Nhất Hạnh brought related traditions to international audiences.

Practices

Central practices include zazen (just sitting, shikantaza), kōan study with a teacher, chanting, prostrations, work practice (samu) and intensive retreats (sesshin). Zen has deeply influenced Japanese arts, including ink painting, calligraphy, tea ceremony, garden design and martial arts.
