Madhyamaka

Madhyamaka (Sanskrit: माध्यमक, "Middle Way"; Chinese: 中觀派), also known as Śūnyavāda ("the emptiness doctrine"), is a tradition of Buddhist philosophy and practice founded by the Indian philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE). The school and its adherents, the Mādhyamikas, developed a sophisticated analysis of emptiness (śūnyatā), holding that all phenomena are empty of inherent existence (svabhāva) because they arise dependently (pratītyasamutpāda).

Nāgārjuna, founder of the Madhyamaka school

Foundational texts

The foundational text of the school is Nāgārjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā ("Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way"), which analyzes motion, the self, the Buddha, causality, and nirvana, applying the catuṣkoṭi (four-cornered) logical structure and the method of prasaṅga (reductio ad absurdum). Nāgārjuna's other works, including the Vigrahavyāvartanī and Śūnyatāsaptati, elaborate the philosophy. Important later thinkers include Buddhapālita, Bhāviveka, Candrakīrti — whose Madhyamakāvatāra is a key commentary — and Śāntideva, who applied Madhyamaka in the Bodhicaryāvatāra.

Central doctrines

Madhyamaka's core claim is that emptiness is itself identical with dependent origination and with the middle way between eternalism and annihilationism. Because things exist dependently, they cannot possess intrinsic, independent natures; yet because they function conventionally, they are not mere nothing. The doctrine of the two truths distinguishes conventional truth (saṁvṛti-satya), within which persons, tables, and causation operate, from ultimate truth (paramārtha-satya), the emptiness of all phenomena. Crucially, emptiness is itself empty: it is a designation, not an absolute substrate.

Sub-schools and debate

Tibetan doxography divides Madhyamaka into Svātantrika (associated with Bhāviveka, favoring autonomous syllogisms) and Prāsaṅgika (associated with Buddhapālita and Candrakīrti, favoring reductio arguments that draw out opponents' contradictions without asserting counter-theses). The Prāsaṅgika reading became dominant in Tibetan Buddhism, especially in the Gelug school of Tsongkhapa, while Jonang and other traditions defended alternative readings such as "other-emptiness" (shentong).

Influence

Madhyamaka strongly shaped East Asian Buddhism (as the Three Treatise school, Sanlun), Tibetan Buddhism, and, through Zen, aspects of East Asian thought. In modern times it has attracted interest from philosophers exploring anti-foundationalism, semantic theory, and the parallels between emptiness and interdependence.
