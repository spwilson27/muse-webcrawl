Lotus Sutra

The Lotus Sūtra (Sanskrit: Saddharma Puṇḍarīka Sūtra, "Sūtra on the White Lotus of the True Dharma"; Chinese: 妙法蓮華經) is one of the most influential and venerated Mahāyāna sūtras. It is the main scripture on which the Tiantai, Tendai, Cheontae, and Nichiren schools of Buddhism were established. It is also influential for other East Asian Buddhist schools, such as Zen. According to the British Buddhologist Paul Williams, "For many East Asian Buddhists since early times the Lotus Sūtra contains the final teaching of the Buddha, complete and sufficient for salvation."

Frontispiece of a Japanese Lotus Sūtra manuscript

Title and history

The earliest known Sanskrit title is Saddharma Puṇḍarīka Sūtra, which translates to "Scripture of the Lotus Blossom of the Fine Dharma". The sūtra was probably composed between the 1st century BCE and the 2nd century CE, in stages, possibly beginning in verse and later being expanded in prose. It was translated into Chinese several times; the most influential translation is that of Kumārajīva (406 CE), whose 28-chapter version became standard in East Asia.

Main teachings

The sūtra is known for its teaching of the One Vehicle (ekayāna): the three vehicles of the śrāvaka, pratyekabuddha, and bodhisattva are not separate final goals but skillful means (upāya) leading all beings to the single goal of Buddhahood. Famous parables illustrating this include the burning house, the prodigal son, the conjured city, and the hidden jewel in the garment.

The sūtra also teaches the immeasurable lifespan of the Buddha: Śākyamuni did not first awaken under the Bodhi tree but attained Buddhahood countless aeons ago and continues to teach. Chapter 25, devoted to the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara, circulated widely as an independent text. The sūtra predicts Buddhahood for all beings, including the Buddha's antagonist Devadatta and the nāga king's eight-year-old daughter, underlining its universalism.

Influence

The Chinese Tiantai school of Zhiyi classified the Lotus Sūtra as the supreme teaching of the Buddha's final period. In Japan, Tendai, and later the Pure Land, Zen, and Nichiren traditions all grew out of Lotus devotion. Nichiren Buddhism bases its practice on chanting the title of the sūtra, Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō. The text also inspired a vast body of art, literature, and devotional practice across East Asia, and copies were among the Dunhuang manuscripts and early printed books.

Translations

English translations include those by Hendrik Kern (1884), Leon Hurvitz (1976), Burton Watson (1993), and Gene Reeves (2008). Fragments of early Sanskrit manuscripts survive from Central Asia and Gilgit, and complete Sanskrit manuscripts are preserved in Nepal and Tibet.
