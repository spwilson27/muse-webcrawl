Theravada

Theravāda (Pāli: थेरवाद, lit. "School of the Elders") is the most commonly accepted name of Buddhism's oldest existing school. The school's adherents, termed Theravādins, have preserved their version of Gautama Buddha's teaching or dhamma in the Pāli Canon for over two millennia. The Pāli Canon is the most complete Buddhist canon surviving in a classical Indian language.

Theravāda Buddhism is the dominant religion in Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia and Laos, and is practised by minorities in many other countries. It emphasises the doctrine of anattā (no-self), the Four Noble Truths, the Noble Eightfold Path and the analysis of phenomena in the Abhidhamma. Its soteriological ideal is the arahant, though the bodhisattva path (bodhisatta in Pāli) is also recognised, particularly in connection with the Buddha's previous lives recounted in the Jātaka tales and with the future Buddha, Metteyya.

History

Theravāda descends from the Vibhajjavāda tradition that arose during the reign of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE. According to Sri Lankan chronicles, Buddhism was brought to Sri Lanka by Mahinda, son of Ashoka, and the canon was committed to writing at Aluvihāra in the 1st century BCE. From Sri Lanka the tradition spread through mainland Southeast Asia between the 11th and 15th centuries.

In the 19th and 20th centuries Theravāda underwent reform and revival, including the vipassanā (insight meditation) movements of Burma, and came into contact with the West, influencing modern "secular" mindfulness movements. Prominent modern figures include Buddhaghosa's commentarial legacy, the scholar-monks of the Thai forest tradition such as Ajahn Chah, and meditation teachers such as Mahasi Sayadaw and S. N. Goenka.
