Mahayana

Mahayana (Sanskrit: महायान, mahāyāna, "Great Vehicle") is a term for a broad group of Buddhist traditions, texts, philosophies and practices developed in ancient India, beginning around the 1st century BCE. Mahayana is one of the two main branches of Buddhism, alongside Theravada, and is practised throughout East and Central Asia, including China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Tibet, Bhutan, Mongolia and Nepal. Approximately half of the world's Buddhists follow Mahayana traditions.

History

Mahayana emerged within Indian Buddhism over several centuries, producing a large new literature of sutras attributed to the Buddha, such as the Prajnaparamita ("Perfection of Wisdom") texts, the Lotus Sutra, the Vimalakirti Sutra and the Pure Land sutras. It spread along the Silk Road to China, where it absorbed Daoist and Confucian influences and generated new schools — Tiantai, Huayan, Chan (Zen) and Pure Land — which were transmitted onward to Korea, Japan and Vietnam. A later transmission carried Indian Mahayana and tantric Vajrayana to Tibet from the 7th century onward.

Doctrine

The central ideal is the bodhisattva: a being who generates bodhicitta, the aspiration to attain full Buddhahood in order to liberate all beings, and who practises the six perfections over countless lifetimes. Key doctrines include emptiness (śūnyatā), systematised by Nagarjuna's Madhyamaka school; the Yogacara school's analysis of consciousness; Buddha-nature (tathāgatagarbha), the potential for awakening present in all beings; and skillful means (upāya), the idea that teachings are adapted to the capacities of their audience. Celestial Buddhas and bodhisattvas such as Amitābha and Avalokiteśvara became major objects of devotion.

Practices

Mahayana practice spans meditation, chanting of sutras and mantras, devotion and offerings to Buddhas and bodhisattvas, Pure Land recitation of Amitābha's name, Zen seated meditation (zazen) and koan study, and, in Vajrayana, tantric ritual and visualisation. Lay and monastic communities alike participate in festivals, pilgrimage and merit-making.
