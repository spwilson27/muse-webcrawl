Mogao Caves

The Mogao Caves (Chinese: 莫高窟; pinyin: Mògāo kū), also known as the Thousand Buddha Grottoes or Caves of the Thousand Buddhas, form a system of 492 temples 25 km (16 mi) southeast of the center of Dunhuang, an oasis strategically located at a religious and cultural crossroads on the Silk Road, in Gansu province, China. The caves contain some of the finest examples of Buddhist art spanning a period of 1,000 years.

History

According to tradition, in 366 CE the monk Le Zun had a vision of a thousand golden Buddhas on the cliff face at Sanwei Mountain and excavated the first cave. The site grew as Dunhuang flourished on the Silk Road, with caves sponsored by merchants, officials, and local clans through the Northern Wei, Sui, Tang, Song, and Yuan dynasties. Construction continued for roughly a millennium; 735 caves survive in total, of which the 492 caves in the southern section preserve painted murals and sculptures.

Art

The caves preserve about 45,000 square metres of murals and more than 2,000 painted sculptures, making Mogao the largest and richest repository of Buddhist art in the world. Murals depict sūtra stories, Buddha fields (Pure Lands), jataka tales, portraits of donors, and scenes of daily life, music, and dance, providing an unparalleled record of art, costume, architecture, and society in medieval China and Central Asia. Iconography evolved over time, blending Indian, Central Asian, and Chinese styles; Tang-dynasty caves are celebrated for their opulent paradise scenes and the two giant Buddha statues (35.5 m and 26 m high).

The Library Cave

In 1900, the Daoist caretaker Wang Yuanlu discovered the sealed "Library Cave" (Cave 17), which contained some 50,000 manuscripts, paintings, and printed documents dating from the 5th to the early 11th centuries, in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Khotanese, Sogdian, Uyghur, and other languages. The explorer Aurel Stein purchased thousands of manuscripts in 1907, followed by Paul Pelliot for France and expeditions from Japan and Russia; the remainder went to Beijing. The cache included the Diamond Sūtra of 868 CE, the world's earliest surviving dated printed book. The dispersal of the manuscripts remains controversial in China, and the International Dunhuang Project now digitally reunites the collections.

Preservation

Mogao was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987. The Dunhuang Academy manages conservation, research, and increasingly digital replicas; some caves are reproduced in high-fidelity facsimile to reduce pressure on the originals, and visitor numbers are strictly managed to protect the fragile pigments from humidity and carbon dioxide.
