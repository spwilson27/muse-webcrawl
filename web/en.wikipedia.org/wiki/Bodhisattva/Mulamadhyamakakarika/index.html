Mūlamadhyamakakārikā

The Mūlamadhyamakakārikā (Sanskrit: मूलमध्यमककारिका, "Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way"), abbreviated MMK, is the foundational text of the Madhyamaka school of Mahāyāna Buddhist philosophy. Written by Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE), it consists of 448 verses in 27 chapters that systematically demonstrate, through dialectical argument, that all phenomena are empty (śūnya) of inherent existence (svabhāva) because they arise in dependence on causes, parts, and conceptual designation.

A Sanskrit manuscript of the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā

Opening homage

The work opens with a famous homage to the Buddha:

"I salute him, the fully enlightened one, the best of speakers, who taught the doctrine of dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), according to which there is neither cessation nor origination, neither annihilation nor the eternal, neither singularity nor plurality, neither the coming nor the going [of any particular entity], for the purpose of blissful cessation of all conceptual proliferation (prapañca) and the attainment of peace."

Content

The 27 chapters examine, in sequence: conditions, motion (coming and going), the senses, the aggregates, the elements, desire and the desirer, arising and dissolution, action and agent, fire and fuel, beginning and end (saṁsāra), suffering, composition, contact, inherent nature, bondage and liberation, karma, the self, time, conjunction, arising and abiding, the Tathāgata, error, the four noble truths, nirvāṇa, the twelve links of dependent origination, and (in conclusion) wrong views. In each case, Nāgārjuna argues that the phenomenon in question can be established neither as inherently existent, nor as inherently nonexistent, nor both, nor neither — the tetralemma or catuṣkoṭi.

Key verses

Chapter 24, on the noble truths, contains the celebrated verse 18:

"Whatever is dependent origination, that is explained to be emptiness. That, being a dependent designation, is itself the middle way."

Chapter 25 analyzes nirvāṇa and concludes that there is not the slightest difference between saṁsāra and nirvāṇa — a conclusion that anchors the Mahāyāna non-duality of bondage and liberation. The final chapter refutes the sixty-two speculative views catalogued in early Buddhist texts.

Commentaries

The MMK generated a vast commentarial literature. Surviving Indian commentaries include the anonymous Akutobhayā, Buddhapālita's commentary, Bhāviveka's Prajñāpradīpa, and Candrakīrti's Prasannapadā, the only Indian commentary surviving in Sanskrit and the basis for the Prāsaṅgika interpretation dominant in Tibet. The text was translated into Chinese by Kumārajīva (as the Zhong Lun) and into Tibetan, where it remains central to monastic education. Standard English translations include those by Frederick Streng (1967), Kenneth Inada (1970), and Jay Garfield (1995).
