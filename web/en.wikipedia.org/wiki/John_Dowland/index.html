John Dowland (1563 – buried 20 February 1626) was an English Renaissance composer, lutenist and singer. He is today best known for his melancholy songs such as "Flow, my tears," "Come, heavy sleep," "Now, O now, I needs must part" and "In darkness let me dwell," and for his instrumental collection Lachrimae (1604). Dowland spent much of his career abroad, at the courts of German princes and of Christian IV of Denmark, and cultivated an image of the sorrowful artist summed up in his punning motto Semper Dowland semper dolens ("Always Dowland, always doleful").

Life

Little is certain about Dowland's early life; he may have been born in London or in Ireland. From about 1580 he served Sir Henry Cobham, the English ambassador in Paris, where he converted to Roman Catholicism — a fact he later blamed for being passed over for a post at Elizabeth I's court. He took a Bachelor of Music degree at Oxford in 1588 and travelled in Germany, working for the Duke of Brunswick and the Landgrave of Hesse. After an episode in Italy shadowed by fears of Catholic plotting against the Queen, Dowland wrote a famous letter of self-exculpation to Sir Robert Cecil in 1595.

Career

His First Booke of Songes or Ayres (1597) was a publishing sensation — the first English lute-song collection, reprinted many times. From 1598 to 1606 Dowland was lutenist to Christian IV of Denmark, receiving an enormous salary while his debts mounted. Returning to England, he published further books of ayres (1600, 1603), the Lachrimae pavans for viol consort and lute (1604), and translations and treatises. In 1612, late in life, he finally obtained a court post as one of James I's lutenists. He died in London in early 1626.

Music and reputation

Dowland's output — some 90 solo lute pieces, consort music, and over 80 songs — is dominated by the fashionable melancholy of the Elizabethan age, yet displays masterly counterpoint and expressive harmony. His pavan Lachrimae Antiquae, expanded into the song "Flow, my tears," was the most famous piece of music in Europe. Rediscovered by the early-music movement of the twentieth century, Dowland is now regarded as one of England's greatest composers; his songs have been recorded by performers from countertenor Alfred Deller to the rock musician Sting, whose 2006 album Songs from the Labyrinth is devoted to Dowland.
