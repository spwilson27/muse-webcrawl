Political repression in the Soviet Union

Political repression in the Soviet Union was a systematic practice of the Soviet state throughout its history, from the Red Terror of 1918 until the late 1980s. It was carried out against political opponents, real or imagined, and against entire social and ethnic categories of people deemed "enemies of the people" or socially harmful elements.

Periods and campaigns

The Red Terror (1918–1922), conducted by the Cheka under Felix Dzerzhinsky, answered the Russian Civil War with mass arrests and executions of alleged counter-revolutionaries. During the period of collectivization (1929–1933), millions of peasants labelled kulaks were expropriated, deported to remote regions, or shot.

The Great Purge (1936–1938), directed by Joseph Stalin and executed by the NKVD under Nikolai Yezhov, combined show trials of Old Bolsheviks in Moscow with mass operations against "former people", national minorities and ordinary citizens. Historians estimate roughly 750,000 executions in 1937–38 alone.

The Gulag

The Gulag system of forced-labour camps held millions of prisoners — political detainees sentenced under the notorious Article 58 of the criminal code alongside common criminals. Camp populations peaked at about 2.5 million in the early 1950s. Conditions were often lethal, with deaths from hunger, cold, disease and overwork.

Later decades

After Stalin's death in 1953, Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956 denounced the purges and most political prisoners were released. Repression continued in milder forms: dissidents were harassed by the KGB, confined in psychiatric hospitals, deprived of employment, or forced into exile. Figures such as Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, Andrei Sakharov and the Helsinki Groups faced sustained persecution. Political imprisonment effectively ended under Mikhail Gorbachev's glasnost in the late 1980s.

Estimates

Estimates of excess deaths attributable to Soviet repression between 1917 and 1953 vary widely, commonly ranging from about 6 to 20 million depending on whether famine deaths from collectivization are included.
