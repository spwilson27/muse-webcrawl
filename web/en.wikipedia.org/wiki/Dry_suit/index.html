Dry suit

A dry suit is a garment worn to protect the wearer from the environment, most commonly by divers and boaters working in cold water. Unlike a wetsuit, which admits a thin layer of water against the skin, a dry suit is waterproof and seals at the neck and wrists, keeping the wearer dry; thermal insulation is provided by the air trapped in the suit and by insulating undergarments worn beneath it.

Construction

Dry suits are made from materials such as crushed or compressed neoprene, trilaminate membranes, vulcanised rubber, or coated fabrics. Watertight seals (latex, silicone, or neoprene) at the neck and wrists, plus a waterproof zipper, prevent water entry. An inflation valve connected to the diver's air supply lets gas be added to the suit to counteract "suit squeeze" as pressure increases with depth, and an exhaust valve vents expanding gas during ascent.

Use

Dry suits are standard equipment for diving in water colder than about 15 °C, for long decompression dives, and for contaminated-water and commercial diving. Managing buoyancy in a dry suit is an additional skill: excess gas can migrate to the legs and cause an uncontrolled ascent if not vented. Dry suits are also worn by kayakers, sailors, rescue personnel, and military aviators (as immersion suits) for survival in cold water.

History

Early dry suits evolved from the rubberised standard diving dress of the 19th century. Modern sport dry suits developed from the 1950s onward, with neoprene suits popularised in the 1970s and lightweight membrane suits following later.
