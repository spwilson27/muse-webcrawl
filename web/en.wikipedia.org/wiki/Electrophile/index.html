Electrophile

An electrophile is a chemical species that forms a bond with a nucleophile by accepting an electron pair. The term derives from "electron-loving". Because electrophiles accept electrons, they are Lewis acids. Most electrophiles are positively charged, have an atom that carries a partial positive charge, or have an atom that does not have an octet of electrons.

Electrophilic attack

Electrophiles attack the region of a molecule with the highest electron density, such as the π system of alkenes and aromatic rings. In electrophilic aromatic substitution, typical electrophiles include the nitronium ion (NO2+) in nitration, bromine activated by a Lewis acid catalyst in bromination, and carbocations generated in Friedel–Crafts alkylation and acylation. In carbonyl chemistry, the carbonyl carbon is electrophilic and is attacked by nucleophiles; protonation or coordination to a Lewis acid increases this electrophilicity.

Electrophilicity scales

Reactivity of electrophiles has been quantified by several scales. Herbert Mayr's scale relates reaction rates of electrophile–nucleophile combinations through the equation log k = s(N + E), where E is an electrophilicity parameter and N and s describe the nucleophile. Hard and soft character (in Pearson's HSAB theory) also governs preferences: hard electrophiles such as protons and carbocations prefer hard nucleophiles, while soft electrophiles prefer soft, polarizable nucleophiles.

Examples

Common electrophiles include hydronium and protons generally, carbocations, carbonyl compounds, sulfur trioxide in sulfonation, halogens activated toward aromatic rings, Michael acceptors such as α,β-unsaturated carbonyls, and epoxides. Super-electrophiles—species of dramatically enhanced reactivity formed in superacid media—were characterized by George Olah.
