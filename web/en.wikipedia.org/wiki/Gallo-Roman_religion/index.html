Gallo-Roman religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gallo-Roman religion is the fusion of Roman religious practices with the traditions of the Celtic peoples of Gaul following the Roman conquest under Julius Caesar in the 1st century BC. Through the process known as interpretatio romana, native Gallic gods were identified with Roman counterparts, while many Celtic deities continued to be worshipped under combined or Latinized names in distinctive local cults.

Syncretism

Roman Mars, for example, absorbed the functions of numerous local war and healing gods, appearing as Mars Camulos or Mars Lenus, while the Celtic horse goddess Epona achieved empire-wide popularity without a Roman equivalent. The healer god Apollo Grannus, the sky god Taranis and the mother goddesses (Matres or Matronae) were venerated across Gaul. Sanctuaries often combined a Roman-style temple plan with a square Celtic shrine, the fanum, surrounded by a portico.

Practices and priesthood

The druids, suppressed under Tiberius and Claudius partly on the pretext of human sacrifice, disappeared as an organized order, but local priesthoods and the imperial cult flourished. Votive offerings, curse tablets, healing sanctuaries at sacred springs, and pilgrim festivals like that of Sequana at the source of the Seine were characteristic features. Elements of Gallo-Roman cult persisted into the Christian era, with some temples converted to churches and spring shrines rededicated to saints.
