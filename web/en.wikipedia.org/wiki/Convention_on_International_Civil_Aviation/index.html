The Convention on International Civil Aviation, commonly known as the Chicago Convention, is the foundational treaty of international air law. Signed on 7 December 1944 by 52 states at the Chicago Conference, it established the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), affirmed that every state has complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory, and set out the rules governing international air navigation, aircraft nationality and registration, and the standardisation of aviation practice. It entered into force on 4 April 1947 and has been ratified by 193 states, making it one of the most widely adhered-to treaties in existence.

Background and signing

Convened by the United States as the Second World War drew to a close, the International Civil Aviation Conference met in Chicago from 1 November to 7 December 1944. Delegates debated how far international air services should be liberalised: the United States favoured open competition, while the United Kingdom and others preferred regulated capacity. The Convention itself settled the legal framework but left commercial traffic rights largely to bilateral air service agreements; two companion instruments, the International Air Services Transit Agreement and the International Air Transport Agreement, dealt with the exchange of the so-called freedoms of the air, of which only the Transit Agreement (covering the first two freedoms) gained wide acceptance.

Key provisions

Article 1 recognises state sovereignty over national airspace. Article 5 grants non-scheduled flights the right of transit and non-traffic stops, while scheduled international services require the permission of the state overflown (Article 6). Cabotage may be reserved to national carriers under Article 7. Other articles require aircraft to bear nationality and registration marks, oblige states to recognise certificates of airworthiness and personnel licences issued by other contracting states that meet ICAO minimum standards (Article 33), and pledge states to collaborate in securing the highest practicable uniformity in regulations and practices (Article 37), the basis for the Standards and Recommended Practices contained in the Convention's 19 Annexes.

Annexes

The Annexes cover, among other subjects, personnel licensing (Annex 1), rules of the air (Annex 2), aeronautical charts and meteorology, airworthiness (Annex 8), air traffic services (Annex 11), aerodromes (Annex 14), accident investigation (Annex 13), aviation security (Annex 17) and environmental protection (Annex 16). They are adopted and amended by the ICAO Council by a two-thirds vote.

Amendments

The Convention has been amended several times, notably to enlarge the ICAO Council, to add Article 3 bis (1984) prohibiting the use of weapons against civil aircraft in flight — adopted after the Soviet destruction of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 — and to make the Russian, Arabic and Chinese texts equally authentic.
