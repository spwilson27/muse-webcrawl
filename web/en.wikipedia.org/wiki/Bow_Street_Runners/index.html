The Bow Street Runners were the first professional police force in London. Founded in 1749 by the novelist and magistrate Henry Fielding, they were attached to the Bow Street Magistrates' Court in Westminster and were officially known as police officers or "runners". Unlike the thief-takers who preceded them, the Runners were salaried and operated under the direction of the magistrates, although they also received rewards for successful captures.

History

Fielding, appointed a justice of the peace for Westminster in 1748, established a small band of constables operating from his office at No. 4 Bow Street. His half-brother John Fielding, who succeeded him as chief magistrate in 1754, expanded the force and secured government funding. John Fielding — known as the "Blind Beak" because he was blind and reputedly could recognise thousands of criminals by their voices — also founded the Police Gazette to circulate descriptions of wanted offenders.

The Runners initially numbered about half a dozen men and had no formal uniform, though they later carried a tipstaff and handcuffs and became recognisable by their red waistcoats, earning them the nickname "Robin Redbreasts". They travelled throughout the country in pursuit of criminals and were frequently hired by banks and other institutions.

Decline and legacy

The creation of the Metropolitan Police in 1829 under Sir Robert Peel gradually eclipsed the Runners. The Bow Street force continued as a small mounted and foot patrol before finally being disbanded in 1839, ten years after the "Peelers" took over general policing of the capital. The Runners nevertheless established key precedents: centralised records of criminals, coordinated detective work, and the principle of a publicly accountable police office.
