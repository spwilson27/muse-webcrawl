Common good

Public parks, clean air and public education are commonly cited examples of the common good.

In philosophy, economics, and political science, the common good (also: commonwealth, general welfare, or public benefit) refers to either what is shared and beneficial for all or most members of a given community, or alternatively, what is achieved by citizenship, collective action, and active participation in the realm of politics and public service.

History of the concept

The idea traces to the works of Plato, Aristotle, and Cicero. For Aristotle, the polis exists not merely to enable life but to enable the good life for its citizens. In medieval thought, Thomas Aquinas placed the common good at the centre of law and political authority, and it remains a foundational principle of Catholic social teaching. Later thinkers including Rousseau, with his notion of the general will, and modern communitarians developed the concept in different directions.

In economics

In economics, goods that are non-excludable and non-rivalrous or partially rivalrous are called common goods or common-pool resources. Elinor Ostrom's work showed that communities can devise institutions to govern such resources sustainably without either privatisation or central state control, a finding recognised by her 2009 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

Modern usage

The phrase is used in debates about public health, infrastructure, climate policy, and digital platforms, often contrasted with purely private or individual interests. Critics from liberal and libertarian traditions caution that appeals to the common good can justify excessive state power, while supporters argue that some goods can only be secured collectively.
