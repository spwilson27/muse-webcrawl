Prairie schooner

A prairie schooner is a type of covered wagon used by emigrants travelling west across the North American prairies in the nineteenth century, especially along the Oregon, California, and Mormon trails. The name came from the wagon's white canvas cover, which from a distance resembled the sails of a schooner ship crossing a sea of grass.

Design

Unlike the heavier Conestoga wagon used for freight in the eastern states, the prairie schooner was a lighter farm wagon adapted for the trail. The wooden bed was about 4 feet wide and 10–12 feet long, with sides roughly 2 feet deep, sometimes caulked to float across rivers. Over hoops of hickory or iron rose a double-thickness canvas bonnet waterproofed with linseed oil. The running gear had large rear wheels (about 5 feet in diameter) to roll over obstacles and reduce draft effort; a typical wagon could carry about 2,000–2,500 pounds of supplies and belongings.

Use on the trails

Wagons were usually drawn by oxen, valued for their strength, endurance, and lower cost, though mules and horses were also used. Most emigrants walked beside the wagon to spare the animals, riding only when ill or injured. A loaded schooner covered 15–20 miles on a good day; the journey from Missouri to Oregon or California took four to six months.

Legacy

The prairie schooner became an enduring symbol of American westward expansion and pioneer settlement, commemorated in monuments, state symbols (it is depicted on the flag and seal of Kansas' pioneer heritage), and countless museum exhibits; restored examples survive at trail interpretive centres such as Chimney Rock and Fort Kearny.
