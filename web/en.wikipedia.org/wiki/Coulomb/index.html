The coulomb (symbol: C) is the unit of electric charge in the International System of Units (SI). It is named after the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1736–1806), known for Coulomb's law. One coulomb is the charge transported by a constant current of one ampere in one second: 1 C = 1 A × 1 s.

Coulomb's torsion balance, used to measure electrical force.

Definition

Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI base units, the coulomb is defined by fixing the elementary charge e at exactly 1.602176634×10−19 C. One coulomb therefore equals approximately 6.241509074×1018 elementary charges — the charge of that many protons (or the negative of the charge of that many electrons).

Scale

The coulomb is a large unit for static electricity: charges built up by friction are typically microcoulombs or less. A lightning bolt may transfer on the order of 15 C. Battery capacity is often expressed in milliampere-hours; 1 mAh = 3.6 C, so a 3,000 mAh phone battery stores about 10,800 C of charge over its discharge.

Related units

The Faraday constant — the charge per mole of electrons — is about 96,485 C/mol. Historically, the coulomb was defined via the ampere and the force between current-carrying wires before being tied to the elementary charge.
