Chemical equilibrium is the state of a reversible reaction in which the forward and reverse reactions proceed at equal rates, so concentrations stay constant (though molecules keep reacting — a dynamic balance). It is reached in a closed system and described by the equilibrium constant, K.

Le Chatelier's principle (1884) predicts how equilibrium responds to disturbance: add a reactant, raise the pressure, or change the temperature, and the system shifts to partly oppose the change. Industry exploits this — the Haber process balances yield (favoured by high pressure and low temperature) against rate (needing heat and a catalyst).

[Image: A balance diagram showing forward and reverse reactions at equal rates]

Equilibrium constant

For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, K = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / [A]ᵉ[B]ᵊ. Large K means products dominate. K changes only with temperature; catalysts speed both directions equally and do not move the position of equilibrium.

Acid–base equilibria

Weak acids and bases sit in equilibrium with their ions, quantified by Ka and Kb and buffered in buffer solutions that resist pH change — vital in blood and in analytical chemistry.
