Albert-László Barabási (born 30 March 1967) is a Hungarian-American physicist and a founder of modern network science. He is best known for discovering, with Réka Albert, that many real-world networks are scale-free, and for proposing the growth-and-preferential-attachment mechanism that generates them.

Career

Born into an ethnic Hungarian family in Transylvania, Romania, Barabási studied physics in Bucharest and Budapest and earned his PhD at Boston University in 1994. After a postdoctoral period at IBM he joined the University of Notre Dame, and in 2007 moved to Northeastern University, where he directs the Center for Complex Network Research and holds appointments including at Harvard Medical School.

Scale-free networks

In their 1999 Science paper "Emergence of scaling in random networks", Barabási and Réka Albert showed that networks as diverse as the World Wide Web, metabolic systems and scientific citations share a power-law degree distribution, and introduced the Barabási–Albert model, in which networks grow by adding nodes that attach preferentially to already well-connected nodes — "the rich get richer".

Books

His popular book Linked: The New Science of Networks (2002) introduced network science to a general audience, followed by Bursts (2010), on patterns in human behaviour, Network Science (2016), a widely used textbook, and The Formula (2018), on the science of success.
