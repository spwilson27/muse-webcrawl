Export-oriented industrialization

Export-oriented industrialization (EOI), sometimes called export-led industrialisation, is a trade and industrial policy in which a country industrialises by producing goods primarily for export to world markets. It is contrasted with import substitution industrialization (ISI), which seeks to replace imports with domestic production behind protective barriers.

History and examples

The strategy is most famously associated with the East Asian "miracle" economies — South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, and Hong Kong — which from the 1960s achieved rapid growth by promoting manufactured exports, first in labour-intensive goods such as textiles and electronics assembly and later in steel, shipbuilding, automobiles, and semiconductors. Japan had earlier followed a similar path. Later adopters included Malaysia, Thailand, and coastal China after 1978, where special economic zones and foreign direct investment drove export growth.

Rationale and policies

Proponents argue that exporting forces firms to meet world standards of price and quality, earns foreign exchange, permits economies of scale beyond small domestic markets, and exploits comparative advantage. Typical policies include realistic (often undervalued) exchange rates, duty drawbacks for exporters, export subsidies and credit, and investment in infrastructure, education, and port facilities. Contrary to a purely laissez-faire reading, scholars such as Alice Amsden and Robert Wade emphasised that the East Asian states intervened heavily — directing credit, disciplining firms with export targets, and protecting infant industries selectively ("governing the market").

Criticism

Critics point to dependence on foreign demand, suppression of wages and labour rights, vulnerability to external shocks, and the difficulty of replicating the model when many countries pursue it simultaneously. The 1997 Asian financial crisis prompted reappraisal of some associated financial policies, though the export orientation itself largely persisted.
