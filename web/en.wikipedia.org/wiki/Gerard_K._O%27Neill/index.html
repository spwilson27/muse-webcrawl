Gerard Kitchen O'Neill (February 6, 1927 – April 27, 1992) was an American physicist and space activist. A professor at Princeton University, he invented the particle-storage ring used in high-energy physics experiments and later became famous for his studies of human settlements in space, popularised in his 1976 book The High Frontier: Human Colonies in Space.

Early career and physics work

O'Neill graduated from Swarthmore College in 1950 and earned his doctorate at Cornell University in 1954. At Princeton he led the development of storage rings that allowed particle beams to collide head-on, greatly increasing the energies available to experimenters.

Space colonisation

Beginning with a 1969 freshman physics seminar, O'Neill explored whether the surface of a planet was "really the right place" for an expanding technological civilisation. His designs centred on enormous rotating cylinders — "Island Three" colonies, later called O'Neill cylinders — up to 32 kilometres long, providing artificial gravity and Earth-like environments at the Earth–Moon Lagrange points. In 1977 he founded the Space Studies Institute to fund research into space manufacturing and resources.

Mass drivers and later work

O'Neill developed the mass driver, an electromagnetic catapult for launching payloads from the Moon, and co-founded companies working on satellite navigation and space transportation. His ideas helped inspire the L5 Society and a generation of space-colonisation advocates.
