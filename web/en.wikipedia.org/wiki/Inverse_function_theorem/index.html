Inverse function theorem

In mathematics, specifically differential calculus, the inverse function theorem gives a sufficient condition for a continuously differentiable function to have a local inverse: if the derivative (Jacobian) of the function at a point is invertible, then the function is invertible in a neighbourhood of that point, and its inverse is also continuously differentiable.

Statement

If f : ℝⁿ → ℝⁿ is continuously differentiable and det Jf(a) ≠ 0, then f is locally invertible near a.

More precisely, let f be a continuously differentiable map from an open subset U of ℝⁿ into ℝⁿ, and let a ∈ U. If the Jacobian matrix of f at a is invertible (equivalently, its determinant, the Jacobian determinant, is non-zero), then there exist open neighbourhoods V of a and W of f(a) such that f : V → W is a bijection with a continuously differentiable inverse. The derivative of the inverse at a point is the inverse matrix of the derivative of f there: D(f⁻¹)(f(a)) = [Df(a)]⁻¹.

Counterexample in the singular case

The condition is not vacuous: the function f(x) = x³ on ℝ is globally invertible, but its inverse, the cube root, is not differentiable at 0, where f′(0) = 0. A map may also fail to be locally invertible where the derivative vanishes, as with f(x) = x² near 0, which remains two-to-one on every neighbourhood of the origin.

Applications and generalizations

The theorem underpins the implicit function theorem, which can be derived from it and vice versa, and together they are foundational for the theory of manifolds: they justify local coordinates, the notion of a submersion and immersion, and the rank theorem. Versions of the theorem hold on Banach spaces and are a standard step in proving existence and uniqueness results for nonlinear equations; the Nash–Moser theorem is a far-reaching generalization for Fréchet spaces where the ordinary proof fails.
