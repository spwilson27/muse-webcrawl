Electronic amplifier

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An electronic amplifier is an electronic device that increases the power of a signal. It does so by taking energy from a power supply and controlling the output to match the shape of the input signal, but with a larger amplitude. In this sense an amplifier acts as a modulator of the power supply output.

Amplifiers are fundamental building blocks of electronic systems, used in audio equipment, radio transmitters and receivers, instrumentation, and virtually every other signal-processing application.

History

The first practical electronic amplifier was based on the vacuum tube triode, developed by Lee de Forest in the early 1900s from his Audion device. Tube amplifiers dominated electronics until the transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, allowed smaller, more efficient designs. Integrated-circuit operational amplifiers became widespread from the 1960s onward.

Types and classification

Amplifiers are commonly classified by their input and output quantities (voltage, current, transconductance, transresistance), by frequency range (audio, radio frequency, DC), and by the conduction angle of the active device in power amplifiers: classes A, B, AB, C, and switching classes such as D.

Properties

Important characteristics include gain, bandwidth, efficiency, linearity (and its complement, distortion), noise, slew rate, and stability. Feedback — feeding a portion of the output back to the input — is widely used to trade raw gain for improved linearity and bandwidth.
