Norwegian language

Norwegian (endonym: norsk) is a North Germanic language spoken mainly in Norway, where it is the official language, by around 5.3 million people. Together with Danish and Swedish it forms a dialect continuum of mutually intelligible Scandinavian languages; Norwegian descends from Old Norse, the language of the Vikings, and is written in the Latin alphabet with the additional letters æ, ø, and å.

Norway has two official written forms of Norwegian: Bokmål and Nynorsk

Two written standards

Unusually, Norwegian has two official written forms. Bokmål ("book language"), used by roughly 85–90% of the population, developed from the Danish written language used during the centuries of Danish rule, gradually Norwegianised. Nynorsk ("new Norwegian") was constructed in the mid-19th century by Ivar Aasen from rural dialects he considered least influenced by Danish. Every municipality and school district chooses a primary form; pupils learn both. There is no officially approved spoken standard—dialects enjoy high prestige and are used in broadcasting and public life.

History

Old Norse split into western and eastern branches in the Viking Age. After the Black Death and the union with Denmark (1380–1814), Danish became the language of administration and writing in Norway, while Norwegian dialects continued to be spoken. The 19th-century language conflict (språkstriden) between Danish-derived and dialect-based norms shaped modern Norwegian identity, and reforms through the 20th century repeatedly adjusted both standards.
