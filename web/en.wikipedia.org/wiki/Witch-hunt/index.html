A witch-hunt, or witch purge, is a search for people labelled as witches or evidence of witchcraft. The classical period of witch-hunts in early modern Europe and colonial America took place between about 1450 and 1750, peaking between 1560 and 1630, and resulted in tens of thousands of trials and executions. The term is now commonly used metaphorically for campaigns of persecution against a targeted group, often driven by moral panic.

Early modern hunts

The hunts combined popular belief in harmful magic (maleficium) with a developed demonology that portrayed witches as members of a satanic conspiracy. Heinrich Kramer's Malleus Maleficarum (1487) became the best-known witch-hunting manual. Trials were especially intense in the fragmented jurisdictions of the Holy Roman Empire, Switzerland, France, and Scotland. Accusations often followed local misfortunes such as crop failure, illness or sudden death, and confessions were frequently extracted by torture. Major episodes include the Trier, Würzburg and Bamberg trials in Germany and the Salem witch trials of 1692–93 in colonial Massachusetts.

Decline

Scepticism grew among jurists and theologians as the legal abuses of the trials became apparent, and centralizing states increasingly restrained local prosecutions. Executions ended across most of Europe during the late 17th and 18th centuries; the last commonly cited European execution was that of Anna Göldi in Switzerland in 1782.

Modern witch-hunts

Accusations of witchcraft continue to cause violence in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, India, Nepal and Papua New Guinea, where alleged witches — often elderly women — may be expelled, attacked or killed. In figurative usage, "witch-hunt" describes investigations perceived as persecutory, such as the anti-communist hearings associated with McCarthyism in the 1950s United States.
