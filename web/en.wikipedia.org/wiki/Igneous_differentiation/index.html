Igneous differentiation

Igneous differentiation, also called magmatic differentiation, is an umbrella term for the various processes by which magmas change their bulk chemical composition while cooling and crystallizing within the Earth's crust. Starting from a single parent magma, differentiation generates the great variety of igneous rock types — from basalt to rhyolite — and concentrates valuable elements into ore deposits. The concept was developed chiefly by Norman L. Bowen in the early twentieth century through his experiments on silicate melts and his formulation of the reaction series.

Fractional crystallization

The most important differentiation process is fractional crystallization: as a magma cools, the first minerals to crystallize (typically magnesium- and iron-rich olivine and pyroxene) are removed from the melt — by settling to the floor of the magma chamber, floating, or being left behind as the melt migrates — so the residual liquid becomes progressively enriched in silica, alkalis, and incompatible elements. Bowen's reaction series describes this orderly sequence: a discontinuous series from olivine through pyroxene, amphibole, and biotite, and a continuous series of plagioclase feldspar becoming ever richer in sodium. Layered intrusions such as the Skaergaard (Greenland), Bushveld (South Africa), and Stillwater (Montana) complexes preserve spectacular records of this crystal settling.

Other processes

  • Partial melting — the source-side counterpart: low-melting components melt first, so a partial melt differs in composition from its parent rock.
  • Magma mixing — two magmas of different composition blend, producing hybrids and distinctive textures such as banded pumice.
  • Assimilation — magma melts and incorporates surrounding country rock, shifting its chemistry; combined with fractional crystallization this is the AFC process.
  • Liquid immiscibility — a single melt separates into two coexisting liquids, for example a sulfide melt that scavenges nickel, copper, and platinum-group elements from silicate magma, as at Norilsk and Sudbury.
  • Volatile transfer and filter pressing — gas streaming and the squeezing of residual melt from a crystal mush further modify compositions.

Significance

Differentiated magmas produce the contrast between mafic and felsic igneous suites, the zoning observed in volcanic provinces, and many of the world's great ore deposits — chromite and platinum in layered intrusions, tin and rare elements in evolved granites and pegmatites, and porphyry copper systems. Trace-element ratios and isotope systems let geochemists reconstruct differentiation histories and identify the parent magmas of volcanic fields.
