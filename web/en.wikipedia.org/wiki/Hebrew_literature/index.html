Hebrew literature

Hebrew literature consists of ancient, medieval, and modern writings in the Hebrew language. It is one of the primary forms of Jewish literature, though there have been cases of literature written in Hebrew by non-Jews. Hebrew literature was produced in many different parts of the world throughout the medieval and modern eras, while contemporary Hebrew literature is largely Israeli literature.

Hebrew literary tradition extends from the biblical canon to modern Israeli writing.

Ancient period

The foundational work of Hebrew literature is the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh), composed over centuries and including the Torah, the Prophets (Nevi'im) and the Writings (Ketuvim). Poetry such as the Psalms and the Song of Songs, wisdom literature such as Proverbs and Job, and historical narratives form the core of the ancient corpus. The Dead Sea Scrolls and apocryphal works expand the picture of Second Temple-era writing.

Rabbinic literature

Following the biblical period, rabbinic literature in Hebrew and Aramaic — the Mishnah (c. 200 CE), the Talmud, and midrashic collections — dominated Jewish writing for over a millennium. Medieval Hebrew literature flourished especially in Islamic Spain, where poets such as Solomon ibn Gabirol and Judah Halevi wrote secular and liturgical poetry of lasting influence, while Maimonides wrote his Mishneh Torah in Hebrew (his philosophical Guide for the Perplexed was written in Judeo-Arabic).

Haskalah and revival

The Jewish Enlightenment (Haskalah) of the 18th and 19th centuries produced modern Hebrew prose and periodicals in Europe. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, figures such as Eliezer Ben-Yehuda promoted the revival of Hebrew as a spoken language, and poets like Hayim Nahman Bialik and Shaul Tchernichovsky created a modern Hebrew poetic idiom.

Modern Israeli literature

After the founding of the State of Israel in 1948, Hebrew literature developed a distinct Israeli character. Shmuel Yosef Agnon received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1966. Major modern and contemporary authors include A. B. Yehoshua, Amos Oz, David Grossman, Meir Shalev, Etgar Keret and poet Yehuda Amichai. Modern Hebrew writing explores war, immigration, religious-secular tensions, and everyday Israeli life, and is widely translated.
