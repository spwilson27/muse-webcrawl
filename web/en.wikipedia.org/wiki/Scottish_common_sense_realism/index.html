Scottish common sense realism

Scottish common sense realism, also known as the Scottish school of common sense, is a school of philosophy that originated in the ideas of the Scottish Enlightenment thinkers Thomas Reid, Adam Ferguson, James Beattie, and Dugald Stewart during the 18th century. It arose as a response to the skepticism of David Hume and the idealism of George Berkeley.

Core ideas

Against the "theory of ideas" — the claim that the mind directly perceives only its own ideas — Reid argued for direct realism: perception puts us in immediate contact with external objects, and certain first principles (the existence of the external world, the reliability of our faculties, the uniformity of nature) are irresistibly believed by all people and cannot coherently be doubted.

Influence

Common-sense philosophy dominated Scottish universities and was exported widely, especially to the United States, where it shaped teaching at Princeton and other colleges through figures such as John Witherspoon and James McCosh, and influenced later thinkers including G. E. Moore and, in its method of appealing to ordinary belief, aspects of ordinary-language philosophy.
