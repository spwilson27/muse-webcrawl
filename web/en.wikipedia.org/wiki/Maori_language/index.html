The Māori language (Māori: te reo Māori, literally "the language"; commonly shortened to te reo) is an Eastern Polynesian language, the indigenous language of the Māori people of mainland New Zealand. Closely related to Cook Islands Māori, Tuamotuan and Tahitian, it arrived with Polynesian voyagers who settled New Zealand around the late thirteenth century. Alongside English and New Zealand Sign Language, it is one of the country's three official languages, a status recognised by the Māori Language Act 1987.

History

Before European contact, Māori was an oral language with a rich tradition of oratory (whaikōrero), song (waiata) and genealogical recitation (whakapapa). Missionaries devised a Latin-based orthography in the early nineteenth century, and Māori-language literacy and newspapers flourished; a Māori version of the Treaty of Waitangi was signed in 1840. Through the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries, however, colonisation, urbanisation and schooling policies that discouraged (and in practice often punished) the use of Māori in schools drove a steep decline: by the 1970s fewer than a fifth of Māori children could speak the language, and its survival appeared threatened.

Revitalisation

A major revitalisation movement began in the 1980s. The kōhanga reo ("language nest") movement, launched in 1982, immersed pre-school children in Māori; it was followed by Māori-medium primary and secondary schools (kura kaupapa Māori) and three Māori universities (wānanga). The Māori Language Act 1987 made the language official and established the Māori Language Commission, Te Taura Whiri i te Reo Māori. Māori Television (now Whakaata Māori) began broadcasting in 2004, and Māori Language Week (Te Wiki o te Reo Māori) and the government's Maihi Karauna strategy aim to have one million people speaking basic Māori by 2040. Census figures show a rising proportion of young speakers, though the language remains classed as endangered.

Phonology and grammar

Māori has a small phoneme inventory: five vowels (each short or long, with length marked by a macron) and ten consonants — p, t, k, m, n, ŋ (written ng), ɸ (wh), ɾ (r), h and w. Syllables are open, with no consonant clusters. Grammar is analytic, with verb–subject–object word order, particles marking tense and aspect, and possessive systems distinguishing alienable (a) from inalienable (o) possession. The language has contributed many loanwords to New Zealand English, including kiwi, haka, whānau (family), mana and Aotearoa, the Māori name for New Zealand.
