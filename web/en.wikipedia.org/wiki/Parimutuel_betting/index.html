Parimutuel betting is a betting system in which all bets of a particular type are placed together in a pool; taxes and the "house take" are deducted, and the payoff odds are calculated by sharing the pool (the parimutuel, French for "mutual stake") among all winning bets. Unlike fixed-odds bookmaking, the operator takes no risk on the outcome: bettors effectively bet against one another, and final odds are not known until betting closes.

History

The system was devised in Paris around the 1860s by Catalan entrepreneur Pierre Oller, whose pari mutuel replaced bookmakers at French racecourses and was legalised in France in 1891 after lobbying by the racing industry. New Zealander George Julius invented the automatic totalisator in 1913 — the first was installed at Ellerslie Racecourse, Auckland — mechanising the continuous recalculation of odds displayed on the tote board.

How it works

Suppose a win pool holds 100,000 units after deductions, of which 30,000 were staked on the winning horse. Each 1-unit winning ticket then returns 100,000 ÷ 30,000 ≈ 3.33 units, i.e. odds of about 7/3 plus the stake back. Because money continues to enter the pool until the off, displayed odds are provisional and fluctuate — heavily backed horses "shorten", while unfancied ones "drift". Takeout rates vary by jurisdiction and bet type, commonly 15–25% for exotic wagers (exactas, trifectas, Pick 6 pools), which split pools into place, show and multi-horse combinations.

Uses and variations

Parimutuel wagering is the legal standard for horse and greyhound racing in the United States, France (PMU), Japan (where racing pools are among the largest in the world), Australia and Hong Kong (the Hong Kong Jockey Club runs one of the biggest tote operations anywhere). The same pooling mathematics underlies many state lotteries' prize division and some prediction-market designs. Bettors value the system's transparency — payouts derive from the pool itself — while its drawback is that a skilful bettor's winnings shrink as other winning tickets share the pool.
