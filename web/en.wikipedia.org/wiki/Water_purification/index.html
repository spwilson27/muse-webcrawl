Water purification

PurposeRemove undesirable chemicals, biological contaminants and suspended solids from water
Main stagesCoagulation, flocculation, sedimentation, filtration, disinfection
Common disinfectantsChlorine, chloramine, ozone, ultraviolet light

Water purification is the process of removing undesirable chemicals, biological contaminants, suspended solids, and gases from water. The goal is to produce water fit for specific purposes. Most water is purified and disinfected for human consumption (drinking water), but purification is also carried out for medical, pharmacological, chemical, and industrial applications.

Sources and quality

Raw water for public supplies is drawn from surface sources (rivers, lakes, reservoirs) or from groundwater aquifers. Groundwater is usually lower in suspended matter and pathogens but may contain dissolved minerals, iron, manganese or naturally occurring contaminants such as arsenic and fluoride. Water quality is regulated by standards such as the U.S. Safe Drinking Water Act and the European Union's Drinking Water Directive, which set maximum levels for dozens of contaminants.

Treatment processes

Conventional treatment of surface water typically proceeds through:

  • Screening and pre-treatment to remove debris.
  • Coagulation and flocculation — chemicals such as alum are added to destabilise colloidal particles, which are then gently mixed so they aggregate into flocs.
  • Sedimentation in clarifiers, where flocs settle out by gravity.
  • Filtration through sand, anthracite or membrane filters to remove remaining particles and many microorganisms.
  • Disinfection — most commonly chlorination, which also leaves a protective residual in the distribution network; ozone and ultraviolet light are alternatives that do not provide a residual.

Additional processes include aeration, softening (removal of calcium and magnesium), activated-carbon adsorption for taste and organic compounds, and, for seawater or brackish sources, desalination by reverse osmosis or distillation.

Point-of-use and portable methods

Household and portable purification ranges from boiling and cloth filtration to ceramic and hollow-fibre filters, chemical disinfection tablets, and solar disinfection (SODIS). These methods are important in emergencies and in regions without piped treated supplies.

History

Ancient Sanskrit and Greek writings recommended boiling and filtering water through sand and charcoal. Modern purification began with John Snow's epidemiological work on cholera in the 1850s, the introduction of slow sand filters, and the adoption of municipal chlorination in the early twentieth century, which produced dramatic declines in typhoid and other waterborne diseases.
