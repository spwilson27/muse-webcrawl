The 1953 Worcester tornado was an extremely powerful F4 tornado that struck the city of Worcester, Massachusetts, and surrounding towns on the afternoon of Tuesday, June 9, 1953. It killed 94 people and injured nearly 1,300, making it the deadliest tornado in New England history and, at the time, one of the costliest in United States history.

Meteorological history

The tornado was part of the Flint–Worcester tornado outbreak sequence of June 1953, the same storm system that had produced the deadly Flint, Michigan tornado the previous evening. The Worcester storm touched down near Petersham around 4:25 p.m., tracked east-southeast through Barre, Rutland, Holden, and into Worcester, where it reached peak width—up to a mile wide—and devastated the northern neighborhoods of the city, including the Assumption College campus and the Burncoat and Great Brook Valley areas. It continued through Shrewsbury, Westborough, and Southborough before dissipating near 6:04 p.m. after a 46-mile path.

Damage and casualties

More than 4,000 buildings were damaged or destroyed and some 10,000 people left homeless. Debris from Worcester was carried as far as the Atlantic coast; a frozen mattress was famously found in Massachusetts Bay. The 94 deaths made it the 21st deadliest tornado in U.S. history as of its occurrence. Damage was estimated at $52 million (1953 USD).

Aftermath

The disaster, striking a region that believed itself essentially immune to tornadoes, helped spur investment in severe-weather forecasting and spotter networks in New England. Annual memorials are held in Worcester, and the event remains the benchmark tornado disaster for the northeastern United States.
