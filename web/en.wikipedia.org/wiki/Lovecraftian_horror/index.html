Lovecraftian horror, also known as cosmic horror, is a subgenre of horror fiction and weird fiction that emphasises the horror of the unknowable and incomprehensible over gore or shock. Named after American author H. P. Lovecraft, it stresses humanity's insignificance in a vast, indifferent cosmos inhabited by ancient, godlike beings.

Themes

Central themes include cosmic indifferentism — the idea that the universe neither knows nor cares about humankind; forbidden knowledge that drives seekers insane; non-human entities (such as Cthulhu and the other Great Old Ones) so alien that merely perceiving them shatters the mind; and the fragility of sanity and civilisation. Lovecraft's shared fictional universe, later dubbed the Cthulhu Mythos by August Derleth, provides the setting for many such works.

History and influence

Lovecraft developed the mode in stories published mainly in Weird Tales in the 1920s and 1930s, including "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928), At the Mountains of Madness (1936) and "The Shadow over Innsmouth" (1936). Influences on him included Edgar Allan Poe, Lord Dunsany, Arthur Machen and Algernon Blackwood. His circle of correspondents and imitators expanded the Mythos, and the subgenre later shaped writers such as Stephen King and Thomas Ligotti, films, role-playing games (Call of Cthulhu) and video games.
