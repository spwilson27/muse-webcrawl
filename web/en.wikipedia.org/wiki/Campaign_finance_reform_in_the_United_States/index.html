Campaign finance reform in the United States

Campaign finance reform in the United States is the political effort to change the involvement of money in politics, primarily in political campaigns. Although attempts to regulate campaign finance by legislation date back to 1867, the modern era of "campaign finance reform" in the United States begins with the development of the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) and its amendments in the 1970s. In addition to the FECA, the Federal Election Commission (FEC) was established to oversee federal campaign finance regulations.

Reformers argue that large donations and independent expenditures from wealthy individuals, corporations, and labor unions distort democracy, while opponents argue that restrictions on political spending burden the fundamental right to free speech protected by the First Amendment. The tension between these positions has shaped a series of landmark Supreme Court decisions, including Buckley v. Valeo (1976), McConnell v. FEC (2003), and Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission (2010).

History

The first attempts at federal campaign finance legislation came during the presidency of Theodore Roosevelt, who called for a ban on corporate contributions in 1905. Congress responded with the Tillman Act of 1907, which prohibited monetary contributions to national political campaigns by corporations and nationally chartered banks. The Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1910 and subsequent amendments introduced disclosure requirements and spending limits for House and Senate campaigns, though these rules were weakly enforced.

The Watergate scandal of the early 1970s exposed widespread abuses in the financing of the 1972 presidential campaign and prompted the most comprehensive reforms of the modern era. Congress passed amendments to the Federal Election Campaign Act in 1974 that imposed limits on contributions and expenditures, established public financing for presidential elections, and created the Federal Election Commission to administer and civilly enforce the law.

Key legislation

  • Tillman Act (1907) – banned corporate contributions to federal candidates.
  • Federal Election Campaign Act (1971, amended 1974) – established contribution limits, disclosure rules, and public financing of presidential elections.
  • Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act (2002), commonly known as McCain–Feingold – banned "soft money" contributions to national political parties and regulated issue advocacy advertisements.

Major court cases

In Buckley v. Valeo (1976), the Supreme Court upheld limits on contributions to candidates but struck down limits on expenditures, reasoning that spending money for political communication is a form of speech. In Citizens United v. FEC (2010), the Court held that corporations and unions may spend unlimited amounts on independent political advocacy, giving rise to so-called super PACs. Later decisions, including McCutcheon v. FEC (2014), further loosened aggregate contribution limits.

Current debates

Contemporary reform proposals include broader disclosure of "dark money" spent by nonprofit organizations, small-donor matching systems, constitutional amendments to overturn Citizens United, and changes to the composition and powers of the Federal Election Commission. The impact of money on elections remains one of the most contested issues in American politics.
