Library Genesis (also known as LibGen) is a file-sharing based shadow library website that provides free access to copyrighted books, academic journal articles, comics, magazines and other material that is ordinarily behind paywalls or not digitally available. It is one of the largest such libraries, holding several million books and tens of millions of research articles, and is widely used by students and researchers around the world.

History

Library Genesis grew out of Russian online book-collecting communities of the 2000s, in which scanned textbooks and scientific literature circulated on forums and file archives; it was established around 2008 by a group of Russian scientists and programmers who consolidated these collections into a single searchable catalogue. From early on, its holdings leaned heavily towards scientific, technical and academic literature, especially the expensive textbooks and reference works of Western publishers. In 2011 it absorbed and began indexing the article collection that also feeds Sci-Hub, the related shadow library founded by Alexandra Elbakyan, and the two projects have remained closely associated.

Operation

The site is maintained as a bibliographic catalogue: each record describes an edition of a book (author, title, publisher, year, identifiers and file hash) and links to copies of the file held on mirror servers and, later, distributed storage. Because its primary domains have repeatedly been seized or blocked, Library Genesis is reached through a shifting set of mirror domains, of which the gen.lib.rus.ec address reproduced in this crawl is one of the longest-lived. Uploading is open to registered users, and the catalogue's metadata database dumps are themselves shared, which has allowed the collection to be reconstituted whenever individual sites disappear.

Legal status and opposition

Publishers regard Library Genesis as a major source of copyright infringement and have pursued it in several jurisdictions. In 2015 the publisher Elsevier obtained a default judgment against it and Sci-Hub in a United States federal court, and several of its domains were suspended; courts in the United Kingdom, France and other countries have ordered internet providers to block its domains. The site has nevertheless remained available through mirrors. Its defenders argue that it provides essential access to knowledge for researchers in poorer countries and institutions; its critics, including major academic publishers, argue that it undermines the economics of scholarly publishing.
