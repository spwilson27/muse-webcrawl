Heat pipe

A heat pipe is a heat-transfer device that employs phase transition to transfer heat between two solid interfaces. At the hot interface of a heat pipe, a volatile liquid in contact with a thermally conductive solid surface turns into a vapor by absorbing heat from that surface. The vapor then travels along the heat pipe to the cold interface and condenses back into a liquid, releasing the latent heat. The liquid then returns to the hot interface through either capillary action, centrifugal force, or gravity, and the cycle repeats.

A heat pipe consists of a sealed tube, a wick structure and a working fluid.

Structure and design

A typical heat pipe consists of a sealed pipe or tube made of a material that is compatible with the working fluid, such as copper for water heat pipes, or aluminium for ammonia heat pipes. A vacuum pump is used to remove the air from the empty pipe, and the pipe is partially filled with a working fluid and then sealed. The working fluid mass is chosen so that the heat pipe contains both vapor and liquid over the operating temperature range.

A vacuum is necessary inside the pipe to allow the working fluid to evaporate and condense at the desired temperature. Different fluids are used depending on the temperature range: helium or hydrogen for cryogenic applications, water for intermediate temperatures, and liquid metals such as sodium or lithium for high-temperature use.

Wick structures

Heat pipes employ a wick structure to return the liquid from the condenser to the evaporator. Common wick types include sintered metal powder, screen mesh, and axial grooves. Grooved wick designs have low capillary pressure but work well in gravity-assisted orientations, while sintered powder wicks generate high capillary pressure.

History

The general principle of heat pipes using gravity-driven two-phase systems was patented by Angier Perkins and his son Loftus Perkins as the "Perkins tube" in the 19th century. The modern heat pipe was proposed by Richard S. Gaugler of General Motors in 1942 and patented in 1944. George M. Grover at Los Alamos National Laboratory independently developed the concept in 1963, using sodium as the working fluid, and coined the term "heat pipe" in his 1964 journal paper.

Applications

  • Electronics cooling: Heat pipes conduct heat away from CPUs and GPUs to finned heat sinks, and are ubiquitous in laptop computers.
  • Spacecraft: Heat pipes equalize temperatures on satellites and carry heat from sun-facing surfaces to radiators.
  • Permafrost preservation: In Alaska, heat pipes are used in the supports of the Trans-Alaska Pipeline to keep the surrounding permafrost frozen.
  • Solar heating: Evacuated-tube solar collectors often use heat pipes to transfer heat to a manifold.
  • Nuclear power: High-temperature heat pipes transfer heat out of reactor cores, including proposed microreactor designs.

Limitations

Heat pipes have effective thermal conductivities far beyond any solid material, but they are subject to several limits: the capillary limit, the sonic limit, the entrainment limit, the boiling limit, and the viscous limit. When the capillary pressure is insufficient to return enough condensed fluid to the evaporator, the evaporator dries out and overheats.
