Storming of the Winter Palace
Date7–8 November 1917 (25–26 October O.S.)
LocationWinter Palace, Petrograd, Russia
Part ofOctober Revolution
ResultArrest of the Provisional Government; Bolshevik takeover

The storming of the Winter Palace was a pivotal event of the October Revolution of 1917, when Bolshevik Red Guards, soldiers, and sailors seized the Winter Palace in Petrograd, the seat of the Russian Provisional Government, on the night of 7–8 November 1917 (25–26 October in the Julian calendar).

Events

The signal for the assault is traditionally said to have been a blank shot fired by the cruiser Aurora, anchored in the Neva River. In reality, resistance was light: the palace was defended by Cossack units, a women's battalion, and military cadets, many of whom had already departed or surrendered. The ministers of the Provisional Government (except Prime Minister Alexander Kerensky, who had fled) were arrested in the small dining room and taken to the Peter and Paul Fortress. Casualties were few — a handful killed — in contrast to the dramatic mass assault depicted in later Soviet propaganda.

Myth and commemoration

The storming was greatly dramatised in Sergei Eisenstein's 1928 film October: Ten Days That Shook the World, and in a 1920 mass spectacle re-enactment involving thousands of performers. These versions, showing vast crowds overwhelming the palace, shaped popular memory far more than the relatively low-key actual event.
