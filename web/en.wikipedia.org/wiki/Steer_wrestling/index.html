Steer wrestling

The steer wrestler leaves his horse at full gallop to seize the steer's horns.

Steer wrestling, also known as bulldogging, is a rodeo event in which a horse-mounted rider chases a steer, drops from the horse onto the steer, and then wrestles the steer to the ground by twisting its horns. It is one of the standard timed events of professional rodeo and is regarded as among the most physically dangerous.

The event

The steer is given a head start out of a chute, with a barrier ensuring fairness. The wrestler, riding on one side of the steer, is assisted by a second rider called a hazer, who rides on the other side to keep the steer running straight. As the wrestler draws alongside, he slides from his horse, hooks an arm around the steer's horns, digs his heels into the dirt to slow the animal, and throws it onto its side; the clock stops when the steer is flat on its back or side with all four legs extended. Winning times at major rodeos are often under four seconds.

Origins

The technique is credited to Bill Pickett, an African-American cowboy from Texas who, in the early 1900s, developed and exhibitionised the feat of leaping onto a steer and bringing it down — according to legend originally by biting its upper lip, as cattle dogs do. Pickett's performances with the 101 Ranch Wild West Show made "bulldogging" famous, and the event became a rodeo staple. Steers used today typically weigh between 450 and 700 pounds.

Animal welfare

Like other rodeo events, steer wrestling has been criticised by animal rights organisations, which argue it risks injury to the steer's neck and legs; rodeo associations respond with stock-handling rules, weight requirements, and veterinary oversight.
