The Louis XVI style is a style of architecture, furniture, decoration and art developed in France during the 19-year reign of Louis XVI (1774–1792), just before the French Revolution. It represents the final phase of French Baroque-derived classicism and the polished peak of early Neoclassicism in France, following the exuberant Rococo of the Louis XV style.

Characteristics

Where Rococo favoured asymmetry and shells and scrolls, the Louis XVI style embraced straight lines, symmetry and ornament drawn from classical antiquity: Greek key patterns, laurel wreaths, urns, swags of flowers, ribbons, medallions and fluted columns. Archaeological discoveries at Pompeii and Herculaneum, and the influence of architects such as Ange-Jacques Gabriel (whose Petit Trianon, completed in 1768, anticipated the style), drove the taste for a purer, more severe antiquity — the so-called goût à la grecque.

Furniture

Furniture in the style is characterised by straight, tapered and often fluted legs, light construction, mahogany and satinwood veneers, delicate marquetry, and mounts in gilded bronze. Leading ébénistes (cabinetmakers) included Jean-Henri Riesener, David Roentgen, Martin Carlin and Georges Jacob, the last famed for his carved chairs. The Queen's boudoir at Fontainebleau and the apartments of Marie Antoinette at Versailles are exemplary interiors.

Legacy

The Revolution abruptly ended royal patronage, but the style passed into the Directoire and Empire styles of the following decades, and was repeatedly revived in the 19th and 20th centuries as a symbol of French refinement in the decorative arts.
