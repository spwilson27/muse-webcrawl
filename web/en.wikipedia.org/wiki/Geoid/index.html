Overview

The geoid is the surface of equal gravitational potential that coincides with mean sea level, imagined extended continuously beneath the continents. It is the surface water 'feels': a canal cut along the geoid would have no current, whatever its geometric height. Heights on maps — elevations above sea level — are measured from the geoid, not from the smooth mathematical ellipsoid used for latitude and longitude.

Because Earth's interior is uneven, the geoid undulates, rising and dipping by about a hundred metres relative to the ellipsoid — a gentle landscape of gravity mapped in recent decades by dedicated satellites, and famously pictured as a lumpy potato. Geodesy converts between geoid heights (used by engineers and flood planners) and the ellipsoidal heights given natively by GPS.
