Hero's journey

The hero's journey, also known as the monomyth, is a common template of stories that involve a hero who goes on an adventure, is victorious in a decisive crisis, and comes home changed or transformed. The concept was introduced by the American mythologist Joseph Campbell in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), which drew on earlier work in comparative mythology, ritual studies, and the psychology of Carl Jung.

The hero's journey as a cycle of departure, initiation and return

Campbell's structure

Campbell summarized the monomyth as follows: "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man." He divided the journey into three acts — Departure, Initiation, and Return — subdivided into seventeen stages, including the Call to Adventure, Refusal of the Call, Supernatural Aid, the Crossing of the First Threshold, the Belly of the Whale, the Road of Trials, the Meeting with the Goddess, Atonement with the Father, Apotheosis, the Ultimate Boon, Refusal of the Return, the Magic Flight, Rescue from Without, the Crossing of the Return Threshold, Master of the Two Worlds, and Freedom to Live.

Background and influences

Campbell drew on the anthropologist Adolf Bastian's idea of universal "elementary ideas" in myth, on Otto Rank's and Lord Raglan's analyses of hero myths, on Arnold van Gennep's three-part structure of rites of passage (separation, initiation, return), and on James Joyce, from whose novel Finnegans Wake he borrowed the word "monomyth". Jungian archetypes and Freud's writings also shaped his reading of myth as a symbolic expression of the psyche's development.

Influence

The hero's journey has been hugely influential in popular storytelling. George Lucas credited Campbell's work as an inspiration for Star Wars, and screenwriter Christopher Vogler condensed the model into a twelve-stage structure in The Writer's Journey (1992), which became a standard reference in Hollywood and in creative-writing instruction. The template has since been applied to video games, advertising, biography and memoir, and self-help literature.

Criticism

Scholars of folklore and mythology have criticized the monomyth as overly reductive: Campbell has been accused of selecting and distorting examples to fit his scheme, of flattening the cultural specificity of the myths he compared, and of reflecting a masculine, individualist bias. Folklorists such as Alan Dundes and Barre Toelken argued that many narratives — including trickster tales, women's stories and non-Western traditions — do not conform to the pattern, and that the theory's popularity owes more to its usefulness to writers than to its descriptive accuracy.
