Counting rods

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Counting rods (Chinese: 筹; pinyin: chóu) are small bars, typically of bamboo, ivory, or iron, that were used by mathematicians in ancient China and neighbouring East Asian cultures for calculation. In use from at least the Warring States period (5th century BC) until they were gradually displaced by the abacus around the 16th century, counting rods formed one of the earliest decimal place-value counting systems.

Representation

Discharged digits were formed from patterns of rods: vertical rods for units, with orientation alternating between vertical and horizontal by decimal place to avoid ambiguity, and an empty space representing zero. Red rods represented positive numbers and black rods negative numbers (or vice versa), allowing sophisticated arithmetic — including fractions, extraction of roots, and the solution of polynomial equations — to be carried out on a chequered counting board.

Influence

The rod numerals underlie the Suzhou numerals later used in Chinese markets, and a related system was used in Japan and Korea.
