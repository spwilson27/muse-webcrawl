Crimes against humanity

Crimes against humanity are certain serious offences committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against a civilian population, with knowledge of the attack, pursuant to or in furtherance of a state or organisational policy. Unlike war crimes, they may be committed in peacetime; unlike genocide, they do not require intent to destroy a protected group as such.

Acts included

Article 7 of the Rome Statute lists murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation or forcible transfer of population, imprisonment in violation of fundamental rules of international law, torture, rape and other sexual violence, persecution against an identifiable group, enforced disappearance, apartheid, and other inhumane acts of a similar character intentionally causing great suffering or serious injury.

History

The phrase was used politically as early as 1915, when Britain, France, and Russia denounced the Ottoman massacres of Armenians as "crimes against humanity and civilisation". It entered law in 1945 in the Nuremberg Charter, which tied the offences to the war; the nexus requirement was dropped by later instruments, including the statutes of the tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda and the Rome Statute. Compulsory sterilization and other eugenic abuses featured in the subsequent Nuremberg proceedings (the RuSHA case).

Prosecution

Crimes against humanity have been prosecuted by the ICTY, the ICTR, hybrid courts in Cambodia and Sierra Leone, and the International Criminal Court, and under universal jurisdiction in national courts (for example, German trials of Syrian officials in Koblenz, 2021–22). No single comprehensive treaty on crimes against humanity yet exists; the UN International Law Commission adopted draft articles in 2019, and negotiations toward a convention have been under discussion in the General Assembly.
