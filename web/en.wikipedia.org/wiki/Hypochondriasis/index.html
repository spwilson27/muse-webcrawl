Hypochondriasis

Hypochondriasis, also known as hypochondria, is a condition in which a person is excessively and unduly worried about having a serious illness. The preoccupation persists despite appropriate medical evaluation and reassurance, and it causes significant distress or impairment. In DSM-5, most people formerly diagnosed with hypochondriasis are classified under somatic symptom disorder or illness anxiety disorder.

Hypochondriasis has been described since antiquity; the term derives from the hypochondrium, once thought to be its seat.

Signs and symptoms

Sufferers misinterpret normal bodily sensations as evidence of serious disease, repeatedly check their bodies, seek reassurance from doctors or the internet (a pattern sometimes called cyberchondria), or conversely avoid medical care for fear of bad news. The worry typically shifts from one feared disease to another over time, and comorbid anxiety and depression are common.

Treatment

Cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for health anxiety — targeting catastrophic misinterpretation, checking and reassurance-seeking — is the best-supported treatment. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors can also help, particularly where anxiety or depression is prominent. In primary care, a consistent, scheduled approach with one physician and limited unnecessary investigations reduces iatrogenic harm.

History

The term comes from the Greek hypochondrion, the region below the ribs, believed in humoral medicine to be the source of melancholy vapours. Hypochondriasis was long classed with hysteria and melancholia before becoming a distinct psychiatric diagnosis in the twentieth century.
