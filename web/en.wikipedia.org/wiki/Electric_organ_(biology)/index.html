Electric organ (biology)

Stacks of electrocytes in the electric organ of a ray.

An electric organ is a tissue found in electric fish, composed of modified muscle or nerve cells, that generates an electric field. Electric organs evolved independently in at least six different groups of fish, and are used for electrolocation, communication, navigation, defense and, in strongly electric species, predation.

Structure

The organ is made up of stacks of disc-shaped cells called electrocytes (or electroplaques), aligned in columns. Each electrocyte is innervated on one face only. When the fish triggers the organ, neurotransmitter release causes one face of each cell to depolarize, producing a small voltage (about 0.1–0.15 V) per cell; thousands of cells in series sum to produce discharges of tens to hundreds of volts, and parallel columns increase the available current — an arrangement analogous to a voltaic pile.

Types of discharge

Weakly electric fish (such as gymnotiform knifefishes and mormyrids) generate low-voltage fields continuously or in pulses for active electrolocation and social communication. Strongly electric fish — including the electric eel, electric catfish, torpedo rays and stargazers — produce brief high-voltage discharges used to stun prey or deter predators. Many species possess multiple organs: the electric eel, for example, has a main organ and Hunter's organ for strong discharge, and Sachs' organ for weak electrolocation pulses.

Evolution

Electric organs are derived in most groups from skeletal muscle that has lost the ability to contract; in one family (Apteronotidae) the organ is instead derived from nerve cells. Molecular studies show that a sodium-channel gene duplication and subsequent specialization of one copy for expression in the electric organ underlies the convergent evolution of these structures in teleost fishes.
