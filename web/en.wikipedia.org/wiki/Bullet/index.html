Bullet

A bullet is a kinetic projectile, a component of firearm ammunition that is shot from a gun barrel. It is made of a variety of materials, typically lead with a copper jacket, and is propelled by the rapid expansion of gases from a burning propellant in the cartridge. The bullet itself does not contain explosives (unlike a shell); its destructive effect comes from its kinetic energy and penetration of the target.

Components and terminology

In common usage, "bullet" is often used loosely for a complete cartridge, but technically a cartridge (round) consists of four parts: the bullet (projectile), the case (usually brass), the propellant (gunpowder), and the primer that ignites it. Bullet sizes are expressed by calibre — the approximate diameter of the bullet or bore, in inches or millimetres (e.g. 9 mm, .45 ACP, 5.56×45 mm NATO).

Designs

  • Full metal jacket (FMJ) — a soft core completely encased (except the base) in a harder metal jacket; standard for military use.
  • Hollow point — a cavity in the nose causes the bullet to expand ("mushroom") on impact, increasing tissue damage and reducing over-penetration; common in police and defensive ammunition.
  • Soft point — exposed lead tip that expands on impact; widely used for hunting.
  • Boat tail — a tapered base reducing drag for long-range accuracy.
  • Tracer, armour-piercing and incendiary — specialised military bullets with pyrotechnic or hardened-core features.

History

Early firearms fired spherical balls of stone, iron or lead. The invention of rifling, the Minié ball (1849), which expanded to grip the rifling, and the self-contained metallic cartridge in the 19th century transformed accuracy and rate of fire. The spitzer (pointed) bullet, adopted around the turn of the 20th century, improved ballistic efficiency and remains the standard shape for rifle bullets. The Hague Conventions restricted the use of expanding bullets in warfare between signatory states.

Physics

A bullet's behaviour in flight is the subject of external ballistics: muzzle velocities range from roughly 250 m/s for small pistol rounds to over 900 m/s for high-powered rifle rounds. Spin imparted by rifling gyroscopically stabilises the bullet. On striking a target, terminal ballistics describes penetration, fragmentation and expansion.
