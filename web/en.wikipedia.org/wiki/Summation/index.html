Summation

In mathematics, summation is the addition of a sequence of numbers, called addends or summands; the result is their sum or total. Besides numbers, other types of values can be summed as well: functions, vectors, matrices, polynomials, and, in general, elements of any type of mathematical object on which an operation denoted "+" is defined. Summations of infinite sequences are called series.

Notation

Summation is written with the Greek capital letter sigma, Σ, introduced by Leonhard Euler in 1755. The notation

∑i=mn ai = am + am+1 + … + an

means: sum the terms ai as the index i runs through the integers from the lower bound m to the upper bound n. The index i is a dummy variable: any letter may be used. Variants specify the index set below the sigma, for example Σx ∈ S f(x) for a sum over a set, or allow conditions such as 0 ≤ k < 100.

Basic properties

Summation is linear: Σ(c·ai) = c·Σ ai and Σ(ai + bi) = Σ ai + Σ bi. Ranges can be split, shifted, and — for finite sums — freely reordered. Several classic closed forms exist, such as the sum of the first n integers, n(n+1)/2 (the formula of the young Gauss anecdote); the sum of the first n squares, n(n+1)(2n+1)/6; and the geometric sum Σi=0n ri = (rn+1 − 1)/(r − 1).

Infinite sums

For infinitely many terms the sum is defined as the limit of the partial sums, and the result is a series, which may converge or diverge. Summation interacts with calculus through integrals — a sum approximates an integral and vice versa — and through summation by parts, the discrete analogue of integration by parts.

Computation

In computer science, summation is a basic loop operation; numerical accuracy is improved by techniques such as Kahan summation, which compensates for floating-point rounding error when many terms are accumulated. The capital-sigma notation also underlies the product notation (capital pi, Π) and generalizations in measure theory and linear algebra.
