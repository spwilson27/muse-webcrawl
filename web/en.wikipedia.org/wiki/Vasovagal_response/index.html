Vasovagal response

Vasovagal response
Also calledVasovagal syncope, neurocardiogenic syncope, reflex syncope, common faint
MechanismParasympathetic overactivity (bradycardia) plus sympathetic withdrawal (vasodilation)
Typical triggersSight of blood, pain, emotional distress, prolonged standing, heat

The vasovagal response is a reflex of the autonomic nervous system that produces a sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure, often culminating in vasovagal syncope — the common faint. It is the most frequent cause of fainting in otherwise healthy people, particularly the young.

Mechanism

After an initial phase of sympathetic activation (for example by pain, fear or standing still for a long time), a paradoxical reflex — related to the Bezold–Jarisch reflex — causes abrupt withdrawal of sympathetic tone, dilating the blood vessels, together with vagus-nerve-mediated slowing of the heart. Blood pools in the legs, cerebral perfusion falls, and consciousness is lost. Lying flat restores cerebral blood flow, so recovery is usually rapid and complete.

Symptoms and triggers

An episode is typically preceded by a prodrome of pallor, sweating, nausea, light-headedness, warmth, tunnel vision and ringing in the ears. Classic triggers include the sight of blood, venepuncture and medical procedures, emotional shock, pain, prolonged standing in heat, and straining (such as during defecation or coughing). Brief jerking movements during the faint can mimic a seizure, but the short duration, clear trigger and rapid recovery distinguish syncope.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on the history; a tilt-table test can reproduce the reflex in uncertain cases, and cardiac causes must be excluded when episodes are atypical. Management is mostly reassurance and education: recognising the prodrome, lying down with legs raised, physical counter-pressure manoeuvres (leg crossing, hand gripping, arm tensing), and maintaining adequate fluid and salt intake. Recurrent cases may be treated with medicines such as fludrocortisone or midodrine, and rarely with a pacemaker when profound cardioinhibition predominates.
