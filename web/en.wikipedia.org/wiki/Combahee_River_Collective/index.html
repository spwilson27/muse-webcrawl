Combahee River Collective

The Combahee River Collective was a Black feminist lesbian socialist organisation active in Boston, Massachusetts, from 1974 to 1980. The collective is best known for its 1977 Combahee River Collective Statement, a foundational document of Black feminism that coined the term identity politics and articulated an analysis of interlocking systems of oppression — race, class, gender, and sexuality — anticipating later theories of intersectionality.

The Collective took its name from the Combahee River Raid of 1863, led by Harriet Tubman.

Origins and name

The collective formed as the Boston chapter of the National Black Feminist Organization and took its name from the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, in which Harriet Tubman led Union forces in 1863 to liberate more than 700 enslaved people — the only military campaign in American history planned and led by a woman. Key members included Barbara Smith, Beverly Smith, Demita Frazier, Cheryl Clarke, Akasha Gloria Hull, and Audre Lorde (associated with the group's wider circle).

The Statement

The 1977 statement declared the members' commitment to struggling against racial, sexual, heterosexual, and class oppression simultaneously, arguing that these systems are interlocking and must be dismantled together. It criticised both the racism of the mainstream (white) feminist movement and the sexism of Black liberation movements, and introduced "identity politics" as a legitimate basis for political organising: the idea that the most profound politics come from one's own identity and experience. The phrase was later taken up — and sometimes redefined — across political theory and activism.

Activities and legacy

The Collective held retreats for Black women writers and activists, campaigned against violence against women (notably in response to the murders of Black women in Boston in 1979), and participated in reproductive rights and sterilisation-abuse campaigns. Although the group dissolved in 1980, its statement has become one of the most widely assigned texts in women's, gender, and African American studies, and its core ideas fed directly into Kimberlé Crenshaw's formulation of intersectionality in 1989.
