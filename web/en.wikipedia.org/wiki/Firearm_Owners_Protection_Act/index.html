Firearm Owners Protection Act

The Firearm Owners Protection Act (FOPA) of 1986 is a United States federal law that revised many provisions of the Gun Control Act of 1968. Supported by gun-rights organisations, it eased some restrictions on interstate sales and transport of firearms and limited federal inspection of licensed dealers, while also — through the Hughes Amendment — prohibiting the civilian ownership of machine guns manufactured after 19 May 1986. President Ronald Reagan signed the act into law on 19 May 1986.

Provisions

FOPA prohibited the federal government from maintaining a central registry of firearms, dealers or owners; limited warrantless compliance inspections of licensed dealers by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms to one per year in most circumstances; allowed mail-order sales of ammunition; and permitted licensed dealers to sell long guns to out-of-state residents in face-to-face transactions. It also eased the rules on individuals selling firearms from private collections without a dealer licence.

The act's "safe passage" provision, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 926A, protects travellers transporting firearms through jurisdictions where possession would otherwise be illegal, provided the firearm is unloaded and not readily accessible and the traveller may lawfully possess it at the origin and destination.

Hughes Amendment

Late in the legislative process, Representative William J. Hughes of New Jersey added an amendment closing the federal machine-gun registry: it banned the transfer or possession of machine guns made after the act's enactment, effectively freezing the stock of machine guns legally available to civilians at roughly those registered before May 1986. The amendment remains one of the most contested parts of the law, and its voice-vote adoption has been challenged by gun-rights advocates ever since.

Background and reception

The act grew out of complaints that the Gun Control Act of 1968 had been enforced abusively and that record-keeping requirements criminalised trivial errors by dealers and collectors. Studies commissioned by Congress in the late 1970s and early 1980s documented enforcement practices that helped build support for reform. Gun-control advocates opposed the act, and its passage is often regarded as a turning point that moved federal policy from further restriction toward protection of gun owners.
