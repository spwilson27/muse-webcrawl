Confederate Ireland, also referred to as the Irish Catholic Confederation, was a period of Irish Catholic self-government between 1642 and 1649, during the Eleven Years' War. Formed by Catholic aristocrats, landed gentry, clergy and commoners, it controlled about two-thirds of Ireland from its capital at Kilkenny, while the rest was held by Protestant English Parliamentarian and Scottish Covenanter forces.

Formation

The Irish Rebellion of October 1641 began amid fears among Ireland's Catholic landowners of invasion by anti-Catholic forces of the English Long Parliament and Scottish Covenanters, following the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. In May 1642 Catholic leaders met at Kilkenny and set up the Confederation, swearing the Oath of Association to defend the Catholic religion, the king (Charles I), and the liberties of Ireland. A General Assembly — effectively a parliament — and an elected Supreme Council governed the territory, raised taxes, minted coins, and ran armies under generals such as Owen Roe O'Neill and Thomas Preston.

War and diplomacy

The Confederates sought a settlement with Charles I that would secure Catholic religious freedom and land, negotiating the "cessation" of 1643 and the Ormond treaties. The papal nuncio Giovanni Battista Rinuccini, arriving in 1645, pushed for maximal Catholic demands and opposed compromise, deepening a split between the "peace party" of Old English gentry and the clerical/Ulster faction. Confederate forces won the Battle of Benburb (1646) but failed at Dungan's Hill and Knocknanuss (1647).

Collapse

After the execution of Charles I in 1649, the Confederates joined a royalist alliance under Ormond, but Oliver Cromwell's invasion from August 1649 crushed resistance; the Confederation dissolved as its institutions gave way to the royalist coalition, and catastrophic war and confiscation followed.
