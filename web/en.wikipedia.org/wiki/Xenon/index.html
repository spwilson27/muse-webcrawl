Xenon is a chemical element with the symbol Xe and atomic number 54. It is a dense, colourless, odourless noble gas found in Earth's atmosphere in trace amounts (about 0.087 parts per million). Xenon was discovered in 1898 by the Scottish chemist William Ramsay and the English chemist Morris Travers by fractional distillation of liquid air, shortly after their discovery of krypton and neon; its name derives from the Greek xénon, "foreign" or "strange".

Properties and chemistry

Xenon is monatomic and normally chemically inert, but it is the most reactive of the noble gases under forcing conditions. In 1962 Neil Bartlett at the University of British Columbia oxidised xenon with platinum hexafluoride, producing the first noble-gas compound and overturning the textbook doctrine of complete inertness. Xenon fluorides (XeF2, XeF4, XeF6), oxides (XeO3, XeO4) and oxyfluorides are now well characterised; xenon difluoride is used as a fluorinating agent and as an etchant for silicon in microfabrication. Naturally occurring xenon has nine isotopes, several of which are produced in nuclear fission — the isotope xenon-135 is a notorious neutron "poison" in reactors, a factor in the Chernobyl disaster. Liquid xenon's scintillation properties make it the target medium of major dark-matter detectors such as XENONnT and LUX-ZEPLIN.

Applications

Xenon emits a brilliant light when excited by an electrical discharge and is used in high-intensity discharge lamps, cinema projectors, photographic flashes, automotive headlamps and the "xenon" short-arc lamps of IMAX projection. Xenon is the propellant of choice for ion thrusters on deep-space probes such as NASA's Dawn spacecraft, because it is easily ionised, dense and inert. Medically, xenon acts as an anaesthetic at high concentrations and isotope xenon-133 is used in lung imaging; polarised xenon-129 enhances MRI studies.
