Quark–gluon plasma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Visualisation of a heavy-ion collision producing an expanding droplet of quark–gluon plasma]
A lead–lead collision at the LHC creating quark–gluon plasma.

A quark–gluon plasma (QGP) is a state of matter in Quantum chromodynamics that exists at extremely high temperatures and/or densities: quarks and gluons, normally confined inside hadrons, become free to move over distances larger than a hadron (deconfinement). The universe is believed to have been filled with QGP during its first ~20 microseconds, before cooling through the QCD transition (at about 156 MeV, 1.8 trillion kelvin) froze quarks into protons and neutrons.

QGP is recreated by colliding heavy nuclei (gold at RHIC, lead at the Large Hadron Collider) at nearly light speed, reaching temperatures above 5 trillion kelvin — the hottest matter ever produced in the laboratory. Surprisingly it behaves not as a gas but as a nearly perfect liquid with the lowest viscosity-to-entropy ratio known, properties computed using the AdS/CFT correspondence from black-hole physics. Jet quenching, elliptic flow and strangeness enhancement are its diagnostic signatures.

Significance

The QGP programme studies the strong interaction in its deconfined phase — the matter of the infant universe and possibly of neutron-star cores. Its perfect-fluid behaviour, anticipated by holography, is one of the concrete triumphs of gauge/gravity duality applied to a real system.
