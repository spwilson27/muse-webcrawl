Quasi-contract

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A quasi-contract is a legal device used by courts to impose obligations where no real contract exists, in order to prevent unjust enrichment. The claimant typically recovers the value of a benefit conferred on the defendant — for work done under a void contract, money paid by mistake, or necessaries supplied to a person lacking capacity — on the principle that no one should be unjustly enriched at another's expense. The old forms of action called the claims indebitatus assumpsit and “money had and received”; the fiction was that the defendant had impliedly promised to pay.

Modern law, especially after the recognition of unjust enrichment as an organising principle (in England associated with Lipkin Gorman v Karpnale, 1991, and the work of Peter Birks), treats most quasi-contractual claims as restitution for unjust enrichment, requiring an enrichment, at the claimant's expense, with an “unjust factor” such as mistake, failure of consideration or duress, subject to defences such as change of position. In the United States the field is systematised by the Restatement (Third) of Restitution and Unjust Enrichment (2011).
