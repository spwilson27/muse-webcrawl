Electric eel

The electric eel, Electrophorus electricus.

The electric eel (Electrophorus electricus) is a South American electric fish, and, together with its congeners, the best known of the genus Electrophorus. Despite its common name, it is not a true eel but a member of the knifefish order Gymnotiformes, more closely related to catfish and carp than to true eels.

Electric eels inhabit fresh waters of the Amazon and Orinoco basins in South America, favoring muddy bottoms of rivers, swamps, creeks and coastal plains. They are obligate air-breathers, rising to the surface every few minutes to gulp air, and can grow to about 2 m (6 ft 7 in) in length and 20 kg (44 lb) in weight.

Anatomy and electricity

Most of the animal's length is taken up by three electric organs — the main organ, Hunter's organ and Sachs' organ — composed of modified muscle cells called electrocytes stacked in series like batteries. The eel can generate discharges of up to about 860 volts and 1 ampere of current, among the strongest of any electric fish. Low-voltage discharges from Sachs' organ are used for electrolocation and communication, while high-voltage volleys are used for defense and to stun prey.

Biology and behavior

Electric eels are nocturnal predators feeding on invertebrates, fish, and small mammals. Studies in the 2010s showed that eels use high-voltage volleys to remotely control prey muscle (causing involuntary twitching that reveals the prey's position), and that they may curl around prey to intensify the electric field, or leap from the water to press their chins against a perceived threat. In 2019–2021 the genus was split into three species, and group hunting by packs of eels was documented in E. voltai.
