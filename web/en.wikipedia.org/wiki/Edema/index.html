Edema

Edema (also spelled oedema in British English; formerly called dropsy) is an abnormal accumulation of fluid in the interstitium — the tissue spaces between cells — causing swelling. The condition is characterised by an excess of watery fluid collecting in the cavities or tissues of the body. Edema can occur in any tissue but is most readily visible in the skin of the lower legs and feet, where it may be described as "pitting" if pressure leaves a temporary indentation, or in the lungs (pulmonary edema), brain (cerebral edema) or around the eyes (periorbital edema).

Causes

Edema results from disturbances in the Starling forces that govern fluid exchange across capillary walls: increased capillary hydrostatic pressure, decreased plasma oncotic pressure, increased capillary permeability, or obstruction of lymphatic drainage. Common causes include:

  • Heart failure — elevated venous pressure produces dependent leg swelling.
  • Kidney disease — nephrotic syndrome causes heavy protein loss in urine and a fall in plasma albumin, often with periorbital swelling.
  • Liver cirrhosis — portal hypertension and low albumin produce ascites and leg edema.
  • Venous insufficiency and deep vein thrombosis — local obstruction or valve failure, classically causing one-sided leg swelling in thrombosis.
  • Lymphedema — impaired lymph drainage, e.g. after lymph-node surgery or in filariasis.
  • Medications — calcium-channel blockers (such as amlodipine), corticosteroids, NSAIDs and thiazolidinediones.
  • Inflammation and allergy — increased vascular permeability, as in angioedema.

Specific forms

Pulmonary edema — fluid in the lungs, usually from left-sided heart failure — causes breathlessness and is a medical emergency. Cerebral edema raises intracranial pressure and can be fatal. Hydrops fetalis is severe fetal edema, historically associated with Rh incompatibility. Generalised edema is called anasarca.

Diagnosis and treatment

Assessment considers whether swelling is unilateral or bilateral, pitting or non-pitting, and its duration, together with examination, blood tests (renal and liver function, albumin, natriuretic peptides), urinalysis and imaging as indicated. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause, supplemented by sodium restriction, leg elevation, compression stockings and diuretic drugs such as furosemide where appropriate.
