Richard Merritt Montague (September 20, 1930 – March 7, 1971) was an American mathematician and philosopher who taught at the University of California, Los Angeles. He is best known for Montague grammar, a theory of the semantics of natural language that treats fragments of English with the same model-theoretic precision used for formal logical languages, famously asserting that there is "no important theoretical difference between natural languages and the artificial languages of logicians."

Life and career

Montague was born in Stockton, California. He studied at the University of California, Berkeley, earning his bachelor's degree in philosophy in 1950, a master's in mathematics in 1953, and a PhD in philosophy in 1957 under the supervision of Alfred Tarski. His dissertation concerned the axiomatic foundations of set theory. In 1955 he joined the philosophy department at UCLA, where he remained for the rest of his career.

Montague worked on a wide range of topics in logic and philosophy: the foundations of probability and measurement, decision theory (the subject of his joint work with Donald Kalish on deduction), intensional logic, and the semantics of natural language. With Kalish he co-developed a widely used system of natural deduction pedagogy and taught logic to generations of UCLA students. He was also a skilled organist and an investor in Los Angeles real estate.

Montague grammar

In a series of papers published around 1970–1973 — including "English as a Formal Language" (1970), "Universal Grammar" (1970), and "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English" (1973, published posthumously) — Montague proposed that natural-language syntax and semantics can be specified by explicit mathematical rules. His approach combined the lambda calculus, intensional logic, and possible-worlds semantics (in the tradition of Rudolf Carnap and Saul Kripke) to assign truth conditions to English sentences compositionally. The framework became foundational for formal semantics in linguistics, influencing scholars such as Barbara Partee and shaping the field's model-theoretic turn.

Death

Montague was murdered at his Los Angeles home on March 7, 1971, by unidentified assailants. His collected papers were published posthumously as Formal Philosophy (1974), edited by Richmond Thomason.
