Gastrinoma


Gastrinomas most often arise in the duodenum and pancreas.

A gastrinoma is a neuroendocrine tumour that secretes the hormone gastrin, causing the clinical picture known as Zollinger–Ellison syndrome: excessive gastric acid production, severe peptic ulcer disease and diarrhoea. Most gastrinomas arise in the "gastrinoma triangle" of the duodenum, pancreatic head and peripancreatic tissues; the duodenum is now recognised as the most common primary site. About 25% of gastrinomas occur as part of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1).

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically present with recurrent, multiple or unusually located peptic ulcers refractory to standard therapy, abdominal pain, gastro-oesophageal reflux, and secretory diarrhoea that improves with nasogastric suction or proton-pump inhibitors. Complications include ulcer perforation, bleeding, and oesophageal strictures.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a markedly elevated fasting serum gastrin level (often >1,000 pg/mL) with low gastric pH, confirmed by the secretin stimulation test. Tumour localisation uses CT, MRI, endoscopic ultrasound and somatostatin-receptor imaging such as Ga-68 DOTATATE PET. High-dose proton-pump inhibitors control acid hypersecretion in nearly all patients; surgical resection offers the only chance of cure for sporadic, localised tumours, while metastatic disease is managed with somatostatin analogues and other systemic therapies.
