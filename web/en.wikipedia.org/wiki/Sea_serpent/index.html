Sea serpent

The sea serpent is a legendary aquatic creature, described in mythology and sailors' reports from many cultures as a huge serpent-like animal living in the ocean. Sightings were reported for centuries — by Norse, Greek and biblical tradition (the Leviathan) among others — and peaked in the 18th and 19th centuries, but no specimen has ever been obtained and the creature is regarded by science as mythical or the result of misidentification.

Notable reports

Olaus Magnus depicted sea serpents on his Carta Marina (1539) and in his History of the Northern Peoples (1555). Bishop Erik Pontoppidan described the Norwegian sea serpent in the 1750s. In 1848 the crew of HMS Daedalus reported a large serpentine animal between the Cape of Good Hope and St Helena, prompting public debate in The Times. The Gloucester sea serpent of 1817 was investigated by a committee of the Linnaean Society of New England.

Explanations

Sceptics attribute reports to misidentified known animals: giant oarfish (long, ribbon-like fish that may underlie many accounts), whales, lines of porpoises, floating seaweed, waves and optical effects. Cryptozoologists have proposed surviving plesiosaurs or primitive whales, but these ideas lack evidence. Folklorists treat the sea serpent as a durable motif of maritime folklore.
