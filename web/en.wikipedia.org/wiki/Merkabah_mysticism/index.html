Merkabah mysticism (or Chariot mysticism) is an early form of Jewish mysticism that flourished from roughly the first century BCE through the early medieval period. It centres on visions of the divine throne-chariot (Hebrew merkabah) described in the opening chapter of the Book of Ezekiel, and on techniques by which adepts sought to ascend through a series of heavenly palaces (hekhalot) to behold the glory of God.

Origins

The tradition grows out of biblical throne visions — Ezekiel's chariot of cherubim and wheels (Ezekiel 1), Isaiah's vision of God enthroned in the Temple (Isaiah 6), and Daniel's "Ancient of Days" (Daniel 7) — together with Second Temple apocalyptic literature such as 1 Enoch, in which a visionary journeys through the heavens. Rabbinic sources treated the "Account of the Chariot" (Ma'aseh Merkavah) as esoteric knowledge to be taught only to qualified disciples; the Mishnah (Hagigah 2:1) restricts its exposition.

Hekhalot literature

The principal texts, known collectively as Hekhalot literature, were composed and redacted between late antiquity and the early Middle Ages, mainly in Palestine and Babylonia. Major works include Hekhalot Zutreti, Hekhalot Rabbati, Ma'aseh Merkabah, the Shi'ur Qomah ("Measure of the Body"), which describes the gigantic dimensions of the divine form, and Sefer Hekhalot (3 Enoch), in which Rabbi Ishmael ascends and meets the angel Metatron, identified as the transformed patriarch Enoch.

The adept, called a yored merkavah ("descender to the chariot," paradoxically using the language of descent for ascent), prepared through fasting, purity, and the recitation of hymns and divine names, passing guardian angels at the gates of seven palaces by means of seals and passwords. The Talmudic story of the four who entered Pardes (Hagigah 14b) — of whom only Rabbi Akiva emerged in peace — dramatises the dangers of the ascent.

Legacy

Merkabah traditions influenced medieval Jewish pietism (the Hasidei Ashkenaz) and supplied imagery, angelology, and contemplative techniques later absorbed into Kabbalah. The scholar Gershom Scholem, whose Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism (1941) founded the modern academic study of the subject, regarded Merkabah mysticism as the earliest stratum of Jewish mystical experience, continuous with ancient Gnostic currents.
