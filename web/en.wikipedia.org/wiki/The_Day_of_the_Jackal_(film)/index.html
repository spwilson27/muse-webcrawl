The Day of the Jackal is a 1973 political thriller film directed by Fred Zinnemann, adapted by Kenneth Ross from Frederick Forsyth's 1971 novel of the same name. It stars Edward Fox as "the Jackal," a professional assassin hired by the French paramilitary OAS to assassinate President Charles de Gaulle in the summer of 1963, following France's granting of independence to Algeria.

Plot

After the failed Petit-Clamart ambush on de Gaulle, the OAS leadership hires an unnamed English contract killer, codenamed the Jackal, for $500,000. In meticulous detail he commissions a custom collapsible rifle, forges identities (including that of a dead child whose birth certificate he obtains), and cases the Liberation Day ceremony in Paris. Meanwhile Deputy Commissioner Claude Lebel (Michel Lonsdale), alerted by intelligence from a captured OAS courier, races to identify and stop him, mobilizing the full machinery of the French state. The final confrontation in a Paris apartment overlooking the ceremony ends with Lebel killing the Jackal, who is buried in an unmarked grave.

Reception and legacy

The film was praised for its procedural realism and quiet suspense, and was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Film Editing. It is frequently cited as one of the finest political thrillers of the 1970s. A loose remake, The Jackal (1997), starred Bruce Willis and Richard Gere. In 2024 the story was re-adapted as a British television series starring Eddie Redmayne as the Jackal and Lashana Lynch as the intelligence officer hunting him.
