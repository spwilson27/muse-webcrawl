Enhanced geothermal system

An enhanced geothermal system (EGS) is a geothermal power technology that creates an artificial reservoir in hot, dry rock where natural permeability or water is insufficient. Water is injected down a well into fractured hot rock, circulates through the fracture network absorbing heat, and is drawn back up a production well to drive a turbine, usually via a binary-cycle plant. EGS aims to make geothermal energy available far beyond the naturally occurring hydrothermal fields exploited since the twentieth century.

Technology

Development typically involves drilling wells 3–5 km deep into rock hotter than 150 °C, then stimulating the formation — often by high-pressure water injection ("hydro-shearing") — to open and connect natural fractures. Because the resource is simply the heat stored in the Earth's crust, EGS has a very large theoretical potential; a widely cited 2006 MIT study estimated that EGS could supply a substantial fraction of United States electricity demand.

Projects

The pioneering Fenton Hill project in New Mexico (1970s–1990s) first demonstrated the concept. Subsequent demonstration plants include Soultz-sous-Forêts in France, Landau and Insheim in Germany, and the Cooper Basin project in Australia. In the United States, the Department of Energy's FORGE site in Utah and start-ups such as Fervo Energy — which began supplying power from its Project Red plant in Nevada in 2023 using horizontal drilling adapted from shale oil practice — have renewed commercial interest.

Challenges

Obstacles include high drilling costs, maintaining flow between wells without short-circuiting or excessive water loss, and induced seismicity: the Basel project in Switzerland was halted in 2006 after stimulation triggered a magnitude 3.4 earthquake, and a 2017 magnitude 5.4 event in Pohang, South Korea, was attributed to a nearby EGS project, leading to its cancellation. Careful site selection, monitoring and staged stimulation are now standard practice.
