Gyokuro (Japanese: 玉露, "jade dew" or "jewel dew") is a type of shaded green tea from Japan. It differs from the standard sencha (an unshaded green tea) in being grown under shade for approximately three to four weeks before harvest, which gives it a distinctive sweet, rich, savoury (umami) flavour and a high content of the amino acid theanine. Gyokuro is considered one of the finest and most expensive Japanese teas.

History

Gyokuro was developed in 1835 by the tea merchant Yamamoto Kahei in the Uji region of Kyoto, who discovered that covering tea bushes with straw mats during part of the growing season — a technique originally used to protect the plants from frost — dramatically improved the flavour of the leaves. The method was refined by Ogura Masakichi and others, and gyokuro became established as a luxury tea during the late Edo and Meiji periods.

Cultivation

Gyokuro is made from the tea plant Camellia sinensis, typically cultivars such as Asahi, Saemidori, Okumidori and Yamakai. About twenty to thirty days before plucking, the tea field is covered with reed screens and straw, or in modern practice black synthetic netting (a method called kabuse when applied for a shorter period), reducing the light reaching the plants to as little as 10 percent.

Shading forces the plant to compensate by increasing chlorophyll production, giving the leaves a deep green colour, and suppresses the formation of bitter catechins while boosting amino acids, especially L-theanine, which is responsible for the tea's characteristic sweetness and umami. Only the tender first-flush spring leaves are used, and they are steamed, dried and rolled in the same way as sencha, into fine needle-like shapes.

Preparation

Gyokuro is brewed differently from most green teas. A large quantity of leaf — roughly 7 to 10 grams — is used with a small amount of water (about 50–60 ml) cooled to around 40–50 °C, and steeped for two to three minutes. The result is a concentrated, almost viscous liquor with an intense sweet-savoury taste, sometimes compared to broth or seaweed. The leaves are traditionally eaten after brewing, sometimes with ponzu or soy sauce. Successive infusions are made with progressively hotter water.

Production regions

The most famous gyokuro comes from Uji in Kyoto Prefecture. Other major producing areas include Yame in Fukuoka Prefecture — whose gyokuro regularly wins top prizes at Japanese national tea competitions — Asahina in Shizuoka Prefecture, and parts of Mie and Aichi prefectures. Total gyokuro production is very small, accounting for well under one percent of Japan's tea output, which contributes to its high price.
