The Adal Sultanate, also known as the Sultanate of Adal (Somali: Saldanadda Cadal), was a medieval Muslim sultanate in the Horn of Africa. Founded by Sabr ad-Din II in the early 15th century, it was centred first at Dakkar and later at the walled city of Harar, in present-day Ethiopia and Somaliland. At its height, the polity controlled large parts of the eastern Horn, from the Red Sea coast to the Ethiopian highlands.

Origins

Adal emerged from the earlier Sultanate of Ifat, whose territories were conquered by the expansionist Christian Ethiopian Empire under Emperor Amda Seyon I in the 14th century. Members of the Walashma dynasty, which had ruled Ifat, regrouped in the eastern lowlands and re-established Muslim rule in the region around 1415.

Ethiopian–Adal War

Adal is best known for its prolonged conflict with the Ethiopian Empire, culminating in the Ethiopian–Adal War of 1529–1543. Under the imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, known as Ahmad Gragn ("the Left-handed"), Adalite forces, reinforced by Ottoman musketeers and artillery, overran much of the Ethiopian highlands between 1529 and 1535. The tide turned after Portuguese intervention; Ahmad was killed at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543.

Decline

After Ahmad's death the sultanate steadily contracted under pressure from Ethiopian rulers, Oromo migrations, and internal strife. In 1577 the capital was moved from Harar and the sultanate effectively dissolved, its legacy passing to the Emirate of Harar and the Imamate of Aussa.

Economy and society

Adal's prosperity rested on trade through the Red Sea port of Zeila, exporting ivory, gold, slaves, and livestock and importing textiles and manufactured goods from Arabia and India. Harar became a noted centre of Islamic scholarship, with its own coinage and a tradition of bookbinding and manuscript production.
