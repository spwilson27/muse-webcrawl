Eteocretan (from Greek Ἐτεόκρητες, "true Cretans") was the language of the Eteocretans, a pre-Greek people of eastern Crete who survived into the historical period. It is attested in a small number of inscriptions written in the Greek alphabet between the late 7th and the 3rd century BC, mainly from the sites of Praisos and Dreros. The language has never been deciphered and is unclassified.

Corpus

The corpus consists of a handful of inscriptions, several of them fragmentary. The longest is a 7th-century BC inscription from Dreros, a bilingual text in which a Greek version accompanies the Eteocretan one, though the two do not correspond closely enough to unlock the language. The Praisos inscriptions, written in a local archaic alphabet, include what appear to be funerary and dedicatory texts. The latest attestations date to the 3rd century BC, after which the language disappears, presumably absorbed by Greek.

Relationship to Minoan

The Eteocretans are mentioned by Homer and Strabo as one of the ancient peoples of Crete. Because they inhabited the island long before the Greek-speaking Mycenaean period, Eteocretan is widely suspected to descend from the language of the Minoans, written in the undeciphered Linear A script; however, the attested material is too scant to prove the connection or to establish any relationship with other languages.
