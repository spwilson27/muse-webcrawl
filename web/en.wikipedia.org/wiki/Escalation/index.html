Escalation

Escalation is the process by which a conflict, dispute, or competition intensifies over time, with each party responding to the actions of the other in a way that raises the stakes, scope, or severity of the confrontation. The term is used in international relations, military strategy, psychology, customer service, and organisational management.

In international relations

In the study of war and diplomacy, escalation describes the deliberate or inadvertent increase in the intensity or scope of a conflict. The American strategist Herman Kahn, in his 1965 work On Escalation: Metaphors and Scenarios, proposed a 44-rung "escalation ladder" ranging from ostensible crisis through conventional war to all-out nuclear exchange. Kahn's framework influenced Cold War strategic thinking about how states might signal resolve while retaining control over a conflict's trajectory.

Escalation may be vertical, involving greater intensity of violence, or horizontal, involving the geographic or functional spread of a conflict to new theatres or domains. It may also be inadvertent, arising from misperception, organisational routines, or the autonomous dynamics of mobilisation, rather than from deliberate choice.

Escalation dominance

Escalation dominance is a military strategy concept in which one side is able to win at each successive level of escalation, thereby deterring an adversary from raising the stakes. The concept became central to United States defence planning in the later Cold War and post-Cold War periods.

Commitment escalation

In psychology and management, escalation of commitment refers to the tendency of individuals and organisations to continue investing in a failing course of action because of prior investments — the so-called sunk cost fallacy. Research by Barry Staw and others showed that decision-makers who are personally responsible for an initial decision are especially prone to throw good money after bad.

De-escalation

De-escalation is the counterpart process: steps taken to reduce tension and lower the level of conflict. Techniques include graduated reciprocation in tension-reduction (GRIT), confidence-building measures, hotlines between adversaries, and negotiated ceasefires.
