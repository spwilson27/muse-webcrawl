The Dictatus papae survives as an entry in the register of Pope Gregory VII in the Vatican Archives.

Dictatus papae ("Sayings of the Pope") is a compilation of 27 statements of powers arrogated to the Pope that was included in Pope Gregory VII's register under the year 1075. The principles expressed in the Dictatus papae are mostly those proclaimed by the Gregorian Reform movement and stand behind the great conflicts of the Investiture Controversy.

Contents

Among its assertions, the Dictatus claims that the Roman Church was founded by God alone; that the pope alone is called universal; that he alone can depose or reinstate bishops; that no one may judge the pope; that the Roman Church has never erred and never will; that the pope may depose emperors and absolve subjects of unjust rulers from their allegiance; and that all princes shall kiss the feet of the pope alone. Several statements — notably that the pope "may be judged by no one" and that he can depose emperors — became central texts in medieval debates over the relationship between spiritual and temporal power.

Authorship and interpretation

Although traditionally attributed to Gregory VII himself, scholars debate whether he personally composed the text or whether it was compiled by associates such as Cardinal Deusdedit. Historians also disagree over its purpose: it may have been a list of headings for a planned collection of canon law rather than a promulgated decree, since it was never published as legislation in its own right.
