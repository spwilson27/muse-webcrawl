Adventism is a branch of Protestant Christianity that believes in the imminent Second Coming (or "Second Advent") of Jesus Christ. It originated in the United States in the 1840s from the preaching of Baptist minister William Miller, whose followers became known as Millerites.

Origins

Miller concluded from his study of the Book of Daniel that Christ would return to earth around 1843–1844, a calculation based on the prophecy of the 2,300 evenings and mornings of Daniel 8:14. When the predicted date of 22 October 1844 passed without the Advent, the movement suffered the "Great Disappointment," and most followers abandoned it. A remnant reinterpreted the prophecy as marking the beginning of Christ's ministry in the heavenly sanctuary — the doctrine of the investigative judgment — and continued to expect the soon return of Christ.

Denominations

From the Millerite remnant arose several Adventist bodies. The largest by far is the Seventh-day Adventist Church, formally organised in 1863, which observes the seventh-day (Saturday) Sabbath and emphasises health, education and evangelism; it counts over 20 million baptised members worldwide. Other Adventist groups include the Advent Christian Church, the United Seventh-Day Brethren, and the Davidian Seventh-day Adventists. The Church of God (Seventh Day) shares Adventist and Sabbatarian roots.

Beliefs

Common Adventist beliefs include the imminent visible return of Christ, the resurrection of the dead, conditional immortality (the "sleep" of the dead until the resurrection), the observance of the biblical Sabbath in Sabbatarian branches, and an emphasis on biblical prophecy, especially the books of Daniel and Revelation. Many Adventists also practise vegetarianism and health reform, following the teachings of Ellen G. White, a co-founder of the Seventh-day Adventist Church regarded by its members as a prophet.
