Timing belt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A timing belt, or toothed belt, is a part of an internal combustion engine that synchronizes the rotation of the crankshaft and the camshaft(s) so that the engine's valves open and close at the proper times during each cylinder's intake and exhaust strokes. In an interference engine, the timing belt is also critical in preventing the piston from striking the valves.

Construction

Timing belts are typically made of a flexible rubber or polymer reinforced with fiberglass or aramid cords, with molded teeth on the inner surface that engage matching grooves on the crankshaft and camshaft pulleys. The toothed design prevents the slipping possible with smooth friction belts, preserving exact valve timing.

Failure and replacement

Belts degrade with age and mileage; manufacturers specify replacement intervals, commonly between 60,000 and 100,000 miles. Because a snapped or stripped belt can stop the engine immediately — and in an interference engine can bend valves and damage pistons — the water pump and tensioner pulleys driven by the belt are often replaced at the same time. Many modern engines instead use a metal timing chain, which is designed to last the life of the engine, though chains and their tensioners can also wear.

History

Toothed rubber belts were first used in a production car engine in the 1960s; Fiat's twin-cam engine of 1966 was an early widely produced application, and the technology spread rapidly through the 1970s and 1980s.
