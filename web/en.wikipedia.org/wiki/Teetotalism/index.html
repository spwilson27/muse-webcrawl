Teetotalism is the practice or promotion of total personal abstinence from the consumption of alcoholic beverages. A person who practises teetotalism is called a teetotaler (or teetotaller). Teetotalism emerged as a distinct, stricter strand of the temperance movement in the 1830s, insisting on abstinence from all alcohol rather than merely moderation or avoidance of spirits.

Origins of the term

The word is usually traced to Preston, England, in 1833, where abstinence advocate Richard Turner — a member of the Preston Temperance Society founded by Joseph Livesey — declared in a speech that nothing but "tee-total" abstinence would do; the emphatic doubling of the initial T (also written on pledge cards as "T-total") gave the movement its name. Rapidly, temperance societies split between "moderationists" and teetotalers, with the latter building organisations such as the British Temperance League, the Rechabites and, later, the Band of Hope for children.

Spread and influence

Teetotalism spread through Britain's industrial towns — where it was bound up with Methodism, self-improvement and working-class respectability — and to the United States, Canada, Australia and beyond. In America it fed into the broader temperance campaigns of the Woman's Christian Temperance Union and ultimately national Prohibition (1920–1933). Today people abstain for many reasons beyond temperance ideology: health, religion (Islam, Mormonism, Seventh-day Adventism and some other faiths require or encourage abstinence), recovery from alcohol dependence (as in Alcoholics Anonymous), pregnancy, or personal preference — often described today as being "sober curious" or alcohol-free.
