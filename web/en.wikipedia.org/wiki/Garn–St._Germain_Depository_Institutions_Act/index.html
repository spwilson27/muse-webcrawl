Garn–St. Germain Depository Institutions Act

The Garn–St Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 (Pub. L. 97–320, H.R. 6267, enacted October 15, 1982) is an Act of Congress that deregulated savings and loan associations and allowed banks to provide adjustable-rate mortgage loans. It is named after its principal sponsors, Utah Senator Jake Garn and Rhode Island Congressman Fernand J. St Germain. The Act was one of a set of laws — alongside the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 — that contributed to the savings and loan crisis of the late 1980s.

Provisions

  • It removed the deposit interest rate ceilings that had been in place since the 1960s (Regulation Q), phasing them out and allowing depository institutions to offer money market deposit accounts.
  • It authorized banks and savings and loans to offer adjustable-rate mortgages.
  • It expanded the lending and investment powers of federal savings and loan associations, allowing them to make commercial, corporate, business, and agricultural loans and to invest in nonresidential real estate.
  • Its Title VIII, the Alternative Mortgage Transaction Parity Act, preempted state restrictions on alternative mortgage instruments for state-chartered lenders.
  • It raised the deposit insurance limit and provided for assistance to troubled thrift institutions, including "net worth certificates".

Effects

By deregulating the savings and loan industry, the Act is credited with helping healthy institutions adapt to high interest rates, but it also enabled weakened thrifts to engage in riskier lending with insured deposits. Along with other deregulatory measures and fraud, this contributed to the savings and loan crisis, in which more than a thousand thrift institutions failed.
