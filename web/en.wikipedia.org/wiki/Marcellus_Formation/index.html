The Marcellus Formation or Marcellus Shale is a Middle Devonian-age unit of organic-rich black shale in the Appalachian Basin of eastern North America. Named for a distinctive outcrop near the village of Marcellus, New York, it underlies some 95,000 square miles across New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, West Virginia, and Maryland, at depths of roughly 4,000–8,500 feet in the productive fairway.

Geology

The shale was deposited about 390 million years ago in a deep, oxygen-poor sea west of the rising Acadian mountains. Its dark colour reflects abundant organic matter (total organic carbon commonly 2–10%), which with burial and heating generated natural gas held in fractures and nanopores. The formation is divided into members including the Union Springs and Oatka Creek shales; it is mildly radioactive owing to uranium in the organic fraction, a property long used to identify it on well logs.

The Marcellus gas boom

Known for a century but considered uneconomic, the Marcellus was unlocked in the 2000s by combining horizontal drilling with high-volume hydraulic fracturing ("fracking"), pioneered commercially by Range Resources' 2004 Renz well in Washington County, Pennsylvania. Production soared: the Marcellus became the largest natural-gas-producing formation in the United States, transforming Appalachian economies and US energy markets, and resetting estimates of national gas reserves upward.

Controversy

Development has been contentious. New York State banned high-volume fracking in 2014–15, while Pennsylvania embraced the industry without a severance tax for many years. Debates continue over water use, wastewater disposal, methane leakage, road damage, health effects, and climate impact, making "Marcellus" a byword in the global shale-gas argument.
