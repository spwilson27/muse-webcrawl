Automatic dependent surveillance – broadcast (ADS-B) is a surveillance technology in which an aircraft determines its own position using satellite navigation and periodically broadcasts it, enabling the aircraft to be tracked by air traffic control ground stations and by other aircraft. It is "automatic" because no pilot or controller input is required, "dependent" because it depends on the aircraft's own navigation systems, and "surveillance – broadcast" because position and other data are continuously transmitted to all receivers in range.

How it works

An ADS-B-equipped aircraft broadcasts its identity, GPS-derived position, altitude, velocity and other data about twice per second. Most airliners transmit on 1090 MHz using Mode S extended squitter (1090ES); in the United States, general aviation aircraft may instead use the 978 MHz Universal Access Transceiver (UAT), which can also receive weather and traffic information (ADS-B In). Ground stations, multilateration systems and — since 2019 — the space-based Aireon network of receivers on Iridium satellites collect the broadcasts, enabling surveillance over oceans and remote regions beyond radar coverage.

Regulation and adoption

The United States Federal Aviation Administration required ADS-B Out in most controlled airspace from 1 January 2020 as part of the NextGen modernisation programme; the European Union, Australia, Canada and other states have adopted similar mandates. ADS-B is gradually replacing secondary radar as the primary surveillance method in many regions.

Public tracking and concerns

Because ADS-B transmissions are unencrypted and broadcast in the clear, hobbyist networks such as ADS-B Exchange and commercial services such as Flightradar24 aggregate feeds from thousands of volunteer receivers to track flights publicly in near real time. This transparency has supported journalism and safety research but raised privacy concerns for business and military operators, leading to programmes such as the FAA's Limiting Aircraft Data Displayed (LADD) list. Spoofing and jamming of the signals are also documented security concerns.
