The International Brigades (Spanish: Brigadas Internacionales) were a series of military units set up by the Communist International (Comintern) to assist the Popular Front government of the Second Spanish Republic during the Spanish Civil War. The organization existed from 1936 until 1938, when it was disbanded and the volunteers withdrawn under the League of Nations' Non-Intervention Committee arrangements.

An estimated 35,000 to 59,000 foreign volunteers from more than 50 countries served in the Brigades; roughly one in five was killed. Volunteers were motivated largely by anti-fascism, seeing Spain as the front line against the expansion of Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy, which openly supported Franco's Nationalists.

Organization

Recruitment was organized through Communist parties and affiliated committees in Paris and other capitals; volunteers traveled to the Brigades' base at Albacete, where they were formed into battalions grouped largely by language and nationality. The numbered brigades (XI through XV) included the German-speaking Thälmann Battalion, the French-Belgian Commune de Paris Battalion, the Italian Garibaldi Battalion, the Polish Dąbrowski Battalion, and the English-speaking Abraham Lincoln Battalion from the United States, together with the Mackenzie–Papineau Battalion from Canada.

Combat history

The first Brigades arrived in time to play a conspicuous role in the defense of Madrid in November 1936. They subsequently fought at the Jarama (February 1937), Guadalajara, Brunete, and Belchite, and in the offensives at Teruel and across the Ebro in 1938. Casualties were severe: the Lincoln Battalion lost two-thirds of its strength at Jarama, and overall the Brigades suffered some of the highest loss rates of any Republican units.

Withdrawal and legacy

On 21 September 1938, Prime Minister Juan Negrín announced the unilateral withdrawal of all foreign combatants before the League of Nations. The Brigades were formally disbanded and marched in a farewell parade through Barcelona on 28 October 1938, addressed by Dolores Ibárruri ("La Pasionaria"). Many veterans later served in the Second World War, in resistance movements, or in the armed forces of their home countries; others faced internment in France or persecution on returning to Nazi-dominated Europe.

Notable members

  • George Orwell served in a POUM militia rather than the Brigades proper; Homage to Catalonia is the era's most famous English-language memoir.
  • Ernest Hemingway reported alongside the Brigades and wrote For Whom the Bell Tolls.
  • Willy Brandt, later Chancellor of West Germany, worked in Spain as a journalist.
