Vedic Sanskrit

Vedic Sanskrit is the earliest attested form of the Sanskrit language, and the language of the Vedas, the foundational texts of Hinduism, composed in north-western India roughly between 1500 and 500 BCE. It is an early Indo-Aryan language, closely related to Old Iranian languages such as Avestan, and belongs to the Indo-Iranian branch of the Indo-European family. The oldest stratum is the language of the Rigveda, whose hymns preserve archaic features later lost in Classical Sanskrit.

History and corpus

The Vedic corpus is conventionally divided into four layers: the Samhitas (the Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda and Atharvaveda), the Brahmanas (prose commentaries on ritual), the Aranyakas ("forest texts"), and the early Upanishads. Composition was oral: the texts were memorised and transmitted with extraordinary fidelity through elaborate mnemonic techniques, long before being written down. As the language evolved over the roughly thousand-year Vedic period, later texts show progressive levelling of archaic forms and the approach towards the classical standard.

Linguistic features

Vedic Sanskrit retains a pitch accent (udātta, anudātta and svarita), marked in the Rigveda text, of which the Classical language kept only a vestige. Its verbal system is far richer than that of Classical Sanskrit: it possesses a fully productive subjunctive, an injunctive, multiple infinitive formations, and a large stock of aorist and perfect formations. Nominal morphology includes fuller case and dual forms. Vocabulary likewise contains many words that fell out of use or changed meaning later. The later systematisation by the grammarian Pāṇini (around the 5th–4th century BCE), whose Aṣṭādhyāyī describes the classical language, marks the conventional dividing line.

Relationship to Classical Sanskrit

Classical Sanskrit is not the direct linear descendant of any single Vedic dialect but a codified standard based on the later Vedic language of the educated elite. Study of Vedic remains central to Indo-European comparative linguistics — its accent and verbal system illuminate reconstructions of Proto-Indo-European — and to the religious and cultural history of South Asia.
