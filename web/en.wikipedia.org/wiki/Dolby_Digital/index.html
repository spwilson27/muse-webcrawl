Dolby Digital, originally synonymous with its codec AC-3, is the name for a family of audio compression technologies developed by Dolby Laboratories. The first use of Dolby Digital was to provide digital sound in cinemas from 35 mm film prints. It has since also been used for TV broadcasts, DVDs, Blu-ray Discs, streaming services, and video game consoles.

History

Dolby Digital debuted in 1992 with the film Batman Returns, with the AC-3 data stream printed between the sprocket holes of the 35 mm film. It reached the home in 1995 on LaserDisc releases and became the mandatory standard audio format for DVD-Video. Dolby Digital supports channel configurations from mono up to 5.1 discrete surround channels (left, centre, right, left surround, right surround, and a low-frequency effects channel).

Variants

  • Dolby Digital Plus (E-AC-3) — higher bit rates, up to 7.1 channels, widely used for streaming.
  • Dolby Digital EX — adds a matrixed centre rear surround channel.
  • Dolby Digital Live — real-time encoding for interactive media such as games.
  • Dolby TrueHD — a lossless companion codec used on Blu-ray.
  • Dolby AC-4 — a later-generation codec designed for broadcast and streaming, including object-based audio alongside Dolby Atmos.

Technical details

AC-3 uses the modified discrete cosine transform (MDCT) to convert audio into the frequency domain and applies perceptual coding to discard information masked by louder sounds. Bit rates for consumer 5.1 material typically range from 384 to 640 kbit/s. Metadata carried in the stream supports dialogue normalisation and dynamic range control.
