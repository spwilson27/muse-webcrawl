Principle of non-contradiction

Placeholder image: Aristotle, who gave the classical formulation

In logic, the principle of non-contradiction (PNC), or law of non-contradiction, states that contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time — for example, the two propositions "p is the case" and "p is not the case" are mutually exclusive. Formally, ¬(p ∧ ¬p).

Aristotle's account

Aristotle called it "the most certain of all principles," holding that it cannot be demonstrated directly — since any demonstration would presuppose it — but can be defended elenctically: anyone who says anything meaningful implicitly relies on it. In the Metaphysics he gave ontological, logical, and psychological versions of the principle.

Relation to other laws

Together with the law of identity and the law of excluded middle, non-contradiction forms the traditional three laws of thought. In classical logic, violating it leads to triviality through the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet): from a contradiction, anything follows.

Challenges

Paraconsistent logics and dialetheism (notably defended by Graham Priest) deny that all contradictions are explosive and argue some contradictions might even be true. Hegel and later dialectical traditions have also been read as qualifying the principle, though interpretations differ on whether they truly reject it.
