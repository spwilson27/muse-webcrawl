Iroquoian languages

The Iroquoian languages are a language family indigenous to North America, historically spoken around the eastern Great Lakes, the St. Lawrence valley and, in the case of Cherokee, the southern Appalachians and later Oklahoma. They are known for their complex polysynthetic morphology.

Classification

The family divides into a northern branch — including Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca and Tuscarora (the languages of the Haudenosaunee/Iroquois Confederacy), together with Huron-Wyandot, Susquehannock and the St. Lawrence Iroquoian languages — and a southern branch consisting of Cherokee, whose speakers were separated geographically long before European contact. Several languages, including Erie, Neutral and Laurentian, became extinct in the 17th century amid the Beaver Wars and dispersal of their speakers.

Features

Iroquoian languages are head-marking and polysynthetic: verbs carry pronominal prefixes that index both agent and patient, and noun incorporation is pervasive. They typically have small consonant inventories, nasal vowels in some members, and a contrast involving glottal stop and /h/. All the surviving languages are endangered, with speaker numbers ranging from a few dozen to a few thousand; Cherokee and Mohawk have the largest communities and active revitalisation programmes, including immersion schools.
