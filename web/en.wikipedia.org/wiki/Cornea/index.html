Cornea

The cornea is the transparent front part of the eye that covers the iris, pupil, and anterior chamber. Together with the lens, the cornea refracts light and accounts for roughly two-thirds of the eye's total optical power — about 43 dioptres in humans.

Structure

The cornea has five recognised layers: the epithelium, Bowman's layer, the stroma (substantia propria), Descemet's membrane, and the endothelium. It has no blood vessels — it receives nutrients from tears and aqueous humour — but is one of the most densely innervated tissues in the body, making it extremely sensitive to touch and pain.

Function

Because the cornea's curved surface borders air, the air–cornea interface provides the single greatest refractive power in the eye. Its precise shape and transparency are maintained by the endothelial pump that keeps the stroma in a state of relative dehydration.

Clinical significance

Disorders include keratoconus (thinning and cone-like bulging), infections (keratitis), and dystrophies such as Fuchs' dystrophy. Damaged corneas can be treated by corneal transplantation (keratoplasty), and refractive errors are corrected by glasses, contact lenses worn on the cornea, or laser surgery such as LASIK.
