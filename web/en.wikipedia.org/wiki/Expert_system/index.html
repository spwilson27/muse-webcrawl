Expert system

Structure of a rule-based expert system.

In artificial intelligence, an expert system is a computer system that emulates the decision-making ability of a human expert. Expert systems are designed to solve complex problems by reasoning through bodies of knowledge, represented mainly as if–then rules, rather than through conventional procedural code.

Expert systems were among the first truly successful forms of AI software, developed in the 1970s and commercially deployed in the 1980s. They consist of two main components: a knowledge base, containing domain facts and rules elicited from human specialists, and an inference engine, which applies the rules to known facts to deduce new information or conclusions. The process of eliciting and encoding the knowledge is called knowledge engineering.

History

Early systems included DENDRAL (begun 1965) for inferring molecular structure from mass spectra, MYCIN (1970s) for diagnosing bacterial infections and recommending antibiotics, and PROSPECTOR for mineral exploration. XCON, developed for Digital Equipment Corporation from 1978, configured VAX computer orders and is credited with saving the company large sums annually. The success of such systems helped fuel the commercial AI boom of the 1980s and the development of tools such as Lisp machines and expert-system shells; their difficulty of maintenance and brittleness outside narrow domains contributed to the subsequent "AI winter".

Advantages and limitations

Expert systems can explain their reasoning step by step, preserve scarce human expertise, and perform consistently in narrow domains. However, they lack common sense, learn poorly from experience, and suffer from the "knowledge acquisition bottleneck" — the difficulty of extracting and maintaining rules from experts. Modern descendants survive in business-rules engines, configuration software, troubleshooting systems and parts of clinical decision support.
