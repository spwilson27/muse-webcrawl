Boa constrictor

The boa constrictor (Boa constrictor), also known as the common boa, is a species of large, non-venomous, heavy-bodied snake that is frequently kept and bred in captivity. It is a member of the family Boidae, native to Central and South America. It is also known as the jiboia in Brazil and as the mazacuata in parts of Mexico.

Description

The boa constrictor is a heavy-bodied snake reaching lengths of up to 13 ft (4.0 m), though most adults measure 5–8 ft (1.5–2.4 m). Females are considerably larger than males. Its coloration is highly variable yet generally a tan or grey base with dark saddle markings along the body. The species gives birth to live young rather than laying eggs, with litter sizes averaging 25 snakes.

Behavior

Boa constrictors are usually solitary and nocturnal or crepuscular. They kill prey by constriction: after striking and seizing prey with their teeth, they wrap coils around it and apply pressure until it dies, then swallow it whole. They feed on small to medium-sized mammals, birds and occasionally other reptiles.

Distribution

The species is widely distributed through Central and South America, from northern Mexico to Argentina, inhabiting tropical rainforest, savanna and semi-arid scrub. Humans have also introduced populations to Florida and the Caribbean.

Conservation

The boa constrictor is listed in Appendix II of CITES; trade is regulated, although poaching and habitat loss remain concerns. Related topics: pythons, ambush predators.
