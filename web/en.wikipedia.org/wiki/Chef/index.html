A chef is a professional cook and tradesperson who is proficient in all aspects of food preparation, often focusing on a particular cuisine. The word "chef" is derived (and shortened) from the term chef de cuisine (French pronunciation: [ʃɛf də kɥizin]), the director or head of a kitchen. Chefs can receive formal training from an institution, as well as by apprenticing with an experienced chef.

Terminology

There are different terms that use the word chef in their titles, and deal with specific areas of food preparation. Examples include the sous-chef, who acts as the second-in-command in a kitchen, and the chef de partie, who handles a specific area of production. The kitchen brigade system, developed by Georges Auguste Escoffier in the late nineteenth century, is a hierarchy found in restaurants and hotels employing extensive staff, commonly referred to as "kitchen staff" in English.

Kitchen hierarchy

The classic brigade includes the chef de cuisine (head chef), the sous-chef (deputy), chefs de partie (station chefs) such as the saucier, poissonnier, rôtisseur, garde manger and pâtissier, the commis (junior cooks), and apprentices. In smaller operations a single chef may perform several of these roles.

Training and career

Many chefs are trained through apprenticeships or culinary schools, progressing from prep work through the stations of the kitchen over a number of years. Executive chefs and chef-owners additionally manage menus, food costs, staffing, and the overall culinary direction of an establishment. The profession has been popularised in recent decades by celebrity chefs and competitive cooking television programmes.

Uniform

The standard chef's uniform includes a tall pleated hat (toque blanche), a white double-breasted jacket, an apron, and trousers, traditionally in a black-and-white houndstooth pattern. The uniform is both practical — protecting against heat and splashes — and a marker of professional identity.
