Stylistic originsMusique concrète, sampling, collage, experimental music

Plunderphonics builds new music entirely from recognisable fragments of existing recordings

Plunderphonics is a music genre in which tracks are constructed by sampling recognisable musical works and altering them in some way. The term was coined by Canadian composer John Oswald in his 1985 essay "Plunderphonics, or Audio Piracy as a Compositional Prerogative", and his practice challenges notions of originality, authorship and copyright in recorded music.

John Oswald's work

Oswald's early plunderphonic pieces chopped pop songs into micro-fragments — his 1989 EP Plunderphonics included "Dab", built from a single Michael Jackson recording slowed and re-edited. Distributed free, the record was destroyed after legal threats from the Canadian Recording Industry Association. His 1988 self-released album of the same name reworked artists from the Beatles to Dolly Parton. Subsequent commissions included Grayfolded, a vast re-composition of the Grateful Dead's "Dark Star", and Plexure (1993), which compressed thousands of pop fragments into twenty minutes.

Characteristics

Unlike much sampling-based music, plunderphonics keeps its sources deliberately recognisable — the friction between the familiar original and its transformation is the point. Techniques include pitch-shifting, time-stretching, cutting a performance into single notes or phonemes, and layering multiple versions of the same work.

Influence

The concept anticipated and influenced mashup culture, vaporwave, and wider debates about fair use in the digital age. Oswald received the Governor General's Award in Visual and Media Arts in 2004.
