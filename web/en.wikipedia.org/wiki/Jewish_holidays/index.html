Jewish holidays

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jewish holidays, also known as Jewish festivals or Yamim Tovim (Hebrew: ימים טובים, "Good Days"), are holidays observed in Judaism and by Jews throughout the Hebrew calendar. They include religious, cultural, and national elements, derived from three sources: biblical mitzvot (commandments), rabbinic mandates, and the history of the State of Israel.

Biblical festivals

The Torah prescribes three pilgrimage festivals (Shalosh Regalim) on which Israelites journeyed to the Temple in Jerusalem: Passover (Pesach), commemorating the Exodus from Egypt and observed with the seder meal and abstention from leaven; Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, marking the wheat harvest and traditionally the giving of the Torah; and Sukkot, the Feast of Tabernacles, when meals are eaten in a temporary booth (sukkah). Closely associated are Shemini Atzeret and Simchat Torah.

High Holy Days

The autumn season opens with Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish New Year, marked by blowing the shofar, and culminates ten days later in Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, a 25-hour fast devoted to repentance and prayer. The intervening Days of Awe are a period of introspection.

Every week, Shabbat — from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall — is observed as a day of rest. Modern Israeli national holidays include Yom HaShoah (Holocaust Remembrance Day), Yom HaZikaron (Memorial Day), and Yom HaAtzmaut (Independence Day).

Observance

Major holidays involve prohibitions on work similar to Shabbat (with some allowances for food preparation on festivals), special prayers, Torah readings, and festive meals. Dates follow the lunisolar Hebrew calendar, so holidays move relative to the Gregorian calendar; in the Diaspora, several festivals are traditionally observed for an extra day.
