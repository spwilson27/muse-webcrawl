The University of Michigan (U-M, UMich, or Michigan) is a public research university in Ann Arbor, Michigan. Founded in 1817 in Detroit as the Catholepistemiad, or the University of Michigania, it moved to Ann Arbor in 1837 onto 40 acres (16 ha) of what is now its Central Campus. It is the state's oldest university and one of the foremost research universities in the United States, spending more than $1.7 billion annually on research and counting among its alumni and faculty numerous Nobel laureates, a U.S. president (Gerald Ford), and Google co-founder Larry Page.

History

The university was chartered by the Michigan Territory in 1817 under the direction of Reverend John Monteith and Father Gabriel Richard. After statehood it was re-established in Ann Arbor in 1837. The 19th century brought rapid growth under presidents Henry Philip Tappan and James Burrill Angell, and Michigan became a model for other public research universities. The university admitted women in 1870. In the modern era, the university's race-conscious admissions policies were litigated in the landmark cases Grutter v. Bollinger (2003) and Gratz v. Bollinger (2003).

Academics and campuses

Ann Arbor comprises 19 schools and colleges, including the College of Literature, Science, and the Arts, the Ross School of Business, the College of Engineering and the Medical School, together with Michigan Medicine, a leading academic medical center. Related campuses operate in Dearborn and Flint. The university library system holds more than 13 million volumes.

Athletics

The Michigan Wolverines compete in the Big Ten Conference. Their football team plays at Michigan Stadium — "The Big House," the largest stadium in the United States — and won the 2023 national championship. The fight song is "The Victors."
