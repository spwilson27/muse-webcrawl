Braess's paradox

Braess's paradox is the observation that adding one or more roads to a road network can end up impeding overall traffic through it. The paradox was first articulated by German mathematician Dietrich Braess in 1968.

The paradox may have analogies in electrical power grids and biological systems. It has been suggested that in theory, the improvement of a malfunctioning network could be accomplished by removing certain parts of it.

Explanation

Braess's explanation of the paradox relies on the fact that a Nash equilibrium of a network is not necessarily the optimal flow. When drivers act selfishly to minimize their own travel time, adding a shortcut road can attract so many drivers that overall congestion increases for everyone. Removing the road restores the better equilibrium.

Examples

Cited real-world examples include road closures in cities such as Stuttgart, New York City, and Seoul, where traffic reportedly improved after capacity was removed.
