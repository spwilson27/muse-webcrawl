Protein NMR

Protein NMR spectroscopy
TechniqueNuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy
Typical nuclei1H, 13C, 15N
Key experimentsHSQC, HNCA, NOESY, TOCSY
PioneersKurt Wüthrich, Richard R. Ernst

Protein NMR spectroscopy is a branch of nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy concerned with the study of proteins in solution. It is used to determine protein three-dimensional structures, characterize dynamics on timescales from picoseconds to seconds, and study interactions with ligands and other molecules — all under near-physiological conditions, complementing X-ray crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy.

Sample preparation

Proteins for NMR are usually produced by recombinant expression in Escherichia coli grown on media enriched with the stable isotopes 15N and 13C, because the naturally abundant NMR-active isotopes are otherwise too rare in carbon and nitrogen. Samples are typically 0.1–1 mM protein in aqueous buffer, often partially deuterated for larger proteins to slow relaxation.

Assignment and structure determination

The cornerstone experiment is the two-dimensional 1H–15N HSQC spectrum, in which each backbone amide gives one cross-peak — a "fingerprint" of the folded state. Triple-resonance experiments (HNCA, HNCACB, CBCA(CO)NH and related pulse sequences) correlate neighbouring residues so that resonances can be assigned sequentially to the amino-acid sequence. Distance restraints are then obtained from NOESY spectra via the nuclear Overhauser effect, dihedral restraints from chemical shifts and coupling constants, and orientational restraints from residual dipolar couplings. Simulated annealing against these restraints yields an ensemble of conformers representing the solution structure.

Dynamics and interactions

Relaxation measurements (R1, R2, heteronuclear NOE) and relaxation-dispersion experiments reveal motions from fast bond librations to slow conformational exchange. Chemical-shift perturbation mapping in HSQC titrations is widely used to locate binding sites and measure dissociation constants, for example in fragment-based drug discovery.

Limitations

Because transverse relaxation accelerates with molecular size, conventional solution NMR is most effective for proteins below roughly 25–40 kDa, although TROSY techniques, deuteration and methyl-specific labelling extend the range to assemblies of several hundred kilodaltons. Kurt Wüthrich shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing NMR methods for determining the structures of biological macromolecules in solution.
