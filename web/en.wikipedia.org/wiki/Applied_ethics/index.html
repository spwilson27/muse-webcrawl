Applied ethics

Applied ethics is the practical application of moral considerations. It is ethics with respect to real-world actions and their moral considerations in the areas of private and public life, the professions, health, technology, law, and leadership. Whereas normative ethics asks which general moral principles are correct, and meta-ethics asks what moral claims mean, applied ethics examines controversial moral issues — abortion, euthanasia, animal rights, capital punishment, environmental protection, and the conduct of war and business — in the light of ethical theory, empirical facts, and reasoned argument.

Applied ethics addresses concrete dilemmas in medicine, law, technology, and public policy.

History

Although philosophers from Aristotle onward addressed practical conduct, applied ethics emerged as a distinct, rapidly growing field in the late 1960s and 1970s, driven by civil rights and anti-war movements, medical scandals and technological advances (organ transplantation, life support, genetic engineering), and environmental awareness. Dedicated journals such as Philosophy & Public Affairs (founded 1971) and the Journal of Applied Philosophy (1984) institutionalised the field. Peter Singer's work on world poverty and animal liberation, and the principlist framework of Beauchamp and Childress in biomedical ethics, became paradigmatic examples.

Major areas

Bioethics and medical ethics address patient autonomy, informed consent, end-of-life decisions, research on human subjects, and allocation of scarce medical resources; institutional ethics committees and codes such as the Declaration of Helsinki embody its conclusions. Business ethics examines corporate responsibility, whistleblowing, marketing, and workplace fairness. Environmental ethics considers duties to nature, future generations, and non-human animals, informing climate policy debates. Professional ethics codify standards for lawyers, engineers, journalists, and soldiers. Newer branches include computer and information ethics, covering privacy, algorithmic bias, and artificial intelligence, and neuroethics, addressing cognitive enhancement and brain interventions.

Methods

Practitioners apply frameworks — consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, and principlism — to cases, use casuistry (reasoning from paradigm cases), and seek a "reflective equilibrium" between principles and considered judgements. Debates in applied ethics frequently shape law and public policy, from research regulation to environmental standards.
