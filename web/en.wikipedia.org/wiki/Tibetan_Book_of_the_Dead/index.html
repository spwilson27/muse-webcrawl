The Bardo Thodol (Tibetan: "Liberation Through Hearing in the Intermediate State"), popularly known in the West as the Tibetan Book of the Dead, is a funerary text of the Nyingma school of Tibetan Buddhism. It describes the experiences of consciousness in the bardo, the interval between death and rebirth, and is read aloud to the dying and the recently dead to guide them toward liberation.

Origins

Tradition attributes the text to the eighth-century Indian master Padmasambhava, who is said to have concealed it as a terma (treasure text). It was revealed in the fourteenth century by the tertön Karma Lingpa on Mount Gampodar.

Contents

The text guides the deceased through three bardos of death:

  • The chikhai bardo, the moment of death, in which the Clear Light of reality dawns; recognising it brings liberation.
  • The chonyid bardo, the experience of reality, in which the peaceful and then the wrathful deities appear — understood as projections of the deceased's own mind.
  • The sidpa bardo, the bardo of rebirth, in which the deceased, having failed to attain liberation, is drawn toward a new birth, judged by Yama and pursued by karmic visions.

The bardos are traditionally said to last up to forty-nine days. Throughout, the reader exhorts the dead person to recognise the visions as their own mental projections rather than fleeing or clinging to them.

In the West

The first English translation, edited by Walter Evans-Wentz and translated by Lama Kazi Dawa-Samdup, appeared in 1927 and gave the work its famous English title, modelled on the Egyptian Book of the Dead. Carl Jung wrote a psychological commentary for a later edition. The text influenced the 1960s counterculture — Timothy Leary's The Psychedelic Experience (1964) adapted it as a guide for LSD sessions — and remains the best-known Tibetan Buddhist text in the West. Later translations include those of Robert Thurman (1994) and the first complete translation of the full treasure cycle (2005).
