Georgism, also called in modern times geoism, is an economic ideology holding that people should own the value they produce themselves, while the economic rent derived from land — including natural resources and commons — should belong equally to all members of society. It derives from the writings of the American economist and social reformer Henry George, above all his bestselling book Progress and Poverty (1879).

The single tax

George's central policy proposal was a tax on the unimproved value of land — the land value tax — set high enough to replace other taxes, hence the movement's slogan of the "single tax". Because the supply of land is fixed, Georgists argue such a tax cannot discourage production or be shifted onto tenants, and that it removes the incentive to speculate in idle land while untaxing wages, trade and improvements.

History and influence

Georgism became a mass movement in the 1880s–90s: George nearly won the 1886 New York City mayoral election, and his ideas influenced figures from Leo Tolstoy and Sun Yat-sen to Winston Churchill's "People's Budget" era Liberal Party. Land value taxation was implemented in various forms in places including Denmark, Australia, New Zealand, Taiwan and parts of Pennsylvania. Planned "single-tax colonies" such as Fairhope, Alabama and Arden, Delaware were founded on Georgist principles.

Modern Georgism

Contemporary Georgists extend the argument to spectrum rights, pollution (Pigouvian charges), natural resource royalties and citizen's dividends on the Alaska Permanent Fund model. The Henry George theorem in public economics, and the observation that "all taxes come out of rent" (ATCOR), are often cited in its support. Critics question whether land rent alone could fund a modern state and the difficulty of valuing land apart from its improvements.
