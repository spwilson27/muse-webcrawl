South Slavic languages

The South Slavic languages are one of three branches of the Slavic languages, spoken by the South Slavs in the Balkans. There are approximately 30 million speakers, primarily in Bosnia and Herzegovina, Bulgaria, Croatia, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, and Slovenia.

Classification

Unlike the other branches of Slavic, the South Slavic languages form a dialect continuum interrupted in the middle by the non-Slavic languages Hungarian, Romanian, and Albanian. They are conventionally divided into two subgroups:

  • Eastern group: Bulgarian and Macedonian, which share the loss of most noun cases, a postposed definite article, and the loss of the infinitive — features of the Balkan sprachbund shared with neighbouring non-Slavic languages.
  • Western group: Slovene and the Serbo-Croatian continuum (Bosnian, Croatian, Montenegrin, and Serbian), which retain case systems and infinitives.

History

The South Slavs settled the Balkans in the 6th and 7th centuries CE. The first Slavic literary language, Old Church Slavonic, was based on the South Slavic dialect of the Thessaloniki region and was codified in the 9th century by the missionaries Cyril and Methodius using the Glagolitic alphabet, from which the Cyrillic script developed. The Torlakian dialects of southeastern Serbia form a transition between the eastern and western groups.

Writing systems

South Slavic languages use both major Slavic scripts: Bulgarian, Macedonian, and Serbian use Cyrillic (Serbian also officially uses Latin), while Croatian, Bosnian, Montenegrin, and Slovene use Latin alphabets.
