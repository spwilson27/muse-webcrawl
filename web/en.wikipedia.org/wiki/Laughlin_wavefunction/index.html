Laughlin wavefunction

Laughlin wavefunction
Proposed byRobert B. Laughlin (1983)
DescribesGround state of the fractional quantum Hall effect at filling ν = 1/m, m odd
Key predictionsFractionally charged quasiparticles (charge e/m), fractional statistics

The Laughlin wavefunction is an ansatz for the many-body ground state of a two-dimensional electron gas in a strong magnetic field, proposed by Robert Laughlin in 1983 to explain the fractional quantum Hall effect observed at filling fraction ν = 1/3. It is one of the most celebrated trial wavefunctions in condensed matter physics and earned Laughlin a share of the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Form

Writing the position of the j-th electron as a complex number zj = xj + iyj in units of the magnetic length, the wavefunction takes the form

ψm(z1, …, zN) = ∏j<k (zj − zk)m · exp(−¼ Σl |zl|2)

where m must be an odd integer so that the wavefunction is antisymmetric under exchange of two electrons, as required for fermions. The state at odd m corresponds to filling fraction ν = 1/m of the lowest Landau level. The factor (zj − zk)m builds in strong correlations: the probability of finding two electrons close together vanishes rapidly, minimizing the Coulomb repulsion.

Quasiholes and fractional charge

Laughlin showed that the elementary excitations of this state — created by threading an additional flux quantum through the system — carry fractional electric charge e* = e/m and obey fractional (anyonic) exchange statistics. Shot-noise experiments in the late 1990s confirmed the e/3 charge of the quasiparticles at ν = 1/3, and interferometry experiments have since probed their braiding statistics.

Plasma analogy

The squared modulus of the wavefunction can be mapped onto the Boltzmann weight of a classical two-dimensional one-component plasma, an analogy Laughlin used to argue that the state is a uniform incompressible liquid. The incompressibility explains the energy gap protecting the quantised Hall plateau.
