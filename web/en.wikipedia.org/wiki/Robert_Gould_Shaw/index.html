Robert Gould Shaw

Born: October 10, 1837, Boston, Massachusetts
Died: July 18, 1863, Fort Wagner, South Carolina
Service: Union Army; Colonel
Unit: 54th Massachusetts Infantry Regiment

Robert Gould Shaw (October 10, 1837 – July 18, 1863) was an American officer in the Union Army during the American Civil War. As colonel of the 54th Massachusetts Infantry Regiment, one of the first African-American units in the Union Army, he was killed leading the assault on Fort Wagner in South Carolina.

Early life and war service

Shaw was born into a wealthy Boston abolitionist family; his parents, Francis George and Sarah Shaw, were Unitarian philanthropists in the circle of Emerson and other reformers. He left Harvard to join the Army at the outbreak of war, served as a lieutenant in the 2nd Massachusetts, fought at Antietam, and was promoted to captain. In early 1863, Governor John Andrew offered him command of the newly authorized 54th Massachusetts, recruited from free Black men across the North. Shaw initially hesitated, then accepted, and threw himself into training the regiment at Camp Meigs in Readville, insisting on its dignity—including a protest, joined by his men, against unequal pay.

Fort Wagner and death

Sent to South Carolina, the 54th fought at Grimball's Landing and then, on July 18, 1863, was chosen to lead the frontal assault on Battery Wagner, a Confederate strongpoint guarding Charleston Harbor. Shaw mounted the parapet waving his sword and urging his men forward before being shot through the heart. The attack failed, with roughly 270 casualties in the regiment, but its gallantry decisively refuted claims that Black soldiers would not fight and spurred the enlistment of some 180,000 Black troops. Confederates buried Shaw in a mass grave with his men—intended as an insult, which his father declared the fittest resting place for him.

Legacy

Augustus Saint-Gaudens's Shaw Memorial on Boston Common, unveiled in 1897, is considered a masterpiece of American sculpture. Shaw's wartime letters home were published by his family, and the story of the 54th was retold in the 1989 film Glory.
