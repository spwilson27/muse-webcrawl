Petersen family

In graph theory, the Petersen family is a set of seven undirected graphs that includes the Petersen graph and forms a forbidden minor for linklessly embeddable graphs. The family is named after the Danish mathematician Julius Petersen, whose 1898 paper introduced the Petersen graph.

Definition

The Petersen family consists of the seven graphs obtained from the complete graph K6 by repeatedly applying Δ–Y and Y–Δ transformations, which replace a degree-three vertex by a triangle or vice versa while preserving intrinsic knottedness and linkedness properties.[1] Its members are K6, the Petersen graph, and five intermediate graphs with 6 to 9 vertices; the largest intermediate member, sometimes denoted K3,3,1,1-related forms, connects the two extremes of the family.

Linkless embedding

A graph is linklessly embeddable if it can be embedded in three-dimensional space so that no two disjoint cycles form a nontrivial link. Neil Robertson, Paul Seymour, and Robin Thomas proved that a graph is linklessly embeddable if and only if it does not contain any member of the Petersen family as a graph minor — the analogue, for linkless embedding, of Wagner's theorem characterising planar graphs by the forbidden minors K5 and K3,3. Every member of the Petersen family is minor-minimal intrinsically linked: each is itself not linklessly embeddable, but every proper minor is.
