A governor-general (plural governors-general) is the title of an office-holder appointed to represent a monarch or head of state in a territory where that head of state does not reside. The office is best known from the Commonwealth realms, where a governor-general serves as the representative of the monarch in an independent country such as Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Jamaica, and the other realms.

Role and powers

In the Commonwealth realms, the governor-general performs the ceremonial and constitutional functions of the head of state domestically: summoning and dissolving parliament, giving royal assent to legislation, appointing the prime minister and other officials on ministerial advice, serving as commander-in-chief, and exercising reserve powers in exceptional circumstances. Appointment is formally by the monarch, in practice on the advice of the realm's prime minister, usually for a term of about five years.

Historical usage

Historically, governors-general governed major colonial possessions — British India (Governor-General of India, later styled Viceroy), French Indochina, the Dutch East Indies, and others. Some modern states outside the Commonwealth also use the title, such as the governor-general of Iran's provinces (ostandar is distinct) — most prominently today, South Korea used the title for its provincial governors in translation, and the office of Governor-General of Taiwan existed under Japanese rule. Within federations, lieutenant governors and state governors occupy analogous subnational roles, distinct from the national governor-general.
