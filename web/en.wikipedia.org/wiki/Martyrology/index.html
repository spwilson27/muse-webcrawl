Martyrology

A martyrology is a catalogue or list of martyrs and other saints arranged in the order of their anniversaries (feast days), along with brief notices of their lives and deaths. Martyrologies are a characteristic product of the cult of the saints in the Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church, and were historically read aloud daily in chapter houses and refectories, particularly at the office of Prime.

Development

The earliest martyrologies grew out of local church calendars recording the depositiones (burial dates) of martyrs. The most important early example is the Martyrologium Hieronymianum, falsely attributed to Saint Jerome, compiled in the 5th–6th centuries from Roman, African and Eastern lists. In the early Middle Ages, the Venerable Bede produced the first "historical" martyrology with narrative notices for each entry; the form was developed by Florus of Lyon, Ado of Vienne, and Usuard of Saint-Germain, whose 9th-century martyrology became the direct ancestor of the Roman Martyrology.

Roman Martyrology

The Martyrologium Romanum (Roman Martyrology) was first published in 1583 under Pope Gregory XIII, chiefly through the work of Cardinal Caesar Baronius, in connection with the Gregorian calendar reform. It lists several thousand saints and blessed with the place and manner of their martyrdom or death. Revised editions appeared in the 17th and 18th centuries (notably under Benedict XIV) and in 1913. A substantially revised edition was promulgated in 2001 (second typical edition 2004), incorporating modern historical scholarship and saints canonised in the intervening centuries.

Other traditions

Analogous books include the Byzantine Synaxarion and Menologion, and calendars of saints in Anglican and Lutheran usage. In the Reformation era, John Foxe's Acts and Monuments (1563, "Foxe's Book of Martyrs") functioned as a Protestant martyrology, while the Mennonite Martyrs Mirror (1660) records Anabaptist martyrs.
