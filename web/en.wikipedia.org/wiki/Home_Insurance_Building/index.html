Home Insurance Building

The Home Insurance Building, completed in Chicago in 1885, is generally regarded as the first skyscraper. Designed by William Le Baron Jenney for the Home Insurance Company of New York, it was the first tall office building whose weight was carried largely by an internal iron and steel frame rather than by its masonry walls, freeing the exterior for large windows and light construction.

The Home Insurance Building, LaSalle and Adams Streets, Chicago.

Design and construction

Jenney, a civil engineer who had served in the Civil War, designed a ten-storey building (two storeys were added in 1891) in which beams and columns of cast iron, wrought iron and — in the upper floors — Bessemer steel carried the floors, with the brick and stone facade hung as a curtain rather than bearing the full load. The innovation reduced weight dramatically — the building weighed about one-third of an equivalent masonry building — on Chicago's soft clay soils, and allowed the distinctive broad "Chicago windows". The structure survived scrutiny during the debates over its safety and became a model for the Chicago School of architecture.

Significance

Whether it was literally the "first" skyscraper is debated — earlier buildings used partial iron framing — but the Home Insurance Building is widely credited with pioneering the metal skeleton frame that made ever-taller buildings practical, developed further by firms such as Burnham and Root and by Louis Sullivan. It helped establish Chicago as the birthplace of the skyscraper after the Great Fire of 1871 created demand for rapid, fire-resistant rebuilding.

Demolition

The building was demolished in 1931 to make way for the Field Building (now the LaSalle Bank Building). An investigation during demolition confirmed the extent of its early metal framing, cementing its historical reputation. A plaque marks the site in the Chicago Loop.
