Labor economics is the branch of economics that studies how labour markets work: how workers and employers interact to determine wages, employment, working conditions, and the distribution of income. It examines both the supply of labour by households — decisions about participation, hours, education, and migration — and the demand for labour by firms, which in the neoclassical model hire workers up to the point where the wage equals the value of the marginal product of labour.

Major topics

  • Human capital: education and training raise productivity and earnings; Gary Becker's work formalised the analysis of investment in schooling and the resulting earnings profiles.
  • Wage differentials: differences in pay are explained by skill, occupation, and compensating differentials for unpleasant or risky jobs (an idea going back to Adam Smith), as well as by signalling and efficiency wages.
  • Unemployment: frictional unemployment from job search, structural unemployment from skill mismatch, and the search-and-matching framework of Diamond, Mortensen, and Pissarides.
  • Institutions: trade unions, minimum wages, and employment protection. David Card and Alan Krueger's 1994 study of fast-food employment famously questioned simple predictions that minimum wages must reduce employment.
  • Discrimination: taste-based and statistical discrimination by gender and race, and the persistent earnings gaps they produce.
  • Monopsony: employer market power over wages, revived in modern research on labour-market concentration.

Data and methods

Labor economics is among the most empirical fields of economics, relying on household surveys (such as the US Current Population Survey), administrative records, and — since the "credibility revolution" — natural experiments and quasi-experimental designs to identify causal effects of policies.
