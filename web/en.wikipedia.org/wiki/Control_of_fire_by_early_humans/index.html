Control of fire by early humans

The control of fire by early humans was a critical technological innovation in human evolution. Fire provided warmth, protection from predators, light, and a way to cook food, and its habitual use is associated with major anatomical and social changes in the genus Homo.

Evidence

Claims for the earliest controlled fire reach back more than a million years: burned bones and ash at sites such as Swartkrans and Koobi Fora, and in situ ash at Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa dated to about 1 million years ago, are often cited. Burned flint and wood from Gesher Benot Ya'aqov in Israel date to roughly 790,000 years ago. Unambiguous, habitual fire use — hearths in caves such as Qesem (about 400,000 years ago) — becomes widespread in the Middle Pleistocene, and by about 100,000 years ago fire was used by Neanderthals and early modern humans alike for cooking, heat-treating stone and making adhesives.

Consequences

Richard Wrangham's "cooking hypothesis" argues that cooked food — softer, safer and more energy-dense — enabled the smaller guts and larger brains of Homo erectus. Fire also extended the active day, enabled colonisation of colder climates, and created a social focus around the hearth.
