Fluvial terrace
FieldFluvial geomorphology
ProcessRiver incision and deposition
TypesPaired and unpaired; fill and strath

Fluvial terraces are elongated, step-like landforms that flank the sides of floodplains and fluvial valleys all over the world. They consist of a flat surface (the tread) bounded on one side by a rising slope or escarpment (the scarp or riser) and on the other by the valley side. Terraces are remnants of former floodplains or valley floors that were abandoned when the river incised downward—because of tectonic uplift, falling base level (often sea level), or changes in climate, discharge, and sediment supply.

Formation

When a river aggrades, it builds up a valley fill; when conditions shift so that incision dominates, the channel cuts down into that fill, stranding the old surface as a terrace. Repetition of this cycle produces staircases of terraces, with the highest terraces being the oldest. Glacial–interglacial climate cycles of the Quaternary have driven terrace formation along many mid-latitude rivers.

Types

Paired terraces occur at the same elevation on both sides of a valley, indicating relatively rapid, steady incision. Unpaired terraces form at differing heights as the channel migrates laterally while incising. A fill terrace is cut into thick alluvial deposits, whereas a strath terrace is a bedrock surface thinly veneered with gravel, recording lateral planation before incision.

Significance

Terrace sequences are important archives of landscape evolution and past climate, and their deposits can be dated by radiocarbon, luminescence, and cosmogenic nuclide methods. Terraces have also preserved archaeological sites and artifacts, and terrace gravels are widely exploited as aggregate resources.
