Slip (materials science)

In materials science, slip is the large-scale plastic deformation of a crystalline material in which one part of a crystal slides relative to another along specific crystallographic planes and directions. Slip is the primary mechanism of ductility in metals: it proceeds by the motion of dislocations — line defects in the crystal lattice — across slip planes, driven by resolved shear stress.

Slip systems

A slip system is the combination of a slip plane and a slip direction lying in it. Slip occurs most easily on the most densely packed planes and in the closest-packed directions, where the energy barrier to dislocation motion (the Peierls stress) is lowest. Face-centred cubic metals such as aluminium and copper have twelve {111}<110> slip systems and are correspondingly ductile. Body-centred cubic metals slip mainly on {110}<111> systems, while hexagonal close-packed metals such as magnesium have few easy systems and are less formable at room temperature.

Schmid's law

Slip begins on the system experiencing the highest resolved shear stress, given by Schmid's law: the applied tensile stress multiplied by the product of the cosines of the angles between the load axis and the slip-plane normal and slip direction (the Schmid factor). The critical resolved shear stress at which slip starts is a material property. Slip leaves visible slip bands and steps on polished surfaces, and intersecting slip systems, forest dislocations, and grain boundaries impede motion — the basis of work hardening and grain-size strengthening.
