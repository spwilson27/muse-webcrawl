George Whitefield (27 December 1714 – 30 September 1770), also known as George Whitfield, was an English Anglican cleric and evangelist who was one of the founders of Methodism and the leading figure of the evangelical revival known as the First Great Awakening in Britain and the North American colonies. His open-air preaching drew crowds of tens of thousands, and he was arguably the best-known religious figure in the American colonies before the Revolution.

Early life and conversion

Whitefield was born in Gloucester, the son of an innkeeper. At Oxford he joined the "Holy Club" led by John and Charles Wesley, underwent a conversion experience in 1735, and was ordained in the Church of England in 1736. His preaching in London churches immediately attracted huge congregations.

Field preaching and the Great Awakening

When pulpits were closed to him, Whitefield pioneered preaching in the open air, addressing miners at Kingswood near Bristol in 1739 and vast crowds on Moorfields and Kennington Common in London. Possessed of a powerful, perfectly projected voice — Benjamin Franklin calculated that he could be heard by more than 30,000 people at once — Whitefield made seven preaching tours of America between 1738 and 1770. His 1739–41 tour from New England to Georgia ignited the colonial Great Awakening, and his published journals and sermons made him an early transatlantic celebrity. In Georgia he founded the Bethesda orphanage near Savannah.

Theology and relations with the Wesleys

Whitefield was a convinced Calvinist, which set him at odds with John Wesley's Arminian theology; their public disagreement in 1739–41 split the early Methodist movement into Calvinistic and Wesleyan branches, though the two men later reconciled personally. Whitefield remained a priest of the Church of England while leading the Calvinistic Methodist societies in Wales and England and supporting the Countess of Huntingdon's chapels.

Death and legacy

Whitefield died at Newburyport, Massachusetts, in 1770 while on his seventh American tour, and was buried there beneath the pulpit of the Old South Presbyterian Church. Through his preaching, itinerancy and mastery of print publicity, he helped create a shared evangelical culture across the English-speaking Atlantic world.
