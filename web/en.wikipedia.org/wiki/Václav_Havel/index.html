Václav Havel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Václav Havel (5 October 1936 – 18 December 2011) was a Czech statesman, playwright, essayist and dissident. As a banned playwright under communist Czechoslovakia he co-founded Charter 77 (1977), wrote the era's defining dissident essay The Power of the Powerless (1978) — arguing that the system rested on everyone “living within the lie” and could be subverted by “living in truth” — and spent some five years in prison. In November 1989 he led the Civic Forum through the Velvet Revolution and was elected president of Czechoslovakia; after its dissolution he served as the first president of the Czech Republic (1993–2003), steering it into NATO (1999) and toward the EU.

Havel's presidency was morally rather than administratively cast: apologies for the expulsion of the Sudeten Germans, advocacy for human rights from Cuba to Burma (and support for the Iraq war, controversial among his admirers), and a public philosophy of conscience against consumer indifference. His plays (The Garden Party, 1963; The Memorandum, 1965; Audience, 1975) and essays made him the prototype of the dissident-intellectual as statesman — a role he both fulfilled and ironised in his final work, the play Leaving (2007). Samizdat circulation made his essays famous before they could be published.
