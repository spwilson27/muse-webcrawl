Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geometric dimensioning and tolerancing (GD&T) is a system for defining and communicating engineering tolerances by means of a symbolic language on engineering drawings and computer-generated three-dimensional solid models. It specifies the geometry of features — their form, orientation, location, and run-out — relative to datum reference frames, expressing the allowable variation that still permits a part to function and assemble correctly.

Principles

Unlike traditional coordinate tolerancing, which gives rectangular tolerance zones, GD&T defines tolerance zones matched to the geometry of the feature, such as cylindrical zones for holes. This often yields larger, more functional tolerances. Key concepts include datums (theoretically exact reference points, axes, and planes), feature control frames (the rectangular boxes containing the characteristic symbol, tolerance value, and datum references), and material condition modifiers such as maximum material condition (MMC), which can grant "bonus tolerance" as a feature departs from its worst-case size.

Characteristics

The principal geometric characteristics are grouped as form (straightness, flatness, circularity, cylindricity), profile (of a line and of a surface), orientation (angularity, perpendicularity, parallelism), location (position, concentricity, symmetry), and run-out (circular and total run-out).

Standards

In the United States, GD&T is governed by the ASME Y14.5 standard (current version Y14.5-2018), descended from military standards of the 1940s developed with the work of Stanley Parker at the Royal Torpedo Factory in Scotland. Internationally, the corresponding concepts appear in the ISO GPS (Geometrical Product Specifications) system of standards, which differs from ASME practice in terminology and some defaults.
