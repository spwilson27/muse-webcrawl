Simone Weil (1909–1943) was a French philosopher, mystic and political activist whose writings — almost all published after her early death — have made her one of the most revered and unsettling thinkers of the twentieth century. A brilliant student (she entered the École Normale Supérieure in Simone de Beauvoir's cohort), she taught philosophy in provincial lycées while organising for trade unions; in 1934 she took a year to work incognito on factory assembly lines in Paris, an experience of affliction (malheur) that marked everything she later wrote. She served briefly with an anarchist militia in the Spanish Civil War, and in 1938, at Solesmes, underwent the mystical experiences that turned her thought toward God — though she refused baptism and remained, deliberately, outside the Church, at the threshold.

Thought

Weil's central concepts are attention — the selfless, waiting regard that is, for her, the substance of both prayer and justice ("Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity") — affliction, suffering that crushes the soul and uproots the person, distinct from ordinary pain; decreation, the undoing of the self's false centrality so that God, and the neighbour, may be; and gravity and grace, the two forces of her posthumous book's title — the mechanical weight of force and ego pulling all things down, and the supernatural lightness that alone interrupts it. Politically she was unsparing: Reflections Concerning the Causes of Liberty and Social Oppression (1934) criticised Marxism's illusions as sharply as capitalism; her last work, The Need for Roots (written in London for the Free French, 1943), proposed the reconstruction of French society around obligations before rights, and rootedness against the uprooting she diagnosed everywhere. She died at Ashford in 1943, of tuberculosis and — refusing food beyond what the people of occupied France could eat, by the usual account — self-starvation; the coroner recorded that she killed herself by refusing to eat, a verdict her admirers read as the final, terrible consistency of her ethic of attention.

Legacy

Albert Camus, who edited her works, called her "the only great spirit of our times"; her notebooks and essays — Gravity and Grace, Waiting for God, the Iliad essay ("The Poem of Force", which reads Homer as the West's truest mirror) — have influenced philosophy, theology, literary criticism and political thought out of all proportion to her 34 years and posthumous, fragmentary corpus.
