Superiority theory

The superiority theory of humour holds that laughter arises from a feeling of superiority over others — or over one's own former state — typically at their misfortunes, defects, or ignorance. It is one of the classical theories of humour, alongside relief theory and incongruity theory, and traces its origins to Plato and Aristotle.

History

Plato, in the Philebus, analysed the pleasure in comedy as containing malice: we laugh at the self-ignorance of others, an enjoyment mixed with pain. Aristotle treated wit as "educated insolence" and comedy as an imitation of people worse than average. The theory was developed most fully by Thomas Hobbes, who in Leviathan (1651) defined laughter as "sudden glory" arising from "the apprehension of some deformed thing in another, by comparison whereof they suddenly applaud themselves". Later proponents and discussants include René Descartes, Charles Baudelaire, and Henri Bergson.

Criticism

Critics note that much laughter — at wordplay, absurdity, or pleasant surprise — involves no obvious target of derision, and that superiority cannot readily explain laughing at oneself or with others. Modern research generally treats superiority (or "disparagement") as one component of humour, particularly relevant to jokes about out-groups, rather than a complete explanation.
