Rape of Belgium

The Rape of Belgium was the treatment of Belgian civilians by German forces during the invasion and occupation of Belgium in the First World War. In August and September 1914, fearing francs-tireurs (guerrilla resisters), the German army carried out reprisals that killed several thousand civilians and destroyed towns including Leuven, where the university library was burned, as well as Dinant, where 674 civilians were executed in the largest single massacre.

Events

Altogether approximately 6,000 Belgians were killed during the invasion period, and some 17,700 died during the occupation, which also saw mass deportations of Belgian workers to Germany in 1916 and the systematic extraction of Belgian industry and resources.

Propaganda and historiography

Reports of atrocities — some documented, some exaggerated or invented — became a powerful theme of Allied propaganda through works such as the Bryce Report (1915) and helped turn international (especially British and American) opinion against Germany. After the war the atrocity stories were partly discredited as propaganda, but modern historians, notably John Horne and Alan Kramer, have re-established the broad reality of the 1914 killings and destruction as a matter of German military policy in response to perceived irregular warfare.
