Bohemond I of Antioch (c. 1054 – 3 March 1111), born Mark of Hauteville, was a Norman prince of the Hauteville family, the eldest son of Robert Guiscard, and one of the leaders of the First Crusade. He founded the Principality of Antioch, the northernmost of the crusader states. His imposing stature and military skill impressed contemporaries, including the Byzantine princess and historian Anna Komnene, who described him in the Alexiad with a mixture of admiration and distrust.

Early career

Bohemond campaigned with his father against the Byzantine Empire in the Balkans in the 1080s, winning victories at Ioannina and Larissa. Passed over in the Apulian succession in favour of his half-brother Roger Borsa, he compensated himself with the principality of Taranto and other lands in southern Italy.

First Crusade and Antioch

In 1096 Bohemond took the cross, ostensibly in devotion but also with an eye to carving out an eastern lordship. At Constantinople he swore the oath demanded by Emperor Alexios I to restore former imperial lands. During the siege of Antioch (1097–98), Bohemond arranged the city's betrayal by the tower guard Firuz and argued successfully that the city should be his, breaking his oath to the emperor on the pretext that Alexios had failed to aid the crusade. From 1100 to 1103 he was held captive by the Danishmendid Turks; his nephew Tancred governed Antioch in his absence.

Later years and death

Returning to Europe in 1105, Bohemond toured France, married Constance, daughter of King Philip I, and raised an army to attack the Byzantine Empire itself. His invasion of 1107–08 ended in failure before Dyrrhachium, and the Treaty of Devol (1108) forced him to acknowledge Alexios as his overlord — terms the Antiochenes later repudiated. Bohemond died in Apulia in 1111 and was buried at Canosa di Puglia. His son Bohemond II eventually succeeded him in Antioch.
