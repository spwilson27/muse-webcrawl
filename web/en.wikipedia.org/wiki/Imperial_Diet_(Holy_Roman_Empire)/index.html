Imperial Diet (Holy Roman Empire)

The Imperial Diet (Latin: Dieta Imperii or Comitium Imperiale; German: Reichstag) was the deliberative body of the Holy Roman Empire. It was not a legislature in the modern sense; its members were the Imperial Estates, and its meetings were as much courtly and diplomatic occasions as parliamentary ones. A "diet" was originally an assembly convened by the emperor (or king) at irregular intervals, in various cities of the Empire.

Composition

By the early modern period the Diet consisted of three colleges:

  • The Council of Electors (Kurfürstenrat) — the prince-electors who chose the emperor.
  • The Council of Princes (Fürstenrat) — secular and ecclesiastical princes, subdivided into the ecclesiastical and secular benches; some votes were individual (viril votes) and others shared by counts and lords as collective (curiate) votes.
  • The Council of Imperial Cities (Städterat) — representatives of the free imperial cities, whose assent was long treated as advisory before being recognised as necessary in 1648.

Decisions required agreement between the colleges and ratification by the emperor; the resulting decree was called a Reichsabschied (recess of the Diet).

History

Famous diets include the Diet of Worms (1521), which condemned Martin Luther; the Diets of Speyer (1526, 1529), the second giving rise to the term "Protestant"; and the Diet of Augsburg (1555), which produced the Peace of Augsburg between Lutheran and Catholic estates. From 1663, the Diet sat continuously at Regensburg as the Perpetual Diet (Immerwährender Reichstag), in practice a permanent congress of ambassadors of the Imperial Estates, until the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806.
