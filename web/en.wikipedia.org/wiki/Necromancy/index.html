Necromancy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Necromancy
DefinitionMagic involving communication with the dead
EtymologyGreek nekromanteia: nekros (corpse) + manteia (divination)
PurposeDivination, prophecy, obtaining hidden knowledge

Necromancy is the practice of magic involving communication with the dead by summoning their spirits as apparitions or visions, or by raising them bodily, for the purpose of divination — imparting the means to foretell future events, discover hidden knowledge, or "return a person to life". The word derives from the Greek nekromanteía, a compound of nekrós ("dead body") and manteía ("divination").

Antiquity

Necromantic rites are attested across the ancient world. In Book 11 of Homer's Odyssey, Odysseus travels to the edge of the underworld and summons the shades of the dead with blood sacrifices (the nekyia) to question the prophet Tiresias. Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greek, and Roman sources describe specialists who consulted ghosts at tombs and oracles of the dead (nekromanteia), such as the oracle at Ephyra. Practitioners were sought for knowledge of the future, the location of buried treasure, or the identification of thieves and murderers.

Biblical and early Christian views

The Hebrew Bible expressly prohibits necromancy (Deuteronomy 18:10–12), yet recounts the famous episode of the Witch of Endor, who at King Saul's request raises the spirit of the prophet Samuel (1 Samuel 28). Early Christian writers generally condemned necromancy as commerce with demons rather than with genuine souls of the dead — a framing that dominated the medieval understanding of the practice.

Medieval and early modern necromancy

In medieval Latin, nigromantia ("black divination", by folk etymology from niger, black) came to denote learned ceremonial magic generally: the summoning and binding of spirits and demons by clerically educated practitioners using conjurations, magic circles, and ritual implements. Surviving manuals (grimoires) such as the Munich Manual of Demonic Magic and the Sworn Book of Honorius preserve such rites. The Church prosecuted necromancy as heresy and sorcery, and the practice was a recurring charge in trials from the later Middle Ages into the witch-trial era.

Modern usage and fiction

In the 19th century, necromancy was loosely associated with Spiritualism and séances. In modern popular culture — fantasy literature, role-playing games, and video games — "necromancy" has broadened to mean black magic centred on death: raising undead servants, draining life, and manipulating corpses and souls, as in the works of Tolkien (the Necromancer of Dol Guldur), role-playing systems such as Dungeons & Dragons, and countless fantasy novels.
