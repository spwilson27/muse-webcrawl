Space colonization
Also calledSpace settlement, space humanization
Proposed sitesMoon, Mars, asteroids, free-space habitats, outer planet moons
Key conceptsIn-situ resource utilization, closed life support, radiation shielding
StatusNo permanent off-Earth settlement exists yet

Space colonization (also called space settlement) is the concept of permanent, autonomous human habitation at locations outside Earth. It is a major theme in science fiction and a long-term goal discussed by space agencies and private companies. Proponents argue that establishing self-sustaining settlements beyond Earth would ensure the survival of humanity against planetary catastrophes, open new resources, and extend scientific knowledge.

Proposed locations

  • The Moon – close to Earth, with polar water ice and lava tubes; proposed for outposts and as a proving ground.
  • Mars – a thin atmosphere, water ice, a near-24-hour day and raw materials for propellant manufacture; the focus of many long-term settlement plans.
  • Asteroids and the asteroid belt – rich in metals and volatiles; could supply materials for construction in space.
  • Free-space habitats – large rotating structures such as O'Neill cylinders that provide artificial gravity.
  • Outer Solar System – moons such as Titan, Callisto and Europa have been discussed for far-future settlement.

Challenges

Human settlement of space must overcome radiation exposure, the health effects of low or microgravity, the absence of breathable atmospheres, extreme temperatures, and the enormous cost of lifting mass from Earth. Proposed solutions include in-situ resource utilization (making fuel, water and building materials from local resources), closed ecological life-support systems, underground or regolith-shielded habitats, and rotating structures for artificial gravity.

Ethics and law

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibits national appropriation of celestial bodies, and questions remain about property rights, planetary protection of possibly life-bearing environments, and the governance of settlements. Critics question the expense and argue that Earth's problems should be prioritised; supporters respond that the two goals are not mutually exclusive.
