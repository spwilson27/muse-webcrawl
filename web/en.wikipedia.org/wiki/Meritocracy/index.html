Meritocracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Meritocracy is a political system in which economic goods or political power are vested in individual people on the basis of ability and talent rather than wealth, inheritance or social background. The word was coined, as a warning, by the British sociologist Michael Young in his 1958 satire The Rise of the Meritocracy, which imagined meritocratic sorting by IQ plus effort hardening into a smug hereditary caste — the origin's irony is routinely forgotten by the term's celebrants.

History and debate

Institutional meritocracy's oldest grand system was imperial China's examination-based bureaucracy (formalised under the Song dynasty), which Europe's reformers admired; modern civil services adopted competitive examination after Northcote–Trevelyan (1854) and the US Pendleton Act (1883), displacing patronage. As ideology, meritocracy underwrites equal-opportunity politics: outcomes are legitimate if the race was fair. Critics (Sandel's The Tyranny of Merit, Markovits, Guinier) argue that merit reproduces advantage (purchased test preparation, legacy preferences, cultural capital), that "merit" definitions smuggle in the winners' traits, and that meritocratic hubris corrodes solidarity by teaching winners their worth and losers their fault. Defenders answer that the alternatives — birth, wealth, connection — are worse, and that open competition remains the least unjust sorting principle yet administered at scale.
