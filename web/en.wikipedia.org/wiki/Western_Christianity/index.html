Western Christianity

Western Christianity is one of two sub-divisions of Christianity (Eastern Christianity being the other). Western Christianity is composed of the Latin Church and Western Protestantism, together with their offshoots such as Old Catholicism and Independent Catholicism. The large majority of the world's 2.3 billion Christians are Western Christians (about 2 billion). The remaining roughly 260 million are Eastern Christians.

History

Western Christianity developed in the Latin-speaking western half of the Roman Empire, centred on the Bishop of Rome, the Pope. Cultural, theological, and liturgical differences from the Greek-speaking East accumulated over centuries and culminated in the East–West Schism of 1054, which separated the Roman Catholic Church from the Eastern Orthodox Church.

Protestant Reformation

In the 16th century the Protestant Reformation split Western Christianity further, giving rise to Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, and many other traditions. Western Christianity subsequently spread worldwide through European colonisation and missionary activity, and today its largest populations are in Europe, the Americas, sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Asia and Oceania.

Features

Distinctive features historically associated with the Latin tradition include the use of unleavened bread in the Eucharist, the Filioque clause in the Nicene Creed, clerical celibacy in the Latin Church, and a strong emphasis on the juridical and institutional authority of the papacy.
