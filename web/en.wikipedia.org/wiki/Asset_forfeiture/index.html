Asset forfeiture

Property seized by police pending forfeiture proceedings.

Asset forfeiture or asset seizure is the confiscation of assets by the state, usually by law enforcement agencies, on the legal theory that the property itself is connected to criminal activity. It operates through legal actions against the property (in rem) or as part of criminal proceedings against a person (in personam). Forfeiture is used in many countries against the proceeds of drug trafficking, organised crime, corruption and money laundering.

Types

  • Criminal forfeiture — imposed as part of a sentence after conviction, requiring the prosecution to prove the property's connection to the offence beyond a reasonable doubt.
  • Civil forfeiture — a lawsuit against the property itself (reflected in case names such as United States v. $124,700 in U.S. Currency), which in many U.S. jurisdictions does not require the owner to be charged or convicted, and places part of the burden of proof on the owner.
  • Administrative forfeiture — an uncontested seizure processed by an agency without a court hearing.

History

Forfeiture has roots in English common law and in early customs and admiralty statutes used against smugglers and pirate ships. In the United States it expanded dramatically during the war on drugs, notably under the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970 and the RICO Act, and through the federal Equitable Sharing Program, which lets local police share proceeds of federal seizures.

Criticism and reform

Civil forfeiture has been criticised as creating "policing for profit" incentives, since seized assets often fund the seizing agencies, and because low-income owners may find contesting a seizure more expensive than the property's value. Investigative reports and cases of seizures from innocent owners have prompted statutory reforms in many U.S. states, and in 2019 the U.S. Supreme Court held in Timbs v. Indiana that the Eighth Amendment's ban on excessive fines applies to state forfeitures.
