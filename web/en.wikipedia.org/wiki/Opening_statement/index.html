Opening statement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An opening statement is an introductory outline of the case given by each party's counsel at the beginning of a trial, before evidence is presented. The prosecution (or plaintiff) opens first, the defence may open immediately after or reserve until the close of the prosecution case. The statement previews the facts the party expects the evidence to prove — a roadmap of witnesses and events — and is formally not evidence itself, nor a place for argument.

Function and limits

Trial lawyers treat the opening as structurally decisive: it gives jurors the narrative framework into which testimony will be fitted (primacy and story-model effects are well documented in jury research), promises only what can be delivered, and humanises or problematises the opposing case in advance. Counsel who over-promise ("the evidence will show" what it then does not) lose credibility that the closing cannot restore. Judges police the boundary with argument: arguing inferences, vouching for witnesses, invoking inadmissible evidence or appealing to prejudice draws objections and occasionally mistrials — errors in the prosecution's opening being a recurrent appellate theme. In bench and inquisitorial proceedings the opening's role shrinks, the dossier having already framed the case.
