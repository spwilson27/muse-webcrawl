The Kingdom of Judah (Hebrew: מַמְלֶכֶת יְהוּדָה) was an Iron Age kingdom of the southern Levant, centred on Jerusalem and the Judean hills. According to the Hebrew Bible it formed when the united monarchy of Israel split after the death of Solomon (c. 930 BCE), leaving the tribes of Judah and Benjamin under Solomon's son Rehoboam while the northern tribes formed the Kingdom of Israel. Judah outlived its northern neighbour by well over a century, surviving as a vassal of Assyria and then Babylon, until Nebuchadnezzar II destroyed Jerusalem and its Temple in 586 BCE and deported much of its population in the Babylonian exile.

History

The early history of Judah is known mainly from the biblical books of Kings and Chronicles, supplemented by archaeology. For much of the ninth and eighth centuries BCE Judah was the smaller and poorer of the two Hebrew kingdoms, dominated at times by Israel, Aram-Damascus, and Philistine and Edomite neighbours. Finds such as the Siloam inscription and the royal LMLK stamped jar handles attest to growing state organisation by the late eighth century.

After Assyria destroyed the northern kingdom in 722 BCE, Judah became an Assyrian vassal and its population swelled with refugees. King Hezekiah (r. c. 715–686 BCE) rebelled against Sennacherib; the Assyrian invasion of 701 BCE devastated Judah's fortified cities — the siege of Lachish is depicted on reliefs from Nineveh — but Jerusalem itself survived, an event the Bible attributes to divine intervention. Hezekiah's preparations, including the Siloam Tunnel cut to bring water into the city, remain visible today. Under Manasseh Judah recovered as a loyal vassal; under Josiah (r. 640–609 BCE), after Assyria's collapse, the kingdom expanded and underwent the centralising religious reform associated with the "Book of the Law" found in the Temple (622 BCE).

Judah's final decades were spent between the great powers: Josiah was killed by Pharaoh Necho II at Megiddo (609 BCE), and the kingdom passed to Babylon. Jehoiakim's rebellion brought the first Babylonian siege and deportation in 597 BCE; Zedekiah's later revolt brought the final catastrophe. In 586 BCE Nebuchadnezzar II captured Jerusalem after a long siege, burned the First Temple, executed the royal house, and exiled the elite to Babylon. A remnant remained under the governor Gedaliah, whose assassination ended organised Judahite life in the land until the Persian conquest of Babylon allowed exiles to return under Cyrus after 539 BCE.

Archaeology

Excavations in the City of David, at Lachish, Arad, Beersheba and Ramat Rachel, and inscriptions such as the Lachish letters and the Siloam inscription, provide the main extrabiblical evidence for the kingdom, while the historicity and scale of the earlier united monarchy remain debated among scholars.
