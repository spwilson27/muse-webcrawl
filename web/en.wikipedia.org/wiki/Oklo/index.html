Oklo is a region near the town of Franceville in Gabon, Central Africa, famous as the only known site of natural nuclear fission reactors: uranium deposits that, about 1.7 billion years ago, sustained self-regulating nuclear chain reactions for hundreds of thousands of years with the aid of groundwater as a neutron moderator.

Discovery

In 1972, workers at a French uranium enrichment plant noticed that uranium ore from Oklo contained slightly less uranium-235 than the universal natural ratio — 0.717% instead of 0.720% — as if part of the fissile isotope had already been "burned". Investigation by the French Commissariat à l'énergie atomique confirmed the presence of fission products and at least sixteen separate reactor zones in the Francevillian basin deposits.

How the reactors worked

Two billion years ago, natural uranium was enriched to roughly 3% U-235 — comparable to modern reactor fuel — because U-235 decays faster than U-238. Where rich ore bodies became saturated with groundwater, the water moderated neutrons and a chain reaction ignited. Heat boiled the water away, shutting the reaction down until water returned, producing a geyser-like cycle of perhaps 30 minutes on and 2.5 hours off. The reactors generated fission products that have remained largely immobilised in the ore ever since, making Oklo a natural analogue studied for the geological disposal of nuclear waste.

Scientific significance

Isotope ratios in Oklo samples have been used to test whether fundamental constants, in particular the fine-structure constant, have changed over geological time, and to constrain models of nuclear waste migration.
