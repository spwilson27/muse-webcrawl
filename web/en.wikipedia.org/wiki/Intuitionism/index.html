Intuitionism

In the philosophy of mathematics, intuitionism is an approach where mathematics is considered to be purely the result of the constructive mental activity of humans rather than the discovery of fundamental principles claimed to exist in an objective reality. The logic and mathematics developed under this approach, known as intuitionistic logic and constructive mathematics, reject non-constructive existence proofs and the unrestricted law of the excluded middle.

L. E. J. Brouwer (1881–1966), the founder of intuitionism.

History

Intuitionism was founded by Dutch mathematician L. E. J. Brouwer in the early 20th century, drawing on Kant's idea of the pure intuitions of space and time and on earlier constructivist tendencies in Kronecker and Poincaré. Brouwer held that mathematical objects are mental constructions, that language is an imperfect vehicle for them, and that a statement is true only when a construction verifying it is given. His student Arend Heyting formalized intuitionistic logic and arithmetic in 1930.

Principles

  • Rejection of excluded middle for infinite domains: from the absence of a proof of ¬P one cannot conclude P; proofs by contradiction of existence claims are not accepted.
  • Constructive existence — to prove ∃x φ(x) one must exhibit a witness and a proof that it satisfies φ.
  • Choice sequences — Brouwer's theory of the continuum uses freely evolving sequences, leading (with continuity principles) to results contradicting classical analysis, e.g. that all total real functions are continuous.
  • BHK interpretation — the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov semantics explains logical connectives in terms of proofs.

Relations and influence

Intuitionism was one party in the foundational crisis debates (the Grundlagenstreit) with Hilbert's formalism; Gödel and Gentzen later showed classical arithmetic can be embedded in intuitionistic arithmetic by double-negation translation. Constructive mathematics influenced computer science through the Curry–Howard correspondence, Martin-Löf type theory, and proof assistants, where proofs are programs. Related schools include Bishop's constructive analysis and Russian constructivism (Markov).
