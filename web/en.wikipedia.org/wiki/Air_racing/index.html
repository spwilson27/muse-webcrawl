Air racing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Air racing is a type of motorsport that involves airplanes competing over a fixed course, with the winner either returning the shortest time, the one to complete it with the most points, or to come closest to a previously estimated time. Air races may be flown over closed pylon circuits, cross-country over long distances, or through slalom-style courses marked by inflatable gates.

History

The first organised air race was held in 1909 at Reims, France, during the Grande Semaine d'Aviation, where Glenn Curtiss won the Gordon Bennett Cup. Long-distance "handicap" and city-to-city races flourished before the First World War, including the London–Manchester race of 1910 and the 1911 Circuit of Europe. In the United States, the Pulitzer Trophy races of the 1920s led to the National Air Races, which included the Thompson Trophy for closed-course speed racing and the transcontinental Bendix Trophy from 1931. British events included the King's Cup Race, first held in 1922, and the Schneider Trophy seaplane races, won outright by Britain in 1931 with the Supermarine S.6B, a direct ancestor of the Spitfire.

Classes and formats

Pylon racing

At the Reno Air Races (founded in 1964) in Nevada, aircraft race in classes including Unlimited (heavily modified Second World War fighters such as P-51 Mustangs and Hawker Sea Furies reaching speeds above 500 mph), T-6, Sport, Biplane, Formula One, and Jet classes around an oval course marked by pylons.

Formula air racing

Formula One air racing uses small single-seat aircraft built to a standard formula, with engines such as the Continental O-200, keeping costs down and emphasising pilot skill. Similar formula classes exist for biplanes and sport aircraft.

Slalom and time-trial formats

The Red Bull Air Race World Championship (2003–2019) featured aerobatic pilots flying singly through a course of inflatable pylons in timed runs. Other formats include rally-style precision events, air rally races combining navigation and timing, and record attempts sanctioned by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI).

Safety

Air racing has historically been dangerous, with numerous fatal accidents, including the 1949 Cleveland crash of a modified P-51 into a crowd-adjacent house and the 2011 Reno accident in which the modified Mustang The Galloping Ghost crashed into spectators, killing the pilot and ten people on the ground. The Reno Air Racing Association subsequently tightened course design and safety rules, and announced the event would move from Reno after 2023; the races relocated to Roswell, New Mexico, in 2025.

Notable racers and aircraft

  • Roscoe Turner, three-time Thompson Trophy winner
  • Jimmy Doolittle, winner of the 1931 Bendix Trophy and 1932 Thompson Trophy
  • Steve Wittman, designer-racer of Bonzo and Chief Oshkosh
  • Rare Bear, a modified Grumman F8F Bearcat, holder of the piston-engine speed record
  • Voodoo and Strega, championship-winning modified P-51 Mustangs
