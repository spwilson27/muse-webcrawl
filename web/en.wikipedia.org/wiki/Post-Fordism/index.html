Post-Fordism

Post-Fordism is a term used to describe the dominant system of economic production, consumption, and associated socio-economic phenomena in most industrialized countries since the late 20th century. It is contrasted with Fordism, the system of mass production and mass consumption epitomized by Henry Ford's assembly lines.

Characteristics

Theories of post-Fordism identify a shift from standardized mass production to flexible specialization: small-batch, customized production using computerized machinery and skilled, adaptable labour. Associated features include just-in-time supply chains, outsourcing and global production networks, the growth of service and information industries, niche marketing, product differentiation, and more precarious forms of employment such as temporary and gig work. The Toyota production system is often cited as a model of flexible production.

Theory

The French Regulation School (Michel Aglietta, Alain Lipietz) framed post-Fordism as a new "regime of accumulation" succeeding the Fordist regime that entered crisis in the 1970s. Michael Piore and Charles Sabel's The Second Industrial Divide (1984) popularized the flexible-specialization thesis, while David Harvey linked post-Fordism to the "flexible accumulation" and time–space compression of postmodern capitalism.

Criticism

Critics argue that mass production never disappeared — it was relocated — and that "post-Fordism" bundles together diverse and contradictory trends. Others note the concept can understate continuities in labour discipline and the persistence of Fordist methods in logistics and fast food.
