Pixel density

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

UnitsPixels per inch (ppi), pixels per centimetre
Applies toDisplays, image scanners, digital camera sensors

Pixel density is the number of pixels packed into a given physical area of a display, image sensor, or printed image, usually expressed in pixels per inch (ppi). Higher pixel density produces finer detail and smoother edges, because individual pixels become harder to distinguish at normal viewing distances.

Calculation

For a rectangular display of width w and height h pixels and physical diagonal d inches, the pixel density is the diagonal pixel count, √(w² + h²), divided by d. For example, a 1920 × 1080 display of 24 inches has a density of about 92 ppi, while a smartphone screen with the same resolution at about 5 inches exceeds 440 ppi.

Displays

Historically, desktop displays clustered around 72–110 ppi. Apple popularized much higher densities with the "Retina display" branding from 2010, arguing that at typical viewing distances pixels at around 300 ppi are indistinguishable to the human eye. Operating systems handle high-density displays by scaling interface elements so that text and icons keep their intended physical size.

Printing and imaging

In printing, density is often quoted in dots per inch (dpi); photographic prints are commonly produced at 300 ppi. In digital cameras, pixel density of the sensor (pixel pitch) affects noise and dynamic range, with smaller pixels generally gathering less light per pixel.
