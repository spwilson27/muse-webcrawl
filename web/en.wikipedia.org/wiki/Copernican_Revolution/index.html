Copernican Revolution

The Copernican Revolution was the paradigm shift from the Ptolemaic model of the heavens, which described the cosmos as having Earth stationary at the center of the universe, to the heliocentric model with the Sun at the centre of the Solar System. This revolution was one of the major events of the Scientific Revolution.

Beginning with the publication of Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres) in 1543, the shift proceeded gradually over more than a century, as Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion, Galileo Galilei's telescopic observations, and finally Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) provided a physical framework for the new cosmology.

Background

The geocentric Ptolemaic system, codified in the 2nd century CE, explained planetary motion with epicycles and deferents. Heliocentric ideas had been proposed in antiquity by Aristarchus of Samos, but geocentrism dominated for well over a millennium. Copernicus, a Polish canon and polymath, developed his heliocentric model over decades and published it in the year of his death, reportedly receiving a printed copy on his deathbed.

Development

Copernicus retained circular orbits and epicycles, so his system was not much simpler than Ptolemy's, but it explained retrograde motion naturally as an effect of the Earth's own motion. Kepler, working with Tycho Brahe's precise observations, replaced circles with ellipses (1609). Galileo's observations of the phases of Venus and the moons of Jupiter (1610) undermined the Ptolemaic system, though they did not by themselves prove heliocentrism. Newton's Principia completed the revolution by grounding planetary motion in universal gravitation.

Impact

The displacement of the Earth from the centre of the universe had profound philosophical and theological consequences, contributing to a broader re-evaluation of humanity's place in nature. The term "Copernican revolution" was used by Immanuel Kant, by analogy, for his own epistemological turn, and Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962) took the episode as the archetype of a paradigm shift.
