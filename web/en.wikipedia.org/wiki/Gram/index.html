The gram (originally gramme; SI unit symbol g) is a unit of mass in the International System of Units (SI) equal to one thousandth of a kilogram. Originally defined in 1795 as the absolute weight of a volume of pure water equal to the cube of the hundredth part of a metre (1 cm³) at the temperature of melting ice, the gram is now defined via the kilogram, which is itself fixed by the Planck constant.

History

The gram was the base unit of mass in the centimetre–gram–second (CGS) system, widely used in science until the mid-20th century. The word derives from the Late Latin gramma, a small weight, via the French gramme. The French National Convention adopted the gramme in its 1795 revision of the metric system.

Uses

The gram is among the most familiar metric units in everyday life: food packaging, cooking recipes, postal weights, jewellery and laboratory measurements commonly use grams and its multiples and submultiples — the milligram (mg, 10−3 g), the kilogram (kg, 10³ g) and the tonne (t, 10⁶ g). A standard paperclip has a mass of roughly one gram.
