Blackout

A blackout is a total loss of electric power in an area. Blackouts are distinguished from brownouts (a drop in voltage) and from localised outages caused by faults on a single distribution circuit; a blackout typically results from an imbalance between power generation and demand, or from cascading failures in a transmission network. The term also has several unrelated meanings, including a wartime restriction on lights, a temporary loss of consciousness, and an alcohol-related loss of memory.

Power blackouts

Electric power grids must continuously balance supply and demand. When a generator or transmission line trips unexpectedly, the load shifts to remaining equipment, which may overload and trip in turn — a cascading failure that can darken entire regions within minutes. Notable events include the Northeast blackout of 1965 and of 2003 (which left some 55 million people in the United States and Canada without power), the 2003 Italy blackout, and the Indian blackouts of July 2012, among the largest in history, affecting over 600 million people.

Prevention and restoration

Grid operators use protective relays, automatic load shedding, reserve generation and islanding schemes to limit blackouts. Restoration after a total collapse may require a "black start" — restarting generation without external power — and can take hours to days.

Other meanings

During the Second World War, blackout regulations required homes and cities to extinguish or cover lights at night to hinder enemy bombers; "blackout curtains" were a fixture of the home front. In medicine, a blackout may refer to syncope (fainting) or to transient global amnesia; in aviation, to loss of vision or consciousness under high g-forces (G-LOC). An alcohol-induced blackout is a period of amnesia during intoxication despite remaining conscious.
