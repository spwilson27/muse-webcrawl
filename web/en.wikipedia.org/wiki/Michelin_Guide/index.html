Michelin Guide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An elegantly plated dish at a Michelin-starred restaurant]

The Michelin Guide is a series of guide books published by the French tyre company Michelin since 1900, awarding one to three stars for excellence to a select few restaurants. One star denotes "a very good restaurant in its category", two "excellent cooking, worth a detour", and three "exceptional cuisine, worth a special journey". It also awards the Bib Gourmand for good value and, since 2020, a Green Star for sustainability.

The guide began as a free handbook for French motorists; its anonymous inspectors and star system made it the most feared and coveted arbiter in gastronomy. It now covers dozens of cities and countries in Europe, Asia and the Americas.

History

Édouard and André Michelin published the first guide in 1900 to encourage motoring — and tyre wear. Restaurant stars began in 1926, the three-tier system in 1931. Post-war, the guide's judgements could make or break chefs; the pressure at the summit has been linked to tragedies such as Bernard Loiseau's suicide in 2003.

Criticism

Critics charge the guide with a bias toward French technique and luxury, sparse coverage outside wealthy cities, and inconsistency between regions. Chefs such as Sébastien Bras have asked to be removed from it. Rival guides and lists — Gault Millau, the World's 50 Best Restaurants — now compete for authority.
