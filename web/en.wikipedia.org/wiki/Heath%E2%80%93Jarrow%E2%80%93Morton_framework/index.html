Heath–Jarrow–Morton framework

The Heath–Jarrow–Morton (HJM) framework is a general framework in mathematical finance for modelling the evolution of interest rates. Developed by David Heath, Robert Jarrow and Andrew Morton in the late 1980s, it models the entire forward rate curve as a stochastic process, rather than a single short rate.

Framework

Instantaneous forward rates f(t,T) evolve under stochastic differential equations driven by Brownian motions. The key result is the HJM drift condition: under the risk-neutral measure, the drift of the forward rates is completely determined by their volatility structure, which guarantees the absence of arbitrage. This contrasts with short-rate models such as Vasicek or Cox–Ingersoll–Ross, which are special cases that can be recovered within the HJM setting.

Applications

The framework underlies the pricing of interest rate derivatives such as caps, floors and swaptions. Practical implementations often restrict the volatility structure; the LIBOR market model (Brace–Gatarek–Musiela) can be viewed as a discretised, lognormal variant of the HJM approach.
