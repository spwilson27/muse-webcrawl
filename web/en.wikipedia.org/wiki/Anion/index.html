Anion

An anion is an ion with more electrons than protons, giving it a net negative charge. The name comes from the Greek word ἄνω (ánō), meaning "up", because during electrolysis anions migrate towards the positively charged electrode (the anode). Anions are opposite to cations, which carry a net positive charge.

Common anions

Many anions are familiar from everyday chemistry. Examples include hydroxide (OH−), chloride (Cl−), nitrate (NO3−), sulfate (SO42−), carbonate (CO32−) and phosphate (PO43−). Monatomic anions such as fluoride and oxide are formed when a neutral atom gains one or more electrons; polyatomic anions consist of groups of atoms with an overall negative charge.

Formation and properties

Anions are formed when atoms or molecules gain electrons, typically in chemical reactions such as the dissociation of salts, acids and bases in water. Because they have gained electrons, anions are generally larger than the corresponding neutral atoms. In ionic compounds, anions pack together with cations to form crystal lattices held together by electrostatic attraction.
