The Viceroyalty of Peru (Spanish: Virreinato del Perú) was a Spanish imperial provincial administrative district, created in 1542, that originally contained most of Spanish-ruled South America, governed from the capital of Lima. It was for two centuries the principal source of Spain's wealth in the Americas, centred on the silver mines of Potosí.

Lima, capital of the Viceroyalty of Peru, in the colonial era.

History

Following Francisco Pizarro's conquest of the Inca Empire in the 1530s and a period of civil war among the conquistadors, the Crown created the viceroyalty in 1542 under the New Laws. The first viceroy, Blasco Núñez Vela, was killed by rebels; effective organisation came under Viceroy Francisco de Toledo in the 1570s, who established the mita labour system that supplied workers to the silver mines of Potosí (in modern Bolivia) and the mercury mines of Huancavelica.

The viceroyalty initially encompassed all Spanish South America except Venezuela. During the 18th-century Bourbon Reforms, large territories were transferred to the new viceroyalties of New Granada (1717/1739) and the Río de la Plata (1776), leaving the Viceroyalty of Peru roughly equivalent to modern Peru, plus, at times, Chile and Bolivia.

Independence

Peru was the stronghold of royalist power in South America. Independence was proclaimed by José de San Martín in 1821, and royalist forces were finally defeated by Simón Bolívar's and Antonio José de Sucre's armies at the Battle of Ayacucho in December 1824, ending the viceroyalty and Spanish rule in South America.
