Thermodynamic potential


The four principal thermodynamic potentials and the variables in terms of which each is naturally expressed.

A thermodynamic potential (or more accurately, a thermodynamic potential energy) is a scalar quantity used to represent the thermodynamic state of a system. Just as in mechanics, where potential energy is defined as the capacity to do work, different thermodynamic potentials can be defined, each a "potential" for a different set of constraints. All thermodynamic potentials are combinations of the internal energy and products of conjugate variable pairs such as pressure–volume or temperature–entropy.

The concept was introduced by Pierre Duhem in 1886, and Josiah Willard Gibbs used the term "fundamental functions". When a system's entropy is represented as a function of its internal energy and other extensive parameters, the system is fully specified; equivalently, any one potential, expressed in its natural variables, contains complete thermodynamic information about the system.

The four main potentials

PotentialDefinitionNatural variablesDifferential
Internal energy, UUS, V, {Ni}dU = T dS − p dV + Σ μi dNi
Helmholtz free energy, FU − TST, V, {Ni}dF = −S dT − p dV + Σ μi dNi
Enthalpy, HU + pVS, p, {Ni}dH = T dS + V dp + Σ μi dNi
Gibbs free energy, GU + pV − TS = H − TST, p, {Ni}dG = −S dT + V dp + Σ μi dNi

Here T is temperature, S entropy, p pressure, V volume, μi the chemical potential of species i, and Ni the amount of that species. The potentials are related to one another by Legendre transformations, which swap an extensive variable for its conjugate intensive variable.

Minimisation principles

The second law of thermodynamics implies that, for a system held under particular constraints, the corresponding potential decreases in any spontaneous process and is minimised at equilibrium:

  • At constant entropy and volume, the internal energy U is minimised.
  • At constant temperature and volume, the Helmholtz free energy F is minimised; its decrease bounds the total work obtainable from the system.
  • At constant temperature and pressure, the Gibbs free energy G is minimised; its decrease bounds the non-expansion work, and ΔG determines whether a chemical reaction is spontaneous.
  • At constant entropy and pressure, the enthalpy H is minimised.

Equations of state and Maxwell relations

Because each potential is a state function of its natural variables, its first derivatives give the equations of state of the system (for example, S = −(∂F/∂T)V and p = −(∂F/∂V)T), and equality of mixed second derivatives yields the Maxwell relations, which connect measurable quantities such as heat capacities and compressibilities. Heat capacities themselves can be written as second derivatives of the potentials, for instance CV = −T (∂²F/∂T²)V.

Other potentials

Further potentials arise in other settings: the grand potential (Landau free energy) ΦG = U − TS − ΣμiNi, natural in the grand canonical ensemble of statistical mechanics; Massieu and Planck functions, the entropy-based analogues used with the entropy representation; and characteristic functions for systems with additional work coordinates (magnetic, elastic, surface, or gravitational).
