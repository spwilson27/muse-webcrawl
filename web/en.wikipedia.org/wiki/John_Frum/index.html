John Frum (also called Jon Frum) is a figure associated with cargo cults on the island of Tanna in Vanuatu. According to believers, John Frum is a messianic being — variously described as a native spirit, a black American serviceman, or a king — who prophesied that if the people of Tanna rejected European colonial ways and returned to traditional custom (kastom), he would return bringing "cargo": abundant manufactured goods, wealth and freedom from colonial rule. The John Frum movement remains an active religion and political force on Tanna today.

Origins

The movement emerged in the late 1930s, after decades of Presbyterian mission dominance that suppressed traditional ceremonies and kava drinking. Villagers in south-east Tanna reported appearances of a mysterious man — some accounts name a figure called Manehivi — who spoke in the voice of John Frum, urging a return to custom. The name may derive from "John from (America)" or from "John Broom," who would sweep away the white colonists. Followers abandoned mission churches, slaughtered livestock and spent their money in expectation of the new age.

The arrival of American forces in the New Hebrides during the Second World War — with their astonishing wealth, heavy machinery and black servicemen treated as equals — massively reinforced the movement. Followers built symbolic wharves, landing strips and bamboo "radio" equipment to receive Frum's cargo, and adopted drills and flags modelled on the U.S. military.

Beliefs and practice

In villages such as Sulphur Bay (Ipikil), devout followers raise the United States flag, parade with bamboo rifles on John Frum Day (15 February), and maintain a cross-dressed shrine of artefacts. Some branches regard John Frum as divine; the related Prince Philip Movement on Tanna venerated Prince Philip, Duke of Edinburgh, as a prophesied figure. Adherents have held seats in Vanuatu's parliament, and the movement's emphasis on kastom influenced the country's independence politics.

Interpretations

Anthropologists class John Frum among the classic Melanesian cargo cults, while noting that the movement is also an organised programme of cultural resistance and revival: its prophecies justified abandoning mission discipline, reviving kava, dance and exchange customs, and negotiating with colonial power from a position of unity. The John Frum movement survives into the twenty-first century, with annual celebrations drawing visitors and journalists to Tanna each February.
