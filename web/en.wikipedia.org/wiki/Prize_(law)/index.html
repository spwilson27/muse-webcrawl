Prize (law)

Enemy vessels captured at sea in wartime were adjudicated as prizes by admiralty courts.

In admiralty law, a prize is property — most often an enemy vessel and its cargo — captured at sea during wartime by a commissioned warship or privateer. The capture does not by itself transfer ownership: the captured property must be brought before a prize court of the captor's state, which determines whether the seizure was lawful and whether the property should be condemned as a lawful prize.

Prize courts and condemnation

Historically, prize courts sat as courts of admiralty and applied the law of nations. If the court found that the vessel or goods belonged to an enemy, or were contraband destined for the enemy, it would condemn them, after which they could be sold and the proceeds — prize money — distributed to the captors according to statute or custom. If the capture was found unlawful, the property was restored to its owners, sometimes with damages against the captor.

Prize money

The prospect of prize money was a major incentive for naval officers and crews and for privateers operating under letters of marque. In the Royal Navy, prize money was divided according to a fixed system of shares, with the captain and flag officers receiving substantial portions. The system declined in importance with the abolition of privateering by the Declaration of Paris of 1856 and the professionalisation of navies.

Modern law

Prize law remains part of the customary law of naval warfare and is reflected in manuals such as the San Remo Manual on International Law Applicable to Armed Conflicts at Sea (1994). Enemy merchant vessels and contraband cargoes may still be captured and condemned, subject to rules protecting neutral shipping, hospital ships and other exempt categories.
