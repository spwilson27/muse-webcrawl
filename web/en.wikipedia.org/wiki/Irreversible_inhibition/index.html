Irreversible inhibition

Irreversible inhibition is a type of enzyme inhibition in which the inhibitor binds covalently to the enzyme, causing permanent inactivation. Unlike reversible inhibition, where the inhibitor binds non-covalently and dissociates readily, irreversible inhibitors form strong covalent bonds with amino acid residues in the enzyme's active site (or elsewhere), and the enzyme can only regain activity when new enzyme is synthesized by the cell.

Irreversible inhibitors are often used as drugs and as tools in biochemical research. They typically contain reactive functional groups such as aldehydes, haloalkanes, alkenes, or fluorophosphonates that react with nucleophilic residues like serine or cysteine.

Examples

  • Penicillin — covalently inhibits the bacterial enzyme DD-transpeptidase (penicillin-binding protein), blocking cell wall synthesis.
  • Aspirin — acetylates a serine residue in cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, irreversibly blocking prostaglandin synthesis.
  • Nerve agents such as sarin — phosphorylate the active-site serine of acetylcholinesterase.
  • Eflornithine — a "suicide inhibitor" of ornithine decarboxylase used against African sleeping sickness.
  • Diisopropyl fluorophosphate (DFP) — a classic serine protease inhibitor.

Suicide inhibition

A special class of irreversible inhibitors are called suicide inhibitors (or mechanism-based inhibitors). These are unreactive until the enzyme's own catalytic mechanism converts them into a reactive species that then binds covalently — giving them high specificity for the target enzyme.

Kinetics

Because irreversible inhibition is time-dependent, it is not fully characterized by a simple inhibition constant. Instead, potency is described by parameters such as KI (the dissociation constant of the initial complex) and kinact (the maximal rate of inactivation).
