William Mathews (1828–1901) was an English land agent and mountaineer, best known as one of the founders of the Alpine Club, the world's first mountaineering club, established in London in 1857. He is generally credited with proposing the formation of the club, in correspondence with his climbing companion John Ball, the club's first president.

Early life and career

Mathews was born at Hagley in Worcestershire and worked as a land agent and surveyor based in Birmingham. He took up climbing in the Alps in the 1850s, during the period later known as the golden age of alpinism, when British climbers and their Swiss guides made the first ascents of most of the great Alpine peaks.

Mountaineering

Mathews made a number of notable climbs. In 1859 he attempted Mont Blanc by the Aiguille du Goûter route; in 1860 he took part in the first ascent attempt of Monte Viso, reaching the summit the following year with a party including Francis Younghusband's early contemporaries; in 1862 he made the first ascent of Monte Viso's neighbour peaks and climbed widely in the Dauphiné Alps, making the first ascent of the Grandes Rousses area peaks. He also explored the Pyrenees, and wrote about the flora and geology of the mountains as well as climbing.

The Alpine Club

In a letter of 1856 to John Ball, Mathews floated the idea of an association of British mountaineers. The Alpine Club was formally established in 1857, becoming the model for national alpine clubs across Europe. Mathews served on its committee and contributed to Peaks, Passes and Glaciers, the club's early publications.

Legacy

Mathews's brother, Charles Edward Mathews, was also a prominent alpinist and club president. William Mathews is commemorated in histories of mountaineering as one of the organisational founders of modern alpinism.
