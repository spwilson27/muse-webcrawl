Terrine (food)

A terrine (French pronunciation: [tɛ.ʁin]), in traditional French cuisine, is a loaf of forcemeat or aspic, similar to a pâté, that is cooked in a covered pottery mould (also called a terrine) in a bain-marie. Modern terrines do not necessarily contain meat or animal fat, but still contain meat-like textures and fat surrogates such as mushrooms and pureed vegetables, blended with a binding base of breadcrumbs, spices, and sometimes egg. The mixture is pressed and chilled, then unmoulded and served sliced, typically cold or at room temperature.

Preparation

Terrine mixtures are commonly made from game, pork, veal, duck, or fish, coarsely chopped rather than finely pureed, which distinguishes a terrine from a smooth pâté. The mould is often lined with bacon or caul fat, filled with the seasoned forcemeat—sometimes layered with contrasting ingredients such as pistachios, prunes, or strips of ham—then covered and baked slowly in a water bath. After cooking, the terrine is weighted while it cools so that it slices cleanly, and may be sealed with clarified butter or aspic for keeping.

Types and serving

Well-known varieties include terrine de campagne (a coarse country-style pork terrine), duck and game terrines, seafood terrines, and vegetable terrines set with aspic or eggs. Terrines are a staple of charcuterie and are traditionally served as a first course with cornichons, mustard, and toasted bread or brioche, accompanied by chutney or a simple salad.
