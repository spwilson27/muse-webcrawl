The Sloan Digital Sky Survey or SDSS is a major multi-spectral imaging and spectroscopic redshift survey using a dedicated 2.5-m wide-angle optical telescope at Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico, United States. The project began in 2000 and was named after the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, which contributed significant funding.

The SDSS created the most detailed three-dimensional maps of the Universe ever made, with spectra of more than three million astronomical objects. It has been cited as one of the most influential projects in the history of astronomy, and its publicly released data have been used in thousands of scientific publications.

Observations

The SDSS used a dedicated 2.5 m wide-angle telescope equipped with a 120-megapixel camera that imaged the sky in five optical bands (u, g, r, i, z). Imaging data were processed automatically to produce catalogues of hundreds of millions of objects. Selected targets were then observed spectroscopically using fibre-optic spectrographs capable of recording 640 spectra simultaneously (later upgraded to 1,000).

Phases

SDSS-I (2000–2005) imaged 8,000 square degrees and obtained spectra of more than 700,000 objects. SDSS-II (2005–2008) included the Sloan Supernova Survey, which discovered hundreds of Type Ia supernovae. SDSS-III (2008–2014) ran four surveys, including BOSS (the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey), which measured the large-scale structure of the Universe to constrain dark energy, and APOGEE, an infrared spectroscopic survey of the Milky Way. SDSS-IV (2014–2020) included APOGEE-2, eBOSS and the MaNGA integral-field survey of nearby galaxies. SDSS-V, begun in 2020, extended observations to both hemispheres with a robotic fibre-positioning system and surveys of the Milky Way, black holes, and the local volume.

Data access

All SDSS data are made public through periodic data releases; the images and catalogues are available online through interfaces such as SkyServer, which is widely used in education. The survey's open-data policy is credited with greatly multiplying its scientific impact.
