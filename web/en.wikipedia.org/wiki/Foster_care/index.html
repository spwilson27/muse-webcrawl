Foster care

Foster care provides temporary family homes for children who cannot live with their parents.

Foster care is a system in which a minor has been placed into a ward, group home (residential child care community), treatment centre, or private home of a state-certified caregiver, referred to as a "foster parent", or with a family member approved by the state. The placement of a "foster child" is normally arranged through the government or a social service agency. The institution, group home, or foster parent is compensated for expenses unless with a family member.

Children enter foster care because of abuse, neglect, parental illness, incarceration, substance misuse, or the death of parents. The stated goal in most jurisdictions is usually reunification with the birth family where possible; where this is not possible, outcomes include adoption, kinship care, long-term fostering, or ageing out of the system into independent living.

History

Informal fostering and "boarding out" of poor children are centuries old. In the United States, Charles Loring Brace's Children's Aid Society (from 1853) placed city children with rural families on the "orphan trains". Modern foster care developed in the early 20th century as states began licensing and paying foster homes, shifting away from large orphanages. In the United Kingdom, the Children Act 1948 and subsequent legislation built a system run by local authorities.

Statistics and outcomes

In the United States, roughly 370,000–400,000 children are in foster care on any given day, with about 200,000 entering each year. Research consistently shows that young people who age out of care face elevated risks of homelessness, unemployment and involvement with the criminal justice system, leading to reforms such as extended care beyond age 18, independent-living support, and a preference for placing children with relatives (kinship care), which studies associate with greater stability. Placement instability — children moving between many homes — is linked to worse educational and mental-health outcomes.

Criticism and reform

Foster systems are criticised for racial and socioeconomic disproportionality in child removals, shortages of foster homes, high caseloads, and poor oversight in some residential settings. Reform movements emphasise prevention services to keep families together, family-finding, and trauma-informed care.
