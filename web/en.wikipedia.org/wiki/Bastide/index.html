Bastide

A bastide is a type of fortified new town built in medieval Languedoc, Gascony and Aquitaine during the 13th and 14th centuries. The word comes from the Occitan bastida, meaning a building or construction. Bastides were founded by kings, counts and other lords to consolidate control over territory, encourage settlement and trade, and provide security in contested border regions — particularly during the period of Anglo-French rivalry that preceded the Hundred Years' War. Some 300 to 350 bastides are known to have been founded, mainly between about 1222 and 1373.

Layout

Bastides were typically laid out on a regular grid of streets around a central market square, often surrounded by arcaded walkways (couverts) with shops and houses behind. A church, a market hall and the house of the lord's representative stood near the square. Building plots of standard size were allocated to settlers, who received charters of liberties defining their rights, taxes and obligations. Many bastides were fortified with walls, towers and gates, though some relied on their church or castle for defence.

History

The earliest bastides were founded by the counts of Toulouse, including Raymond VII. After the Treaty of Paris (1229), Alphonse of Poitiers founded many bastides in the Agenais and Languedoc. The seneschal Eustache de Beaumarchais founded numerous bastides for the French crown in Gascony, while Edward I of England, as Duke of Aquitaine, founded others such as Monpazier (1284) — one of the best-preserved examples — and Beaumont-du-Périgord. Settlers were attracted by promises of land, low rents and legal freedoms.

Surviving examples

Well-known bastides include Monpazier, Domme, Vianne, Cordes-sur-Ciel, Villefranche-de-Rouergue, Sauveterre-de-Rouergue and Monflanquin. Many retain their grid plans, arcaded squares and medieval houses and are now popular tourist destinations in south-western France.
