Taxaceae, the yew family, is a conifer family comprising about six genera and some 30 species of trees and shrubs found mainly in the Northern Hemisphere, with outliers in New Caledonia and Sulawesi. Unlike most conifers, yews do not bear seeds in woody cones: each single seed is partly or wholly surrounded by a fleshy, often bright red, cup-like structure called an aril, which is eaten by birds that disperse the seed.

Characteristics

Members are evergreen, mostly dioecious (male and female plants separate), with flat, needle-like leaves arranged spirally but twisted to lie in two ranks. The wood lacks resin canals. Most parts of most species contain taxine alkaloids and are highly poisonous to humans and livestock, although the fleshy aril itself is not toxic.

Uses and significance

The Pacific yew (Taxus brevifolia) and related species yield the cancer drug paclitaxel (Taxol), originally extracted from bark and now produced semi-synthetically. Yew wood is dense, fine-grained and elastic — it was the classic material of the English longbow — and yews are widely planted as ornamental hedging; individual common yews in churchyards are among the oldest living trees in Europe.
