Battle of Malplaquet

The Battle of Malplaquet was fought on 11 September 1709 in northern France, near the fortress of Mons, during the War of the Spanish Succession. It was the bloodiest battle of the 18th century: the Grand Alliance army under the Duke of Marlborough and Prince Eugene of Savoy forced the French army of Marshal Villars from the field but suffered such heavy losses that the victory proved strategically barren, prompting the French saying that one more such defeat would destroy the victors.

Background

After the fall of Tournai in September 1709, Marlborough moved to besiege Mons. France, bankrupted and starving after the terrible winter of 1708–09, made one more effort: Louis XIV recalled the disgraced Villars to command an army of relief, with instructions that summed up the desperation of the hour — the king told him the fate of France rested on saving the army. Villars took a strong position in the gap between two woods near Malplaquet, fortifying it with trenches, abatis and redoubts, and Marlborough, delayed by negotiations with the Dutch, gave him two days to dig in.

The battle

The allied plan required grinding flank attacks to draw in the French reserves before a central breakthrough. On the allied left, Prince Eugene's Austrians and Imperial troops attacked the French right in the Bois de Sars in ferocious woodland fighting in which Eugene was wounded and thousands fell. On the right, Dutch infantry under the Prince of Orange assaulted the French left and was slaughtered in front of the entrenchments, losing some 5,000 men in half an hour. The attacks nonetheless stretched the French line to breaking point and stripped the centre: when Villars was wounded and carried from the field, Boufflers took command just as Marlborough launched his massed cavalry through the denuded French centre. After a huge cavalry melee the French horse covered an orderly infantry withdrawal; Boufflers retreated in good order toward Bavai, leaving the allies masters of a field covered with dead.

Aftermath

The allies lost more than twice as many men as the French — over 20,000 — and though Mons fell in October, the campaign achieved little more. Villars wrote to Louis XIV that "if God grants us the grace to lose another such battle, Your Majesty may count on his enemies being destroyed." Malplaquet strengthened the British peace party, contributed to the fall of the Whig ministry and Marlborough's dismissal, and set the pattern for the war's negotiated end at Utrecht (1713).
