William Morris Davis (12 February 1850 – 5 February 1934) was an American geographer, geologist, geomorphologist and meteorologist, often called the "father of American geography". He is best known for his cycle of erosion model of landscape development.

Career

Davis graduated from Harvard in 1869 and spent three years at the Argentine National Observatory in Córdoba before joining the Harvard faculty, where he taught for four decades. He founded the Association of American Geographers in 1904 and was hugely influential in establishing geography as an academic discipline in the United States, training a generation of geomorphologists through field excursions across North America and Europe.

Cycle of erosion

Davis proposed that landscapes evolve through a predictable sequence of stages — youth, maturity and old age — following uplift, as rivers and slopes erode the land towards a low, gently rolling plain he called a peneplain. The Davisian cycle of erosion dominated geomorphology for half a century; though later challenged by process-oriented studies, it remains historically foundational.

Other work and legacy

Davis also worked in meteorology and on the classification of coral reefs, supporting Darwin's subsidence theory. The Davis Mountains region association and Davis Strait are not his namesakes, but his extensive publications — including the posthumous compilation of his geographical essays — cemented his influence.
