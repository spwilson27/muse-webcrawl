Vasectomy

Vasectomy is an elective surgical procedure for male sterilization or permanent contraception. During the procedure, the vasa deferentia are cut and tied or sealed, so as to prevent sperm from entering the urethra and thereby prevent fertilization of a female through sexual intercourse. Vasectomies are usually performed in a physician's office or an outpatient clinic.

Procedure

In the conventional procedure, a small incision is made in the scrotum, the vas deferens on each side is drawn out, divided, and the ends closed by sutures, clips, cautery, or fascial interposition. In the "no-scalpel" technique, developed in China in the 1970s, a small puncture replaces the incision, reducing bleeding and infection risk. The procedure typically takes under 30 minutes under local anaesthesia. Sterility is not immediate; semen must be tested after several weeks to confirm the absence of sperm (azoospermia), and other contraception is used until then.

Effectiveness and risks

Vasectomy is one of the most effective contraceptive methods, with a failure rate well under 1 percent once azoospermia is confirmed. Short-term complications include bleeding, hematoma, infection, and sperm granuloma; a small proportion of men develop chronic post-vasectomy pain. Vasectomy does not affect testosterone levels, libido, or the ability to ejaculate, and does not protect against sexually transmitted infections.

Reversal

Vasectomy can sometimes be reversed by vasovasostomy or vasoepididymostomy, but success rates decline with time and reversal is not guaranteed, so the procedure is regarded as permanent.
