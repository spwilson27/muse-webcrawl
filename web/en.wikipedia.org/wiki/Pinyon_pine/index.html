Pinyon pine

Pinyon–juniper woodland in the Great Basin

The pinyon pines (Spanish: piñón) are a group of small pine species in the genus Pinus native to Mexico and the southwestern United States. They are characterised by their large, edible, wingless seeds — pine nuts — and are classified in subsection Cembroides. Together with junipers they dominate the pinyon–juniper woodlands that cover vast areas of the Great Basin, Colorado Plateau and northern Mexico.

Species

Principal species include the Colorado pinyon (Pinus edulis), the single-leaf pinyon (P. monophylla) — unusual for bearing its needles singly — the Mexican pinyon (P. cembroides), and the Parry pinyon (P. quadrifolia). They are slow-growing, drought-tolerant trees of dry mountain slopes and mesas, typically at elevations of 1,000 to 2,800 metres.

Ecology and use

Pinyon nuts are an important wildlife food, dispersed and cached especially by pinyon jays, whose forgotten caches plant new trees. The nuts have been a staple food of Indigenous peoples of the region for thousands of years and remain a valued regional harvest in New Mexico and neighbouring states, where piñón is the official state nut of New Mexico. The aromatic wood is widely used as firewood and for traditional building.

Threats

Pinyon woodlands have suffered extensive die-offs during hotter droughts, driven by bark beetle outbreaks in water-stressed trees, and the group faces mounting pressure from climate change and historical woodland-clearing programmes.
