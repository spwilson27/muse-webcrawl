Equity (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Equity is the body of law developed in England by the Court of Chancery, distinct from the common law administered by the royal courts. Where the common law's rigidity produced injustice in particular cases — rigid writs, no remedy for uses (trusts), harsh forfeitures — petitioners appealed to the king's conscience, administered by the Lord Chancellor ("the keeper of the king's conscience"), who developed equitable principles and remedies.

Principles and remedies

Equity acts on the person (in personam), follows maxims (equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy; he who seeks equity must do equity; equity looks to intent rather than form) and supplies remedies the common law lacked: injunctions, specific performance, rescission, rectification, trusts and fiduciary duties. After jurisdictional conflict (settled in the Earl of Oxford's Case, 1615, for equity's supremacy on conflict), equity itself hardened into precedent — Selden's quip about the Chancellor's foot became history — and the Judicature Acts 1873–75 fused the administration of law and equity into one court system (fusion completed in most common-law countries; Delaware's Court of Chancery keeps the separate tradition alive). Equity's modern heartland is trusts, fiduciary law, corporate remedies and interim injunctions.
