The Salian dynasty (German: Salier) was a dynasty in the High Middle Ages that produced four German kings and Holy Roman Emperors (1024–1125): Conrad II, Henry III, Henry IV, and Henry V. Their rule marked the apex of imperial power in medieval Germany and is remembered above all for the Investiture Controversy with the papacy.

Origins

The Salians took their name, by tradition, from the Salian Franks, though the family first appears in the 10th century as counts in the Wormsgau, Speyergau and Nahegau in the Rhineland. Conrad the Red, duke of Lorraine, married Liutgard, daughter of Emperor Otto I, bringing the family into the imperial circle. His great-grandson Conrad was elected king in 1024 after the Ottonian line died out with Henry II.

The emperors

Conrad II (r. 1024–1039) secured the succession of his son, acquired the kingdom of Burgundy, and founded Speyer Cathedral. Henry III (r. 1039–1056) dominated the papacy, deposing and appointing popes, and extended imperial authority over Bohemia, Poland and Hungary. Under Henry IV (r. 1056–1105), the clash with Pope Gregory VII over lay investiture erupted: excommunicated, Henry walked barefoot to Canossa in 1077 to beg absolution, and Germany descended into civil war. Henry V (r. 1105–1125) forced his father to abdicate and finally settled the investiture question with the Concordat of Worms in 1122. He died childless, and the dynasty ended.

Legacy

The Salian period saw the consolidation of the empire's ministeriales, the growth of royal administration, and monumental Romanesque architecture, above all Speyer Cathedral, where Conrad II, Henry III, Henry IV and Henry V are buried.
