Foco

Foco theory
A theory of revolution through guerrilla warfare associated with Che Guevara and Régis Debray.

The foco (Spanish for "focus" or "hotbed") is a theory of revolution through guerrilla warfare, primarily associated with Marxist revolutionary Ernesto "Che" Guevara and popularized by the French intellectual Régis Debray in his 1967 book Revolution in the Revolution?. According to the theory, a small, dedicated vanguard of revolutionaries — the foco — operating as guerrillas, usually in the countryside, can itself create the conditions for a general uprising against a regime, rather than waiting for those conditions to mature.

Theory

Guevara developed the idea from the experience of the Cuban Revolution (1956–1959), in which a small band of fighters led by Fidel Castro landed from the yacht Granma and built a guerrilla army in the Sierra Maestra that eventually defeated the Batista regime. In his manual Guerrilla Warfare (1960) and later writings, Guevara argued that, where a government has come to power by fraud or rules unconstitutionally, the outbreak of guerrilla fighting itself can generate popular support and revolutionary conditions: "It is not necessary to wait until all conditions for making revolution exist; the insurrection can create them."

The foco operates in rural terrain favorable to guerrillas, wins the peasants' support through its conduct and land reform, escalates from small ambushes to larger engagements, and grows into a people's army capable of confronting the state directly.

Practice and failures

During the 1960s, foco-inspired insurgencies spread across Latin America, including in Guatemala, Venezuela, Colombia, Peru, and Argentina. Almost all failed. The theory's most devastating refutation was Guevara's own expedition to Bolivia in 1966–67: his Ñancahuazú foco found little peasant support, became isolated, and was destroyed by the Bolivian army with United States assistance; Guevara was captured and executed in October 1967.

Criticism

Critics, including orthodox communist parties and theorists such as those influenced by Mao Zedong's doctrine of protracted people's war, charged foquismo with voluntarism — substituting the armed vanguard for the political organization of the masses — and with neglecting urban organizing, party building, and the specific conditions that had made Cuba exceptional (including the corruption and collapse of Batista's regime and the role of the urban underground). Debray himself later revised aspects of the theory. Historians generally credit the Cuban victory to a broader combination of factors than the rural foco alone.

Legacy

Despite its battlefield failures, foco theory was enormously influential on the New Left and on liberation movements worldwide in the 1960s and 1970s. It remains a reference point in debates over vanguardism, armed struggle, and revolutionary strategy.
