Protagoras (/proʊˈtæɡərəs/; Greek: Πρωταγόρας; c. 490 BC – c. 420 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher from Abdera in Thrace and the most celebrated of the Sophists. He is best known for the dictum "man is the measure of all things", an early statement of relativism, and for his professional teaching of rhetoric and aretē (excellence or virtue) for fees—an innovation that made him famous and, to critics such as Plato, notorious. None of his writings survive complete; his thought is known through fragments and through Plato's dialogues, above all the Protagoras and the Theaetetus.

Life

Protagoras was born in Abdera, the home town of Democritus, around 490 BC. Ancient sources disagree about his background: some say his father was a wealthy man named Maeandrius who entertained the Persian king Xerxes; a less flattering tradition made him a porter who carried wood, a story probably invented to mock his rise. He travelled widely through the Greek world giving displays of rhetoric and teaching for pay, and he visited Athens several times, becoming part of the intellectual circle around Pericles. Pericles entrusted him with drafting the laws for the pan-Hellenic colony of Thurii, founded in southern Italy in 444/443 BC.

Later tradition reports that Protagoras was prosecuted for impiety (asebeia) at Athens because his book On the Gods opened with an agnostic declaration, and that his books were publicly burned. The historicity of the trial is doubted by many modern scholars, since it fits a familiar pattern of legends about philosophers prosecuted by the city. He is said to have died around 420 BC, possibly in a shipwreck while travelling to Sicily. Diogenes Laërtius lists among his works treatises on wrestling, mathematics, government, ambition, the virtues, and a work entitled Truth (Alētheia), also called the Refutations (Kataballontes).

"Man is the measure"

The opening of Protagoras's Truth declared: "Of all things the measure is man: of the things that are, that they are, and of the things that are not, that they are not" (πάντων χρημάτων μέτρον ἐστὶν ἄνθρωπος). In Plato's Theaetetus, Socrates interprets the saying as a doctrine of relativism: things are for each person as they appear to that person. If the wind feels cold to one man and warm to another, then it is cold for the one and warm for the other, and there is no further fact of the matter by which either perception could be judged false. On this reading every belief is true for the person who holds it.

Socrates attacks the doctrine with the famous peritropē, or "turning of the tables": if whatever seems true to anyone is true, then the belief of Protagoras's opponents that Protagoras is wrong must also be true, so the doctrine refutes itself. Whether the historical Protagoras held the sweeping doctrine Plato criticises, or a more moderate claim about perception and value, remains disputed among scholars. Closely connected is the reported Protagorean art of "making the weaker argument defeat the stronger" (τὸν ἥττω λόγον κρείττω ποιεῖν), a technique of arguing both sides of a case that conservatives such as Aristophanes lampooned as the essence of sophistic trickery.

Agnosticism

His treatise On the Gods began: "Concerning the gods, I have no means of knowing whether they exist or not, nor of what sort they may be, because of the obscurity of the subject and the brevity of human life." The statement is often cited as one of the earliest surviving expressions of agnosticism, and it made Protagoras a byword for religious scepticism in antiquity, whatever his own intentions were.

Teaching of virtue

In Plato's Protagoras, the sophist boldly claims that, unlike other teachers who ruin young men with technical subjects, he teaches precisely what they come for: good counsel (euboulia) in private and public affairs—"the art of politics" that makes men good citizens. He defends his practice with a great myth of human origins: Epimetheus distributed natural endowments among the animals, and when human beings proved defenceless, Prometheus stole fire and technical wisdom from the gods; but humans still wronged one another until Zeus sent Hermes to distribute aidōs (respect or shame) and dikē (justice) to all alike. Hence, Protagoras argues, everyone shares in political virtue, democratic practices of open debate are rational, and virtue can be taught because society itself is a school of punishment, example and instruction.

Grammar and language

Protagoras also took an interest in the correctness of language (orthoepeia). He classified nouns by grammatical gender—masculine, feminine and neuter (or "implements")—and criticised Homer for grammatical improprieties, such as using what he took to be a feminine noun for wrath (mēnis) and issuing commands where a prayer was intended. He distinguished types and tenses of utterance, an early step toward the theory of speech acts, and he is credited with devising the Socratic method's predecessor in competitive question-and-answer, and with inventing the declamatory set speech.

Legacy

Through Plato's two great treatments, Protagoras became the archetype of the Sophist and a permanent foil for Socratic philosophy. Modern scholarship has been kinder: his measure-doctrine is read as a serious early form of relativism and constructivism, his myth as a foundational text of democratic theory, and his professional teaching as a democratising force in Greek education. The asteroid 11050 Protagoras is named after him.
