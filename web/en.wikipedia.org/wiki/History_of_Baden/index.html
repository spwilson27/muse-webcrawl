History of Baden

Baden was a state of the Holy Roman Empire and later of Germany, occupying territory along the east bank of the Upper Rhine in what is now southwestern Germany. From the medieval margraviate ruled by the House of Zähringen, it grew into the Grand Duchy of Baden — a Napoleonic-era creation and one of the most liberal states of the German Confederation — before becoming a republic in 1918 and merging with Württemberg and Hohenzollern in 1952 to form the modern state of Baden-Württemberg.

Margraviate and division

The Margraves of Baden descended from Herman II, who first used the title in 1112, taking it from Baden Castle (Hohenbaden) above the spa town of Baden-Baden. In 1535 the territory was divided between two brothers into the Protestant margraviate of Baden-Durlach and the Catholic Baden-Baden line — a split that lasted until Margrave Charles Frederick of Baden-Durlach inherited the whole in 1771, reuniting Baden after 236 years.

Grand Duchy

Napoleonic territorial reorganisation vastly enlarged Baden: under the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss (1803) and the Confederation of the Rhine, Charles Frederick gained the Breisgau, the Ortenau, Constance, and Heidelberg and Mannheim from the Electoral Palatinate, roughly quadrupling his state, and took the title Grand Duke in 1806. The 1818 constitution made Baden a byword for German liberalism; its parliament was a centre of liberal and democratic agitation, and Baden was a hotspot of the Revolutions of 1848–49, suppressed by Prussian troops. Baden fought on Austria's side in 1866 but joined the German Empire in 1871.

Republic and merger

Grand Duke Frederick II abdicated in November 1918, and the Free State of Baden became a republic within Weimar Germany. After 1945 the north fell in the American zone (forming Württemberg-Baden) while the south formed the French-zone state of (South) Baden; a 1951 referendum approved the merger, creating Baden-Württemberg on 25 April 1952, with Karlsruhe and Freiburg among its principal cities.
