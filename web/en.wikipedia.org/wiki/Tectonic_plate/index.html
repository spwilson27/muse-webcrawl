Tectonic plate

[Image: The major tectonic plates of Earth's lithosphere]

A tectonic plate is a massive, irregularly shaped slab of solid rock, generally composed of both continental and oceanic lithosphere, that moves as a rigid unit over the more ductile asthenosphere beneath. Earth's lithosphere is divided into seven or eight major plates — including the Pacific, North American, Eurasian, African, Antarctic, Indo-Australian and South American plates — plus numerous smaller ones such as the Nazca, Cocos, Philippine Sea and Juan de Fuca plates.

Movement and boundaries

Plates move at rates of a few centimetres per year, roughly the speed at which fingernails grow, driven by mantle convection, slab pull at subduction zones and ridge push at mid-ocean ridges. Their interactions define three boundary types: divergent boundaries, where plates separate and new crust forms (e.g. the Mid-Atlantic Ridge); convergent boundaries, where one plate subducts beneath another or continental crust crumples into mountains (e.g. the Himalayas); and transform boundaries, where plates slide past each other (e.g. the San Andreas Fault).

Consequences

Most earthquakes, volcanic eruptions and mountain building occur at plate boundaries; the "Ring of Fire" around the Pacific Plate accounts for the majority of Earth's seismic and volcanic activity. The theory of plate tectonics, consolidated in the 1960s from evidence including seafloor spreading, palaeomagnetism and the fit of continental margins, unified geology by explaining continental drift and the distribution of fossils, mountain ranges and ocean basins.
