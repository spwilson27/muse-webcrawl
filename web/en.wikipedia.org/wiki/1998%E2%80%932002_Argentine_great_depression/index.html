The 1998–2002 Argentine great depression was an economic depression in Argentina that began in the third quarter of 1998 and ended in the second quarter of 2002. It followed the Mexican and Asian financial crises and culminated in the abandonment of the peso's fixed peg to the US dollar.

Causes

Under the 1991 Convertibility Law, the Argentine peso was pegged one-to-one to the US dollar, which tamed hyperinflation but removed monetary flexibility. A strong dollar, Brazil's 1999 devaluation, rising foreign debt, and fiscal deficits made Argentine exports uncompetitive and pushed the economy into prolonged recession.

Crisis of 2001

In late 2001, a bank run forced the government to impose the corralito, freezing bank deposits. Protests erupted in December 2001; President Fernando de la Rúa resigned amid riots that left dozens dead. Argentina defaulted on some $132 billion of debt — then the largest sovereign default in history — and in January 2002 abandoned the peg, causing the peso to lose most of its value.

Recovery

From 2003, under President Néstor Kirchner, Argentina grew rapidly on the back of a devalued currency and high commodity prices, though poverty and unemployment remained elevated for years.
