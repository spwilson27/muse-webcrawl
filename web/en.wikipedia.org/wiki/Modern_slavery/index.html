Contemporary slavery

Contemporary slavery, also known as modern slavery or neo-slavery, refers to institutional slavery that continues to exist in present-day society. Although slavery is illegal in every country, forms of coerced and exploitative labour persist worldwide, including forced labour, debt bondage, human trafficking, forced marriage and child labour under coercive conditions.

Estimates

The International Labour Organization (ILO), Walk Free and the International Organization for Migration estimated in their 2022 Global Estimates of Modern Slavery that about 50 million people were living in modern slavery on any given day in 2021 — roughly 28 million in forced labour and 22 million in forced marriages. The numbers have been debated by scholars, since definitions vary and much of the data must be extrapolated from surveys.

Forms

  • Forced labour — work performed involuntarily under threat of penalty, common in agriculture, construction, domestic work, fishing and manufacturing supply chains.
  • Debt bondage — a person is pledged against a debt and cannot leave until it is repaid, with terms often manipulated so the debt is never cleared.
  • Human trafficking — recruitment or movement of people by deception or coercion for exploitation, including sexual exploitation.
  • Forced marriage — marriages entered without free and full consent, from which a person cannot leave.
  • Descent-based slavery — people born into slavery because of their caste or ancestry, persisting in parts of West Africa and elsewhere.

Responses

International law prohibits slavery through instruments such as the 1926 Slavery Convention and the ILO Forced Labour Convention. National measures include the United Kingdom's Modern Slavery Act 2015, which requires large companies to report on steps taken to prevent slavery in their supply chains, and similar legislation in Australia and other countries. Sustainable Development Goal 8.7 commits states to ending modern slavery and child labour.
