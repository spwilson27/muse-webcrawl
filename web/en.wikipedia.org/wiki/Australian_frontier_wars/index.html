The Australian frontier wars were the series of conflicts fought between Indigenous Australians and European settlers during the colonisation of Australia. The wars lasted from the arrival of the First Fleet in 1788 and continued sporadically into the 20th century, with the last major massacre occurring at Coniston in the Northern Territory in 1928.

Background

British colonisation of Australia proceeded under the legal doctrine of terra nullius, which treated the continent as unoccupied despite the presence of hundreds of distinct Aboriginal peoples. As settlers expanded outward from Sydney and other coastal settlements, competition over land, water and game animals escalated into violence. Aboriginal groups resisted through guerrilla tactics, raids on livestock and attacks on isolated settlers.

Course of the conflicts

Notable episodes include the Hawkesbury and Nepean Wars around Sydney in the 1790s and 1810s, in which the Dharug warrior Pemulwuy became a celebrated resistance figure; the Bathurst War of 1824, in which Governor Brisbane declared martial law against the Wiradjuri; and the Black War in Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania) in the 1820s and 1830s. In Queensland, the Native Police, a paramilitary force of Aboriginal troopers under white officers, conducted widespread dispersals of Aboriginal groups from the 1850s into the early 1900s.

Casualties and commemoration

Historian Henry Reynolds estimated in The Other Side of the Frontier (1981) that as many as 20,000 Aboriginal people and roughly 2,000 to 2,500 Europeans died in frontier violence. Subsequent research, including the University of Newcastle's Colonial Frontier Massacres project, has documented hundreds of massacre sites. The frontier wars are not officially commemorated at the Australian War Memorial, a matter of continuing public debate in Australia.
