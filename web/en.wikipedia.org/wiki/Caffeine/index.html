Caffeine

FormulaC8H10N4O2

Elimination half-life~3–7 hours (adults)
MetabolismLiver (CYP1A2), mainly to paraxanthine
Legal statusUnregulated in most countries

Caffeine is a central nervous system stimulant of the methylxanthine class and the world's most widely consumed psychoactive drug. It occurs naturally in the seeds, fruits, and leaves of more than sixty plant species — notably coffee beans, tea leaves, cacao pods, kola nuts, guarana, and yerba mate — where it acts as a natural pesticide that paralyses and kills insects feeding on the plants.

Mechanism of action

Caffeine works primarily by blocking adenosine receptors in the brain. Adenosine accumulates during waking hours and promotes sleepiness; by antagonising its receptors, caffeine temporarily reduces fatigue and increases alertness without reducing the underlying need for sleep. It also modestly increases the release of dopamine and norepinephrine. Caffeine is absorbed completely within about 45 minutes of ingestion, peaks in the blood within 15 minutes to two hours, and is metabolised in the liver, chiefly by the enzyme CYP1A2, mainly into paraxanthine.

Effects

In moderate doses — regulators such as the US FDA cite up to 400 milligrams per day as an amount not generally associated with negative effects for healthy adults — caffeine improves vigilance, reaction time, and endurance performance. A cup of coffee typically contains 80–175 mg. Adverse effects include insomnia, anxiety, tremor, palpitations, and raised blood pressure. Regular use produces tolerance and physical dependence; abrupt cessation can trigger withdrawal symptoms, most famously a headache, within 12–24 hours. Very large doses (in the order of grams, e.g. from pure powdered caffeine) can be lethal.

History

Caffeinated plants have been used for millennia — in tea in China, coffee in Ethiopia and Yemen, and cacao and guarana in the Americas. The pure compound was first isolated from coffee beans in 1819 by the German chemist Friedlieb Ferdinand Runge, working at the suggestion of Goethe. Coffeehouses spread through the Ottoman world and Europe from the 16th and 17th centuries, making caffeine a defining stimulant of modern working life.

Sources and products

Besides coffee and tea, caffeine is added to soft drinks, energy drinks, analgesic preparations (where it enhances pain relief), and over-the-counter stimulant tablets. Decaffeinated coffee retains a small fraction of the original caffeine. The world average consumption is roughly 1.3 kg of coffee per person per year in caffeine terms — a few cups per day for regular drinkers.
