Good to Great: Why Some Companies Make the Leap... and Others Don't is a management book by Jim Collins that describes how companies transition from being good companies to great companies, and why most fail to make the transition. Published in 2001, it drew on a five-year study by Collins and his research team of 1,435 companies, from which eleven "good-to-great" companies were identified by their stock performance — returns at least three times the market over fifteen years following a transition point.

Findings

The book's central concepts include "Level 5 Leadership" (executives combining humility with professional will), "First Who, Then What" (getting the right people before setting strategy), the "Hedgehog Concept" (focus at the intersection of passion, capability, and economic drivers), a "Culture of Discipline", technology as an accelerator rather than a driver, and the "Flywheel" effect of cumulative momentum. The comparison companies included Circuit City's counterpart Fannie Mae cases and the study profiled firms such as Abbott Laboratories, Kimberly-Clark, Nucor, Philip Morris, Pitney Bowes, Walgreens, and Wells Fargo.

Reception

The book was a major bestseller and is widely assigned in business schools. Critics have argued that its conclusions suffer from survivorship bias and the halo effect — several profiled companies, notably Circuit City and Fannie Mae, later failed or required bailouts — a criticism Collins addressed by noting that greatness is not permanent. A follow-up monograph applied the framework to social-sector organisations.
