Passerine

A passerine is any bird of the order Passeriformes, the perching birds — more than half of all bird species, with over 6,500 identified species in about 140 families. Passerines are distinguished by the arrangement of their toes — three pointing forward and one back — adapted for perching, and most have a specialised vocal organ, the syrinx; the largest group, the oscines, are the songbirds.

Characteristics

Most passerines are small, though the group ranges up to ravens and lyrebirds. Their eggs and altricial young develop in nests, often elaborately built. Many are migratory and omnivorous or insectivorous. The order is divided into three suborders: the New Zealand wrens (Acanthisitti), the suboscines or Tyranni (including flycatchers, antbirds and ovenbirds), and the oscines or Passeri (songbirds — including finches, sparrows, thrushes, warblers, crows and starlings).

Evolution and distribution

Passerines likely originated in the Southern Hemisphere (Gondwana) in the early Cenozoic and now occur on every continent except Antarctica and on most islands. Their songs, learning and diversity make them a model group for studies of evolution, speciation and animal communication.
