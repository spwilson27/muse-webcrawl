Dorothy Day

BornNovember 8, 1897, Brooklyn, New York, U.S.
DiedNovember 29, 1980 (aged 83), New York City, U.S.
OccupationJournalist, social activist
Known forCo-founding the Catholic Worker Movement and The Catholic Worker newspaper

Dorothy Day (November 8, 1897 – November 29, 1980) was an American journalist, social activist, and anarchist who co-founded the Catholic Worker Movement with Peter Maurin in 1933. After a bohemian youth in radical and literary circles, her conversion to Catholicism in 1927 led her to combine orthodox faith with a radical commitment to the poor, pacifism, and voluntary poverty. The movement's newspaper, The Catholic Worker, still sold for a penny, spread its ideas, while its "houses of hospitality" fed and sheltered the homeless — a network that grew to hundreds of communities worldwide.

Day is considered for canonisation by the Catholic Church, which has granted her the title Servant of God.

Life

Born in Brooklyn and raised in Chicago, Day worked as a journalist for socialist and radical publications including The Call and The Masses, was jailed in 1917 for picketing the White House for women's suffrage, and moved in Greenwich Village literary circles. The birth of her daughter Tamar and her conversion reshaped her life. In 1932 she met the French émigré thinker Peter Maurin, and on May 1, 1933 they published the first issue of The Catholic Worker.

Activism

Day practised civil disobedience throughout her life: she was arrested repeatedly, including for refusing to take part in compulsory civil defence drills in the 1950s, for protesting against the Vietnam War, and in support of Cesar Chavez's farm workers in 1973, at the age of 75. She wrote the autobiography The Long Loneliness (1952). She died in 1980 at Maryhouse, a Catholic Worker house in New York. In 2000 the Vatican opened her cause for canonisation, and she is recognised as a Servant of God.
