Reflection symmetry

Reflection symmetry, also called line symmetry, mirror symmetry, or bilateral symmetry, is a type of symmetry with respect to a reflection. A figure has reflection symmetry if there is a line (the line of symmetry or axis) such that reflecting the figure across that line maps the figure onto itself — the two halves are mirror images of each other.

A butterfly has reflection symmetry about its body axis

Examples

An isosceles triangle has one line of symmetry; a rectangle has two; a square has four; a circle has infinitely many. Many letters of the Latin alphabet, such as A, H, and O, have reflection symmetry. Most animals, including humans, show approximate bilateral symmetry in their external body plan, a feature that dates to the evolutionary success of the Bilateria.

In three dimensions

In three dimensions the analogous notion is symmetry with respect to a plane of symmetry. An object may have several symmetry planes: a sphere has infinitely many, and a cuboid has three.

Applications

Reflection symmetry is ubiquitous in architecture, from classical temples to the Taj Mahal, and in design, ornament, and religious symbolism. In physics and chemistry, mirror symmetry underlies the classification of molecular structures and the study of chirality: a molecule that is not superposable on its mirror image is chiral. In biology, deviations from bilateral symmetry can signal developmental stress.
