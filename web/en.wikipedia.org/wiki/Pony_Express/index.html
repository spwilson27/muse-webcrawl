The Pony Express was an American express mail service that used relays of horse-mounted riders between April 1860 and October 1861. It operated between St. Joseph, Missouri, and Sacramento, California, a distance of about 1,800 miles (2,900 km), and reduced the time for mail to travel across the continent to about 10 days.

Operation

The service was established by William H. Russell, Alexander Majors and William B. Waddell through their Central Overland California and Pikes Peak Express Company. Riders rode in relays, changing horses at stations spaced roughly 10–15 miles apart and handing the leather mail pouch (mochila) to a fresh rider every 75–100 miles. At its peak the line employed about 80 riders and some 400–500 horses, with around 184 stations.

Notable events

The Pony Express carried news of Abraham Lincoln's election and inauguration, and its fastest run — about 7 days 17 hours — delivered Lincoln's inaugural address to California. Riders included the young William F. "Buffalo Bill" Cody, according to his later accounts. Despite its fame, the enterprise was a financial failure, losing large sums for its backers.

End of the service

The completion of the transcontinental telegraph on 24 October 1861 rendered the service obsolete; operations ceased two days later. Though it lasted only about 18 months, the Pony Express became a lasting symbol of the American West and of individual daring and endurance.
