History of surgery

The history of surgery stretches from prehistoric trepanation to the antiseptic revolution of the 19th century and the transplant, laparoscopic and robotic surgery of today. For most of its history, surgery was limited by pain, bleeding and infection.

Ancient surgery

Trepanation — drilling holes in the skull — was practised worldwide from the Neolithic. Egyptian papyri (Edwin Smith, Ebers) describe wound care and splinting; the Indian surgeon Sushruta (c. 600 BCE) catalogued instruments, rhinoplasty and cataract couching. Hippocratic and Galenic medicine in the Greco-Roman world set fractures and treated wounds, while in China Hua Tuo was credited with an early anaesthetic, mafeisan.

Medieval and early modern surgery

In the Islamic world, al-Zahrawi's Kitab al-Tasrif (c. 1000) illustrated some 200 instruments. European surgery was practised by barber-surgeons; Ambroise Paré (16th century) reintroduced the ligature and replaced boiling-oil cautery with gentler dressings. Vesalius's anatomy (1543) and Harvey's circulation (1628) deepened surgical knowledge, but operations remained crude, fast and often fatal.

Anaesthesia and antisepsis

Ether anaesthesia, publicly demonstrated by William Morton in 1846 (and Crawford Long's earlier use in 1842), abolished operative pain; chloroform followed. Joseph Lister, applying Pasteur's germ theory, introduced carbolic-acid antisepsis in 1867, and German aseptic technique and steam sterilisation followed. Mortality from amputations and abdominal surgery plummeted.

Modern surgery

With anaesthesia, asepsis, blood transfusion and antibiotics, the 20th century opened every body cavity: appendectomy, cardiac surgery (the first heart transplant by Christiaan Barnard, 1967), organ transplantation enabled by immunosuppression, joint replacement and neurosurgery. Laparoscopic ("keyhole") surgery from the 1980s, endovascular techniques and surgical robots have made operations ever less invasive, while trauma systems and transplantation continue to advance.
