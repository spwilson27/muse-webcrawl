Languages of Africa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The languages of Africa number well over two thousand — by most counts between 1,500 and 2,100 distinct languages, roughly a third of the world's total — spoken by more than a billion people. They are conventionally grouped into several large families: Niger–Congo (by far the largest, including the Bantu languages), Afroasiatic, Nilo-Saharan, and the smaller Khoe-Kwadi, Kx'a and Tuu groupings (traditionally lumped under the now-disputed label "Khoisan"). In addition, Africa has important languages of external origin: Arabic, and the colonial-era languages English, French, Portuguese and Spanish, which serve as official languages in many states.

Major families

Niger–Congo

The Niger–Congo family, with some 1,500 languages, spans most of sub-Saharan Africa. Its Bantu branch alone counts about 500 languages (including Swahili, Zulu, Xhosa, Shona and Kikongo) spread across central, eastern and southern Africa as the result of the Bantu expansion. Non-Bantu branches include the West African languages Yoruba, Igbo, Akan and Wolof, and the Adamawa and Gur groups.

Afroasiatic

Afroasiatic languages — about 375 — dominate North Africa and the Horn: Arabic (in many dialects), the Berber (Amazigh) languages, Hausa (a Chadic language and major West African lingua franca), and the Cushitic and Ethiosemitic languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea, such as Oromo, Somali, Amharic and Tigrinya.

Nilo-Saharan and other groups

The proposed Nilo-Saharan grouping covers languages of the Sahel and East African interior, including the Nilotic languages (Luo, Maasai, Dinka, Nuer) and Songhay. The click-consonant languages of the Kalahari and Tanzania belong to several distinct families whose unity is no longer accepted by most specialists.

Widely spoken languages

By number of total speakers (including second-language speakers), the most widespread African languages include Arabic, Swahili (the lingua franca of East Africa), Hausa, Amharic, Yoruba, Igbo, Oromo, Fulfulde, and Malagasy — the Austronesian language of Madagascar. European languages remain dominant in government, higher education and media in many countries, though policies promoting indigenous languages have expanded.

Language endangerment

Many African languages have small speaker communities and are endangered as speakers shift to regional lingua francas or official languages; documentation and mother-tongue education programmes are active areas of work for linguists and African language academies.
