Wikidata

Wikidata is a free and open knowledge base that can be read and edited by both humans and machines. It acts as central storage for the structured data of Wikimedia projects such as Wikipedia, Wikivoyage, Wiktionary, Wikisource and others. Wikidata was launched on 30 October 2012 and was the first new project of the Wikimedia Foundation since 2006.

Development

Wikidata was created by the Wikimedia Foundation with an initial €1.3 million in funding, half of which was donated by the Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence, the Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation and Google Inc. The project was initially led by Wikimedia Deutschland. Its first phase, completed in March 2013, centralised the storage of interlanguage links between Wikipedia articles, so that a change to a link in one language edition no longer had to be repeated in every other edition.

Data model

The content of Wikidata is organised around items, each representing a topic, concept, object or person, and each identified by a unique identifier beginning with the letter Q (for example, Q42 is Douglas Adams). Items are described by statements, which consist of a property (identified by a P-number, such as P31 "instance of" or P569 "date of birth") and a value. Statements can be annotated with qualifiers and references, and values may be ranked as preferred, normal or deprecated. Unlike Wikipedia articles, Wikidata items have labels, descriptions and aliases in hundreds of languages.

Access and use

Data in Wikidata is published under the Creative Commons CC0 public domain dedication, making it freely reusable without restriction. It can be queried with the Wikidata Query Service using the SPARQL language, accessed through a REST API, and downloaded as complete database dumps. The data is used by search engines, voice assistants, and numerous third-party applications for facts such as population figures, film cast lists and geographical coordinates, and Wikipedia infoboxes increasingly draw their contents from Wikidata.

Reception

Wikidata has grown to hold well over one hundred million items. It has been praised as one of the largest open structured-data resources in the world, while critics have raised questions about data quality, vandalism detection and the notability criteria for items.
