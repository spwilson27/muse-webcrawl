Unix wars

The Unix wars were the rivalries between vendors of the Unix computer operating system in the late 1980s and early 1990s, as competing companies promoted incompatible Unix variants and fought over which would become the industry standard.

Background

After the 1984 AT&T divestiture allowed the company to commercialise Unix, numerous vendors licensed the source code and produced their own variants: AT&T's System V, Berkeley's BSD, SunOS, HP-UX, IBM's AIX, DEC's Ultrix and others. Customers and independent software vendors suffered from incompatibility, prompting standardisation efforts such as POSIX and X/Open's portability guides.

The two camps

In 1987 AT&T and Sun Microsystems agreed to merge System V with SunOS, producing System V Release 4 (SVR4). Alarmed rivals — including IBM, HP, DEC and Apollo — founded the Open Software Foundation (OSF) in 1988 to develop a competing system, OSF/1. AT&T and its allies responded by forming Unix International. For several years the industry was split between the two consortia, each claiming the mantle of "open systems".

Resolution

The wars gradually subsided in the mid-1990s: Novell acquired Unix System Laboratories and transferred the UNIX trademark to X/Open in 1993; vendors converged on common specifications; and in 1996 OSF and X/Open merged to form The Open Group. Ironically, the infighting is often blamed for weakening commercial Unix just as Windows NT and, later, Linux rose to take much of the market.
