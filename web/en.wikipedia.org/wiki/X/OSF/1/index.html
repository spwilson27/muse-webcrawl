OSF/1 is a Unix operating system developed by the Open Software Foundation (OSF) in the early 1990s. It was created as an alternative to AT&T's System V Release 4 during the "Unix wars", in which a consortium of vendors — including IBM, Hewlett-Packard and Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) — sought an open Unix platform not controlled by AT&T and Sun Microsystems. The name of this article reflects its original designation in the X/Open standards context as the OSF's reference Unix implementation.

Technology

OSF/1 was built on Carnegie Mellon University's Mach kernel — later releases on the Mach 3.0 microkernel — combined with components drawn from Berkeley BSD and IBM's AIX. It offered symmetric multiprocessing, threads, and conformance to POSIX and the X/Open Portability Guide, and was bundled with the OSF's other technologies: the Motif graphical user interface toolkit, the DCE distributed computing environment, and the DME management environment. The OSF sold the system as a reference implementation (OSF/1 RI) from which member vendors could build their own products.

Commercial use

The most successful descendant of OSF/1 was DEC's Digital UNIX (originally released as DEC OSF/1 AXP for the Alpha processor, later renamed Tru64 UNIX), which powered Alpha workstations and servers for over a decade and was noted for its advanced clustering (TruCluster) and 64-bit support. Other vendors largely kept their own Unix variants — IBM continued AIX and HP continued HP-UX — using OSF technologies selectively. The OSF itself merged with X/Open in 1996 to form The Open Group, ending OSF/1's role as a consortium product.
