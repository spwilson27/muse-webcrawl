William Thomas Tutte (14 May 1917 – 2 May 2002) was an English and Canadian codebreaker and mathematician. During the Second World War he worked at Bletchley Park, where he made a major breakthrough in the cryptanalysis of the German Lorenz cipher — the "Tunny" system used for high-level Army communications — deducing the structure of the machine from intercepted messages without ever seeing it. His achievement has been described as one of the greatest intellectual feats of the war and led directly to the construction of the Colossus computers.

Academic career

Tutte studied chemistry at Trinity College, Cambridge, where as an undergraduate he published pioneering work on "squaring the square". After the war he completed a PhD in mathematics at Cambridge, proving the theorem now associated with his name on perfect matchings. He moved to Canada, teaching at the University of Toronto and then the University of Waterloo, where he helped found its Faculty of Mathematics and its Department of Combinatorics and Optimization — the first such department in the world.

Graph theory

Tutte is regarded as one of the founders of modern graph theory and matroid theory. Concepts bearing his name include the Tutte polynomial, Tutte's theorem on perfect matchings, the Tutte matrix, and his work on graph factorisation and Hamiltonian cycles (including a counterexample to Tait's conjecture). He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1987.
