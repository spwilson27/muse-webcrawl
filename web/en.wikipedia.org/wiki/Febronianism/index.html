Febronianism

Febronianism was an eighteenth-century movement within the Roman Catholic Church that sought to limit papal authority, increase the power of bishops and national churches, and subordinate the Church in important respects to the secular state. It took its name from the pseudonym "Justinus Febronius" used by the German canon lawyer Johann Nikolaus von Hontheim, auxiliary bishop of Trier, in his 1763 treatise De statu ecclesiae et legitima potestate Romani Pontificis ("On the State of the Church and the Legitimate Power of the Roman Pontiff").

Doctrine

Hontheim argued that the pope's supremacy was a matter of order and unity rather than universal jurisdiction: ultimate authority in the Church rested with the whole body of bishops gathered in general council, and the pope's historical accumulation of rights — including those based on the Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals — could lawfully be curtailed. Febronius hoped that reducing papal claims would remove obstacles to the reunion of Protestants with the Catholic Church and strengthen the position of German prince-bishops against Rome.

Influence and condemnation

The book was placed on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1764, and Hontheim eventually made a partial retraction in 1778 under pressure. Nevertheless Febronian ideas influenced church policy in the Habsburg lands (where they merged with Josephinism), the Synod of Pistoia (1786) in Tuscany, and the Emser Punktation of 1786 by German archbishops. Febronianism is often compared with Gallicanism in France and is considered a Catholic counterpart to conciliarism.
