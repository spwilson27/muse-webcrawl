The Funk Brothers

The Funk Brothers were a group of Detroit-based session musicians who performed the backing tracks for most Motown recordings from 1959 until the company moved to Los Angeles in 1972.

History

Motown founder Berry Gordy assembled the Funk Brothers from Detroit's jazz and blues club scene to play in the cramped garage studio at Hitsville U.S.A., nicknamed the "Snakepit." Core members included keyboardists Earl Van Dyke and Johnny Griffith, bassist James Jamerson, drummer Benny Benjamin, guitarists Robert White, Eddie Willis and Joe Messina, percussionist Jack Ashford, and later drummer Uriel Jones and bassist Bob Babbitt.

Working largely uncredited — Motown did not print musician credits on albums until Marvin Gaye's What's Going On (1971) — the Funk Brothers played on more number-one hits than the Beatles, Elvis Presley, the Rolling Stones and the Beach Boys combined, including "My Girl," "I Heard It Through the Grapevine," "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" and "Standing in the Shadows of Love."

Recognition

Allan Slutsky's 1989 book Standing in the Shadows of Motown and the acclaimed 2002 documentary film of the same name brought the group long-overdue recognition. The Funk Brothers received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2004 and were inducted into the Musicians Hall of Fame in 2007; they received a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame in 2013.

Style and legacy

James Jamerson's melodic, syncopated bass lines and Benny Benjamin's propulsive drumming defined the Motown sound. The group's blend of jazz sophistication, gospel feeling and pop economy influenced generations of session musicians and remains a touchstone of American popular music.
