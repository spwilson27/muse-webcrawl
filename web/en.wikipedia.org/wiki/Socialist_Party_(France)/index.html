The Socialist Party (French: Parti socialiste, PS) is a centre-left to left-wing political party in France. It holds social-democratic and pro-European positions and is a member of the Party of European Socialists and the Progressive Alliance. The party was for centuries' turn one of the two major parties of the Fifth Republic, alongside the centre-right, and supplied two presidents of the Republic: François Mitterrand (1981–1995) and François Hollande (2012–2017).

History

The modern PS was founded in 1969, succeeding the old SFIO, and was refounded at the Épinay Congress of 1971 under François Mitterrand, who led it to victory in the 1981 presidential and legislative elections on the "110 propositions" programme. In office, the party nationalised major banks and industries, abolished the death penalty, lowered the retirement age to 60, and later undertook a pro-market turn (the tournant de la rigueur of 1983).

Under Lionel Jospin, the PS led the "plural left" government of 1997–2002, introducing the 35-hour week. Ségolène Royal lost the 2007 presidential election; François Hollande won in 2012 but left office deeply unpopular. In 2017 the party's candidate Benoît Hamon won only 6.4% in the presidential election, beginning a collapse in which the PS lost most of its parliamentary presence and much of its electorate to Emmanuel Macron's centrists and Jean-Luc Mélenchon's La France Insoumise. Under First Secretary Olivier Faure, the party joined the NUPES alliance (2022) and then the New Popular Front (2024), while remaining strong in local government, where it holds many mayoralties and regional presidencies.

Ideology and factions

The PS is a broad church ranging from social-liberal reformists to democratic-socialist currents organised in organised factions (courants). It supports a mixed economy, strong public services, European integration, secularism (laïcité), and social and environmental protections.
