International HapMap Project

The HapMap charted haplotype blocks — stretches of DNA inherited together — across human populations.

The International HapMap Project was an organization that aimed to develop a haplotype map (HapMap) of the human genome — a catalogue of common genetic variants that occur in human beings. The map was intended to help researchers find genes and genetic variations that affect health and disease.

Background and goals

Launched in October 2002, the HapMap Project was a collaboration among scientists and funding agencies in Canada, China, Japan, Nigeria, the United Kingdom, and the United States. It built on the insight that genetic variants close together on a chromosome tend to be inherited together in blocks (haplotypes) because of linkage disequilibrium. By genotyping a relatively small set of "tag" single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within each block, researchers could capture most common variation in a region and test it for association with disease far more efficiently than genotyping every variant.

Phases and samples

DNA samples came initially from 270 individuals: Yoruba in Ibadan, Nigeria (YRI); Japanese in Tokyo (JPT); Han Chinese in Beijing (CHB); and Utah residents with Northern and Western European ancestry (CEU). Phase I, published in 2005, genotyped over one million SNPs. Phase II (2007) extended the map to more than 3.1 million SNPs. Phase III added further populations, including groups of African, Mexican, and South Asian ancestry in the United States and elsewhere.

Impact

The HapMap made genome-wide association studies (GWAS) practical, and in the years after its release hundreds of genetic associations with common diseases such as type 2 diabetes, Crohn's disease, and coronary artery disease were reported using its data as a reference. Its data were released freely and rapidly became a standard public resource; the project's data were later incorporated into, and largely superseded by, the denser reference panels of the 1000 Genomes Project.
