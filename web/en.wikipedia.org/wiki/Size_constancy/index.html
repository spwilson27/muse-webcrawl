Size constancy

Size constancy is the perception of an object as having a fixed size, despite the fact that the size of its image on the retina changes with viewing distance. A person walking away from us does not appear to shrink, even though their retinal image halves every time the distance doubles. Size constancy is one of the perceptual constancies, alongside shape constancy and colour constancy.

Mechanism

The visual system combines the retinal image size (visual angle) with information about the object's distance — supplied by cues such as binocular disparity, convergence, accommodation, and pictorial depth cues — to compute an estimate of the object's real size. This relationship is expressed in Emmert's law: perceived size is proportional to retinal size multiplied by perceived distance. When distance information is unavailable or misleading, size constancy breaks down, producing illusions: in the Ames room, a distorted room viewed through a peephole makes people appear to grow and shrink as they cross it, because the brain assumes the room is rectangular and attributes the changing retinal size to the people rather than their distance.

Related phenomena

The Moon illusion — the Moon appearing larger near the horizon than high in the sky — is partly a failure or misapplication of size–distance scaling. The Ponzo illusion similarly exploits converging lines as a depth cue to make identical objects appear different in size. Experiments from Holway and Boring (1941) onward demonstrated that when observers judge a distant disc's size with full depth cues available, their judgements track physical size; when cues are removed through a reduction screen, judgements collapse toward retinal (visual-angle) size.

Development

Studies using habituation methods indicate that even young infants show a degree of size constancy, suggesting the mechanism is present early, though it refines with experience. Size constancy is also found in many non-human animals.
