English Poor Laws

The English Poor Laws were a system of poor relief in England and Wales that developed out of late-medieval and Tudor statutes and existed until the emergence of the modern welfare state after the Second World War. The system gave every parish a legal duty to relieve its own poor, financed by a compulsory local tax, the poor rate. Its history is conventionally divided between the Old Poor Law, codified in the Elizabethan Poor Relief Act 1601, and the New Poor Law established by the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834, which centred relief on the workhouse.

The workhouse became the central institution of poor relief after 1834.

Origins and the Old Poor Law

Medieval poor relief was largely the work of monasteries and charity, but the dissolution of the monasteries under Henry VIII, population growth and price inflation created a crisis of vagrancy that Tudor governments answered with increasingly systematic legislation. Acts of 1536, 1552 and 1572 introduced parish collections, compulsory contributions and the office of overseer of the poor. The Poor Relief Act 1601 (43 Eliz. c. 2) consolidated the system for the next two centuries: each parish must levy a poor rate, appoint overseers, relieve the "impotent" poor (aged, sick, orphaned), set the able-bodied to work, and apprentice pauper children. The Settlement Act 1662 tied entitlement to a legally defined parish of settlement and allowed newcomers likely to become chargeable to be removed.

Variations and the Speenhamland system

By the eighteenth century many parishes provided "outdoor relief" — cash or kind given to people in their own homes — including wage supplements. The most famous was the Speenhamland system of 1795, adopted in Berkshire and widely copied, which topped up wages according to the price of bread and family size. Critics, including Thomas Malthus, argued such allowances depressed wages and encouraged population growth; by the early 1830s poor rates had risen sharply and the system was widely blamed for rural demoralisation.

The New Poor Law of 1834

The Royal Commission of 1832–34, influenced by Edwin Chadwick and Nassau Senior, produced the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834. Its principles were centralised control (a Poor Law Commission in London), the grouping of parishes into unions administered by elected Boards of Guardians, and less eligibility: relief for the able-bodied was to be available only inside a workhouse whose conditions were deliberately made worse than those of the lowest-paid independent labourer, deterring all but the truly destitute. The regime was applied harshly, provoking the Anti-Poor Law movement in the industrial north and scandals such as Andover (1845), where starving inmates were found gnawing the bones they were set to crush.

Decline and abolition

From the late nineteenth century the workhouse system's share of welfare shrank as specialised institutions (infirmaries, asylums) split off and new forms of provision — old-age pensions (1908), National Insurance (1911) — bypassed the Poor Law entirely. Boards of Guardians were abolished in 1929, and the National Assistance Act 1948 formally repealed the Poor Law, completing the transition to the modern welfare state of the Beveridge settlement.
