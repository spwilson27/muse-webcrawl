Orchiectomy

Orchiectomy

Other names: Orchidectomy, testicle removal

Specialty: Urology

Approach: Inguinal or scrotal

Orchiectomy (also spelled orchidectomy) is a surgical procedure in which one or both testicles are removed. Removal of a single testicle is termed a unilateral orchiectomy; removal of both is a bilateral orchiectomy. The procedure is a form of castration when performed bilaterally, because the testes produce the great majority of the body's testosterone as well as sperm.

Indications

Orchiectomy is performed for a number of reasons:

  • Testicular cancer — radical inguinal orchiectomy is the standard first step in treating most testicular tumours, both for diagnosis and local control.
  • Prostate cancer — bilateral orchiectomy deprives the tumour of testosterone and is an alternative to medical androgen deprivation therapy.
  • Trauma — where a testicle is irreparably damaged.
  • Torsion or infection — where the testicle has become non-viable.
  • Gender-affirming surgery — as part of treatment for some transgender women.
  • Undescended or atrophic testicle — where the organ carries an unacceptable risk of malignancy.

Technique

In the radical inguinal approach, used whenever cancer is suspected, an incision is made in the groin, the spermatic cord is clamped high at the internal inguinal ring, and the testicle is delivered from the scrotum and removed. The scrotum itself is not incised, because violating it could alter the lymphatic drainage of the tumour and risk seeding cancer in the scrotal skin. A simple orchiectomy, used for benign indications, may be performed through a scrotal incision. A testicular prosthesis can be placed for cosmetic purposes.

Effects and complications

Unilateral orchiectomy usually has little effect on fertility or hormone levels because the remaining testicle compensates. Bilateral orchiectomy causes infertility and a sharp fall in testosterone, with consequences including hot flushes, loss of libido, erectile dysfunction, loss of muscle mass, and increased long-term risks of osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease; testosterone replacement may be offered when the indication permits. Surgical complications include bleeding, infection, and chronic pain, and are uncommon.
