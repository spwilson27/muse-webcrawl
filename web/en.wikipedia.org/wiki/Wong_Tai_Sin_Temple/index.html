Wong Tai Sin Temple

Wong Tai Sin Temple (Chinese: 黃大仙祠) is a Taoist temple in the Wong Tai Sin District of Kowloon, Hong Kong, dedicated to the deity Wong Tai Sin (Huang Daxian), the "Great Immortal Wong". Covering some 18,000 square metres, it is one of Hong Kong's most famous shrines, renowned among worshippers for the claim that it "makes every wish come true upon request" (yau kau pit ying).

History

The cult of Wong Tai Sin was brought from Guangdong to Hong Kong in 1915 by the Taoist priest Leung Renyan, who first kept the deity's portrait in a shop and then in a small shrine at what is now the temple site in Chuk Yuen. In 1921, according to the temple's tradition, the deity indicated by divine writing that a new temple should be built there. Construction proceeded in stages, and the temple was administered by the Taoist organisation Sik Sik Yuen, founded the same year. The present main altar buildings largely date from a major reconstruction completed in 1973.

Layout and features

The temple complex incorporates elements of Taoism, Buddhism, and Confucianism. Its architecture follows traditional Chinese palace style with red pillars, golden roofs, and blue friezes. Principal structures include the Main Altar (大雄寶殿), the Three Saints Hall (三聖堂), the Yue Heung Shrine dedicated to the Buddha of the Burning Lamp, the Confucian Hall, and the Bronze Pavilion. Behind the halls, the Good Wish Garden (從心苑), completed in 1991, is a Chinese garden with pavilions, a zigzag bridge, and a replica of the Nine-Dragon Wall of Beijing.

Worship

Worshippers come to pray for health, fortune, and guidance, most visibly through kau chim, shaking a bamboo cylinder of numbered fortune sticks until one falls out, whose poem is then interpreted by fortune tellers in the temple's adjacent arcade. The temple is especially crowded at Chinese New Year, when thousands queue overnight to be the first to plant incense, and on the deity's birthday, the 23rd day of the eighth lunar month. The surrounding district and an MTR station take the temple's name.
