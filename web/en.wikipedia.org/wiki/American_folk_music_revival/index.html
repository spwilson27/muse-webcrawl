American folk music revival

The American folk music revival was a phenomenon in the United States that began during the 1940s and peaked in popularity in the mid-1960s. Its roots went earlier, but performers such as Woody Guthrie, Pete Seeger, Lead Belly and the Almanac Singers brought traditional folk songs to new urban audiences.

Stylistic originsAmerican folk, blues, country
Cultural origins1940s–1960s, United States
Key figuresPete Seeger, Woody Guthrie, Bob Dylan, Joan Baez

Early revival

Collectors John and Alan Lomax recorded rural musicians for the Library of Congress. During the Depression and Second World War, folk song became linked with labour and left-wing movements. The Weavers' 1950 hit "Goodnight Irene" brought folk to the pop charts before McCarthy-era blacklisting curtailed careers.

Commercial boom

The Kingston Trio's "Tom Dooley" (1958) launched a commercial boom. Coffeehouses, college campuses and festivals — notably the Newport Folk Festival, founded 1959 — flourished. Joan Baez, Peter, Paul and Mary, and Bob Dylan brought topical songwriting to mass audiences, closely tied to the civil rights movement and opposition to the Vietnam War.

Legacy

Dylan's move to electric rock at Newport in 1965 symbolized folk-rock's emergence. The revival preserved traditional repertoires, shaped singer-songwriter culture, and influenced rock, country and Americana.
