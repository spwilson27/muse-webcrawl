Sikyatki is a ruined ancestral Hopi village and archaeological site on the eastern flank of First Mesa in northeastern Arizona, and also the name of the celebrated polychrome pottery style associated with it. The village was occupied from roughly the late 14th to the early 17th century. Its ceramics — Sikyatki Polychrome — are regarded as among the finest prehistoric pottery of the American Southwest, characterised by a buff-to-orange slipped surface painted in brown and red with bold, flowing designs of birds, feathers and other motifs.

Excavation

The site was excavated in 1895 by Jesse Walter Fewkes of the Smithsonian Institution's Bureau of American Ethnology. Fewkes recovered large quantities of pottery from burials at Sikyatki and published influential reports illustrating the designs. The excavation took place just as the neighbouring Hopi village of Walpi was experiencing a revival of pottery-making led by the potter Nampeyo.

Pottery and revival

Sikyatki wares are coil-built, stone-polished and fired to yellow-orange tones; their designs abandon earlier rigid geometry for asymmetric, curvilinear compositions. Nampeyo, a Hopi-Tewa potter of Hano village, studied the ancient sherds — including those collected by her husband at Sikyatki — and adapted their motifs, launching the "Sikyatki Revival" that transformed Pueblo pottery into a recognised art form and reshaped the market for Native American ceramics.
