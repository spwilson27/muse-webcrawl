Revenge tragedy
PeriodLate 16th – early 17th century
OriginEngland, adapting Senecan tragedy
Defining workThe Spanish Tragedy by Thomas Kyd

Revenge tragedy (also revenge drama) is a genre of tragedy, most fully developed in early modern English theatre, whose plot turns on a character's quest to avenge a murder, typically that of a close relative. The avenger is frequently urged on by the ghost of the victim and is caught between a moral and religious prohibition against private revenge and a social code of honour that demands it. The term overlaps with revenge play, though "revenge tragedy" is usually reserved for the tragic subset of the form.

History

The genre's immediate ancestor is the Roman tragedian Seneca, whose dramas of blood and retribution were fixtures of the Tudor grammar-school curriculum. The anonymous Ur-Hamlet (now lost) and Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) transplanted Senecan devices—ghosts, soliloquies of vengeance, madness, and piles of corpses—onto the English popular stage.

The vogue continued through the Jacobean period with increasingly cynical and grotesque works such as The Revenger's Tragedy, The Duchess of Malfi and Women Beware Women, which dwell on court corruption and depict the revenger becoming morally indistinguishable from his target.

Themes and motifs

  • The ghost — an apparition of the murdered person who reveals the crime and demands retribution.
  • Madness — the revenger commonly feigns madness or descends into it under the strain of his task, as Hamlet does.
  • Delay — the avenger typically postpones action, generating the play's suspense and moral debate.
  • Memento mori — skulls, graveyards and meditations on decay underscore the wages of vengeance.
  • Reciprocal destruction — the final scene usually kills off revenger and villains alike, restoring order only through catastrophe.

Notable works

  • The Spanish Tragedy — Thomas Kyd
  • Hamlet — William Shakespeare
  • Titus Andronicus — William Shakespeare
  • The Revenger's Tragedy — attributed to Cyril Tourneur or Thomas Middleton
  • The Duchess of Malfi — John Webster
  • The Maid's Tragedy — Beaumont and Fletcher

Influence

Beyond the early modern stage, revenge tragedy shaped Gothic fiction, melodrama, and the modern revenge film. Shakespeare's Hamlet, the genre's supreme achievement, has become a touchstone for later treatments of hesitation, conscience, and the self-destructive nature of vendetta.
