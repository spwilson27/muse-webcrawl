Trans–New Guinea languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Trans–New Guinea languages (TNG) are a proposed major language family spoken in the highlands and interior of New Guinea and neighbouring islands, and one of the world's largest language families by number of languages. In its broadest formulation the family comprises roughly 400–480 languages spoken by more than 3.5 million people, extending from the Bomberai Peninsula in the west to the south-eastern tail of Papua New Guinea.

Classification

The proposal originates with Stephen Wurm and Malcolm Ross. Ross's influential revision (2005), based on shared subject-agreement prefixes on verbs and reconstructed pronoun paradigms, reduced the secure core of the family but retained a large grouping. Major branches commonly accepted include the Madang languages, the Finisterre–Huon languages, the Kainantu–Goroka languages, the Chimbu–Wahgi languages, the Engan languages, and the Dani and Mek groupings, among others. The exact membership of peripheral groups — such as the languages of the south-eastern peninsula and some western groups — remains debated, and specialists caution that Trans–New Guinea is best supported for its central highlands core.

Speakers and largest languages

Most TNG languages have small speaker communities, often a few thousand people. The largest include Enga (about 230,000 speakers), Western Dani, Ekari (Me), Melpa, and Huli, each of which is among the most widely spoken indigenous languages of New Guinea. In Papua New Guinea the lingua franca Tok Pisin increasingly dominates public life, and many smaller TNG languages are endangered.

Typological features

Typical features of the core languages include subject–object–verb word order, clause-chaining constructions with "medial" verbs that carry switch-reference marking (indicating whether the next clause has the same or a different subject), and verb morphology distinguishing subject person and number. Many languages have small consonant inventories but complex systems of medial verb morphology. Sweet potato cultivation, which transformed highlands agriculture and population densities, is argued to be linked to the historical expansion of some TNG groups.

Proto-language

Reconstructions of proto-Trans–New Guinea vocabulary include terms associated with highland agriculture, such as words for taro and other crops, consistent with an origin in the New Guinea highlands several thousand years ago. Pronominal evidence — for example, proto-forms often reconstructed as first person *na and second person *ga — is a central pillar of the classification.
