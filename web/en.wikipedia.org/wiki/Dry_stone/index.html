Dry stone


A dry stone wall built without mortar.

Dry stone construction is a building method in which structures are made from stones fitted together without any mortar to bind them. Stability comes entirely from the careful selection and placement of stones, whose weight and interlocking shapes resist displacement by gravity and friction. It is among the oldest forms of construction in the world.

Technique

A dry stone wall typically has two faces of coursed stone leaning slightly inward (a "batter"), tied together by long through-stones, with the core packed with small rubble ("hearting"). Larger foundation stones sit in a shallow trench; courses rise with joints staggered, and a row of upright coping stones caps the wall, shedding rain. Because no mortar is used, the wall flexes slightly with ground movement and drains freely, which is why well-built examples last for centuries.

History and examples

Prehistoric dry stone structures include the Neolithic village of Skara Brae in Orkney, Scottish brochs and Irish ringforts, the nuraghi of Sardinia, and evocatively fitted Inca walls at Machu Picchu and Sacsayhuamán, where polygonal blocks resist earthquakes. Field walls define the landscapes of the Yorkshire Dales, the Burren, Mallorca and Mediterranean terraces, most built during enclosure movements of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Dry stone bridges, beehive huts, sheepfolds, wells and retaining walls are found wherever suitable stone is plentiful.

Modern practice

Associations such as the Dry Stone Walling Association in Britain and the Dry Stone Conservancy in the United States teach and certify the craft, which has revived for landscaping, habitat value (walls shelter lichens, insects and small mammals) and heritage conservation. In 2018 the art of dry stone walling was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, in a multinational nomination.
