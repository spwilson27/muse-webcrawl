Louis de Buade de Frontenac

Louis de Buade, Comte de Frontenac et de Palluau (22 May 1622 – 28 November 1698) was a French soldier, courtier, and Governor General of New France in North America from 1672 to 1682 and from 1689 to his death in 1698. He established a number of forts on the Great Lakes and the upper Mississippi region, engaged in a series of wars against the Iroquois Confederacy and the English colonies, and defended Quebec against an English invasion in 1690. Frontenac is remembered as one of the dominant figures in the history of French Canada.

Early life

Frontenac was born at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, the son of Henri de Buade, a colonel, and Anne Phélypeaux; King Louis XIII was his godfather. He joined the army as a youth and fought in the Thirty Years' War in the Netherlands and Italy, rising while still young. His marriage in 1648 to the wealthy heiress Anne de La Grange-Trianon, contracted against her family's wishes, brought him position at court but he accumulated heavy debts, and the governorship of New France offered both income and escape from creditors.

First term, 1672–1682

Arriving at Quebec in September 1672, Frontenac convened the inhabitants in three estates to swear allegiance, an act the minister Colbert reprimanded as smacking of republicanism. He promoted exploration and the fur trade, building Fort Frontenac (at present-day Kingston, Ontario) in 1673 and supporting La Salle's expeditions toward the Mississippi. His high-handed manner brought him into constant conflict with the Sovereign Council, the church under Bishop Laval, and the intendant Jacques Duchesneau over jurisdiction and the brandy trade with Indigenous nations. Recalled in 1682, he left the colony ill-prepared for the Iroquois wars that followed.

Second term and the wars, 1689–1698

Reappointed in 1689 amid King William's War, Frontenac ordered frontier raids on English settlements, including the attack on Schenectady in 1690. In October 1690 a New England fleet under Sir William Phips demanded the surrender of Quebec; Frontenac famously replied that he would answer "from the mouths of my cannon and muskets," and the ensuing Battle of Quebec ended in an English rout. His subsequent campaigns against the Iroquois, culminating in the great 1696 expedition against the Onondaga and Oneida, helped force the peace concluded in the Great Peace of Montreal of 1701. Frontenac died at Quebec on 28 November 1698, mourned by the colonists he had championed.

Legacy

Frontenac's name is preserved by the Château Frontenac hotel in Quebec City, numerous places in Canada and the United States, and his reputation as the strong-willed "saviour of Canada" in 1690.
