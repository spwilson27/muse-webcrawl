Agartha

Agartha (also spelled Agartta, Agharti, or Asgartha) is a legendary kingdom said to be located inside a hollow Earth. Closely associated with the hollow Earth theory and with the mythical Tibetan city of Shambhala, Agartha is a recurring subject in esoteric and occult literature. In many accounts, Agartha is a vast subterranean civilisation, sometimes ruled by a "King of the World," connected to the surface by a network of tunnels and occasionally contrasted with a darker counterpart, Shambhala, in some accounts revered as an enlightened realm.

Origins

The legend is usually traced to the French occultist Alexandre Saint-Yves d'Alveydre (1842–1909), who described "Agarttha" in his posthumously published Mission de l'Inde en Europe (1910) as a hidden land, founded in remote antiquity and relocated underground thousands of years ago, governed by a sovereign pontiff and preserving advanced knowledge. Polish explorer Ferdynand Ossendowski popularised the idea in Beasts, Men and Gods (1922), recounting Mongolian lamas' tales of Agharti and its ruler, the King of the World.

In esoteric thought

René Guénon treated Agartha in Le Roi du Monde (1927) as a genuine traditional symbol of a supreme spiritual centre. Related ideas appear in Theosophical writings about subterranean masters and in later New Age literature. Some writers during and after the Second World War speculated—without evidence—about Nazi expeditions searching for Agartha or hollow-Earth entrances; such claims are considered fringe.

In popular culture

Agartha appears in novels, comics, video games, and anime as a hidden underground world. It is frequently paired with or contrasted against Shambhala: in some twentieth-century occult schemes, Agartha represents a force of good while Shambhala represents evil, though other sources reverse or merge the two.
