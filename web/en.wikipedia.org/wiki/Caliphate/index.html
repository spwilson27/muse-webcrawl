A caliphate (Arabic: خِلَافَة, khilāfa) is an Islamic state ruled by a caliph, a political-religious successor to the prophet Muhammad and leader of the entire Muslim community (ummah). The institution emerged in 632 CE after Muhammad's death, when Abu Bakr was chosen as the first caliph.

Major caliphates

The Rashidun Caliphate (632–661) under the "rightly guided" caliphs expanded Islam across Arabia, Persia, and much of the Byzantine world. The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750), ruled from Damascus, became the largest empire in the world to that date, stretching from Iberia to Central Asia. The Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258), centred on Baghdad, presided over the Islamic Golden Age before falling to the Mongols. The Ottoman Caliphate lasted until its abolition by the Turkish Grand National Assembly in 1924.

Rival and regional caliphates

At various times rival caliphates coexisted, including the Fatimid Caliphate in Egypt and the Umayyad Caliphate of Córdoba in al-Andalus, reflecting Sunni–Shia divisions over legitimate succession.

Modern period

After 1924, periodic calls for the caliphate's restoration were made by Islamist movements. In 2014 the militant group Islamic State declared a "caliphate" under Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi, a claim rejected by the overwhelming majority of Muslim scholars and states; its territorial holdings were destroyed by 2019.
