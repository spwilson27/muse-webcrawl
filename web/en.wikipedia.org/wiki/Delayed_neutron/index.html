Delayed neutron

[Image: Fission fragment decay emitting a delayed neutron]
Certain fission fragments beta-decay to states that immediately emit a neutron

In nuclear engineering, a delayed neutron is a neutron emitted not directly by a nuclear fission event but by the radioactive decay of one of the fission products a fraction of a second to minutes later. Certain neutron-rich fission fragments, called delayed neutron precursors (such as bromine-87 and iodine-137), first undergo beta decay to excited states of their daughter nuclei, which then instantly emit a neutron. Although delayed neutrons constitute only about 0.65% of all fission neutrons in uranium-235, they are fundamental to the control of nuclear reactors.

Role in reactor control

Prompt neutrons appear within about 10⁻¹⁴ seconds of fission, and the prompt generation time in a thermal reactor is only around 10⁻⁴ seconds — far too fast for mechanical control systems. The delayed neutrons, emitted with mean delays of roughly 13 seconds across six precursor groups (half-lives from about 0.2 to 56 seconds), stretch the reactor's response time so that, provided reactivity stays below the delayed neutron fraction β, the power rises slowly enough for control rods and operators to manage. If reactivity reaches or exceeds β, the reactor is "prompt critical" and power can surge on the prompt timescale alone; this regime played a role in accidents such as Chernobyl. Reactivity is conventionally measured in "dollars" and "cents" — fractions of β.
