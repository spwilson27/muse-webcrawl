Manumission

Manumission, or enfranchisement, is the act of freeing slaves by their owners. Different approaches to manumission were adopted by slave-owning societies throughout history, varying in legal formality, frequency, and the rights granted to the freed person.

Ancient Greece and Rome

In ancient Greece, manumission was often a religious act: the slave was symbolically "sold" to a god, as recorded in inscriptions at Delphi. In Rome, manumission (manumissio) could be performed formally before a magistrate (vindicta), by will (testamento), or by enrollment in the census. Formal Roman manumission granted citizenship, making Rome unusually generous compared with Greek practice; freedmen (liberti) could accumulate wealth and their freeborn descendants could rise to high office.

Islamic and medieval practice

Under Islamic law, freeing a slave was regarded as a meritorious act of charity and expiation; the mukataba contract allowed a slave to purchase freedom by instalments. In medieval Europe manumission often occurred through church ceremonies or charters, though serfdom largely replaced chattel slavery.

The Americas

In the Atlantic slave systems, manumission rates varied widely: comparatively frequent in Brazil and Spanish America — where coartación allowed self-purchase — and rare and increasingly restricted in the British colonies and the United States. Freed people of color formed significant communities in cities such as New Orleans, Havana, and Salvador.

Motives

Owners manumitted slaves for reasons including religious duty, reward for loyal service, affection or kinship (including the owner's own children), economic calculation, and testamentary generosity. Critics noted that manumission could also serve as an incentive mechanism sustaining the slave system itself.
