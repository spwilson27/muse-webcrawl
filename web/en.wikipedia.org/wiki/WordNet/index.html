WordNet is a lexical database of semantic relations between words, originally developed at Princeton University under the direction of psychologist George Armitage Miller starting in 1985. It links words into semantic relations including synonyms, hyponyms, and meronyms. The synonyms are grouped into unordered sets (synsets) with short definitions and recorded example uses. WordNet can thus be seen as a combination and extension of a dictionary and thesaurus.

Structure

The basic unit of WordNet is the synset, a set of synonyms expressing a single concept, each accompanied by a gloss. Synsets are connected by conceptual-semantic and lexical relations. Nouns are organised in hypernym/hyponym hierarchies (e.g., dog is a hyponym of canine, a hypernym of pug), verbs in troponymy (manner) hierarchies, and adjectives by antonymy and similarity. Other relations include meronymy (part–whole) and entailment. Version 3.0 contains about 117,000 synsets for roughly 155,000 distinct word forms across nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs.

History

The project began in 1985 as an attempt to build a lexical reference system modelled on theories of human semantic memory rather than alphabetical dictionaries. It was distributed freely and became a standard resource in computational linguistics and natural language processing from the 1990s onward. WordNets have since been built for dozens of other languages, often linked to the Princeton original through the Global WordNet Association; the collaborative Open Multilingual Wordnet aggregates many of them.

Uses

WordNet has been used for word-sense disambiguation, information retrieval, text classification, machine translation, semantic similarity measures, and as a knowledge base in the construction of resources such as BabelNet and in linking to ontologies like SUMO. It is bundled with toolkits including NLTK and is accessible through library interfaces in many programming languages.

Limitations

WordNet's sense granularity is very fine, which can hinder annotation agreement; it lacks coverage of many proper names, technical terms, and multiword expressions, and its verb and adjective structures are shallower than its noun hierarchy. Its last major Princeton release was version 3.0 (2006), with 3.1 providing a database update; maintenance has largely been continued by community projects such as the Open English WordNet.
