Many-worlds interpretation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Many-worlds interpretation
Proposed1957, Hugh Everett III
Key ideaUniversal wave function never collapses
MechanismBranching via decoherence
[Image: Illustration of a branching tree of universes, splitting at each quantum event]
The universal wave function branching into many worlds.

The many-worlds interpretation (MWI) is an interpretation of quantum mechanics asserting that the universal Wave function is objectively real and that there is no wave function collapse. This implies that all possible outcomes of quantum measurements are physically realised in some 'world' or universe, in contrast to the Copenhagen interpretation, in which the range of possible outcomes is taken to collapse into one on measurement.

Proposed by Princeton graduate student Hugh Everett III in his 1957 doctoral thesis ('relative state' formulation) and popularised by Bryce DeWitt in the 1970s — who coined the name — the interpretation gained ground with the theory of decoherence, which explains why branches do not interfere. Its main open problem is recovering the Born-rule probabilities from a deterministic branching structure.

The idea

In MWI the Schrödinger equation applies to everything, including observers. A measurement entangles the apparatus and observer with the system; decoherence splits the joint state into effectively autonomous branches, in each of which the observer sees one definite outcome. Nothing is random in the global dynamics — probability enters only as the self-locating uncertainty of an observer about which branch she will find herself in. There is no single 'splitting moment'; branching is continuous and approximate, defined by decoherence.

Support and criticism

Supporters (DeWitt, David Deutsch, Sean Carroll and others) value MWI's economy: it adds no collapse mechanism and takes the mathematics at face value, and it underpins Deutsch's vision of quantum computation as computation across branches. Critics object that it does not by itself yield the Born rule, that the ontology of infinitely many unobservable worlds is profligate, and that 'branch counting' can mislead. Polls at quantum-foundations conferences typically find MWI second to Copenhagen-style views.
