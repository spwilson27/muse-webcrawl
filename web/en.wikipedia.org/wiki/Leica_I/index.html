The Leica I was the first commercially produced Leica camera, introduced by Ernst Leitz of Wetzlar, Germany, in 1925. Based on Oskar Barnack's pre-war prototypes — the "Ur-Leica" of 1913/14 — it was the first mass-marketed camera to use standard 35mm cine film in the 24 × 36 mm format, and it effectively launched the modern era of small, candid photography.

Design

Barnack, an engineer at Leitz suffering from asthma, designed a pocketable camera that turned motion-picture film sideways through the gate, doubling the frame size to 24 × 36 mm. The Leica I (retrospectively called the Model A) was a fixed-lens camera fitted with a collapsible Leitz Elmar 50mm f/3.5, a cloth focal-plane shutter with speeds from 1/20 to 1/500 second, and a simple optical viewfinder. Focusing was by a scale on the lens mount; there was no coupled rangefinder — that arrived with the Leica II of 1932. A later variant, the Model C, introduced interchangeable lenses in a standardised screw mount, laying the foundation of the Leica system.

Significance

The Leica I's compactness and quiet operation revolutionised photojournalism and documentary photography, enabling the unobtrusive "available light" style associated with photographers such as Henri Cartier-Bresson. Priced as a precision instrument, it sold in the tens of thousands and compelled competing manufacturers to develop their own 35mm systems. Original examples, particularly early prototypes, are among the most valuable cameras ever sold at auction.
