Icon
From Greek: eikōn, 'image'
Tradition: Eastern Orthodox, Eastern Catholic
Typical media: egg tempera on wood, gold leaf

An icon (from Greek εἰκών, eikōn, 'image, likeness') is a religious work of art, most commonly a painting, in the cultures of the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox and certain Eastern Catholic churches. Icons typically depict Jesus Christ, Mary the Theotokos, saints, angels or biblical scenes, and are not merely decoration: they are venerated as windows to the divine prototype they represent.

Theology and history

Theologians such as John of Damascus defended images during the Byzantine Iconoclasm controversies (726–843), arguing that honour shown the image passes to the prototype, and that the Incarnation made depicting God the Son legitimate. The Seventh Ecumenical Council (Nicaea II, 787) affirmed veneration while distinguishing it from worship due to God alone; the final restoration in 843 is celebrated as the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

Style and production

Icons use inverse perspective, gold backgrounds signifying divine light, and canonical forms transmitted through pattern books rather than individual invention. They are usually painted in egg tempera on gessoed wood panels, sometimes covered with metal rizas. Famous examples include the Sinai Christ Pantocrator (6th–7th century), the Vladimirskaya, and the works of Andrei Rublev, whose Trinity is a pinnacle of Russian icon painting.

Modern uses of the word

By extension, 'icon' now also means any widely recognised symbol, a cultural figure, and the small pictograms of computer interfaces — graphical icons pioneered at Xerox PARC and popularised by the Macintosh.
