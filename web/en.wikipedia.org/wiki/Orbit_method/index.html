Coadjoint orbits foliate the dual of a Lie algebra and carry natural symplectic structures.

The orbit method (also known as the Kirillov orbit method) in representation theory, introduced by Alexandre Kirillov in 1962, proposes a correspondence between the irreducible unitary representations of a Lie group and the orbits of its coadjoint action on the dual space of its Lie algebra. The coadjoint orbits are symplectic manifolds, and the method interprets representation-theoretic constructions in terms of their geometry, in analogy with quantization in physics.

Overview

For a Lie group G with Lie algebra g, the group acts on the dual space g* by the coadjoint representation. Each orbit of this action carries a canonical symplectic form, the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau form. The orbit method asserts, roughly, that integrality conditions on an orbit allow one to "quantize" it into an irreducible unitary representation of G, and that all such representations arise this way.

History and scope

The method gives complete and precise results for nilpotent Lie groups, where Kirillov proved a bijection between coadjoint orbits and irreducible unitary representations, and for compact groups, where it relates to the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem and highest-weight theory. For general semisimple and solvable groups, the picture is more subtle and required refinements by many authors, including work on polarizations, orbit integrality, and the character formula — Kirillov's character formula expresses the character of a representation in terms of Fourier transforms of orbital measures. Auslander and Kostant extended the theory to solvable groups.

Significance

Beyond representation theory, the orbit method has influenced symplectic geometry, the theory of integrable systems, and mathematical physics, where coadjoint orbits model classical phase spaces of particles with internal symmetries.
