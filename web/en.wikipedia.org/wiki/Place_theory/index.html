Place theory


The basilar membrane encodes frequency by place

Place theory is a theory of hearing that states that our perception of sound depends on where each component frequency produces vibrations along the basilar membrane of the cochlea. According to this theory, the pitch of a sound is determined by the locations of the hair cells that vibrate the most — the tonotopic organization of the inner ear.

Mechanism

The basilar membrane varies in width and stiffness along its length: it is narrow and stiff at the base, near the oval window, and wide and flexible at the apex. High-frequency sounds produce maximal vibration near the base, while low-frequency sounds peak near the apex. Georg von Békésy's experiments in the mid-20th century demonstrated this traveling-wave pattern and earned him the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1961.

Relation to frequency theory

Place theory explains pitch perception well for frequencies above about 1,000 Hz, while frequency theory — in which pitch is encoded by the firing rate of auditory neurons — better accounts for low frequencies. Modern accounts combine both: the volley principle extends frequency coding to a few thousand hertz, and place coding dominates at high frequencies.
