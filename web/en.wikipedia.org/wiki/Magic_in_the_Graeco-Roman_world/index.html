Magic in the Graeco-Roman world

Magic in the Graeco-Roman world — practices classed as mageia or magia — encompassed a wide range of rituals intended to compel or persuade supernatural powers: curse tablets (defixiones), love spells, amulets, divination and healing incantations, practised across the Mediterranean from archaic Greece through the Roman Empire and late antiquity.

Curse tablets and spells

Thousands of lead curse tablets survive, inscribed with bindings against rivals in law, trade, sport and love, often deposited in graves or wells. The Greek Magical Papyri from Egypt preserve elaborate handbooks of spells invoking gods, angels and daimones in multiple languages.

Magic and religion

The boundary between magic and religion was drawn differently in antiquity than later: magic was often characterised less by its techniques than by its private, coercive or socially suspect character, and Roman law periodically penalised harmful sorcery. Figures such as Medea, Circe and the witches of Thessaly shaped the literary image of the magician.
