Electronic monitoring

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electronic monitoring is the use of technology to track the location or behaviour of persons subject to criminal-justice supervision — principally radio-frequency or GPS ankle monitors worn by defendants on bail, offenders on probation, parole or home detention, and (in some systems) persons under immigration control or civil orders. Introduced experimentally in the United States in 1983 (Judge Jack Love's Albuquerque programme, inspired by a Spider-Man comic strip's tracking device, in the oft-told origin story) and scaled up from the 1990s, it now supervises hundreds of thousands of people daily in the US alone, embedded in the alternatives-to-incarceration architecture.

Studies find monitoring reduces absconding and some reoffending while worn, but its net-widening tendency (imposing surveillance on people who would otherwise be free outright), its fees (offenders are often charged daily monitoring costs), technical-violation revocations, and the privacy and stigma burdens of visible devices draw sustained academic and advocacy criticism. France's placement sous surveillance électronique (1997) and England's tagging regime (curfew orders from 1991) are the standard European models; algorithmic risk assessment is increasingly layered over the hardware, extending debates the technology began.
