The Great Famine of 1315–1317 (sometimes dated 1315–1322) was the first of a series of large-scale crises that struck Europe in the early 14th century. Most of Europe was affected, from the Pyrenees to Russia and from Scotland to Italy. Unusually heavy rains beginning in the spring of 1315 caused successive harvest failures, and food shortages continued until 1317, with full recovery not achieved until around 1322.

Causes

The famine occurred as the Medieval Warm Period gave way to the colder, more unstable climate of the Little Ice Age. Torrential rain through 1315 and 1316 made it impossible to plough fields or cure hay, and grain stores were ruined by damp. A murrain of livestock followed in 1319–1320, killing a large share of cattle and sheep.

Effects

Grain prices soared, in some places to five times their normal level. Millions died of starvation and disease; cities such as Ypres and Bruges lost around five to ten percent of their populations. Crime rose sharply, and there were widespread reports of infanticide and even cannibalism. The famine undermined faith in institutions and is considered a precursor to the crises of the later 14th century, including the Black Death.
