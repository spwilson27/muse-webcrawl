Hot desking

Hot desking (sometimes "non-reservation-based office") is an office organisation system in which multiple workers use a single physical workstation or desk during different time periods. Instead of each employee having an assigned desk, workers sit wherever a suitable desk is available when they arrive, or reserve one in advance through a booking system.

Origins

The term derives from the naval practice of "hot bunking", in which sailors on different shifts share the same bunk. Hot desking became common in the 1990s as employers sought to reduce real-estate costs: because a significant proportion of desks are empty on any given day (through holidays, sickness, travel and meetings), sharing desks allows an organisation to maintain fewer workstations than staff.

Operation

Hot-desking offices typically provide lockers for personal belongings, "clean desk" policies, shared IT equipment and telephone systems that let employees log in at any desk. Variants include hoteling, in which desks must be reserved beforehand, and activity-based working, where different spaces are provided for different tasks.

Criticism

Critics argue that hot desking can reduce employees' sense of belonging, make colleagues harder to find, waste time at the start of the day setting up, and raise concerns about hygiene and ergonomics. Supporters counter that it encourages collaboration across teams and reflects the realities of hybrid working.
