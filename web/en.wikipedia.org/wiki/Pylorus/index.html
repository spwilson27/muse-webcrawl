Pylorus

The pylorus (from Greek πυλωρός, "gatekeeper") is the region of the stomach that connects to the duodenum. It is divided into two parts: the pyloric antrum, which connects to the body of the stomach, and the pyloric canal, which connects to the duodenum. The pyloric sphincter, a band of smooth muscle at the end of the pyloric canal, controls the passage of partially digested food (chyme) from the stomach into the duodenum.

Structure

The pylorus is the furthest part of the stomach that joins the small intestine. The pyloric canal is about 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5 cm) long. The pyloric sphincter is a muscular ring formed by a thickening of the circular layer of the stomach's muscular wall; it relaxes periodically to allow small amounts of chyme to pass into the duodenum and otherwise remains tonically contracted to prevent backflow and regulate gastric emptying.

Function

The pylorus regulates the release of chyme from the stomach into the duodenum at a rate suitable for digestion and absorption in the small intestine. Gastric emptying is influenced by the volume and composition of the stomach contents, by hormones such as gastrin and cholecystokinin, and by signals from the enteric nervous system. Acid and fat entering the duodenum trigger feedback mechanisms that slow emptying.

Clinical significance

In infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, the muscle of the pyloric sphincter becomes abnormally thickened, obstructing gastric outflow. The condition typically presents in infants a few weeks old with projectile vomiting and is treated surgically by pyloromyotomy. In adults, pyloric obstruction can result from scarring due to peptic ulcer disease or from malignancy.
