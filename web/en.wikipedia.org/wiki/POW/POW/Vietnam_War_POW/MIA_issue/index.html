Vietnam War POW/MIA issue

The Vietnam War POW/MIA issue concerns the fate of United States servicemen who were unaccounted for after the Vietnam War. Following the return of American prisoners in 1973 (see Operation Homecoming), questions persisted about whether some personnel remained alive in captivity in Vietnam, Laos, or Cambodia, and about the recovery of remains.

Background

During the war, more than 2,500 Americans were listed as prisoners or missing. The Paris Peace Accords of 1973 required the return of prisoners, and 591 Americans came home that spring. Families' groups, notably the National League of Families, pressed the government for a fuller accounting, giving rise to the POW/MIA flag as a symbol of the cause.

Investigations

Multiple congressional inquiries, culminating in the Senate Select Committee on POW/MIA Affairs (1991–1993) chaired by John Kerry with John McCain as a prominent member, concluded there was no compelling evidence that American prisoners remained alive in captivity, while acknowledging that some men could have survived their initial loss incidents. Live-sighting reports were investigated for years by the Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency and its predecessors.

Legacy

Accounting efforts continue: joint field activities recover and identify remains, and as of the 2020s roughly 1,570 Americans remained unaccounted for from the war. The issue profoundly influenced American popular culture, including films portraying rescue missions, and it shaped U.S.–Vietnam normalization in the 1990s.
