The POW/MIA flag is an American flag designed to honor United States military personnel who were prisoners of war (POW) or missing in action (MIA), particularly those unaccounted for from the Vietnam War. It depicts, in black and white, the silhouette of a man's head bowed before a prison guard tower and a strand of barbed wire, above the words "POW ★ MIA" and the motto "You Are Not Forgotten."

History

In 1971, Mary Hoff, the wife of a missing naval aviator, proposed a flag as a symbol for the missing; the National League of Families commissioned the flag company Annin & Co., whose designer Newt Heisley — himself the father of a Marine serving in Vietnam — created the design in 1972. The League adopted the flag, and it spread widely as public concern over the POW/MIA issue grew.

Display

The POW/MIA flag is the only flag besides the flag of the United States to have flown over the White House, and in 1988 it was displayed in the United States Capitol rotunda. Federal law requires it to be flown at the Capitol, the White House, national cemeteries, and other federal sites on specified days, including National POW/MIA Recognition Day, and since 2019 it is required to be displayed whenever the U.S. flag is displayed at those locations. It remains one of the most widely flown flags in the United States.
