During the Vietnam War, hundreds of United States servicemen were held as prisoners of war (POWs) by North Vietnam and the Viet Cong. Most were aviators shot down over North Vietnam beginning in 1964; the first, Navy pilot Everett Alvarez Jr., was captured on 5 August 1964 and remained a prisoner for more than eight years, one of the longest-held POWs in American history.

The prisoners were held in camps in and around Hanoi, including Hỏa Lò Prison — nicknamed the "Hanoi Hilton" — Hoa Lo's annexes, and camps known by nicknames such as Alcatraz, Briarpatch, and the Zoo. Conditions included isolation, inadequate food and medical care, and, especially before 1969, systematic torture intended to extract propaganda statements. The POWs organized under senior officers such as Jeremiah Denton and John McCain, communicated by tap code, and maintained a chain of command despite punishment.

Release

Under the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973, North Vietnam released 591 American POWs during Operation Homecoming in February–April 1973. The fate of personnel still listed as missing in action became a lasting political issue in the United States, symbolized by the POW/MIA flag promoted by the National League of Families.
