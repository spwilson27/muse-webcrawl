Vietnam War

The Vietnam War (1955–1975) was a conflict in Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia between North Vietnam, supported by the Soviet Union and China, and South Vietnam, supported principally by the United States. It was the second of the Indochina Wars and a major episode of the Cold War. The war ended with the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975 and the reunification of Vietnam under communist rule.

Background

After the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu in 1954, the Geneva Accords temporarily partitioned Vietnam at the 17th parallel. Ngo Dinh Diem consolidated power in the South while Ho Chi Minh governed the North. The planned 1956 reunification elections were never held, and insurgency by the Viet Cong, backed by Hanoi, escalated through the late 1950s and early 1960s.

American escalation

After the Gulf of Tonkin incident in 1964, the United States massively expanded its role, peaking at more than 536,000 troops in 1969. The Tet Offensive of 1968, though a military defeat for the communists, eroded American public support. President Nixon's "Vietnamization" policy withdrew U.S. ground forces while expanding bombing, and the Paris Peace Accords of January 1973 set the stage for complete U.S. withdrawal; American prisoners returned in Operation Homecoming.

Aftermath

North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon in April 1975. The war killed an estimated one to three million Vietnamese, over 58,000 Americans, and vast numbers in Cambodia and Laos. Its legacy includes the War Powers Resolution, the end of the U.S. draft, and a lasting debate over American foreign intervention; the fate of missing personnel became the long-running POW/MIA issue.
