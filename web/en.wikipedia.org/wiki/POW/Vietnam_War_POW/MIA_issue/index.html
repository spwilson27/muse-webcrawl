Vietnam War POW/MIA issue

The Vietnam War POW/MIA issue concerns the fate of United States servicemen who were unaccounted for after the Vietnam War. At the end of the war in 1973, Operation Homecoming returned 591 American prisoners of war, while roughly 2,500 personnel remained listed as prisoners or missing. The question of whether any Americans were left behind alive became one of the most emotive legacies of the war.

History

Family organisations, above all the National League of Families, campaigned for full accounting from the early 1970s. In the 1980s the issue surged in public attention, amplified by films and private rescue ventures, and allegations that prisoners had been deliberately abandoned. The Senate Select Committee on POW/MIA Affairs (1991–93), chaired by John Kerry with Bob Smith as vice-chair, concluded there was no compelling evidence that any American remained alive in captivity, while acknowledging that some men known to have been captured were not returned and could not be fully accounted for.

Accounting efforts

Joint US–Vietnamese field teams have excavated crash and burial sites since the 1980s; hundreds of sets of remains have been repatriated and identified, and the number still unaccounted for has fallen to roughly 1,550. The work continues under the Defense POW/MIA Accounting Agency. Mainstream historians and official investigations have found no proof of living prisoners after 1973, though the issue retains deep cultural resonance.
