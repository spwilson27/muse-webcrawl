Vietnam War

The Vietnam War (1955–1975) pitted North Vietnam and its Viet Cong allies against South Vietnam and the United States. For American servicemen taken prisoner, the war raised issues of survival, resistance, and repatriation that continued long after the fighting stopped, most visibly in the ongoing POW/MIA issue.

Prisoners of war

Most American POWs in the North were airmen shot down during the bombing campaigns. They were held in camps around Hanoi, including Hoa Lo Prison (the "Hanoi Hilton"), and subjected to interrogation and torture aimed at extracting propaganda statements. Prisoners organized resistance under senior officers such as Jeremiah Denton and James Stockdale, communicating by tap code. In the South, POWs were held in jungle camps under harsh conditions; some were held by the Viet Cong for years, and others were held in Laos and Cambodia.

Repatriation

The Paris Peace Accords of January 1973 led to Operation Homecoming, in which 591 prisoners were returned between February and April 1973. Organizations of families, above all the National League of Families, had campaigned for years to keep the prisoners' plight before the public, adopting the POW/MIA flag as their emblem.

End of the war

With American forces withdrawn, North Vietnam's 1975 spring offensive captured Saigon on 30 April 1975, ending the war and reunifying the country. The accounting for missing Americans continued for decades afterward through joint recovery operations.
