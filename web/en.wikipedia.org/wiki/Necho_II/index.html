Necho II

Necho II (sometimes Nekau, Neku, Nechoh, or Nikuu; Greek: Νεκώς) was a pharaoh of the Twenty-sixth Dynasty of Egypt, reigning from 610 to 595 BC. Son of Psamtik I, he pursued an ambitious foreign policy in the Levant against the rising power of Babylon, launched major naval and canal projects, and — according to Herodotus — commissioned a Phoenician expedition that circumnavigated Africa. He is a figure in the Hebrew Bible, where he is remembered for killing King Josiah of Judah at Megiddo.

War in the Levant

As Assyria collapsed, Necho marched north in 609 BC to aid the last Assyrian forces against Babylon. On the way, King Josiah of Judah attempted to block him at Megiddo and was killed (2 Kings 23). Necho deposed Josiah's successor Jehoahaz, deported him to Egypt, and installed Jehoiakim as a tributary king. In 605 BC, however, the Egyptian army was decisively defeated by the Babylonian crown prince Nebuchadnezzar II at the Battle of Carchemish; Egypt lost its Levantine possessions, and a Babylonian invasion of Egypt itself in 601 BC was repelled with heavy losses on both sides.

Naval and canal projects

Herodotus reports that Necho built warships on both the Mediterranean and the Red Sea coasts and began a canal joining the Nile to the Red Sea — a precursor of the Suez Canal — abandoning it after an oracle warned that a foreigner would benefit; the canal was later completed by Darius I of Persia. Necho's most famous project is the circumnavigation of Africa: Herodotus (Histories 4.42) relates that Phoenician sailors in Necho's service sailed from the Red Sea around Africa and returned through the Pillars of Heracles in the third year, a voyage many historians regard as plausible, partly because the sailors reportedly claimed the sun was on their right (to the north) as they rounded the continent — consistent with crossing the equator into the Southern Hemisphere.

Legacy

Necho died in 595 BC and was succeeded by his son Psamtik II. His reign marked the high point of Saite expansion abroad, even though the Babylonian defeat at Carchemish ended Egypt's ambitions in Asia.
