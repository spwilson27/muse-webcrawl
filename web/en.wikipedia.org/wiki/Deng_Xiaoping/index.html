Deng Xiaoping

Deng Xiaoping (22 August 1904 – 19 February 1997) was a Chinese revolutionary and statesman who was the paramount leader of the People's Republic of China from 1978 until his retirement in 1989. Never holding the formal offices of head of state or head of government — and after 1989 holding no office but the honorary presidency of the China Bridge Association — Deng ruled through his seniority in the Chinese Communist Party and the Central Military Commission, which he chaired until 1989. He is remembered as the architect of "reform and opening up": the dismantling of the Maoist command economy in favour of markets, foreign investment, special economic zones and agricultural decollectivisation, under the formula of "socialism with Chinese characteristics" — reforms that launched four decades of rapid growth and lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty, transforming China into an economic superpower. His political legacy is equally marked by the insistence on the Party's monopoly of power: the suppression of the Democracy Wall movement, the "Four Cardinal Principles", and the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown, in which troops killed hundreds and perhaps thousands of pro-democracy protesters — the decision that defines his reputation in the West while Chinese official memory honours him as the helmsman of modernisation, second in the Party's pantheon only to Mao Zedong himself, whose "core" status Deng's doctrine formally preserved.

Early life and revolutionary career

Deng was born Xiaoping in Guang'an, Sichuan, to a landlord family, and at sixteen joined the work-study programme that took a generation of future Chinese leaders to France, where he worked in a Renault factory, joined the Communist movement, and met Zhou Enlai. After study in Moscow he returned to China in 1927 and rose as a political commissar in the Red Army, surviving the Long March (1934–35) and serving with distinction in the war against Japan and the civil war as political commissar of Liu Bocheng's Second Field Army — the team that took Nanjing in 1949. In the new People's Republic he governed the south-west, then moved to Beijing as a vice-premier and, from 1956, general secretary of the Party secretariat — one of Mao's closest lieutenants, and, with Liu Shaoqi, manager of the recovery from the Great Leap Forward, for which Mao's radicals marked them as "capitalist roaders".

Fall, return, and the road to power

In the Cultural Revolution (from 1966) Deng was purged as the "number two capitalist roader", paraded and sent to labour in a tractor factory in Jiangxi, while his son Deng Pufang was thrown from a window by Red Guards and left paraplegic. Zhou Enlai engineered Deng's first rehabilitation in 1973 to run the government; after Zhou's death in January 1976 the Gang of Four struck Deng down again in the April Fifth incident. Mao's death that September, the arrest of the Gang of Four, and Deng's second rehabilitation in 1977 set up his quiet outmanoeuvring of Mao's successor Hua Guofeng: at the Third Plenum of December 1978 Deng's line — practice as the sole criterion of truth, modernisation above class struggle — won, and by 1981 he had replaced Hua's men with his reformers (Hu Yaobang as party chairman, Zhao Ziyang as premier) and secured a party verdict on Mao that preserved the founder's legitimacy ("70 per cent achievements, 30 per cent mistakes") while discarding his economics.

Reform and opening

The reforms began in the countryside, where the household responsibility system — families farming leased land and selling surpluses — spread from below and was ratified from above, dissolving the communes by 1984 and doubling peasant incomes in six years. In 1980 four Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, Xiamen) opened the coast to foreign capital and export manufacturing; Shenzhen's growth from border town to metropolis became the era's emblem. Deng normalised relations with the United States (1979, visiting America to famous effect in a cowboy hat), briefly fought a border war with Vietnam (1979), negotiated Hong Kong's return with Margaret Thatcher under his formula of "one country, two systems" (Joint Declaration 1984), disciplined the army toward professional modernisation, imposed the one-child policy (through others' hands), and preached pragmatism in phrases that became folklore: "It doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice"; "crossing the river by feeling the stones"; "to get rich is glorious" (a sentiment attributed rather than documented). Politically, however, reform stopped at the Party's door.

Tiananmen and last years

Inflation and corruption fed the protest movement of spring 1989, triggered by the death of the reformist Hu Yaobang; students occupied Tiananmen Square, and after martial law was declared and initial troop movements were blocked by citizens, Deng — by then retired from every post but the Military Commission chairmanship — backed the hard line. On the night of 3–4 June 1989 the People's Liberation Army fought its way to the square, killing hundreds, by some estimates more than a thousand, of protesters and bystanders. Zhao Ziyang was purged and held under house arrest for life; Jiang Zemin was installed as Deng's successor. Deng formally retired in November 1989 — but in early 1992, aged 87 and fearing reform was stalling in the post-Tiananmen freeze, he made his famous Southern Tour of Shenzhen and Shanghai, demanding faster market reform ("development is the hard truth"), and thereby restarted the boom on his own authority as an ordinary party member. He died in February 1997, five months before the Hong Kong handover he had negotiated; his ashes were scattered at sea, as he had wished.

Legacy

Deng's China — marketised, urbanised, integrated into the world economy, yet ruled by the unreformed Party he refused to democratise — is the central fact of the modern world economy and of twenty-first century geopolitics. Within China his theory is enshrined in the party constitution alongside Mao Zedong Thought; abroad he is honoured as the pragmatic moderniser and condemned for Tiananmen, usually by the same writers in the same paragraph. His chosen style — power without office, decision without debate, experimentation without liberalisation — proved as durable as his economics.
