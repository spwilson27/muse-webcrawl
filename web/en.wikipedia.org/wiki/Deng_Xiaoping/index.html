Deng Xiaoping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Deng Xiaoping
Born: 22 August 1904, Guang'an, Sichuan
Died: 19 February 1997, Beijing
Role: China's paramount leader (1978–1989); architect of reform and opening

Deng Xiaoping (1904–1997) was a Chinese revolutionary and statesman who, as the country's paramount leader from 1978, launched the reform and opening policies that transformed China from a poor, isolated planned economy into the world's second-largest economy — the founding act of the modern economy of China.

A veteran of the Long March and the civil war, Deng was purged twice during the Cultural Revolution (his son Deng Pufang was paralysed by Red Guards). After Mao's death he outmanoeuvred Hua Guofeng and, from December 1978, began decollectivising agriculture (the household responsibility system), creating special economic zones such as Shenzhen, welcoming foreign investment and preaching pragmatism — "it doesn't matter whether a cat is black or white, as long as it catches mice" and "to get rich is glorious" are the phrases attached to him.

His legacy is double: the economic liberation that lifted hundreds of millions from poverty and built modern China, and the political closure sealed by the suppression of the Tiananmen Square protests in 1989, after which he revived reform with his 1992 Southern Tour and then retired from public life. He negotiated Hong Kong's return under "one country, two systems" (1997, months after his death) and is officially honoured as the chief architect of China's socialist modernisation.
