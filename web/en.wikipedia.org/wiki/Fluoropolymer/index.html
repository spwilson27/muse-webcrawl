Fluoropolymer

A fluoropolymer is a fluorocarbon-based polymer with multiple carbon–fluorine bonds. The exceptionally strong C–F bond gives fluoropolymers high resistance to heat, chemicals, solvents and weathering, along with low friction and excellent electrical insulation. The best-known example is polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), sold by Chemours under the brand name Teflon, discovered accidentally in 1938 by Roy J. Plunkett of DuPont.

PTFE thread-seal tape, a familiar fluoropolymer product.

Types

  • PTFE — polytetrafluoroethylene: non-stick coatings, gaskets, seals, wire insulation
  • FEP and PFA — melt-processable copolymers used for tubing and linings
  • ETFE — ethylene tetrafluoroethylene: architectural films, e.g. the Eden Project domes
  • PVDF — polyvinylidene fluoride: piping, piezoelectric films, battery binders
  • PVF — polyvinyl fluoride (Tedlar): protective films
  • Fluoroelastomers (e.g. Viton): high-performance O-rings and seals

Properties and uses

Fluoropolymers withstand temperatures from cryogenic levels to over 250 °C (for PTFE), are attacked by almost no chemicals, and have very low coefficients of friction. Applications span chemical processing plant, cookware, semiconductors, aerospace wiring, medical devices and outdoor architecture. Their drawbacks include high cost, difficult processing, creep under load, and — because many are extremely persistent in the environment — membership of the wider PFAS family of chemicals, whose production and use face growing regulatory scrutiny, including controversies over processing aids such as PFOA.
