The National Convention (French: Convention nationale) was the revolutionary government of France from September 1792 to October 1795, during the most radical phase of the French Revolution. Elected after the insurrection of 10 August 1792 overthrew the monarchy, it proclaimed the First French Republic, tried and executed Louis XVI, and governed through the Reign of Terror before the Thermidorian Reaction curtailed its powers.

Work of the Convention

The Convention's first act was to abolish the monarchy and declare France a republic. It drafted a democratic constitution in 1793, though war and internal revolt meant it was never implemented. To defend the Revolution against foreign invasion and counter-revolution, the Convention concentrated power in executive bodies — above all the Committee of Public Safety, dominated by Maximilien Robespierre and Georges Danton at different times — and authorised the Revolutionary Tribunal and the levée en masse, the first modern mass conscription. Under the Terror of 1793–94, some 17,000 people were officially executed, many by guillotine.

Decline and legacy

The fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) ended the Terror; the Convention then dismantled the revolutionary government, closed the Jacobin Club and drafted the more conservative Constitution of the Year III, which established the Directory in 1795. Despite its violence, the Convention also left lasting reforms: the metric system, the abolition of slavery in the French colonies (1794), the suppression of primogeniture, and early steps toward public education and a unified legal code.
