New World

The New World is one of the names used for the major landmasses of the Western Hemisphere, principally the Americas and nearby island groups. The term originated in the early 16th century, when European explorers and mapmakers recognized that the lands reached across the Atlantic were not Asia but a "new world" previously unknown to Europeans, Africans and Asians — though inhabited for millennia by Indigenous peoples.

Origin of the term

The Florentine explorer Amerigo Vespucci argued in letters published around 1503 that the South American landmass was a Mundus Novus — a new world — not the Indies. The German cartographer Martin Waldseemüller popularized the idea, naming the continent "America" after Vespucci on his 1507 world map. The paired term "Old World" came to denote Europe, Asia and Africa.

Usage

The term survives in contexts such as biology, where "New World monkeys" and "Old World monkeys" distinguish primate lineages, and in wine, where "New World" styles from the Americas, Australia and elsewhere are contrasted with European traditions. Modern historians often prefer "the Americas" or "the Western Hemisphere", noting that the lands were new only from a European perspective.
