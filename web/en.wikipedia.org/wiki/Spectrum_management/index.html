Spectrum management is the process of regulating the use of radio frequencies to promote efficient use and gain a net social benefit. The term commonly refers to the system of procedures used by governments, through national regulatory authorities, to assign radio spectrum to licensees, define the conditions of its use, and prevent harmful interference between users.

History

Unregulated early radio led to chaotic interference, dramatised by the difficulties of wireless communication during the sinking of the Titanic in 1912. The United States responded with the Radio Act of 1912 and, later, the Communications Act of 1934 creating the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). Internationally, the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) coordinates spectrum use through its Radio Regulations and periodic World Radiocommunication Conferences.

Methods of assignment

Historically most spectrum was assigned administratively, by "beauty contests" or first-come-first-served licensing. Since the 1990s, regulators have increasingly used spectrum auctions — pioneered for large-scale commercial use by New Zealand and the United States, and analysed by economists such as Paul Milgrom — to assign mobile telephony licences. Other approaches include licence-exempt ("unlicensed") bands, used by Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, administered spectrum prices, secondary trading, and dynamic spectrum sharing, in which devices use cognitive radio techniques to occupy temporarily idle frequencies.

Issues

Key debates in spectrum management include spectrum scarcity versus artificial scarcity created by legacy allocations, "spectrum farming" and refarming of broadcast bands for mobile broadband (the "digital dividend"), interference protection for passive services such as radio astronomy and weather satellites, and the release of millimetre-wave bands for 5G services.
