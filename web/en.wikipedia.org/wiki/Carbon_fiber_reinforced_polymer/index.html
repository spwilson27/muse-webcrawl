Carbon fiber reinforced polymer

Carbon fiber reinforced polymer (CFRP), also called carbon fiber reinforced plastic or carbon fiber composite, is an extremely strong and light fiber-reinforced plastic that contains carbon fibers embedded in a polymer matrix, most commonly epoxy resin. Despite its high cost, CFRP's exceptional strength-to-weight ratio has made it the material of choice for aerospace structures, high-performance automobiles, racing bicycles, sporting goods and other weight-critical applications.

Structure and properties

The reinforcing phase consists of carbon fibers, typically 5–10 micrometres in diameter and composed mostly of carbon atoms bonded in graphitic crystals aligned along the fiber axis. The fibers are gathered into tows, woven into fabrics or laid as unidirectional tapes, then combined with the matrix, which binds the fibers, transfers load between them and protects them from the environment. The resulting laminate is strongly anisotropic — stiffest and strongest along the fibers — so engineers tailor the stacking sequence to the expected loads. CFRP has a very high strength-to-weight ratio and stiffness-to-weight ratio, excellent fatigue resistance and low thermal expansion, but it is brittle, electrically conductive, and can promote galvanic corrosion of aluminium fasteners.

Manufacturing

Common processes include hand lay-up with vacuum bagging, filament winding for pressure vessels and shafts, pultrusion for constant-section profiles, resin transfer moulding, and — dominant in aerospace — lay-up of pre-impregnated ("prepreg") sheets cured under heat and pressure in an autoclave. Out-of-autoclave prepregs and automated fiber placement have reduced cost and cycle times.

Applications

  • Aerospace: the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 have composite primary structures of roughly 50% CFRP by weight.
  • Automotive: Formula One monocoques (pioneered by the McLaren MP4/1 of 1981) and increasingly road-car components.
  • Sport: bicycle frames, tennis rackets, golf shafts, fishing rods and rowing shells.
  • Civil engineering: strengthening of concrete structures, bridge cables and wind-turbine blades (in part).

Limitations and recycling

CFRP remains expensive because of precursor and energy costs, slow cure cycles and labour. Impact damage can be barely visible yet seriously weaken a part ("barely visible impact damage"), complicating inspection. Thermoset composites are difficult to recycle; pyrolysis and solvolysis can reclaim fibers with some loss of properties, and the growing volume of end-of-life material is an active research problem.
