Nine-dash line

The nine-dash line, also referred to as the nine-dotted line, is a set of line segments on maps of the South China Sea used by the People's Republic of China and the Republic of China (Taiwan) to indicate their territorial claims in the sea. The demarcation line, which evolved from an earlier eleven-dash line, encloses most of the South China Sea and includes the Paracel Islands, the Spratly Islands, Pratas Island and the Macclesfield Bank.

History

The lineage of the dashes traces to maps published under the Republic of China in the 1930s and, most importantly, to an official 1947 map showing an eleven-dash line around the South China Sea islands. After the founding of the People's Republic of China, the line was revised — two dashes in the Gulf of Tonkin were removed, giving the nine-dash version shown on Chinese maps from the 1950s onward. In 2009 China attached a nine-dash-line map to a submission to the United Nations, prompting protests from other claimant states.

Dispute and legal status

China's claims within the line are contested by Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei and Taiwan, and the precise legal meaning of the dashes — whether they claim the islands only, maritime zones generated by them, or "historic rights" to the waters themselves — has long been ambiguous. In 2016, an arbitral tribunal constituted under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea in Philippines v. China concluded that China's claims to historic rights within the nine-dash line were contrary to the Convention and without lawful effect to the extent that they exceeded the maritime zones UNCLOS provides. China declared the award null and void and has not accepted it. The dashes continue to appear on Chinese maps, passports, and official publications, and remain a recurring source of diplomatic friction in Southeast Asia.
