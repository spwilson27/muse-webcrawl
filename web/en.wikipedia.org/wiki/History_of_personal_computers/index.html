History of personal computers

The history of personal computers as mass-market consumer electronic devices began with the microcomputer revolution of the 1970s. A personal computer is one intended for individual use, as opposed to a mainframe or minicomputer shared by many users. The advent of the microprocessor in the early 1970s made such machines affordable.

Precursors

Early machines accessible to individuals included the Kenbak-1 (1971), often considered the first personal computer, and the French Micral (1973), the first commercial microprocessor-based computer. Hobbyist kits such as the Mark-8 appeared in electronics magazines, preparing a community of enthusiasts.

The kit era and the Altair

The MITS Altair 8800, featured on the cover of Popular Electronics in January 1975, is generally credited with igniting the personal computer industry. Programmed initially by toggle switches, it inspired the Homebrew Computer Club, for which Bill Gates and Paul Allen wrote a BASIC interpreter, founding Microsoft. The IMSAI 8080 and other S-100 bus machines followed.

The 1977 trinity

In 1977 three pre-assembled machines reached the market: the Commodore PET, the Apple II and the Tandy/Radio Shack TRS-80 — dubbed the "1977 trinity" by Byte magazine. The Apple II's colour graphics and expansion slots, and the spreadsheet VisiCalc (1979), made microcomputers attractive to small businesses.

The IBM PC and clones

IBM entered the market in August 1981 with the IBM Personal Computer, built from off-the-shelf components and an open architecture. Compaq's portable of 1983 was the first fully compatible clone, and the standardisation around MS-DOS and later Microsoft Windows made the "PC" the dominant platform. Apple's Macintosh (1984) popularised the graphical user interface pioneered at Xerox PARC and on the Apple Lisa.

Home computing and beyond

In the 1980s inexpensive home computers such as the Commodore 64 (the best-selling single computer model of all time), the ZX Spectrum and the Amstrad CPC introduced a generation to computing. Laptops became practical in the 1990s, the World Wide Web drove household adoption, and from the 2000s smartphones and tablets absorbed many roles of the desktop PC, though PCs remain central to work, gaming and content creation.
