Tocopherol

Tocopherols are a class of organic chemical compounds, many of which have vitamin E activity. They are fat-soluble antioxidants that protect cell membranes from oxidation by reacting with free radicals produced during the normal metabolism of fat.

Forms

Four tocopherols are recognized: alpha (α), beta (β), gamma (γ), and delta (δ), distinguished by the number and position of methyl groups on the chromanol ring. Each has a hydrophobic phytyl tail that anchors it in cell membranes. Alpha-tocopherol is the form preferentially retained by the human body and is the form usually meant by "vitamin E" in nutrition, while gamma-tocopherol is the most common form in many seed oils in the North American diet.

Sources and function

Tocopherols occur naturally in vegetable oils, nuts, seeds, and green leafy vegetables. Plants synthesize tocopherols in plastids, where they protect photosynthetic membranes and seed lipids from oxidative damage. In food technology, tocopherol-rich extracts are used as the antioxidant food additive E306, with alpha-, gamma-, and delta-tocopherol designated E307, E308, and E309 respectively.

History

Vitamin E was discovered in 1922 by Herbert Evans and Katharine Bishop, who found that rats fed a purified diet failed to reproduce without a factor present in lettuce and wheat germ. The name tocopherol derives from the Greek tókos (childbirth) and phérō (to bear), with the -ol suffix denoting an alcohol.
