Brouwer fixed-point theorem

[Image: illustration of a fixed point of a continuous map of a disc]
Brouwer fixed-point theorem
FieldAlgebraic topology
Proved byL. E. J. Brouwer (1911)
StatementEvery continuous function from a closed ball to itself has a fixed point

The Brouwer fixed-point theorem is a theorem in topology, named after the Dutch mathematician Luitzen Egbertus Jan Brouwer. It states that for any continuous function f mapping a compact convex set to itself, there is a point x0 such that f(x0) = x0. Such a point is called a fixed point of the function.

The simplest case is a continuous function on a closed interval: the theorem then reduces to a consequence of the intermediate value theorem. For a disc, an intuitive image is that a sheet of paper lying flat on a table, crumpled (without tearing or stretching) and placed back over its original outline, must have at least one point that lies exactly above where it started.

History

Results of this type were explored by Henri Poincaré and Charles Émile Picard in connection with differential equations. Brouwer proved the theorem for continuous maps of the ball in arbitrary finite dimension around 1911, using his newly developed notion of the degree of a mapping. It became one of the founding results of algebraic topology.

Applications

The theorem is a key tool in economics and game theory: John von Neumann used related fixed-point arguments in proving the minimax theorem, and John Nash's proof of the existence of equilibrium in non-cooperative games relies on the Kakutani fixed-point theorem, a generalisation of Brouwer's. Fixed-point theorems also underpin proofs of existence of solutions to differential equations and models of general economic equilibrium.

Generalizations

The Schauder fixed-point theorem extends the result to infinite-dimensional spaces, and the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem counts fixed points using homology.
