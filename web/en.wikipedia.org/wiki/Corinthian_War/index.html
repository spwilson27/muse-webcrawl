Corinthian War

The Corinthian War (395–387 BC) was a conflict in ancient Greece which pitted Sparta against a coalition of four allied states, Thebes, Athens, Corinth and Argos, who were initially backed by the Achaemenid Empire. The war took its name from Corinth, where the majority of the fighting took place.

Causes

The immediate cause of the war was a local conflict in northwest Greece in which Thebes and Sparta intervened. The deeper cause was hostility towards Sparta provoked by its dominance over Greece in the nine years after the end of the Peloponnesian War. The Persian king Artaxerxes II provided funding to the allies to check Spartan ambitions, particularly Sparta's campaign in Asia Minor under King Agesilaus II.

Course of the war

The war was fought on two fronts: on land near Corinth (hence the name) and Thebes, and at sea in the Aegean. On land, the Spartans achieved early successes at the battles of Nemea (394 BC) and Coronea (394 BC), but failed to capitalise on them, and the fighting soon became a stalemate. At sea, the Athenian admiral Conon, commanding a Phoenician fleet funded by Persia, destroyed the Spartan fleet at the Battle of Cnidus (394 BC), ending Sparta's brief bid for naval supremacy. Athens then used Persian funds to rebuild the Long Walls and recover many of its former Aegean possessions.

Outcome

The war ended in 387 BC with the Peace of Antalcidas, also known as the King's Peace, dictated by Artaxerxes II. The treaty guaranteed the independence of all Greek cities except those in Asia (which remained Persian) and established Sparta as the enforcer of the peace, a role Sparta used to consolidate its hegemony — provoking further conflict in the years that followed.
