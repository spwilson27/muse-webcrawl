Bandstand

TypeOutdoor performance structure
Typical formOctagonal or circular raised platform with roof
Period of popularityLate 19th – early 20th century
Found inPublic parks, seaside promenades, town squares

A bandstand (also called a music pavilion) is a small, usually circular or polygonal structure set in a park, garden, pier, or promenade, designed to accommodate concerts by musical groups such as brass bands. British bandstands are typically raised platforms with a roof supported on slender columns, often built of ornate cast iron.

History

As public parks were laid out in Victorian Britain and brass bands became a mass working-class pastime, municipalities began erecting purpose-built stands. The first are usually dated to the 1860s, in Manchester and London parks; by the early 1900s thousands stood across the United Kingdom, the British Empire, and North America, where the similar gazebo was used for band concerts. Sunday afternoon band concerts became a fixture of leisure before the First World War.

Decline and revival

Changing entertainment habits, two world wars (when many iron structures were scrapped for the war effort), and neglect led to widespread demolition in the mid-20th century. From the 1990s, heritage funding and local campaigns restored many survivors, and new bandstands have been built in contemporary designs; the restored Clapham Common bandstand in London, dating from 1890, is among the largest remaining.

Design

The classic design has a raised masonry or brick base, cast-iron columns made by foundries such as George Smith & Co. (Sun Foundry) and Macfarlane's of Glasgow, decorative spandrels and railings, and a bell-shaped or pagoda roof, often topped with a weathervane. The roof also serves as a reflector, projecting sound toward the audience.
