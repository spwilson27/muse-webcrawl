Refractory metals

Members: Tungsten, molybdenum, tantalum, niobium, rhenium
Defining trait: Melting points above 2,000 °C
Uses: Aerospace, lighting, nuclear, tooling

Refractory metals are a class of metals that are extraordinarily resistant to heat and wear. The most widely used definition includes five elements: tungsten, molybdenum, tantalum, niobium and rhenium, all of which have melting points above 2,000 °C. They are characterised by high density, hardness and resistance to thermal shock, though most oxidise readily at high temperatures and must be protected in service.

Properties

Tungsten has the highest melting point of any metal, at 3,422 °C, while rhenium (3,186 °C), tantalum (3,017 °C), molybdenum (2,623 °C) and niobium (2,477 °C) follow. The metals share a body-centred cubic crystal structure and exhibit a ductile-to-brittle transition temperature that complicates fabrication at room temperature.

Applications

Refractory metals are used in lamp filaments, heating elements, rocket nozzles, jet engine components, nuclear reactor parts, cutting tools and electrical contacts. Tantalum is additionally valued for corrosion-resistant chemical plant equipment and capacitors.

Production

Because of their high melting points, refractory metals are generally produced by powder metallurgy rather than conventional casting: metal powders are pressed and sintered, then worked by forging, rolling or swaging.
