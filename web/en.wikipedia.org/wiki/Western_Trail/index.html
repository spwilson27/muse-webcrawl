The Western Trail, also known as the Great Western Cattle Trail and the Dodge City Trail, was a major cattle trail of the 19th-century American West. After the earlier Chisholm Trail shifted west because of settlement, quarantine laws and the spread of barbed wire, the Western Trail became the most heavily used of the great cattle trails, carrying herds from Texas north to railheads and ranges in Kansas, Nebraska and beyond, and ultimately as far as Canada.

Route

The trail began at gathering points in South and Central Texas, including Brownsville and Fort Worth, and crossed the Red River near Doan's Crossing in present-day Wilbarger County. From there it ran north through Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma), past the site of Fort Supply, to Dodge City, Kansas — which succeeded Abilene and Ellsworth as the great cattle-shipping boomtown — and on to Ogallala, Nebraska, and the northern ranges of Wyoming, Montana and the Dakotas.

History

The trail was pioneered around 1874, when John T. Lytle drove a herd from Texas toward the new railhead at Dodge City. At its peak in the early 1880s, hundreds of thousands of longhorn cattle moved north each season; estimates credit the Western Trail with moving more cattle — perhaps six million or more — than any other trail. The era ended in the mid-1880s as Kansas quarantine laws against Texas fever, the spread of fenced farmland, and the expansion of railroads into Texas closed the trail drives. The great blizzards of 1886–87 finished the open-range system the trail had served.

Legacy

The route is commemorated by the Great Western Cattle Trail Association and local markers, notably at Doan's Crossing, where an annual picnic has been held since the 1880s. The trail figures prominently in Western literature and in the mythology of the American cowboy.
