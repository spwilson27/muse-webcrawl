Maxwell's demon
TypeThought experiment
Proposed byJames Clerk Maxwell (1867)
FieldThermodynamics, information theory

Maxwell's demon is a thought experiment proposed by the physicist James Clerk Maxwell in 1867 in which a hypothetical being appears to violate the second law of thermodynamics by sorting fast and slow molecules between two chambers without expending work.

The thought experiment

Maxwell imagined a container of gas divided by a wall with a small door. A "demon" — in Maxwell's words, a being with faculties "sharpened" so it can follow every molecule — opens the door only to let fast molecules pass one way and slow ones the other, creating a temperature difference from which work could be extracted, apparently defeating entropy increase.

Resolutions

Leó Szilárd (1929) showed the demon must acquire information about the molecules, linking the paradox to information entropy. Léon Brillouin argued that measuring the molecules' positions costs energy. Later work by Rolf Landauer and Charles Bennett completed the standard resolution: erasing the demon's memory of its measurements dissipates at least as much entropy as the sorting saves, so the second law is preserved once the demon is treated as part of the physical system.

Significance

The demon became a foundational problem connecting thermodynamics and information theory, inspiring experimental realizations with single-electron boxes and colloidal particles in the 21st century.
