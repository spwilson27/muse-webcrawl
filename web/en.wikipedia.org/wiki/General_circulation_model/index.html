A general circulation model (GCM) is a type of climate model that employs a mathematical model of the general circulation of a planetary atmosphere or ocean. It applies the Navier–Stokes equations on a rotating sphere with thermodynamic terms for radiation, moisture and other energy sources, dividing the fluid into a three-dimensional grid of cells and numerically integrating the primitive equations over time.

Structure

Atmospheric GCMs (AGCMs) model the atmosphere and impose sea surface temperatures as boundary conditions, while oceanic GCMs (OGCMs) model the ocean. Fully coupled atmosphere–ocean GCMs (AOGCMs) combine both and form the basis of most projections of future climate change assessed by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC). Processes too small to be resolved by the grid — typically tens of kilometres across horizontally — such as convection, cloud formation and turbulence, are represented through semi-empirical parameterizations.

History

In 1955 Norman Phillips produced the first successful numerical simulation of the general circulation of the atmosphere. Joseph Smagorinsky and Syukuro Manabe developed more complete models at the Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory through the 1960s, culminating in the first coupled ocean–atmosphere model in 1969. Kirk Bryan and others developed the oceanic counterpart, influencing the design of modern Earth system models.

Applications

Beyond climate projection, GCMs are used for seasonal forecasting, studies of past climates (palaeoclimate), and modelling the atmospheres of other planets and exoplanets. Modern Earth system models add interactive carbon cycles, atmospheric chemistry, ice sheets, and vegetation dynamics to the physical GCM core.
