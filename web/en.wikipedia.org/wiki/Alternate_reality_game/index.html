Alternate reality game

An ARG unfolds across many media at once
GenreInteractive narrative / transmedia game
PlatformsWeb, email, telephone, print, live events, physical locations
First major exampleThe Beast (2001)
Distinguishing ethos"This Is Not a Game" (TINAG)

An alternate reality game (ARG) is an interactive networked narrative that uses the real world as a platform and employs transmedia storytelling to deliver a story that may be altered by players' ideas or actions. The story unfolds across websites, emails, phone calls, text messages, printed material, and physical locations, and players collectively solve puzzles and piece together the plot, usually coordinating through online communities.

Characteristics

ARGs are guided by designers known as "puppetmasters", who adjust the story in response to the players while typically remaining hidden. A defining aesthetic is the slogan "This Is Not a Game" (TINAG): the game's fictional world is presented as if it were real, with characters' websites never acknowledging the fiction. Entry points called "rabbit holes" or "trailheads" — a mysterious ad, a strange phone number — draw players in. Puzzles are often designed to be unsolvable by any single person, requiring the pooled skills of thousands of participants.

History and examples

The form emerged in 2001 with The Beast, created by Microsoft to promote the film A.I. Artificial Intelligence; it attracted millions of players and a dedicated solving group, the Cloudmakers. I Love Bees (2004), promoting Halo 2, famously used ringing payphones across the United States. ARGs have since been used to promote films and television (The Dark Knight's "Why So Serious?" campaign, Lost), to accompany albums and games (Year Zero, Portal 2), and as standalone or educational works such as the BBC's and Channel 4's serious games and the alternate-reality experiences of museums.
