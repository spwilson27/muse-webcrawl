Quorum sensing

Quorum sensing is a process of cell-to-cell communication that allows bacteria to share information about cell population density and to adjust gene expression accordingly. Bacteria that use quorum sensing secrete signalling molecules called autoinducers; as the population grows, the concentration of autoinducer rises, and once it passes a threshold the bacteria detect it and coordinately switch on sets of genes—for example genes for bioluminescence, virulence factor production, biofilm formation, sporulation or competence. The process was first recognised in the bioluminescent marine bacterium Aliivibrio fischeri (formerly Vibrio fischeri), which lives symbiotically in the light organs of squid and fish.

Mechanism

In Gram-negative bacteria the classic circuit uses acyl-homoserine lactone (AHL) autoinducers produced by a LuxI-type synthase and detected by a LuxR-type receptor that acts as a transcription factor. Gram-positive bacteria typically use processed oligopeptide signals detected by two-component systems. A third signal, autoinducer-2 (AI-2), derived from the LuxS enzyme, is produced by many species and is thought to permit interspecies communication. Key research figures in the field include Bonnie Bassler, whose work on Vibrio harveyi revealed multiple parallel signalling channels.

Examples

  • Bioluminescence in A. fischeri and V. harveyi.
  • Virulence: Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus (via the agr system) time toxin production to population density.
  • Biofilm formation and dispersal in many pathogens.
  • Competence for DNA uptake in Bacillus subtilis and Streptococcus pneumoniae.

Quorum quenching and applications

Interfering with quorum sensing—termed quorum quenching—using enzymes that degrade AHLs or molecules that block receptors is studied as an anti-virulence strategy that might disarm pathogens without killing them, potentially imposing weaker selection for resistance than conventional antibiotics. Quorum sensing also has applications in synthetic biology, where its circuits are used to programme population-level behaviour in engineered microbes.
