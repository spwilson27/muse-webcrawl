Posse comitatus (common law)

Posse comitatus (Latin for "power of the county") is the common-law authority of a county sheriff, or another law officer, to summon able-bodied men to assist in enforcing the law — for example, to pursue felons, quell riots, or execute legal process. The summoned body is itself called a posse. The institution descends from Anglo-Saxon England and was long a cornerstone of local law enforcement in England and the United States.

History

Roots lie in the Anglo-Saxon fyrd and the hue and cry, by which all able-bodied men were obliged to join in pursuing criminals. Under English common law, sheriffs could raise the posse comitatus of the whole county; refusing a summons was an offence. The power passed into American practice, where posses became iconic in the frontier West — pursuing outlaws, guarding prisoners, and suppressing disturbances — and where figures like Pat Garrett organized celebrated posses.

Modern status

In England and Wales, the sheriff's power to raise a posse was effectively superseded by professional police forces and removed by the Criminal Law Act 1967. In the United States, the authority survives in many state statutes and sheriffs occasionally deputize citizens, though refusal is rarely penalized in practice.

Distinction from the Posse Comitatus Act

The common-law doctrine should not be confused with the U.S. Posse Comitatus Act of 1878, which limits the use of the federal Army (and later the Air Force, and by policy the Navy and Marines) as a domestic law-enforcement force.
