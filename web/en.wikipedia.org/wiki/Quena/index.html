Quena

Quena
Classification: Aerophone, end-blown flute
Origin: Andes, South America
Related instruments: Quenacho, tarka, recorder

The quena is a traditional end-blown notch flute of the Andes, used in the folk music of Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and neighbouring regions of South America. It is typically made of wood or cane (bamboo) and has six finger holes in front and one thumb hole in back. Its sound is produced by blowing against the sharp edge of a U- or V-shaped notch cut into the top rim of the tube.

Description

A standard quena is about 37 cm (15 inches) long and is most often built in the key of G major, sounding over a range of roughly three octaves. Traditionally quenas were carved from a single piece of wood — including hardwoods such as cocobolo and ebony — or made from the leg bone of animals; modern instruments are commonly made of cane, wood, or even plastic. The smaller, lower-pitched variant tuned in D is called the quenacho.

History and use

Notched flutes have a long pre-Columbian history in the Andes, and the quena became central to Andean folk ensembles alongside the charango, guitar, zamponas (panpipes), and bombo drum. It carries many of the characteristic melodies of the region, such as "El Cóndor Pasa". In the 1960s and 1970s the quena spread worldwide with the nueva canción movement and Andean folk groups such as Los Kjarkas and Inti-Illimani, and it is now also heard in world music and film scores.

Playing technique

The player presses the notch against the lower lip and directs a focused stream of air across it, much as with the Japanese shakuhachi, to which the quena is often compared. Vibrato, half-holing, and cross-fingering extend its chromatic range beyond the diatonic scale of its basic fingering.
