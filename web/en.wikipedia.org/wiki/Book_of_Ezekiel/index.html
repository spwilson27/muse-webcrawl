The Book of Ezekiel is the third of the Latter Prophets in the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible) and one of the major prophetic books of the Old Testament, following Isaiah and Jeremiah. It records the visions and symbolic acts of Ezekiel, a priest carried into exile in Babylon with King Jehoiachin in 597 BC, who prophesied among the exiles at Tel Abib by the river Chebar.

Structure

The book falls into three broad sections. Chapters 1–24 announce judgement on Judah and Jerusalem: the inaugural vision of God's chariot-throne (Merkabah), Ezekiel's call as a "watchman", acted parables of the coming siege, and the vision of the glory of God departing from the Temple. Chapters 25–32 contain oracles against the foreign nations — Ammon, Moab, Edom, Philistia, Tyre, Sidon and Egypt. Chapters 33–48 turn to consolation after Jerusalem's fall: the promise of a new heart and spirit, the valley of dry bones restored to life, the defeat of Gog of Magog, and a long closing vision (chapters 40–48) of a restored Temple, priesthood, and land divided among the tribes.

Themes

Ezekiel stresses the holiness and sovereignty of God, whose glory is not confined to Jerusalem; the responsibility of the individual ("the soul that sins shall die", ch. 18); and God's purpose that nations "shall know that I am the LORD" — a refrain repeated throughout. Its strange imagery, sign-acts and priestly concerns strongly influenced later Jewish and Christian apocalyptic literature, including the Book of Revelation.

Composition

The book shows careful literary organisation, with dated oracles spanning about twenty years. Most scholars see a core of authentic Ezekiel material edited by a school of disciples. The dry bones vision (ch. 37) became a central text in Jewish hope for resurrection and national restoration.
