Demosthenes (general)

Demosthenes (Greek: Δημοσθένης, died 413 BC), son of Alcisthenes, was an Athenian general during the Peloponnesian War — not to be confused with his younger contemporary Demosthenes the orator. Thucydides portrays him as one of Athens' most imaginative and energetic commanders, whose career combined brilliant improvisation with catastrophic failure.

Early campaigns

Demosthenes first appears in 426 BC leading an Athenian expedition into Aetolia, where his hoplites were ambushed by light-armed Aetolian javelin men in rough country and soundly defeated at Aegitium, costing him his command's reputation for a time. He redeemed himself the following year in operations around the Corinthian Gulf, defeating Ambraciot and Peloponnesian forces at Olpae and Idomene in Acarnania.

Pylos and Sphacteria

His fame rests on the campaign of 425 BC. Sailing with a fleet bound for Sicily, Demosthenes persuaded his fellow commanders to fortify the deserted headland of Pylos in Messenia during a storm-enforced stop. The post provoked a Spartan assault; after the Athenian fleet defeated the Spartans at sea, a body of Spartan hoplites was trapped on the island of Sphacteria. When negotiations stalled, Demosthenes and the demagogue Cleon completed the victory — landing light troops who wore down the Spartans with missile fire until they surrendered, an event that stunned the Greek world. Demosthenes' occupation of Pylos gave Athens a lever that helped produce the Peace of Nicias in 421 BC.

Sicily and death

In 413 BC Demosthenes was sent with a large reinforcement fleet to rescue the faltering Sicilian Expedition before Syracuse. Arriving after the Athenian position had deteriorated, he urged an immediate attack and, when his night assault on Epipolae failed, advised withdrawal; disagreements with Nicias and a lunar eclipse fatally delayed the retreat. The Athenian army and fleet were destroyed. Demosthenes was captured after the surrender of his division and was executed by the Syracusans, despite the efforts of Gylippus to save the generals for Sparta.

Assessment

Thucydides and later historians credit Demosthenes with innovative use of light-armed troops and terrain, and with recognising the strategic value of Pylos. His career illustrates both the strengths and the institutional weaknesses of Athenian generalship: elected annually, generals could be visionary one campaign and ruined by political second-guessing the next.
