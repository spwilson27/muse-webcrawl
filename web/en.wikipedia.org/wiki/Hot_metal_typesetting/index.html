Hot metal typesetting

Hot metal typesetting is a technology for setting text in letterpress printing in which type is cast in molten metal on the spot, rather than assembled by hand from pre-cast sorts. The principal systems were the Linotype, which cast whole lines ("slugs") of type, and the Monotype, which cast individual characters.

History

Ottmar Mergenthaler patented the Linotype in the 1880s, and it was first used commercially by the New-York Tribune in 1886. Tolbert Lanston's Monotype system followed in the 1890s. Together these machines revolutionised the newspaper and book trades, greatly increasing the speed at which text could be set. Type metal — an alloy of lead, tin and antimony — was melted in the machine's pot, cast, used for one print run, and then melted down again for reuse.

Decline

Hot metal dominated typesetting for most of the twentieth century, but from the 1960s it was displaced first by phototypesetting ("cold type") and then by digital desktop publishing. A small number of machines remain in use for specialist and fine-press work.
