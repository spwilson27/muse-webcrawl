Ancestor veneration
Practised inEast and Southeast Asia, Africa, Madagascar, ancient Mediterranean, and elsewhere
Core ritesOfferings of food, incense, libations; care of graves and spirit tablets

Ancestor veneration is a set of religious beliefs and practices centred on the continuing influence and care of deceased relatives. In most traditions the ancestors are not worshipped as gods but honoured as senior family members who remain interested in the family's welfare and who, if properly attended with offerings and remembrance, bring protection and good fortune; neglected or aggrieved ancestors, by contrast, may cause misfortune. The practice is found in many cultures, from ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome to contemporary China, Vietnam, Korea, Japan, sub-Saharan Africa, and Madagascar.

East and Southeast Asia

In China, ancestor veneration is intertwined with Confucian filial piety: families maintain ancestral tablets on home altars or in clan halls, burn incense, and sweep graves during the Qingming Festival; the Ghost Festival honours hungry spirits. Vietnamese practice is similar, with death anniversaries (giỗ) as major family occasions. In Japan, household Buddhist altars (butsudan) and the summer Obon festival commemorate ancestral spirits. Korean families perform memorial rites (jesa) on holidays such as Chuseok.

Africa and Madagascar

Many African traditional religions treat the ancestors — sometimes called the "living dead" — as mediators between the human community and the divine, receiving libations and prayers. In Madagascar, the Merina practice of famadihana, the periodic rewrapping of ancestors' remains, dramatically reaffirms kinship ties.

Ancient world and modern survival

Roman families kept masks and shrines of ancestors (lararia) and honoured the di Manes; Egyptian households maintained offering cults for the dead. In modern societies, practices such as Mexico's Day of the Dead and All Souls' observances preserve elements of ancestral commemoration, and the practice continues vigorously in much of Asia and Africa, often alongside Buddhism, Christianity, or Islam.
