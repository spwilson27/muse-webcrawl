In Islam, angels (Arabic: ملائكة, malāʾika) are celestial beings created by God from light who act as his messengers and servants. Belief in angels is one of the six articles of faith in Sunni Islam. Unlike humans, angels have no free will to disobey: they carry out God's commands exactly, neither eat nor drink, and are not described as male or female.

Principal angels

The most important is Jibril (Gabriel), who transmitted the revelations of the Quran to the Prophet Muhammad and announced the birth of Jesus to Mary. Mikail (Michael) is associated with providence and rain. Israfil will blow the trumpet announcing the Day of Judgement, and Azrael (Malak al-Mawt, the Angel of Death) separates souls from bodies. Other named angels include Ridwan, guardian of Paradise, and Malik, keeper of Hell.

Roles

Angels record every person's deeds: the Kiraman Katibin, the "honourable scribes", sit on each person's right and left shoulders. Munkar and Nakir question the dead in their graves. Angels bear God's Throne, seek out gatherings of remembrance (dhikr), greet the inhabitants of Paradise, and, according to tradition, pray for believers. Guardian angels (mu'aqqibat) accompany each person.

Distinctions

Islamic theology distinguishes angels, made of light and unfailingly obedient, from humans, made of clay, and from the jinn, made of smokeless fire, who like humans possess free will; Iblis (Satan) is understood by most scholars to be a jinn rather than a fallen angel, since angels cannot rebel. Angels are generally invisible, though they may appear in human form, as Jibril is said to have done.
