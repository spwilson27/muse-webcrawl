Kharasch effect

The Kharasch effect, also called the peroxide effect, is the anti-Markovnikov addition of hydrogen bromide (HBr) to an alkene in the presence of peroxides. It was described by the American chemist Morris S. Kharasch and his co-workers in the 1930s. Whereas ordinary (ionic) addition of HBr to propene gives 2-bromopropane, the peroxide-initiated reaction gives 1-bromopropane as the major product.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds by a free-radical chain mechanism. Heat or light cleaves the weak O–O bond of the peroxide to give alkoxy radicals, which abstract hydrogen from HBr to form bromine radicals. A bromine radical adds to the alkene so as to form the more stable carbon radical; that radical then abstracts hydrogen from HBr, yielding the anti-Markovnikov alkyl bromide and regenerating a bromine radical to continue the chain.

Scope

The peroxide effect is observed only for HBr. With HCl and HI the corresponding radical steps are energetically unfavourable: hydrogen abstraction from HCl is too endothermic, while iodine radicals add reversibly to alkenes. The effect historically helped establish the importance of free-radical mechanisms in organic chemistry, and Kharasch's work explained earlier inconsistent results caused by traces of peroxide in reagents.
