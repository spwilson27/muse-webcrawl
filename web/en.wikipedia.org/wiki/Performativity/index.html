Performativity

Performativity is the concept that language can function as a form of social action and have the effect of change. The concept has multiple applications in diverse fields including anthropology, literary studies, performance studies, philosophy, linguistics, gender studies, political science, and economics.

Austin and speech acts

The term was developed by philosopher J. L. Austin in his 1955 William James lectures, published posthumously as How to Do Things with Words (1962). Austin distinguished performative utterances — which do not describe a state of affairs but perform an action, such as "I now pronounce you husband and wife" or "I apologise" — from constative utterances that describe facts and can be true or false. He later generalised this into a theory of speech acts, distinguishing locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts, and argued that all utterances have a performative dimension.

Subsequent developments

John Searle systematised speech act theory in Speech Acts (1969). Jacques Derrida, in "Signature Event Context" (1972), criticised Austin's reliance on intention and context, emphasising iterability — the capacity of a sign to function in the absence of its original context. Jean-François Lyotard used "performativity" differently, in The Postmodern Condition (1979), to describe the legitimation of knowledge by efficiency in postmodern societies.

Gender and identity

Judith Butler's theory of gender performativity, introduced in Gender Trouble (1990) and elaborated in Bodies That Matter (1993), holds that gender is not an innate essence expressed through behaviour but is constituted through the stylised repetition of acts over time. Butler stresses that performativity is not a voluntary "performance" but a process of citational reiteration constrained by social norms.

Economics and other fields

In economic sociology, scholars such as Michel Callon and Donald MacKenzie have argued that economic theories are performative: models like the Black–Scholes options pricing formula did not merely describe markets but helped shape them (an idea MacKenzie titled "an engine, not a camera").
