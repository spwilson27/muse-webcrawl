Buddhas of Bamiyan

The niches of the two Buddhas in the Bamiyan cliff

LocationBamiyan Valley, Bamyan Province, Afghanistan
Built6th century CE
DestroyedMarch 2001
MaterialSandstone, stucco, pigment
Heights55 m (Eastern) and 38 m (Western)
StatusUNESCO World Heritage Site (2003)

The Buddhas of Bamiyan were two monumental 6th-century statues of Gautama Buddha carved into the side of a cliff in the Bamyan valley in the Hazarajat region of central Afghanistan, 230 kilometres northwest of Kabul at an elevation of 2,500 metres. The statues represented a classic blended style of Gandhara art.

History

Bamiyan lay on the Silk Road, and by the 6th century it was a thriving Buddhist centre with more than ten monasteries and a thousand monks. The larger, Eastern Buddha, 55 metres tall, was built around 544 CE; the smaller, Western Buddha, 38 metres tall, around 591 CE. The figures were carved directly out of the sandstone cliff, with details modelled in mud mixed with straw and coated with stucco and painted. The faces were probably made of wood and later lost; the figures' robes were originally painted, and the niches were decorated with frescoes.

The statues survived the arrival of Islam in the region and centuries of iconoclasm, partly because they were so large and so high. The Mughal emperor Aurangzeb reportedly tried to destroy them with cannon in the 17th century, and the Persian king Nader Shah directed cannon fire at them in the 18th century, but both attempts failed to do more than damage the legs.

Destruction in 2001

In March 2001, after the Taliban government declared the statues idols, they were destroyed with dynamite and artillery over several weeks, despite international condemnation and offers to preserve them. The destruction was widely condemned as an act of cultural vandalism.

Aftermath and reconstruction debate

The empty niches remain, and the site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2003, simultaneously placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger. There has been extensive debate about whether to reconstruct the statues, leave the niches empty as a memorial, or use anastylosis — reassembling surviving fragments. A light projection of the Eastern Buddha was shown in the niche in 2015.
