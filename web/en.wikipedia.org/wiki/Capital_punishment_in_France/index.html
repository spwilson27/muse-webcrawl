Capital punishment in France was abolished by law in 1981 and written into the French Constitution in 2007. France is notable for having used the guillotine as its sole official method of execution from the Revolution until abolition, and for being the last country in Western Europe to carry out an execution by that method.

History

Before the Revolution, executions took varied and often cruel forms depending on the crime and the condemned's rank, including breaking on the wheel and hanging. In 1791 the guillotine was adopted as the egalitarian standard. Public executions continued until 1939, when the filmed guillotining of Eugène Weidmann prompted a ban on public spectacle. The death penalty remained in force through the Fourth and Fifth Republics, and Presidents had the power of clemency; Georges Pompidou commuted most sentences, while Valéry Giscard d'Estaing allowed three executions.

Abolition

François Mitterrand, elected President in 1981 on a platform that included abolition, appointed Robert Badinter as Minister of Justice. Badinter's impassioned speech to the National Assembly helped secure the law of 9 October 1981 abolishing the penalty. The last person executed was Hamida Djandoubi, guillotined at Marseille on 10 September 1977. In 2007, abolition was entrenched in Article 66-1 of the Constitution, and France is also bound by Protocols 6 and 13 to the European Convention on Human Rights.
