Murabaha

Murabaha (Arabic: مرابحة), also known as cost-plus financing, is a contract of sale used extensively in Islamic banking and finance as an alternative to interest-bearing loans, which are prohibited as riba. In a murabaha, the bank purchases an asset requested by the client and then resells it to the client at a price that includes a disclosed mark-up, payable in instalments.

Mechanics

The contract requires that the cost price and the profit margin be transparently disclosed to the buyer, and that the seller genuinely own and bear the risk of the asset before resale. Because the client's obligation is a fixed deferred price rather than a loan, the transaction is treated by most Sharia scholars as a sale rather than a prohibited money-for-money exchange.

Use and criticism

Murabaha is the single most common instrument in Islamic banking, underlying trade finance, working-capital facilities and synthetic structures such as tawarruq (commodity murabaha on metal exchanges). Critics within and outside the industry argue that when the mark-up is benchmarked to interest rates and the bank's ownership is momentary, the arrangement replicates a conventional loan in all but form; supporters reply that the legal structure, asset-backing and risk allocation differ meaningfully.
