Investment fund

An investment fund is a way of investing money alongside other investors in order to benefit from the inherent advantages of working as part of a group, such as reducing the risks of the investment by a significant percentage. These advantages include an ability to hire professional investment managers, diversify portfolios, and pay lower transaction and management costs.

Types

Investment funds take many legal and organisational forms, including:

  • Mutual funds — open-ended funds that issue and redeem shares at the net asset value calculated each day.
  • Closed-ended funds — issue a fixed number of shares that trade on an exchange at prices that may differ from net asset value.
  • Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) — traded on exchanges like shares, usually tracking an index.
  • Hedge funds — lightly regulated private funds using leveraged and alternative strategies.
  • Private equity and venture capital funds — invest in unlisted companies.
  • Unit investment trusts, money market funds, and real estate investment trusts (REITs).

Structure and operation

A fund is typically established by a management company, which appoints a fund manager or adviser to make investment decisions, a custodian to hold the assets, and an administrator to value the fund and process subscriptions and redemptions. Investors buy units or shares representing a proportionate interest in the fund's assets.

Costs

Investors bear ongoing charges (management fees and operating expenses, summarised in the expense ratio) and sometimes entry or exit loads. Costs are a persistent drag on returns and a major focus of investor-protection regulation.

Regulation

Retail investment funds are heavily regulated in most jurisdictions — for example under the Investment Company Act of 1940 in the United States and the UCITS directives in the European Union — with rules on custody, disclosure, leverage, and eligible assets.
