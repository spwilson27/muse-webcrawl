Trust

This article is about the social and psychological concept. For the legal arrangement, see Trust law. For the fiduciary officer, see Trustee.
Trust
Fields:Psychology, sociology, economics, philosophy
Related to:Social capital, cooperation, reputation

Trust is the belief that another person will do what is expected — a willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on positive expectations of their intentions or behaviour. In social science, trust is studied as a foundation of cooperation, exchange, and institutional life: it reduces transaction costs, supports collective action, and is often counted as a key component of social capital.

Dimensions

Scholars commonly distinguish trust in specific persons (particularised trust) from trust in strangers and institutions (generalised or social trust). Organisational research often analyses trustworthiness along three dimensions — ability, benevolence, and integrity — and notes the asymmetry of trust: it is slow to accumulate and quick to destroy. Survey instruments such as the World Values Survey measure generalised trust by asking whether "most people can be trusted".

Economic and political significance

Economists and political scientists, including Francis Fukuyama in Trust: The Social Virtues and the Creation of Prosperity (1995), have argued that high-trust societies enjoy advantages in forming large organisations and sustaining prosperous markets. Robert Putnam's work connected declining trust and civic engagement in the United States with weakening social capital. Trust is also central to contract, money, and democratic legitimacy; its absence is associated with corruption and reliance on kin-based networks.

Breach and repair

Violations of trust — betrayal, fraud, broken promises — produce distinctive harms beyond material loss. Research on trust repair examines the roles of apology, penance, denial, and structural safeguards, and the conditions under which trust in institutions can be rebuilt after scandals.
