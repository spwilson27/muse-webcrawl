The McDonnell Douglas DC-9 is an American five-abreast, single-aisle airliner designed by the Douglas Aircraft Company. It was initially produced by Douglas, and later by McDonnell Douglas after the companies' merger in 1967. The DC-9 first flew in 1965 and entered service with Delta Air Lines later that year.

The DC-9 features two rear-mounted Pratt & Whitney JT8D turbofan engines, a T-tail, and a narrow fuselage with five-abreast seating for shorter routes. Its built-in airstairs and auxiliary power unit allowed it to operate from smaller airports with limited ground equipment. A total of 976 DC-9s were built before production ended in 1982.

Development

Douglas began studying a short-haul jet airliner in the late 1950s to complement the larger DC-8. After a proposed joint venture with Sud Aviation to market the French Caravelle in the United States fell through, Douglas proceeded with its own clean-sheet design. The DC-9 was designed for frequent, short flights and rapid turnarounds.

Variants

The original Series 10 was followed by the stretched Series 30, which became the most popular variant, the longer-range Series 20 with uprated engines and leading-edge slats, the Series 40, and the Series 50, the longest of the original family. A military version, the C-9, served with the United States Air Force and Navy as an aeromedical evacuation and transport aircraft.

The DC-9 family was further developed into the MD-80 series in the 1980s, the MD-90 in the 1990s, and finally the Boeing 717 after Boeing's acquisition of McDonnell Douglas in 1997 — making the basic DC-9 design one of the longest-lived airliner families in production.

Operators and legacy

Major operators included Delta Air Lines, Northwest Airlines, TWA, and numerous carriers worldwide. Delta retired the last scheduled passenger DC-9 service in the United States in January 2014, although cargo and charter examples continued flying longer. The DC-9 is remembered as a rugged, reliable workhorse of the early jet age.
