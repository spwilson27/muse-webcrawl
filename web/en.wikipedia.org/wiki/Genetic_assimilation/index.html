Genetic assimilation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic assimilation is a process described by the British biologist Conrad Hal Waddington in the 1940s and 1950s, by which a phenotype originally produced only in response to an environmental stimulus becomes, after generations of selection, produced constitutively — that is, without the stimulus. It is a form of phenotypic plasticity followed by evolutionary canalisation, and is closely related to the Baldwin effect and to genetic accommodation.

Waddington's experiments

In his classic experiments, Waddington exposed pupae of the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster to a heat shock. Some of the emerging adults lacked the posterior crossvein in their wings (the "crossveinless" phenocopy). By selecting the crossveinless flies and repeating the procedure over generations, Waddington found that after about fourteen generations, some flies produced the crossveinless phenotype even without heat shock. The environmentally induced trait had been "assimilated" into the genotype. In a second series, ether vapour induced a bithorax-like phenotype that likewise became constitutive under selection.

Mechanism

Genetic assimilation requires no Lamarckian inheritance of acquired characters. The explanation is orthodox population genetics: the environmental stress reveals cryptic genetic variation that is normally buffered (canalised). Selection for the induced phenotype concentrates alleles that lower the threshold for producing it, until eventually the trait appears spontaneously. The developmental biologist's concepts of canalisation and the epigenetic landscape were developed by Waddington partly to frame this work.

Significance

Assimilation is cited as a mechanism by which plasticity can lead evolutionary change ("plasticity-first" evolution), allowing populations to track novel environments before genetic change catches up. Modern discussions link it to the release of cryptic variation when chaperones such as Hsp90 are compromised, as shown in experiments by Susan Lindquist and colleagues, and it remains an active topic in evolutionary developmental biology.
