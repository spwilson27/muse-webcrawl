Scots baronial

Scots baronial — also called Scottish baronial — is an architectural style of the Gothic Revival that developed in 19th-century Scotland, drawing on the forms of Scottish castles and tower houses of the 16th and 17th centuries. The term was popularised by the architectural historian and architect Robert William Billings, whose four-volume The Baronial and Ecclesiastical Antiquities of Scotland (1848–52) catalogued the historic buildings that inspired the revival.

Characteristics

Scots baronial buildings typically display asymmetrical massing, conical-roofed turrets (bartizans) corbelled out at corners, crow-stepped gables, small windows irregularly placed, tall chimney stacks and castellated parapets. Interiors often feature great halls with timber ceilings and heraldic decoration. The style was applied to country houses, urban terraces, schools, railway hotels and public buildings.

Examples

The rebuilt Balmoral Castle in Aberdeenshire (completed 1856 for Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, to designs by William Smith) gave the style royal endorsement. Other famous examples include Abbotsford, Sir Walter Scott's house in the Borders; the Palace of Holyroodhouse's surviving towers; Donaldson's Hospital and the former Free Church College in Edinburgh; and, in an urban palace form, the Scots baronial tenements and hotels of Victorian Glasgow and Aberdeen. In Edinburgh, architects such as David Bryce and William Burn were leading exponents; Bryce's work at Fettes College and the former Royal Infirmary helped codify the style.

Legacy

Scots baronial became an emblem of Scottish national identity in architecture, influencing designs across the British Empire — in Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa — and in the United States. It declined after the First World War, but its silhouette of turrets and crow-steps remains instantly recognisable as "Scottish".
