The flight and expulsion of Germans during and after the Second World War (1944–1950) was the forced migration of at least 12 million ethnic Germans from Central and Eastern Europe, the largest of a series of post-war population transfers in Europe. Germans fled or were expelled from the former eastern territories of Germany incorporated into Poland and the Soviet Union, and from Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, and Yugoslavia.

Background and course

From late 1944, millions fled westward before the advancing Red Army, in evacuations marked by catastrophes such as the sinking of the refugee ship Wilhelm Gustloff. After Germany's defeat, "wild" expulsions began in Poland and Czechoslovakia; Article XIII of the Potsdam Agreement (August 1945) then authorised the "orderly and humane" transfer of German populations. Organised transports continued until around 1950.

Human cost

Demographic estimates attribute between roughly 500,000 and 2 million deaths to the flight and expulsions, from violence, disease, starvation, and conditions in internment and labour camps; the higher figures, produced by West German government studies in the 1950s, remain contested by historians.

Legacy

The expellees (Vertriebene) formed a powerful political constituency in West Germany, and their fate remains a sensitive topic in German–Polish and German–Czech relations. Successive agreements, including the 1997 German–Czech Declaration, have sought to close the issue.
