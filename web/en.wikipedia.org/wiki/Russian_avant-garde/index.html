Russian avant-garde

The Russian avant-garde was a large, influential wave of modern art that flourished in the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union between roughly 1890 and the early 1930s — some date its peak to 1917–1932. It combined and transformed European movements such as Fauvism, Cubism and Futurism with native traditions, producing path-breaking experiments in painting, sculpture, architecture, photography, theatre, design and film.

Development

Early groups such as the Jack of Diamonds (founded 1910) and Donkey's Tail promoted neo-primitivism and Cubo-Futurism; Mikhail Larionov and Natalia Goncharova developed Rayonism. At the 0.10 Futurist exhibition in Petrograd in 1915, Kazimir Malevich launched Suprematism with the Black Square, hung across the corner in the position of an icon. Vladimir Tatlin's counter-reliefs and his proposed Monument to the Third International (1919–20) seeded Constructivism, which after the Revolution sought to put art into production — typified by Alexander Rodchenko's design and El Lissitzky's "Proun" works and photomontages.

After the Revolution

The Bolsheviks initially patronised avant-garde art: the Vkhutemas art and technical workshops (founded 1920) paralleled the Bauhaus, and avant-garde artists designed agitprop trains, posters, textiles and workers' clubs. Filmmakers such as Sergei Eisenstein and Dziga Vertov revolutionised cinema through montage. Through the 1920s, figures like Wassily Kandinsky and Marc Chagall took Russian abstraction abroad.

Suppression and legacy

Under Stalin, independent artistic groups were dissolved; the 1932 decree "On the Restructuring of Literary and Artistic Organisations" and the 1934 proclamation of Socialist Realism ended the avant-garde as a public movement, and many works were hidden in storerooms for decades. Rediscovered internationally from the 1960s onward, the Russian avant-garde is now regarded as one of the foundational episodes of modernism, with major holdings at the State Russian Museum, the Tretyakov Gallery and MoMA.
