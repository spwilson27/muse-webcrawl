Morris dance is a form of English folk dance usually accompanied by music and characterised by bells worn on the dancers' shins and the use of implements such as sticks, swords and handkerchiefs. It is based on rhythmic stepping and the execution of choreographed figures by a group of dancers, usually six in number, organised as a team or "side". Morris dancing is most commonly performed outdoors at festivals, fêtes and outside public houses, particularly in spring and summer and around calendar customs such as May Day and Whitsun.

History

The earliest surviving written reference in England dates to 1448, when the Goldsmiths' Company of London recorded a payment to morris dancers. The name is generally linked to "Moorish" — the dance was long supposed, probably fancifully, to derive from dances of the Moors of Spain; early performers sometimes blackened their faces as part of the disguise tradition, a practice modern teams have largely abandoned or replaced with other disguises. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries morris was established at church ales, Whitsun ales and civic pageantry, and the clown Will Kempe famously morris-danced from London to Norwich in 1599, recording the feat in his Nine Daies Wonder.

The dance declined during the nineteenth century under the pressure of industrialisation, but was revived after Cecil Sharp began collecting dances from 1899, famously after watching the Headington Quarry side on Boxing Day of that year. The folk revival, the founding of the Morris Ring in 1934 and later the Morris Federation and Open Morris made the dance a widespread hobby across England and in the English-speaking diaspora.

Traditions and styles

  • Cotswold morris — from the villages of the Cotswolds and south Midlands; six dancers with handkerchiefs or sticks, complex "hey" figures; the tradition Sharp principally collected.
  • Border morris — from the Welsh border counties of Shropshire, Herefordshire and Worcestershire; traditionally performed in winter, with tattered jackets, energetic stick dances and simpler figures.
  • Northwest morris — from Lancashire and Cheshire mill towns; processional in origin, with clogs, ornate dress and figures derived from rushbearing ceremonies.
  • Molly dancing — from East Anglia; performed by ploughboys in winter, traditionally with one dancer dressed as a woman.
  • Sword dances — rapper (flexible swords, from the North East) and long sword (from Yorkshire), culminating in the interlaced "lock" or star of swords.

Music and costume

The traditional accompaniment was the pipe and tabor, played by a single musician; today the melodeon, fiddle, concertina and accordion predominate. Tunes, often named for their villages ("Constant Billy", "Lads a Bunchum", "Bean Setting"), are closely associated with particular dances. Bells have been a hallmark since at least the sixteenth century. Most sides also include a Fool, whose antics with the crowd are part of the performance, and sometimes a hobby horse or other animal character.

Modern practice

Several hundred morris sides are active in the United Kingdom, with further teams in Australia, North America and elsewhere. The admission of women, once controversial within the Morris Ring (which voted to admit women's and mixed sides in 2018), is now the norm across the three national organisations. Morris remains a fixture of May Day dawn dancing, village fêtes and folk festivals such as Sidmouth and Whitby.
