Eric L. Charnov
Born: 29 October 1947
Alma mater: University of Washington (PhD)
Known for: Marginal value theorem; sex allocation theory
Fields: Evolutionary ecology

Eric L. Charnov (born 29 October 1947) is an American evolutionary ecologist. He is known for his work in behavioural ecology, life history theory, and sex allocation, and in particular for the marginal value theorem, which predicts how long a forager should stay in a depleting patch of resources.

Career

Charnov received his PhD from the University of Washington in 1973, working on foraging behaviour. He has held positions at the University of Utah and the University of New Mexico, where he became a Distinguished Professor of Biology. He was awarded a MacArthur Fellowship ("genius grant") in 1987.

Contributions

In a classic 1976 paper in Theoretical Population Biology, Charnov derived the marginal value theorem: a forager should leave a patch when its instantaneous intake rate drops to the average rate for the whole environment, accounting for travel time between patches. The theorem became a cornerstone of optimal foraging theory and has been tested in animals from birds and bees to humans.

His 1982 book The Theory of Sex Allocation (Princeton University Press) systematically developed the theory of how organisms should divide resources between male and female reproduction, building on R. A. Fisher's principle and W. D. Hamilton's work on local mate competition, with applications to sex change in fishes and sex ratios in parasitoid wasps. Charnov also contributed to life-history theory, proposing dimensionless invariants — relationships among growth, mortality and reproduction that remain roughly constant across taxa — and, with later collaborators, to metabolic scaling approaches to life histories.
