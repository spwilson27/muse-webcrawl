Edwin (Old English: Ēadwine; c. 586 – 12 October 633), often called Saint Edwin, was King of Deira and Bernicia — the two kingdoms that later united as Northumbria — from about 616 until his death. He converted to Christianity and was baptised in 627; his reign marked the first Christian kingship in northern England, and he was recognised by the Venerable Bede as one of the seven Anglo-Saxon kings who held overlordship (imperium) over the southern English kingdoms.

Exile and accession

Edwin was the son of Ælle, king of Deira. After Æthelfrith of Bernicia seized Deira, Edwin spent years in exile, sheltered at various courts, including that of Rædwald of East Anglia. At the Battle of the River Idle (616/617) Rædwald defeated and killed Æthelfrith, and Edwin took the throne of a united Northumbria.

Reign and conversion

Edwin extended Northumbrian power over the Isle of Man and Anglesey and, in Bede's famous account, maintained such peace that a woman with a newborn child could walk from sea to sea unmolested. His marriage to the Christian princess Æthelburh of Kent brought the missionary Paulinus to York. After long deliberation — including, in Bede's story, the council scene in which a thane compared human life to a sparrow flying through a firelit hall — Edwin was baptised at York on Easter Day, 12 April 627, in a wooden church on the site of the later York Minster. Paulinus became the first bishop of York, and Edwin promoted Christianity throughout his dominions and beyond.

Death and veneration

In 633 Edwin was defeated and killed at the Battle of Hatfield Chase by an alliance of Cadwallon ap Cadfan of Gwynedd and Penda of Mercia. Northumbria briefly relapsed into pagan rule before the Christian restoration under Oswald. Edwin was venerated as a saint and martyr; his head was buried at St Peter's, York, and his feast day is 12 October. The village of Edwinstowe in Nottinghamshire is traditionally named for the resting place of his body.
