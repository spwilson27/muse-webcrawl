Irving Penn (June 16, 1917 – October 7, 2009) was an American photographer known for his fashion photography, portraits and still lifes. In a career spanning almost seventy years, mostly at Vogue magazine, Penn revolutionised fashion photography after the Second World War with elegantly spare images shot against plain grey or white backgrounds, and became renowned for psychologically penetrating portraits, meticulous still lifes and masterful platinum-palladium prints.

Early life and career

Penn was born in Plainfield, New Jersey, to Russian-Jewish immigrant parents; his younger brother Arthur became a noted film and theatre director. He studied design at the Philadelphia Museum School of Industrial Art under Alexey Brodovitch, worked as a graphic designer and art director, and bought his first camera in 1938, photographing shop windows and signs in New York. After war service with the Field Service ambulance corps, he joined Vogue in 1943, initially in the art department; art director Alexander Liberman encouraged him to photograph, and his first cover — a still life — appeared in October 1943.

Work

Penn's 1947 group portrait of the great figures of the era began his famous series of portraits of artists, writers and performers — from Duchamp, Stravinsky and Capote to Picasso, Ingmar Bergman and his wife, the model Lisa Fonssagrives, whom he married in 1950. He often placed sitters in a narrow corner formed by two studio walls, a device of 1948 that made subjects feel constrained and revealed their character. His "Small Trades" series (1950–51) portrayed Paris, London and New York tradespeople in their work clothes with the tools of their trade against a bare backdrop. From 1967 he travelled with a portable studio tent, making ethnographic portraits in New Guinea, Dahomey, Cameroon, Morocco and elsewhere. His still lifes ranged from sumptuous fashion accessories and frozen foods to cigarette butts, crushed objects and skulls photographed with clinical detachment.

Technique

Penn was a consummate craftsman: he favoured large-format and Rolleiflex cameras, controlled natural light, and from the 1960s revived the platinum-palladium printing process, producing prints of exceptional tonal range. He printed almost all his own work and kept a famously disciplined studio, at Vogue under successive Condé Nast regimes for over sixty years — the longest association of any photographer with the magazine.

Legacy

Major retrospectives were held at the Museum of Modern Art (1984) and the Metropolitan Museum of Art ("Irving Penn: Centennial", 2017). The Irving Penn Foundation preserves his archive. He is consistently ranked among the most influential photographers of the 20th century; his corner portraits, Small Trades and platinum prints are canonical works of the medium.
