Self-reference

The Droste effect: a picture containing a smaller copy of itself

Self-reference is the phenomenon of a sentence, idea, or formula referring to itself. It occurs in language ("this sentence has five words"), in mathematics and logic, in computation, and in art. Depending on structure, self-reference may be harmless, productive — as in recursion — or paradoxical, as in the liar paradox, "this statement is false", which is true only if it is false.

Paradoxes

Self-reference underlies several classical paradoxes. The liar paradox dates to Eubulides of Miletus; Russell's paradox (1901) showed that the set of all sets that do not contain themselves both must and cannot contain itself, shaking the foundations of naive set theory and prompting type theories and axiomatic set theories that restrict self-membership. The barber paradox and Grelling–Nelson paradox (of "heterological" words) are close relatives.

Mathematics and computation

Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) harness self-reference indirectly: by Gödel numbering, statements of arithmetic can encode claims about their own provability, yielding a true sentence that the system cannot prove. Alan Turing's proof of the undecidability of the halting problem uses a similar diagonal argument. Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) popularised the "strange loop" as the essence of these phenomena and of consciousness itself. In computer science, self-reference appears in recursion, in quines (programs that print their own source code), reflective programming languages, and self-reproducing automata designed by John von Neumann. Natural languages also display it in self-referential jokes, metalepsis, and the Droste effect in visual art and mise en abyme in literature.
