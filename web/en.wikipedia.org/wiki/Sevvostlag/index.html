Sevvostlag

Sevvostlag (Russian: Севвостлаг, from Severo-Vostochny Ispravitelno-Trudovoy Lager, "North-Eastern Corrective Labour Camp") was a system of forced-labour camps of the Gulag in the far north-east of the Soviet Union, centred on Magadan in the Kolyma region. Administered from 1932 in connection with the Dalstroy trust, its prisoners mined gold and other minerals and built roads and infrastructure under some of the harshest conditions in the Soviet camp system.

History

Sevvostlag was created in 1932 as the camp arm of Dalstroy, the Far Northern Construction Trust charged with developing the Kolyma region's gold deposits using prisoner labour. Prisoners arrived by sea from Vladivostok at Magadan's Nagaev Bay and were distributed to camps along the Kolyma Highway — the "Road of Bones" — and to mines in the interior. The camp system expanded rapidly in the later 1930s, absorbing victims of the Great Terror, and continued as a major destination for prisoners through the Second World War and into the early 1950s.

Conditions and mortality

Prisoners faced extreme cold, inadequate food and clothing, and brutal work norms in the mines. Mortality was correspondingly high, and Kolyma became, in memoir literature such as Varlam Shalamov's Kolyma Tales, a byword for the deadliest part of the Gulag. Estimates of deaths in the Kolyma camps vary widely, from tens of thousands to around a million over the camp era; archival research since 1991 has generally supported lower figures than the earliest estimates, while confirming very high death rates, particularly in 1938 and the war years.

Dissolution

After the death of Stalin in 1953 and the subsequent amnesties, Dalstroy was reorganised and Sevvostlag was progressively wound down, with the camp administration abolished in the later 1950s as the Kolyma camps were dismantled.
