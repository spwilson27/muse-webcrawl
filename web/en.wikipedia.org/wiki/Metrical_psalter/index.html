Metrical psalter

A metrical psalter is a book containing translations of the Book of Psalms into verse in the vernacular, set in regular metre so that the psalms can be sung in congregational worship to a small stock of tunes. Metrical psalters became a defining feature of Reformed worship after the Protestant Reformation.

History

The prototype was the Genevan Psalter (completed 1562), produced under John Calvin with versifications by Clément Marot and Théodore de Bèze and melodies associated with Louis Bourgeois; its tunes, such as "Old 100th," spread across Europe. In English, the Sternhold and Hopkins psalter (1562) — the "Old Version" — was bound with the Book of Common Prayer and used for over a century. The Scottish Psalter of 1650, a revision noted for relative fidelity to the Hebrew, remained in use in Presbyterian churches for centuries and is still sung in some congregations. The first book printed in British North America was the Bay Psalm Book (1640), a metrical psalter produced in Massachusetts.

Characteristics

Most metrical psalms are written in common metre (8.6.8.6 syllables), short metre, or long metre, allowing any psalm in a given metre to be sung to any tune of that metre. Later supplements added "paraphrases" of other scriptural passages.
