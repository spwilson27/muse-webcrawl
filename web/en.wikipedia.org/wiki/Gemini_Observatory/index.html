Gemini Observatory

TelescopesGemini North (Mauna Kea, Hawaii); Gemini South (Cerro Pachón, Chile)
Aperture8.1 m (each)
First light1999 (North), 2000 (South)
OperatorNOIRLab (NSF), international partnership

The Gemini Observatory is an astronomical observatory consisting of two identical 8.1-metre telescopes: Gemini North on Mauna Kea in Hawaii, and Gemini South on Cerro Pachón in the Chilean Andes. Together the twin telescopes cover almost the entire sky in both hemispheres. They are operated by an international partnership coordinated through the US National Science Foundation's NOIRLab.

Telescopes and instruments

Each telescope uses a thin meniscus primary mirror of 8.1 m diameter with active optics, an alt-azimuth mount and a silver-coated mirror optimised for infrared performance, housed in a ventilated enclosure designed to minimise dome seeing. Instruments have included the Gemini Multi-Object Spectrographs (GMOS), near-infrared imagers and spectrographs such as NIRI and FLAMINGOS-2, the high-resolution spectrograph GHOST, and the Gemini Planet Imager (GPI), which directly imaged exoplanets. Multi-conjugate adaptive optics (GeMS at Gemini South) delivers near-diffraction-limited infrared images over wide fields.

Operations and science

Gemini pioneered queue-based observing, in which staff execute programmes matched to weather conditions, and fast-turnaround and "bring one, get one" modes for rapid follow-up. The observatory is a key facility for follow-up of gravitational-wave and transient events, exoplanet imaging, and studies of distant galaxies. It was also the source of the first direct image of a multi-planet system (HR 8799, with Keck) and participates in time-domain astronomy networks such as the AEON programme.
