A USB flash drive (also called a thumb drive, pen drive, or memory stick) is a data storage device that includes flash memory with an integrated USB interface. It is typically removable, rewritable, and much smaller than an optical disc, usually weighing less than 30 grams.

History

The first commercial USB flash drives appeared in 2000, with capacities of 8 megabytes — more than five times the capacity of a common floppy disk. Singapore-based Trek 2000 International and IBM both brought early products to market, and the invention was the subject of patent disputes involving the Israeli company M-Systems and others. Capacities grew rapidly, from megabytes to gigabytes within a few years, and by the 2010s drives of 256 GB and more were common; terabyte-class drives followed.

Technology

A flash drive consists of a small printed circuit board carrying a NAND flash memory chip, a USB mass storage controller, and a crystal oscillator, all housed in a plastic, metal, or rubberised casing. The device draws power from the host computer through the USB connection and requires no separate power supply. Because flash drives have no moving parts, they are durable and resistant to shock, scratches, and dust compared with magnetic and optical media.

Uses

USB flash drives are commonly used for storage, data transfer, and backup of data and computer files. They are also used to boot operating systems ("live USBs"), run portable applications, perform system administration and recovery, and transfer files between air-gapped systems. Their use has declined somewhat with the rise of cloud storage, but they remain ubiquitous.
