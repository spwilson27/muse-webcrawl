Anti-D

Anti-D refers to the antibody against the D antigen of the Rh blood group system. The D antigen is the most immunogenic of the Rh antigens, and individuals who lack it (RhD-negative) can produce anti-D after exposure to RhD-positive red blood cells, through transfusion or pregnancy.

Clinical significance

Anti-D is clinically the most important Rh antibody because it can cause haemolytic transfusion reactions and haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN). When an RhD-negative mother carries an RhD-positive fetus, fetal red cells entering the maternal circulation — particularly at delivery — can sensitise the mother. In subsequent pregnancies, maternal IgG anti-D crosses the placenta and destroys fetal red cells.

Prophylaxis

The introduction of anti-D immunoglobulin (anti-D Ig) prophylaxis in the 1960s dramatically reduced the incidence of RhD alloimmunisation. Anti-D Ig is a blood product prepared from pooled human plasma containing high titres of anti-D. It is administered to RhD-negative mothers antenatally (commonly around 28 weeks) and after delivery of an RhD-positive infant, as well as after potentially sensitising events such as miscarriage, amniocentesis or abdominal trauma. The exact mechanism is not fully understood but is thought to involve rapid clearance of fetal red cells and epitope masking.

Detection

Anti-D is detected in antibody screening of patients' plasma, typically by the indirect antiglobulin test. Its titre in pregnancy is monitored to assess the risk and severity of HDFN.
