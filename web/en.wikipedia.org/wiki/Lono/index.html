Lono

Lono
DomainFertility, agriculture, rainfall, peace
FestivalMakahiki
SymbolsCrosspiece banner (akua loa), white kapa cloth

In Hawaiian religion, Lono is one of the four major gods (together with Kū, Kāne, and Kanaloa), associated with fertility, agriculture, rainfall, music, and peace. He is also one of the most prominent akua in the Hawaiian pantheon, credited with bringing cultivated plants and with descending to earth on a rainbow to marry the mortal Kaikilani.

Makahiki festival

Lono is honoured during the annual Makahiki festival, a season of roughly four months beginning with the rising of the Pleiades (Makaliʻi) around October or November. During the Makahiki, war was forbidden (kapu), taxes (hoʻokupu) were collected for the god, and the people took part in sports, games, hula, and feasting. The god's image — a tall crosspiece draped with white kapa cloth, the akua loa — was carried clockwise around each island in a great procession.

Captain Cook

Lono is famously associated with the arrival of Captain James Cook in Hawaii in 1778–1779. Cook's ships reached the islands during the Makahiki season, and his circumnavigation of Hawaiʻi island and the sails of his ships were interpreted by some scholars as having led Hawaiians to receive him as an embodiment or representative of Lono. The extent to which Cook was genuinely regarded as the god has been the subject of a celebrated scholarly debate between Marshall Sahlins and Gananath Obeyesekere. Cook was killed at Kealakekua Bay in February 1779, shortly after departing and returning unexpectedly during what would normally have been the season of Kū.

Mythology

In myth, Lono descended on a rainbow and married the chiefess Kaikilani; hearing a woman resembling her admiring another man, he beat her in jealousy and, stricken with remorse, instituted boxing and wrestling games in her honour, which became part of the Makahiki. He was also associated with the clouds and storms that bring winter rains, the "Lono" clouds and rains (ua Lono) bearing his name.
