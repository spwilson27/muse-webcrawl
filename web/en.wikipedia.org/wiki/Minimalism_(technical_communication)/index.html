Minimalism (technical communication)

Minimalism in technical communication is an approach to designing documentation and training that focuses on the tasks users actually perform, presenting the least amount of explanatory material needed for them to act successfully. The approach was developed principally by John M. Carroll and colleagues at IBM in the 1980s, and is described in Carroll's book The Nurnberg Funnel: Designing Minimalist Instruction for Practical Computer Skill (1990).

Principles

Carroll proposed four design principles for minimalist instruction:

  1. Choose an action-oriented approach — anchor the documentation in the tasks users want to accomplish, and let them start acting almost immediately.
  2. Anchor the tool in the task domain — derive content from real work, using the user's own goals and situations.
  3. Support error recognition and recovery — anticipate common errors and give users the information needed to detect and correct them, treating mistakes as learning opportunities.
  4. Support reading to do, study, and locate — design material so that users can read selectively, studying in short episodes and locating needed information quickly.

Background

Minimalism arose from studies showing that learners typically do not read manuals systematically from cover to cover; instead they plunge into tasks, improvise, and consult documentation only when stuck. Carroll's "training wheels" interfaces and minimalist manuals at IBM's Watson Research Center led to faster learning and better retention than conventional systematic documentation.

Influence

Minimalist principles influenced later documentation methods, including JoAnn Hackos's work on minimalism in technical communication, topic-based authoring, and structured writing standards such as DITA, as well as research on heuristics for evaluating minimalist documentation.
