The Upper Silesia plebiscite was a referendum mandated by the Treaty of Versailles and held on 20 March 1921 to determine whether the industrial region of Upper Silesia would remain part of Germany or join the newly re-established Poland. Overall, about 59.6 percent of votes were cast for Germany and 40.4 percent for Poland, but the results varied sharply by district, and the vote was followed by the Third Silesian Uprising by Polish forces under Wojciech Korfanty in May 1921.

Background

Upper Silesia was one of Europe's great industrial regions, rich in coal and zinc, with a population mixed between German-speakers, Polish-speakers and bilingual Silesians. Both Germany and Poland claimed it at the Paris Peace Conference; a plebiscite was ordered, supervised by an Inter-Allied commission with French, British and Italian troops. Campaigning was intense and sometimes violent, accompanied by the First (1919) and Second (1920) Silesian Uprisings.

Result and partition

Although Germany won the overall vote, the League of Nations decided in October 1921 to partition the region. Poland received roughly 29 percent of the territory and 46 percent of the population, including the majority of the mines and industrial plant around Katowice and Königshütte (Chorzów), while Germany retained the western part including Oppeln (Opole) and Gleiwitz (Gliwice). A German–Polish convention signed at Geneva in 1922 regulated minority rights and economic relations in the divided region for fifteen years.

Consequences

The partition embittered German–Polish relations throughout the interwar period and was cited in German revisionist propaganda. After the Second World War, the whole of Upper Silesia was transferred to Poland and most of its German population was expelled.
