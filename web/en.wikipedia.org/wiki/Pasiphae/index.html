Pasiphae

In Greek mythology, Pasiphaë (Greek: Πασιφάη, "wide-shining", derived from πᾶσι (dative plural) "for all" and φάος/φῶς phaos/phos "light") was a queen of Crete. The daughter of Helios, the sun god, and the Oceanid nymph Perse, she was the sister of Circe and Aeëtes and the wife of King Minos. She is best known as the mother of the Minotaur.

Mythology

When Minos withheld from Poseidon a magnificent white bull meant for sacrifice, the god punished him by making Pasiphaë conceive an overwhelming desire for the bull. She enlisted the craftsman Daedalus, who built a hollow wooden cow covered with hide, in which she concealed herself so the bull would mate with her. The offspring was the Minotaur—Asterion—a man-eating monster with the head of a bull, for whom Daedalus constructed the Labyrinth at Knossos. Pasiphaë's other children by Minos included Ariadne, Androgeus, Phaedra, Deucalion and Glaucus. In some traditions she is also a sorceress like her sister Circe, and in Sparta she was worshipped as an oracular goddess, Pasiphaë being identified there with a moon deity.

Interpretation

Her name, "shining for all," and her parentage have suggested scholars a lunar goddess later demoted to a mortal queen in the developed myth. The story is told most fully by Apollodorus and Hyginus, and echoed by Virgil and Ovid.
