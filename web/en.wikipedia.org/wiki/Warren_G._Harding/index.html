Warren Gamaliel Harding (November 2, 1865 – August 2, 1923) was the 29th president of the United States, serving from 1921 until his death in 1923. A member of the Republican Party, he was one of the most popular sitting US presidents at his death, but a series of scandals — most infamously the Teapot Dome scandal — came to define his posthumous reputation, which has generally been ranked among the worst of American presidents.

Early life and career

Harding was born in Blooming Grove, Ohio, and settled in Marion, Ohio, where he bought the Marion Star newspaper and built it into a profitable business. He served in the Ohio State Senate and as lieutenant governor before election to the US Senate in 1914. At the deadlocked 1920 Republican National Convention, party leaders turned to Harding as a compromise candidate — the famous "smoke-filled room" — and he ran a "return to normalcy" front-porch campaign, winning the general election in a landslide over James M. Cox in the first presidential election in which women voted nationwide.

Presidency

Harding's administration appointed distinguished figures such as Charles Evans Hughes, Andrew Mellon and Herbert Hoover, but also cronies from his Ohio circle. His presidency saw the Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22, which limited naval armaments; budget and tax reforms; and the release of political prisoners such as Eugene Debs. Meanwhile, corruption festered among his appointees: Secretary of the Interior Albert Fall secretly leased naval petroleum reserves at Teapot Dome in exchange for bribes, and other officials looted the Veterans' Bureau and the Alien Property Custodian's office.

Death and legacy

In the summer of 1923, on a "Voyage of Understanding" to Alaska, Harding fell ill and died suddenly at the Palace Hotel in San Francisco on 2 August 1923, most likely of a heart attack complicated by pneumonia. He was succeeded by Vice President Calvin Coolidge. The unfolding scandals of 1924 cemented Harding's low historical standing, though some modern scholars credit his civil-liberties record, his support for anti-lynching legislation and his competent handling of the post-war economy.
