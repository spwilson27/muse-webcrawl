Wye (rail)

In railroad structures and rail terminology, a wye (like the letter "Y") or triangular junction is a triangular joining arrangement of three rail lines with a railroad switch (set of points) at each corner connecting to each incoming line. A turning wye is a specific case.

Uses

Wyes are used to connect two lines, allowing trains to proceed in either direction between them without reversing. A turning wye, which may occupy a considerable area of land, can be used to turn rolling stock — particularly locomotives — around, functioning like a turntable but without moving parts and using existing track geometry. Wyes are also common at the junction where a branch line meets a main line.

Geometry and operation

A wye consists of three legs joined by curves, each corner fitted with a switch. Turning a locomotive on a wye requires travelling around all three legs, with the direction of travel reversing on two of them. Because the radius of the curves can be made generous, wyes can turn large locomotives that would not fit on a turntable, but they require more land and multiple movements.

History

Triangular junctions have existed since the earliest days of railways. In Britain they are usually called triangular junctions; notable examples include junctions on the former Great Western Railway network. In North America, turning wyes were standard facilities at terminals and engine depots before the widespread adoption of bidirectional diesel locomotives.
