Felix Yusupov

Prince Felix Yusupov
Born: 23 March 1887, St Petersburg, Russian Empire
Died: 27 September 1967, Paris, France
Spouse: Princess Irina Alexandrovna

Prince Felix Felixovich Yusupov, Count Sumarokov-Elston (1887–1967) was a Russian aristocrat, heir to one of the empire's greatest fortunes, best known as a principal participant in the murder of Grigori Rasputin in December 1916.

Early life

Yusupov was the younger son of Princess Zinaida Yusupova and Count Felix Sumarokov-Elston. The Yusupov fortune — estates, mines, factories and art collections built up since the 16th century — was said to exceed that of the Romanovs themselves. After his elder brother Nikolai was killed in a duel in 1908, Felix became heir. He studied at Oxford (1909–1912), where he moved in flamboyant social circles, and in 1914 married Princess Irina Alexandrovna, niece of Tsar Nicholas II.

Murder of Rasputin

On the night of 29–30 December 1916 (New Style), Yusupov and co-conspirators including Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich and the monarchist deputy Vladimir Purishkevich lured Rasputin to the Yusupov Palace on the Moika in Petrograd. In Yusupov's later account, Rasputin survived poisoned cakes and wine, was shot, revived, and was finally shot again and drowned in the Malaya Nevka. The story, dramatised in Yusupov's memoirs, is now treated with scepticism by historians, but the killing itself is undisputed. Nicholas II exiled Yusupov to his estate and Dmitri to the Persian front — punishments that likely saved both from the Revolution.

Exile

The Yusupovs left Russia in 1919 aboard HMS Marlborough, taking jewels and two Rembrandts. In Paris they founded the fashion house Irfé (1924–1931). Yusupov won a notable libel suit against MGM in 1934 over the film Rasputin and the Empress, which implied Rasputin's murderer had raped the character based on Irina; the case is credited with prompting Hollywood's standard "all persons fictitious" disclaimer. He published memoirs, including Lost Splendor, and died in Paris in 1967.
