Paleogenetics

Paleogenetics is the study of the past through the examination of preserved genetic material from the remains of ancient organisms, including ancient DNA (aDNA) recovered from archaeological and paleontological specimens such as bones, teeth, hair, seeds and sediments. It is closely related to the broader field of archaeogenetics, which applies the methods to human populations and their migrations.

History

The field began in 1984 with the cloning of short DNA sequences from a museum specimen of the extinct quagga, followed by work on Egyptian mummies. Development was transformed by the polymerase chain reaction and then by high-throughput sequencing in the 2000s, which made it possible to assemble whole genomes from degraded fragments. In 2010 the team of Svante Pääbo published the draft Neanderthal genome and identified a previously unknown archaic group, the Denisovans, from a finger bone in Siberia. Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the sequencing of extinct hominin genomes.

Methods and challenges

Ancient DNA is fragmented, chemically damaged and easily overwhelmed by modern contamination, so work is performed in dedicated clean laboratories with stringent controls. Characteristic damage patterns — such as cytosine deamination at fragment ends — are used to authenticate sequences. Sedimentary DNA can recover genomes of organisms whose remains are absent, and by 2023 DNA up to about two million years old had been recovered from Greenland permafrost sediments.

Findings

Paleogenetics has rewritten accounts of human prehistory: it demonstrated interbreeding between early modern humans, Neanderthals and Denisovans; traced the mass migrations that brought steppe pastoralist ancestry into Europe and South Asia; recovered genomes of extinct animals including mammoths and the quagga; and identified ancient pathogens, including Yersinia pestis strains from Neolithic and Bronze Age victims of plague.
