Tau protein

The tau proteins (abbreviated from tubulin associated unit) form a group of six highly soluble protein isoforms produced by alternative splicing from the gene MAPT (microtubule-associated protein tau). They have roles primarily in maintaining the stability of microtubules in axons and are abundant in neurons of the central nervous system, though they are also expressed at lower levels elsewhere. Tau proteins are intrinsically disordered proteins that interact with tubulin to stabilize microtubules and promote tubulin assembly into microtubules.

When tau proteins are defective and fail to stabilize microtubules properly, pathologies of the nervous system can develop, collectively known as tauopathies, of which the most well known is Alzheimer's disease, where tau forms neurofibrillary tangles inside neurons.

Function

Tau promotes microtubule assembly and stability and may be involved in the establishment and maintenance of neuronal polarity. Its binding to microtubules is regulated by phosphorylation: kinases such as GSK-3β and CDK5 add phosphate groups that reduce tau's affinity for microtubules, while phosphatases such as PP2A remove them. Tau is found mainly in the axons of neurons, in contrast to MAP2, which is found in dendrites.

Isoforms

Alternative splicing of exons 2, 3 and 10 of the MAPT transcript produces six tau isoforms in the adult human brain, differing in the number of N-terminal inserts (0N, 1N, 2N) and in whether they have three (3R) or four (4R) C-terminal repeat domains that bind microtubules. The ratio of 3R to 4R tau is tightly regulated, and its disruption features in several tauopathies.

Clinical significance

Hyperphosphorylated tau is the main component of the paired helical filaments that form neurofibrillary tangles in Alzheimer's disease. Tau pathology also defines frontotemporal dementia with parkinsonism linked to chromosome 17 (FTDP-17), caused by mutations in MAPT, as well as progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal degeneration, Pick's disease, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy. Tau PET imaging and fluid biomarkers (such as phosphorylated tau species p-tau181 and p-tau217 in blood and cerebrospinal fluid) have become important diagnostic and research tools. Tau was first identified in 1975 by Marc Kirschner and colleagues as a factor required for microtubule assembly.
