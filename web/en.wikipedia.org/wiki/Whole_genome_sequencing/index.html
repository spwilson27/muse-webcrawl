Whole genome sequencing

Whole genome sequencing (WGS), also known as full genome sequencing or complete genome sequencing, is the process of determining the entirety, or near-entirety, of the DNA sequence of an organism's genome in a single laboratory procedure. It contrasts with targeted approaches such as whole exome sequencing, which sequences only protein-coding regions, and with genotyping arrays, which assay preselected positions.

History

The first complete genome of an organism to be sequenced was that of bacteriophage φX174 (5,386 base pairs) by Frederick Sanger and colleagues in 1977, using the chain-termination method Sanger developed. The first free-living organism, the bacterium Haemophilus influenzae (1.8 million base pairs), was sequenced in 1995 by a team led by J. Craig Venter using whole-genome shotgun sequencing. The Human Genome Project, completed in draft form in 2001 and declared complete in 2003, produced the first human genome at a cost of roughly US$3 billion over thirteen years; a complete telomere-to-telomere human assembly followed in 2022.

Methods

Modern WGS typically involves extracting DNA, fragmenting it, preparing sequencing libraries, and reading the fragments on a high-throughput platform. Short-read technologies, dominated by Illumina sequencing-by-synthesis, generate reads of 100–300 base pairs at very low cost per base but struggle with repetitive regions. Long-read technologies — Pacific Biosciences single-molecule real-time sequencing and Oxford Nanopore sequencing — produce reads of tens of thousands of bases, enabling de novo assembly of complex genomes and detection of large structural variants. Computational pipelines then align reads to a reference genome or assemble them, call variants (single-nucleotide variants, insertions/deletions, copy-number and structural variants), and annotate their effects.

Applications

  • Medicine: diagnosing rare genetic disorders, guiding cancer treatment by profiling tumour genomes, pharmacogenomics, and newborn screening.
  • Infectious disease: tracking outbreaks and antimicrobial resistance by sequencing pathogen genomes, as was done at scale during the COVID-19 pandemic.
  • Agriculture: breeding crops and livestock, and identifying genes for yield, quality and disease resistance.
  • Basic research and ancestry: evolutionary biology, population genetics, ancient DNA studies, and consumer genetic genealogy.

Cost

The cost of sequencing a human genome fell far faster than Moore's law after 2007 with the arrival of next-generation sequencing: from about $100 million in 2001 to around $10,000 by 2012, reaching the long-sought "$1,000 genome" in the mid-2010s and a few hundred dollars by the early 2020s.

Ethical and practical issues

WGS raises questions about incidental findings of disease risk, genetic privacy and discrimination, data storage at population scale, and the interpretation of variants of uncertain significance. Because a genome can reveal information about relatives as well as the individual, consent and data-governance frameworks remain active areas of policy debate.
