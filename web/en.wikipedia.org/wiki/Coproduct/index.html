Coproduct

In category theory, the coproduct, or categorical sum, of a family of objects is essentially the "least specific" object to which each object in the family admits a morphism. It is the category-theoretic dual notion to the categorical product, which means the definition is the same if all arrows are reversed in the definition of the product. Despite this seemingly innocuous difference of name, coproducts can differ dramatically from products in most examples.

Definition

Let C be a category and let X1 and X2 be objects of C. An object is called a coproduct of X1 and X2, written X1 ∐ X2, if there exist morphisms i1 : X1 → X1 ∐ X2 and i2 : X2 → X1 ∐ X2 satisfying the following universal property: for any object Y and any morphisms f1 : X1 → Y and f2 : X2 → Y, there exists a unique morphism f : X1 ∐ X2 → Y such that f1 = f ∘ i1 and f2 = f ∘ i2. That is, the diagram commutes.

The morphisms i1 and i2 are called the canonical injections or inclusion morphisms; the morphism f is known as the cotupling of f1 and f2, and may be denoted [f1, f2].

Examples

  • In the category of sets, the coproduct is the disjoint union: the canonical injections are the usual inclusion maps, and a pair of functions on the two parts is glued together to form a unique function on the union.
  • In the category of groups, the coproduct is the free product.
  • In the category of abelian groups and of modules over a ring, the coproduct (of a finite family) is the direct sum.
  • In the category of vector spaces, the coproduct is again the direct sum.
  • In the category of topological spaces, the coproduct is the disjoint union with its final topology.

Discussion

The coproduct construction is an example of a colimit in the sense that the coproduct of a family of objects is the colimit of the corresponding discrete diagram. Not all families of objects have a coproduct in every category: for example, in the category of finite sets a coproduct may fail to exist because the disjoint union of two finite sets need not be finite — in that category one requires the cardinalities to remain finite, which they do; but coproducts of infinitely many nonempty sets fail in categories of finite objects. More generally, coproducts may fail to exist when the "union" would be too large, such as in categories of sets bounded in cardinality.

If the coproduct of the empty family exists, it is an initial object of the category.
