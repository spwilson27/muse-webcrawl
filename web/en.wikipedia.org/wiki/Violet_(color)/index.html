Violet (color)

Violet is the colour of light at the short-wavelength end of the visible spectrum, between blue and invisible ultraviolet, with wavelengths of roughly 380–450 nanometres. It is one of the seven colours Isaac Newton assigned to the spectrum. In the RGB colour model used for screens, violet is produced by mixing blue and red light, though spectral violet itself lies beyond what such displays can reproduce exactly.

Violet and purple

Violet is frequently confused with purple. In colour science, violet is a spectral colour — a single wavelength of light — whereas purple is a non-spectral combination of red and blue. In everyday usage, however, "violet" often denotes any bluish-purple hue, including the colour of the violet flower (Viola), from which the name derives.

History and symbolism

Violet and purple pigments were historically costly: Tyrian purple, made from Mediterranean sea snails, clothed Roman emperors, and violet dyes long retained associations with royalty, luxury and power. In the 1850s the synthetic mauveine dye of William Henry Perkin made violet fashionable for a mass market. Violet is associated with spirituality and mourning in some cultures, is the liturgical colour of Advent and Lent in Western Christianity, and became a symbol of the women's suffrage movement (with white and green in Britain, and gold in the United States) and later of feminism.
