Qocho

Qocho (Old Uyghur: Qočo), also known as the Kingdom of Qocho or the Uyghur Idiqut state, was a Uyghur kingdom established in the Turpan region of present-day Xinjiang. Founded around 856 by Uyghur refugees fleeing the collapse of the Uyghur Khaganate in Mongolia, it endured for more than four centuries until it was absorbed into the Chagatai Khanate.

History

After the Kyrgyz destroyed the Uyghur Khaganate in 840, Uyghur groups migrated west and south. One group under Pang Tegin founded a state centred on the oasis city of Qocho (Chinese: Gaochang), with Beshbalik to the north serving as a summer capital. The kingdom controlled key Silk Road routes around the Taklamakan Desert and maintained diplomatic and tributary relations with Chinese dynasties, including the Song, while remaining effectively independent. In 1209 the Qocho ruler, the Idiqut, voluntarily submitted to Genghis Khan, and the Uyghurs became valued administrators and scribes of the Mongol Empire; the Mongolian script itself derived from the Uyghur alphabet.

Culture

Qocho was a flourishing centre of Buddhist art and literature, with a remarkable manuscript culture in Old Uyghur, Tocharian, Sogdian and Chinese. The cave temples of Bezeklik, near the capital, preserve vivid murals of donors and monks. Manichaeism, inherited from the khaganate period, gradually yielded to Buddhism, and there was also a notable Nestorian Christian presence. Uyghur translators rendered Buddhist sutras from Chinese and Tocharian, and the kingdom's agriculture — grapes, melons and cotton irrigated by karez underground channels — supported a prosperous oasis society.
