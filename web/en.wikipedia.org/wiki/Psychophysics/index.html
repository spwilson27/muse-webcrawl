Psychophysics

Psychophysics is the branch of psychology that quantitatively investigates the relationship between physical stimuli and the sensations and perceptions they produce. Founded by Gustav Theodor Fechner in the 19th century, it is one of the oldest areas of experimental psychology.

Key concepts

Central measures include the absolute threshold — the smallest detectable stimulus intensity — and the difference threshold or just-noticeable difference (JND). Ernst Heinrich Weber observed that the JND is a roughly constant fraction of the stimulus magnitude (Weber's law). Fechner proposed that sensation grows logarithmically with stimulus intensity (the Weber–Fechner law), while S. S. Stevens later proposed a power law relating perceived intensity to physical intensity.

Methods

Classical methods include the method of limits, the method of adjustment, and the method of constant stimuli. Modern psychophysics also uses adaptive staircase procedures and signal detection theory, which separates an observer's sensitivity (d′) from their response criterion. Two-alternative forced-choice tasks are widely used to minimize bias.

Applications

Psychophysical methods are used in audiology and ophthalmology for clinical testing, in ergonomics and product design, in measuring pain, and in evaluating display and audio technologies.
