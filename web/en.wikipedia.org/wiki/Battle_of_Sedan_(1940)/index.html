Battle of Sedan (1940)

The Battle of Sedan (12–15 May 1940) was the decisive breakthrough of the German invasion of France in the Second World War. German XIX Panzer Corps under Heinz Guderian crossed the river Meuse at Sedan, defended by second-rate French divisions of General Huntziger's Second Army. The collapse of the French front at Sedan opened the road to the English Channel and led within weeks to the fall of France.

Background

The German plan Fall Gelb, revised along the lines of Manstein's "sickle cut", placed the armoured Schwerpunkt with Army Group A through the Ardennes — terrain the French considered impassable for tanks. While the Allies advanced into Belgium under the Dyle Plan, seven panzer divisions crossed the Ardennes and reached the Meuse on 12 May.

The crossing

On 13 May, after a massive Luftwaffe bombardment that shattered the morale of the French 55th Division's artillery, assault infantry of the 1st, 2nd and 10th Panzer Divisions crossed the Meuse in rubber boats at Sedan, Glaire and Donchery. Engineers built pontoon bridges overnight, and by 14 May German armour was across in strength. French counterattacks at Bulson and Stonne were piecemeal and failed; the panic of Bulson contributed to the rout of the 55th Division. By 15 May the French Second Army's front was broken beyond repair.

Consequences

Guderian's corps turned west and raced for the Channel, reaching Abbeville on 20 May and cutting off the best Allied armies in Belgium. The Sedan breakthrough is a classic case study in shock, tempo and the psychological effects of dive-bombing on troops with inadequate anti-aircraft defence.
