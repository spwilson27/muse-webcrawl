Effective nuclear charge

The effective nuclear charge (often symbolised Zeff) is the net positive charge experienced by an electron in a polyelectronic atom. The term "effective" is used because the shielding effect of negatively charged inner electrons prevents outer electrons from experiencing the full nuclear charge of the nucleus, owing to the repelling effect of inner-layer electrons.

Calculation

The effective nuclear charge is calculated as Zeff = Z − S, where Z is the atomic number (the true number of protons in the nucleus) and S is the average number of electrons between the nucleus and the electron in question, known as the shielding or screening constant.

Slater's rules

A widely used semi-empirical method for estimating S is Slater's rules, devised by John C. Slater in 1930. Electrons are arranged in groups by principal and azimuthal quantum numbers; electrons in groups to the right contribute nothing, electrons in the same group contribute 0.35 each (0.30 for 1s), electrons in the shell below contribute 0.85 each (for s and p electrons), and electrons in shells two or more below contribute 1.00 each. For d and f electrons the coefficients differ.

Periodic trends

Effective nuclear charge increases across a period, because protons are added to the nucleus while electrons are added to the same shell and shield one another poorly. This explains the general decrease in atomic radius across a period. Down a group, Zeff experienced by valence electrons increases only slightly, since additional inner shells shield effectively, and atomic radius grows.
