Roman Jakobson

Roman Osipovich Jakobson (Russian: Рома́н О́сипович Якобсо́н; October 11 [O.S. September 29] 1896 – July 18, 1982) was a Russian-American linguist and literary theorist. A pioneer of structural linguistics, Jakobson was one of the most influential linguists of the twentieth century.

Life

Born in Moscow, Jakobson co-founded the Moscow Linguistic Circle while still a student, then moved to Prague in 1920, where he helped found the Prague Linguistic Circle and developed structural phonology with Nikolai Trubetzkoy. Fleeing the Nazi invasion, he reached the United States in 1941, teaching at Columbia, Harvard, and MIT.

Contributions

Jakobson's work on distinctive features in phonology, carried forward with Morris Halle in Fundamentals of Language (1956), reshaped the study of sound systems. His model of six functions of language (referential, emotive, conative, phatic, metalingual, and poetic) is a standard of communication theory, and his studies of aphasia linked linguistic oppositions — metaphor and metonymy — to brain disorders. He also made foundational contributions to Slavic studies, poetics, and semiotics, influencing Claude Lévi-Strauss and structuralism generally.
