Sir Edwin Chadwick (1800–1890)

Sir Edwin Chadwick KCB (24 January 1800 – 6 July 1890) was an English social reformer known for his work on the Poor Laws and for major improvements in sanitation and public health in the United Kingdom.

Career

Chadwick, a disciple and secretary of Jeremy Bentham, became a Poor Law commissioner and was the principal author of the 1834 report that produced the Poor Law Amendment Act, with its centralizing and deterrent workhouse system. His administrative energy and utilitarian faith in centralized inspection shaped Victorian government.

Sanitary reform

In 1842 Chadwick published the Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain, the landmark document of the sanitary movement. Drawing on evidence from local reports across the country, he linked disease to filth, contaminated water and poor drainage, and argued — in the language of the miasma theory of the day — that removing refuse and supplying clean water and sewers would reduce both disease and poor-relief costs. The report helped produce the Public Health Act 1848 and the General Board of Health, on which Chadwick served until 1854.

Later years and legacy

Chadwick's abrasive manner made him unpopular, and he was forced from office in 1854, but he continued to campaign on sanitation, policing, and education for the rest of his long life. He was knighted in 1889. Historians credit him as a founder of modern public health administration and of the idea that the state should provide clean water and sewerage.
