Pan-Islamism

Pan-Islamism (Arabic: الوحدة الإسلامية) is a political movement which advocates the unity of Muslims under one Islamic country or state — often a caliphate — or an international organization with Islamic principles. It emerged in the late 19th century in response to European colonial expansion into Muslim-majority lands, and differs from pan-nationalist ideologies such as Pan-Arabism in grounding unity in religion rather than ethnicity or language.

Origins

The intellectual roots of Pan-Islamism lie in the concept of the ummah, the worldwide community of Muslims. In the 19th century, the reformer Jamal al-Din al-Afghani travelled across the Muslim world urging unity against British and Russian imperialism and a revival combining Islamic identity with modern science. The Ottoman sultan Abdul Hamid II (reigned 1876–1909) promoted Pan-Islamic policies to bolster his claim to the caliphate and rally Muslims in British India, French North Africa, and Russian Central Asia to the Ottoman cause.

Twentieth century

The abolition of the Ottoman caliphate by the Turkish Republic in 1924 spurred movements to restore a caliphate, including congresses in Cairo and Jerusalem. In British India, the Khilafat movement (1919–1924) mobilized Muslims in defence of the Ottoman caliph. Later in the century, Pan-Islamic cooperation took institutional form in bodies such as the Muslim World League (founded 1962) and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (founded 1969), which coordinate among Muslim-majority states rather than seeking a single state. Various Islamist movements, from the Muslim Brotherhood to more militant groups, have also invoked pan-Islamic ideals, though with differing visions.
