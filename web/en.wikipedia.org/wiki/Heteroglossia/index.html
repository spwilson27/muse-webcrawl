Heteroglossia

Heteroglossia is the coexistence of distinct varieties within a single "language", reflecting differences of class, profession, generation, region and situation. The term was introduced by the Russian literary theorist Mikhail Bakhtin in his 1934 essay "Discourse in the Novel", where it is a central concept of his theory of the novel.

Bakhtin's concept

Bakhtin argued that language is never a unitary, neutral system: at any moment it is stratified into social dialects, professional jargons, generational speech, the languages of authority, fashion and the day. Every utterance participates in this diversity. The novel, for Bakhtin, is the genre that artistically organises heteroglossia — it lets characters and narrators speak in distinct social voices that dialogue with one another, in contrast to epic and lyric poetry, which he associated with monologic, unitary language. Heteroglossia is closely related to his concepts of dialogism, polyphony and the carnivalesque.

Influence

The concept has been widely taken up in literary criticism, sociolinguistics, anthropology and cultural studies, where it is used to analyse how texts encode social difference and competing ideologies within apparently single national languages.
