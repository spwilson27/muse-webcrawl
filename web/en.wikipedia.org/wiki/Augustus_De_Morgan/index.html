Augustus De Morgan

Died18 March 1871 (aged 64), London, England
Alma materTrinity College, Cambridge

Augustus De Morgan (27 June 1806 – 18 March 1871) was a British mathematician and logician. He formulated De Morgan's laws and was the first to give a rigorous treatment of mathematical induction. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern symbolic logic, reviving the subject after a long period of neglect and developing it in parallel with George Boole.

Early life and education

De Morgan was born in Madurai, India, where his father served with the East India Company, and lost the sight of his right eye shortly after birth. He was educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, graduating as fourth wrangler in 1827. Although unable to take a fellowship because he refused a theological test, he was appointed the first professor of mathematics at the newly founded University College London in 1828, at the age of 22.

Career

De Morgan taught at University College London from 1828 to 1831 and again from 1836 to 1866, resigning twice on matters of academic principle. He was a founding member and first president of the London Mathematical Society (1865). Among his students was Ada Lovelace, whom he tutored in mathematics and whose work on Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine he followed with interest.

Work

De Morgan's major works include Elements of Arithmetic (1830), Trigonometry and Double Algebra (1849), and Formal Logic (1847), in which he advanced the logic of relations and stated the laws that now bear his name: the negation of a conjunction is the disjunction of the negations, and vice versa — in modern notation, ¬(A ∧ B) = ¬A ∨ ¬B and ¬(A ∨ B) = ¬A ∧ ¬B.

He also investigated the four colour problem (and was the first to state it in print, in 1852), coined the term "mathematical induction", and collected curiosities of mathematical history in his posthumous A Budget of Paradoxes (1872), a survey of circle-squarers, perpetual-motion inventors, and other pseudoscientific claimants.

Personal life

He married Sophia Elizabeth Frend in 1837; their son George was also a mathematician. De Morgan took an interest in psychical research and wrote approvingly, if cautiously, about spiritualist phenomena, contributing a preface to an anonymous work on the subject in 1863.

Legacy

De Morgan's laws are fundamental to propositional logic, set theory, and computer science, where they are used daily in the simplification of Boolean expressions. The De Morgan Medal of the London Mathematical Society is named in his honour.
