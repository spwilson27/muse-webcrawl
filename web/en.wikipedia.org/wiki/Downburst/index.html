Downburst

Downburst
TypeThunderstorm downdraft phenomenon
SubtypesMicroburst (under 4 km), macroburst (over 4 km)

A downburst is a strong ground-level wind system that emanates from a point source above and blows radially — in straight lines in all directions from the point of contact with the ground. It is caused by a column of intensely cooled air descending from a thunderstorm, which accelerates downward and spreads out explosively on reaching the surface. The term was coined by meteorologist T. Theodore Fujita of the University of Chicago.

Formation

Downbursts form when rain-cooled or evaporation-cooled air within a storm becomes denser than its surroundings and sinks rapidly. Evaporation of rain into dry air below the cloud base (producing "dry" microbursts common over the western United States) and the weight of heavy precipitation and hail ("wet" microbursts) are the principal mechanisms. As the air descends it accelerates; on striking the ground it spreads outward, often curling into a vortex ring.

Microbursts and aviation

A downburst less than 4 kilometres (2.5 mi) across is a microburst. Microbursts are especially dangerous to aircraft on take-off and landing: an aeroplane flying through one first meets a strong headwind, then a powerful downdraft, then a tailwind that can rob it of lift within seconds. Several airliner crashes in the 1970s and 1980s — including Delta Air Lines Flight 191 at Dallas/Fort Worth in 1985 — were attributed to microburst wind shear, prompting the installation of Doppler radar and windshear warning systems at airports.

Damage

Downburst damage is often mistaken for tornado damage, but it has a distinctive divergent pattern: trees and debris are flattened outward in straight lines from a central point rather than twisted in a rotational pattern. Extreme downbursts can produce winds comparable to a strong tornado.
