Japanese Brazilians (Portuguese: nipo-brasileiros; Japanese: 日系ブラジル人, Nikkei Burajirujin) are Brazilian citizens of Japanese ancestry. Brazil is home to the largest population of ethnic Japanese outside Japan, estimated at between 1.5 and 2 million people, concentrated mainly in the states of São Paulo and Paraná.

History

Japanese immigration to Brazil began on June 18, 1908, when the ship Kasato Maru arrived at the port of Santos carrying 781 immigrants recruited to work on São Paulo's coffee plantations. Immigration grew through the 1920s and 1930s, when Japan promoted emigration and Brazil sought agricultural labour; by the start of the Second World War some 190,000 Japanese had settled in Brazil, many becoming independent farmers credited with introducing or expanding crops such as black pepper in Pará.

During the Vargas era and the Second World War the community faced restrictions on Japanese-language schools and newspapers, and conspiracy theories around the immigrants fuelled discrimination; after the war, the "Shindo Renmei" movement of immigrants who refused to accept Japan's defeat caused violent internal conflict. Immigration resumed in the 1950s, including industrial investment by Japanese firms.

Dekasegi movement

From the late 1980s, economic stagnation in Brazil and labour shortages in Japan led hundreds of thousands of Japanese Brazilians to move to Japan as dekasegi workers under a 1990 revision of Japanese immigration law favouring people of Japanese descent. The Brazilian community in Japan peaked at over 300,000 before declining after the 2008 financial crisis.

Culture

The Liberdade district of São Paulo, with its Japanese-style street lanterns and festivals, is the community's cultural heart. Japanese Brazilians have been prominent in Brazilian agriculture, medicine, politics, and the arts, and centenary celebrations of immigration in 2008 were attended by the then Crown Prince of Japan.
