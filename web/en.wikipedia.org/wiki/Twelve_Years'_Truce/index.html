Twelve Years' Truce

The Twelve Years' Truce was a ceasefire between the Habsburg rulers of Spain and the Spanish Netherlands and the Dutch Republic, signed in Antwerp on 9 April 1609 and lasting until 1621. It interrupted the Eighty Years' War and amounted to a de facto recognition of Dutch independence by Spain.

Background

By 1607 both sides were financially exhausted: Spain had declared bankruptcy, and the Dutch were strained by the costs of war, though their trade flourished. Negotiations, mediated by France and England, opened at The Hague in 1608, with Johan van Oldenbarnevelt leading the Dutch delegation and Ambrogio Spinola representing Brussels.

Terms and effects

The truce froze the military status quo: each side kept the territory it held, and Spain treated the Republic "in the manner of" a free state. Dutch trade expanded dramatically during the truce — the Dutch East India Company (VOC) extended its reach in Asia, and Amsterdam consolidated its position as Europe's commercial hub. At home, however, the truce years saw the bitter Remonstrant–Counter-Remonstrant religious conflict, ending in the Synod of Dort (1618–19) and the execution of Oldenbarnevelt in 1619.

Resumption of war

Spain, under renewed ambitions and with the Thirty Years' War beginning in 1618, declined to extend the truce on acceptable terms, and hostilities resumed in 1621. The war continued until the Peace of Münster of 1648 formally recognized Dutch independence.
