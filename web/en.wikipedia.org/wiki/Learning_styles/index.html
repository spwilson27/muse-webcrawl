Learning styles

Learning styles refer to a range of theories that aim to account for differences in individuals' learning. Many theories propose that all people can be classified according to a preferred "style" of learning, and that teaching matched to that style improves outcomes. The best-known framework is the VARK model, which divides learners into visual, auditory, read/write, and kinesthetic types; other schemes include Kolb's experiential learning styles and Gardner's multiple intelligences.

Popularity

The idea is extremely widespread in education: surveys consistently find that over 90% of teachers in many countries believe students learn better when taught in their preferred style. Learning-styles inventories are sold commercially and incorporated into teacher training worldwide.

Scientific assessment

Despite this popularity, the core claim — the "meshing hypothesis" that matching instruction to style improves learning — lacks credible empirical support. A major 2008 review commissioned by the journal Psychological Science in the Public Interest (Pashler and colleagues) found virtually no studies meeting basic methodological criteria that supported meshing, and several that contradicted it. Subsequent controlled studies and reviews have reached similar conclusions, and many psychologists and neuroscientists classify learning styles as a "neuromyth". Students do have preferences, and content often dictates the best modality — geography is inherently visual, music inherently auditory — but evidence does not show that individuals learn better when material is matched to a self-reported style.

Responses

Some proponents argue the research critiques only particular inventories or weak implementations, while others have shifted toward advocating varied, multimodal teaching for all students, which is widely considered good practice regardless of style theory.
