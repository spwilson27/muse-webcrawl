The Newfoundland is a large breed of working dog originating in Newfoundland in present-day Canada. They were used originally as working dogs for fishermen, hauling nets and lines from boats, retrieving lost items from the water, and rescuing people in danger of drowning. Newfoundlands are known for their giant size, intelligence, tremendous strength, calm and gentle disposition, and loyalty.

Characteristics

The breed has a heavy, water-resistant double coat, webbed feet, and a rudder-like tail, all of which make it an exceptionally strong swimmer. Males typically weigh 65–80 kg (143–180 lb), making them among the largest dog breeds. Despite their size, Newfoundlands are famed for a sweet, patient temperament, particularly around children — the writer J.M. Barrie based the fictional dog-nurse "Nana" in Peter Pan on a Landseer Newfoundland.

History

The breed developed on the island of Newfoundland from dogs brought by European fishermen from the 16th century onward. Two historic types existed: the greater St. John's dog, ancestor of the modern Newfoundland, and the lesser St. John's dog, ancestor of the Labrador Retriever. Famous Newfoundlands include Seaman, who accompanied the Lewis and Clark expedition, and the dog said to have rescued survivors from the wreck of the Ethie in 1919. Lord Byron's beloved Newfoundland Boatswain is commemorated by a famous epitaph at Newstead Abbey.

Work today

Newfoundlands still serve in water rescue: their powerful swimming ability is used by lifeguard organisations in several countries, and they have famously been trained to jump from helicopters into the sea to rescue swimmers.
