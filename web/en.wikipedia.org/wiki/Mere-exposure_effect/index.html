Mere-exposure effect

People tend to prefer things merely because they are familiar with them.

The mere-exposure effect (also called the familiarity principle) is a psychological phenomenon by which people tend to develop a preference for things merely because they are familiar with them. It was named and systematically studied by the social psychologist Robert Zajonc, whose 1968 monograph showed that repeated exposure to a novel stimulus — Chinese characters, faces, nonsense words — reliably increased subjects' liking for it.

Evidence

The effect has been demonstrated across stimuli (words, pictures, paintings, music) and species, and even with subliminal exposures too brief for conscious recognition — indeed, effects are sometimes stronger when exposures are not consciously noticed. Liking generally rises with exposure frequency up to a point and may decline with overexposure, producing an inverted-U; very intense or aversive initial encounters can reverse the effect, since repeated exposure to something disliked tends to increase dislike.

Mechanisms

Leading explanations appeal to perceptual fluency: repeated exposure makes a stimulus easier to process, and this fluency is misattributed to liking — an account developed by Piotr Winkielman and colleagues. Zajonc himself argued that preferences need no inferences, proposing that affective reactions can precede and bypass cognition ("preferences need no inferences"), a claim that sparked the Zajonc–Lazarus debate on the primacy of affect. Evolutionary accounts suggest familiarity signals safety: what has been encountered repeatedly without harm is probably benign.

Applications

The effect underlies much advertising practice — repetition builds brand preference — and helps explain the propinquity effect in interpersonal attraction, the appeal of oft-repeated music, and resistance to novel foods and ideas.
