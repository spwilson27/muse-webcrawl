Developmental disability

Community inclusion and early intervention are central supports

Developmental disability is a diverse group of chronic conditions, attributable to mental or physical impairments, that arise before adulthood — typically before the age of 22 in United States law — and are likely to continue indefinitely. Developmental disabilities affect areas such as language, learning, mobility, self-care and independent living.

Causes and examples

Causes include genetic conditions such as Down syndrome and fragile X syndrome, prenatal exposure to infections or toxins, complications of birth, and metabolic disorders. Conditions commonly classed as developmental disabilities include intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, autism spectrum disorder, epilepsy and sensory impairments arising in childhood. Several million people in the United States are estimated to have a developmental disability.

Support and services

Supports have shifted over the twentieth century from large residential institutions toward community-based living, early intervention, inclusive education, supported employment and self-advocacy. In the United States, the Developmental Disabilities Assistance and Bill of Rights Act underpins federal programmes, and the Supreme Court's 1999 decision in Olmstead v. L.C. affirmed the right of people with disabilities to live in the most integrated setting appropriate.

Terminology

Terminology varies between countries and over time; in the United Kingdom "learning disability" is used for many of the conditions described in the United States as intellectual and developmental disabilities. Advocacy movements emphasise person-first language and the social model of disability, which locates disadvantage in barriers and attitudes as much as in individual impairments.
