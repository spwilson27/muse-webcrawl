Sound absorption

Sound absorption refers to the process by which a material, structure or object takes in sound energy when sound waves are encountered, as opposed to reflecting the energy. Part of the absorbed energy is transformed into heat and part is transmitted through the absorbing body. The fraction of incident sound energy absorbed at a given frequency is expressed by the absorption coefficient, a value between 0 (total reflection) and 1 (total absorption).

Mechanisms

Porous absorbers such as mineral wool, fibreglass and open-cell foams dissipate sound energy as heat through friction as air moves within their pores; they are most effective at higher frequencies and when their thickness is a significant fraction of the wavelength. Panel (membrane) absorbers resonate at low frequencies, converting sound into mechanical vibration. Resonant absorbers, including Helmholtz resonators, target narrow frequency bands.

Measurement and ratings

Absorption coefficients are measured in a reverberation chamber according to standards such as ISO 354 and ASTM C423. A single-number summary, the Noise Reduction Coefficient (NRC), averages coefficients at 250, 500, 1000 and 2000 Hz. The total absorption of a room is expressed in sabins, after Wallace Clement Sabine, the founder of architectural acoustics.

Applications

Absorptive treatment controls reverberation in concert halls, classrooms, offices, recording studios and restaurants, improving speech intelligibility and listening comfort. Absorption is distinct from sound insulation: absorbing materials reduce sound within a space but are not, by themselves, effective barriers to sound passing between spaces.
