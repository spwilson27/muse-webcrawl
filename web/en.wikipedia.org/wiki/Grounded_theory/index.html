Grounded theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Grounded theory is a systematic methodology of qualitative research that involves the construction of theory through the analysis of data. Rather than beginning with a hypothesis, the researcher collects data — typically interviews, observations, and documents — and allows concepts and categories to "emerge" from it through iterative coding and comparison. The resulting theory is said to be "grounded" in the data.

Origins

The approach was developed by sociologists Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss, whose 1967 book The Discovery of Grounded Theory challenged the dominance of purely quantitative, hypothesis-testing research in sociology. Their collaboration grew out of a study of dying patients in hospitals, published as Awareness of Dying (1965).

Method

Central procedures include theoretical sampling (collecting data guided by emerging concepts rather than a fixed plan), the constant comparative method (comparing each new incident or interview with existing categories), and memo writing. Coding is usually described in stages: open coding identifies initial concepts; axial coding relates categories to one another (a Strauss and Corbin formulation); and selective coding integrates the analysis around a core category. Data collection continues until theoretical saturation — the point at which new data no longer add new properties to the categories.

Schools and debates

Glaser and Strauss later diverged. Glaser's "classic" grounded theory insists on strict emergence and rejects prescriptive coding procedures, while Strauss, with Juliet Corbin, developed a more structured approach in Basics of Qualitative Research (1990). Kathy Charmaz subsequently proposed constructivist grounded theory, which treats data and analysis as co-constructed by researcher and participants rather than discovered. The methodology is used across sociology, nursing, education, management, and information systems research.
