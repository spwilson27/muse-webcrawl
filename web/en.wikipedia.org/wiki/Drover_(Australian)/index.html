Drover (Australian)


A drover on the track with a mob of cattle.

In Australia, a drover is a person who moves livestock "on the hoof" over long distances, traditionally on horseback. Drovers drove large mobs of cattle or sheep from remote stations to markets, railheads or better pastures, journeys that could last many months and cover thousands of kilometres along recognised stock routes.

History

Droving developed as pastoralism spread into the arid interior in the nineteenth century. Famous routes include the Canning Stock Route in Western Australia, the Birdsville Track, the Strzelecki Track and the Murranji Track. Drovers worked with packhorses, a cook and camp, and hardy horses and dogs, facing drought, floods, crocodiles, poisonous plants and the challenge of keeping mobs together at night ("rushing" stampedes were a constant danger). Legendary drovers entered Australian folklore, among them Harry Redford ("Captain Starlight"), who drove a thousand stolen cattle from Queensland to South Australia in 1870, and women such as Edna Jessop, regarded as Australia's first female boss drover.

Culture

The drover is a central figure of the Australian bush legend, celebrated in Banjo Paterson's poetry, the song "Waltzing Matilda" (whose swagman echoes the travelling life), the novel and film The Shiralee, the film Australia (2008), and the paintings of the outback. The romance novel The Thorn Birds made "Drover" a household name internationally. Long-distance droving declined with road trains and trucking from the mid-twentieth century, though droving continues on the "long paddock" of travelling stock reserves, especially in drought years, and as heritage re-enactments.
