The Great Game was a rivalry between the 19th-century British and Russian Empires over influence in Central Asia, primarily in Afghanistan, Persia and Tibet. The phrase is often attributed to Captain Arthur Conolly of the East India Company and was popularised by Rudyard Kipling's 1901 novel Kim. The classic Great Game period is generally regarded as running from the Russo-Persian Treaty of 1813 to the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, which divided Persia into spheres of influence, recognised Afghanistan as a British sphere, and assigned Tibet to a neutral buffer status.

Origins

British fears centred on the security of India, the "jewel in the crown" of the empire. As Russia expanded southward — annexing the khanates of Khiva, Bukhara and Kokand between the 1840s and 1870s and reaching the borders of Afghanistan — British planners feared a Russian advance through the passes of the Hindu Kush, or Russian intrigue at the court of the Afghan emir. In response, Britain fought the First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842), which ended in the disastrous retreat from Kabul, and the Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880).

Explorers and agents

The rivalry was conducted as much by explorers, surveyors and intelligence officers as by armies. Figures such as William Moorcroft, Alexander Burnes, Arthur Conolly and Charles Stoddart — the latter two executed at Bukhara in 1842 — mapped the region, while the Survey of India's native "pundits" secretly surveyed routes into Tibet and Turkestan. On the Russian side, agents such as Jan Prosper Witkiewicz intrigued in Kabul and Herat.

Crisis points and settlement

The Panjdeh incident of 1885, when Russian and Afghan forces clashed, brought the empires close to war. The Durand Line of 1893 fixed the frontier between British India and Afghanistan, and the Pamir Boundary Commission of 1895 created the narrow Wakhan Corridor as a buffer. British expeditions to Tibet culminated in Francis Younghusband's 1904 mission to Lhasa. The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907, driven by mutual concern about Germany, formally ended the rivalry.

Legacy

The term has since been applied metaphorically to later struggles for influence in Central Asia, including the Soviet war in Afghanistan, post-2001 competition over pipelines and bases, and rivalry involving China — sometimes called the "New Great Game".
