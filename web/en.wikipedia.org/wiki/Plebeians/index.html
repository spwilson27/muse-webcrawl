Plebeians

In ancient Rome, the plebeians or plebs were the general body of free Roman citizens who were not members of the patrician aristocracy. Plebeians were, on average, less wealthy than patricians and initially excluded from high religious and political office, but they comprised the great majority of the population — farmers, artisans, merchants, soldiers, and, over time, many rich and powerful families of their own. The struggle between the orders, the centuries-long effort of the plebeians to win political equality, is known as the Conflict of the Orders.

Origins

The division between patricians and plebeians is as old as Roman tradition itself: the patricians claimed descent from the hundred senators appointed by Romulus, while the plebeians were the rest of the citizenry, including descendants of conquered and immigrated peoples. Early plebeians could not intermarry with patricians, hold the consulship, or sit in the priestly colleges, and they suffered under harsh laws of debt enforced by patrician magistrates whose interpretation of unwritten law was a standing grievance.

Conflict of the Orders

From 494 BC the plebeians developed their own organisation — the Plebeian Council (Concilium Plebis), elected tribunes of the plebs with sacrosanct veto power, and plebeian aediles — and repeatedly used the "secession of the plebs", a collective withdrawal from the city and its army, as leverage. Milestones in their long campaign included the publication of the Twelve Tables (449 BC), the legalisation of patrician–plebeian intermarriage by the Lex Canuleia (445 BC), the opening of the consulship by the Licinian-Sextian laws (367 BC), and finally the Lex Hortensia (287 BC), which made plebiscites binding on the whole people. By the late Republic, wealthy plebeian families had joined the patriciate in a mixed senatorial nobility, and "plebeian" survived chiefly as a social rather than legal distinction.

Later usage

Under the Empire the plebs urbana of Rome — the city crowd sustained in part by the free grain distribution (annona) — remained a political force courted by emperors. In modern usage, "pleb" and "plebeian" have become derogatory terms for a person of the common people, or for tastes and manners regarded as vulgar or uncultured, though the words are sometimes reclaimed with irony.
