Imitation of Life (1959 film)

Imitation of Life
Directed by Douglas Sirk
Starring Lana Turner, John Gavin, Sandra Dee, Susan Kohner, Juanita Moore
Released 1959
Studio: Universal Pictures

Imitation of Life is a 1959 American drama film directed by Douglas Sirk, produced by Ross Hunter, and released by Universal Pictures. It was Sirk's final Hollywood film and the most commercially successful of his career. The film stars Lana Turner and John Gavin, with Sandra Dee, Dan O'Herlihy, Susan Kohner, Robert Alda, and Juanita Moore in supporting roles. It is the second film adaptation of Fannie Hurst's 1933 novel of the same name; the first, directed by John M. Stahl, was released in 1934.

The storyline follows an aspiring white actress who takes in an African-American widow whose mixed-race daughter is desperate to pass for white. The film explores themes of race, gender, ambition, and class in 1950s America.

Plot

Struggling actress Lora Meredith (Lana Turner) meets Annie Johnson (Juanita Moore), a homeless Black woman, at Coney Island. Annie and her light-skinned daughter Sarah Jane come to live with Lora and her daughter Susie, Annie serving as housekeeper while Lora pursues Broadway success. Over a decade, Lora becomes a celebrated comic actress, but her ambition costs her relationships. Meanwhile Sarah Jane (Susan Kohner), ashamed of her heritage, repeatedly runs away to live as white, breaking her mother's heart. When Annie dies of a broken spirit, Sarah Jane, distraught, throws herself on her mother's coffin at a lavish Harlem funeral she had arranged—one of the most famous sequences in American melodrama, accompanied by Mahalia Jackson singing "Trouble of the World".

Reception and legacy

The film was a major box-office success and received two Academy Award nominations: Juanita Moore and Susan Kohner were both nominated for Best Supporting Actress. Though dismissed by some contemporary critics as a "woman's weepie", it was later reappraised as a masterpiece of Sirk's subversive melodrama style, using lush Technicolor surfaces to critique American racism, materialism, and the emptiness of success. In 2015, the film was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry by the Library of Congress as being "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant".
