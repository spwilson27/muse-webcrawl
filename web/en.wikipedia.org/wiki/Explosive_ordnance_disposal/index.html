Explosive ordnance disposal

Explosive ordnance disposal (EOD) is the detection, identification, evaluation, render-safe, recovery, and disposal of explosive ordnance — including unexploded military munitions (UXO), improvised explosive devices (IEDs), and dangerous ammunition and explosives. EOD work is performed by specially trained military units and police bomb squads; its practitioners are often called bomb disposal officers or, colloquially, bomb squad technicians.

History

Organised bomb disposal emerged during the Second World War, when Britain faced large numbers of unexploded German bombs during the Blitz; Royal Engineers bomb disposal sections and Royal Navy mine disposal teams developed techniques at great cost in lives. The United States established formal EOD schools during the war, and the field expanded through the Korean and Vietnam wars — including underwater mine and ordnance clearance — and became central to coalition operations in Iraq and Afghanistan, where IEDs were the leading cause of casualties.

Techniques

The first rule of EOD is to keep people away: cordons and evacuation precede any intervention. Diagnosis uses portable X-ray systems and reconnaissance, increasingly performed by remotely operated robots so that the operator need not approach the device. Render-safe procedures may involve disrupting the firing circuit with water-jet disruptors, or removing fuzes with remotely operated tools. Where safe removal is impossible, controlled detonation is carried out in place or at a demolition range. Technicians working close to devices wear heavy bomb suits of aramid armour with helmets, trading protection against blast and fragments for limited mobility.

EOD also covers humanitarian demining support, clearance of former battlefields and firing ranges, and underwater ordnance disposal by naval divers.
