The Krogh principle is a concept in comparative physiology stating that for any given biological problem there is an organism (or a few organisms) in which the problem can be most conveniently studied. It is named after the Danish zoophysiologist August Krogh, winner of the 1920 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

"For a large number of problems there will be some animal of choice, or a few such animals, on which it can be most conveniently studied." — August Krogh, 1929

Background

Krogh articulated the idea in his 1929 paper "The progress of physiology", arguing that progress in physiology depended on choosing the right experimental organism for each question. The term "Krogh principle" itself was coined and championed decades later by Nobel laureate Hans Krebs (1975) and elaborated by Ewald Weibel and others.

Examples

Classic applications include using the giant axon of the squid to study the nerve action potential (work recognised by the 1963 Nobel Prize to Hodgkin and Huxley), the desert-adapted kangaroo rat for renal water conservation, the hummingbird for hovering-flight metabolism, and extremophile microbes such as Thermus aquaticus, whose heat-stable DNA polymerase made PCR possible. The principle underlies the broader concept of model organisms in biology, while adding the point that the ideal model depends on the question asked — not only on convenience of husbandry.
