The Gulag Archipelago (Russian: Архипелаг ГУЛАГ, Arkhipelag GULAG) is a three-volume non-fiction series written between 1958 and 1968 by Russian writer Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, a former prisoner of the Soviet forced-labour camp system. Published in Paris in December 1973 after the manuscript was smuggled out of the Soviet Union, it documents the Gulag — the network of camps run by the Soviet secret police — from 1918 to 1956, drawing on the author's own experiences and the testimony of 227 fellow former prisoners ("zeks").

The Russian word arkhipelag (archipelago) compares the scattered camps to a chain of islands across the sea of Soviet society, and "Gulag" is the acronym for the Main Administration of Camps. Solzhenitsyn subtitled the work "An Experiment in Literary Investigation".

Background and publication

Solzhenitsyn was arrested in 1945 for criticising Stalin in private letters and spent eight years in camps and three in internal exile. After the success of his novella One Day in the Life of Ivan Denisovich (1962), witnesses began sending him their accounts. When the KGB seized a copy of the manuscript in 1973 — and the typist Elizaveta Voronyanskaya hanged herself after interrogation — Solzhenitsyn authorised the long-prepared Paris publication. He was arrested in February 1974, stripped of Soviet citizenship and deported to West Germany.

Contents

The work moves from arrest, interrogation and transport — the "sewage disposal system" of the camps — through the daily life, labour, punishment and resistance of the prisoners, to the system's history from Lenin's first decrees and the Solovki camp through the Great Terror and the post-Stalin years. It is as much a moral and philosophical meditation as a documentary history, famously concluding: "the line dividing good and evil cuts through the heart of every human being."

Impact

The book profoundly changed Western perceptions of the Soviet Union, particularly among the French and European left, and made "Gulag" a universal term for a repressive camp system. It was not legally published in the Soviet Union until 1990. In 2009 an abridged edition became required reading in Russian schools. The first English translation, by Thomas P. Whitney, appeared in 1974; an authorised one-volume abridgement was published in 1985.
