The Durand Line (Pashto: د ډیورنډ کرښه) is the 2,640-kilometre international land border between Afghanistan and Pakistan in South Asia. It was established in 1893 by agreement between Mortimer Durand, foreign secretary of British India, and Emir Abdur Rahman Khan of Afghanistan, marking the limits of their respective spheres of influence.

History

The line was drawn through the heartland of the Pashtun people, dividing ethnic Pashtun tribes and the territory of Baloch peoples. After the creation of Pakistan in 1947, Afghanistan became the only country to vote against Pakistan's admission to the United Nations, and Afghan governments have historically refused to recognise the Durand Line as a legitimate international border, claiming the Pashtun territories east of it. Pakistan regards the line as settled under international law as the successor state to British India.

Modern disputes

The border region has been central to insurgency and militancy; Pakistan has fenced most of the border since 2017, a project Afghanistan has protested. Clashes between Pakistani and Afghan (both republican and later Taliban) forces have occurred repeatedly, and the status of the line remains one of the main sources of tension between the two countries.
