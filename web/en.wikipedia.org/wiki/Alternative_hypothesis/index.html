Alternative hypothesis

In statistical hypothesis testing, the alternative hypothesis is one of the proposed propositions in the hypothesis test. In general the goal of a hypothesis test is to determine whether there is enough evidence to support a certain hypothesis about a population. The alternative hypothesis is generally denoted by H1 or Ha, and stands in contrast to the null hypothesis, denoted H0.

The alternative hypothesis states that there is an effect, a difference, or a relationship in the population — for example that a parameter differs from a specified value, or that two population means are unequal. The null hypothesis, by contrast, states that there is no effect or no difference. A test statistic computed from sample data is compared against the distribution it would have if the null hypothesis were true; if the data are sufficiently unlikely under the null, the null is rejected in favour of the alternative.

Types

An alternative hypothesis may be two-sided (or two-tailed), stating only that a parameter is not equal to the null value, or one-sided (one-tailed), stating that the parameter is greater than, or less than, the null value. The choice affects the rejection region of the test and must be made before examining the data. Alternatives may also be simple (specifying a single parameter value) or composite (specifying a range of values); most alternatives in practice are composite.

Role in testing

Rejecting the null hypothesis provides support for the alternative, but does not prove it; failure to reject the null likewise does not prove the null. The probabilities of the two possible errors are controlled by the significance level (Type I error: rejecting a true null) and the statistical power of the test (1 minus the probability of a Type II error: failing to reject a false null). The concept of a test framed around an alternative hypothesis is central to the Neyman–Pearson framework of hypothesis testing.

History

The explicit pairing of a null hypothesis with an alternative was developed in the 1920s and 1930s, principally by Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, building on the earlier significance-testing methods of Ronald Fisher. Their formulation emphasized choosing between two competing hypotheses with controlled long-run error rates, an approach that remains the basis of most applied statistical testing.
