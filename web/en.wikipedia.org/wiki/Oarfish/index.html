Oarfish

Oarfish are large, greatly elongated, pelagic lampriform fish belonging to the family Regalecidae. Found in temperate and tropical ocean waters worldwide, they live at depths from near the surface down to about 1,000 metres and are rarely observed alive.

FamilyRegalecidae
GeneraAgrostichthys, Regalecus
HabitatEpipelagic to bathypelagic zones, worldwide
DietKrill, small fish, squid, jellyfish

Description

The giant oarfish (Regalecus glesne) is the longest known bony fish: reliably measured specimens reach 8 metres, and unverified reports describe individuals of 11 metres or more. The body is ribbon-like, laterally compressed, and silvery, with a distinctive red dorsal fin running the entire length of the back. The first rays of the dorsal fin are greatly elongated into a crest, and the pelvic fins are reduced to single long, oar-shaped rays tipped with paddle-like expansions — the origin of the common name.

Oarfish lack scales and have a fragile, gelatinous body texture. They swim by undulating the long dorsal fin while holding the body straight, and have been filmed orienting themselves vertically in the water column, head up.

Behaviour and ecology

Oarfish feed on zooplankton, especially krill, as well as small fish and squid, straining food with specialised gill rakers. They are thought to spawn in the open ocean; their eggs and larvae are planktonic. Little is known of their lifespan or population status, since encounters are typically with dying or dead individuals washed ashore after storms.

In folklore

Because of their size, strange shape, and rare appearances at the surface, oarfish are strong candidates for some historical "sea serpent" sightings. In Japanese folklore the oarfish (ryūgū no tsukai, "messenger from the Sea God's Palace") was traditionally regarded as an omen of earthquakes, an association that attracts popular attention whenever strandings precede seismic events, though no scientific link has been established.
