Hydroxyl

In chemistry, a hydroxy or hydroxyl group is a functional group with the chemical formula –OH, composed of one oxygen atom covalently bonded to one hydrogen atom. In organic chemistry, alcohols and carboxylic acids contain one or more hydroxy groups. The anion OH−, called hydroxide, consists of a hydroxy group with an extra electron. The neutral radical •OH, called the hydroxyl radical, is highly reactive and important in atmospheric and combustion chemistry.

Hydroxy groups in organic chemistry

Hydroxy groups are strongly polar and form hydrogen bonds, which is why simple alcohols such as ethanol are miscible with water and why sugars (polyhydroxy compounds) are highly soluble. The presence of hydroxy groups raises boiling points relative to hydrocarbons of similar mass. Hydroxy groups occur in alcohols, phenols, enols, and carboxylic acids; in carbohydrates, they define much of the chemistry of cellulose and starch.

Hydroxide and basicity

In aqueous solution, bases release hydroxide ions, and pH above 7 corresponds to an excess of OH−. The autoionization of water establishes an equilibrium between hydronium and hydroxide ions, with Kw = 1.0 × 10−14 at 25 °C.

The hydroxyl radical

The hydroxyl radical (•OH) is one of the most reactive species in the atmosphere, often called its "detergent," because it oxidizes methane, carbon monoxide, volatile organic compounds, and many pollutants, controlling their lifetimes. It is generated photochemically, for example from ozone and water vapor in sunlight. In biology, hydroxyl radicals produced during oxidative stress damage DNA, proteins, and lipids. In astronomy, hydroxyl masers are widely observed in star-forming regions and the atmospheres of evolved stars, and the 18-cm lines of OH were among the first molecular radio lines detected from interstellar space (1963).
