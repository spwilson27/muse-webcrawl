Cori disease

Other names: Glycogen storage disease type III, Forbes disease
Cause: Deficiency of glycogen debranching enzyme (AGL gene)
Inheritance: Autosomal recessive

Cori disease (also known as glycogen storage disease type III or Forbes disease) is an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by a deficiency of the glycogen debranching enzyme (amylo-1,6-glucosidase), encoded by the AGL gene. Without this enzyme, glycogen can only be partially broken down, and abnormal glycogen with short outer branches (limit dextrin) accumulates in the liver, skeletal muscle and, in some subtypes, the heart.

Signs and symptoms

The disease usually presents in infancy or early childhood with hepatomegaly (enlarged liver), hypoglycemia, growth retardation, hyperlipidemia and elevated liver transaminases. In subtype IIIa, which involves both liver and muscle, progressive myopathy and sometimes cardiomyopathy develop later in life. As patients age, liver symptoms often improve, though cirrhosis and liver tumours can occur in some adults.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is suggested by clinical features and confirmed by enzyme assay or genetic testing for AGL mutations. There is no cure; management focuses on preventing hypoglycemia with frequent meals, a high-protein diet, and uncooked cornstarch therapy, along with monitoring of liver and cardiac health. The condition is named after Carl and Gerty Cori, who described the abnormal glycogen structure.
