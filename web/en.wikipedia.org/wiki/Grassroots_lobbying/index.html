Grassroots lobbying

Grassroots lobbying, also called indirect lobbying, is a form of advocacy in which an organisation attempts to influence legislation or public policy by mobilising the general public — the "grassroots" — to contact elected officials and voice support or opposition. It contrasts with direct lobbying, in which professional lobbyists communicate with decision-makers themselves.

Techniques

Grassroots lobbying campaigns typically ask supporters to write letters, send emails, make telephone calls, attend town hall meetings or sign petitions. Organisations supply talking points, sample messages and contact tools; modern campaigns use email lists, social media and dedicated advocacy software to generate large volumes of constituent contacts in a short period. Because legislators are most responsive to their own constituents, campaigns usually target the voters of specific districts whose representatives are undecided or sit on key committees.

Grassroots lobbying and the law

In the United States, grassroots lobbying is protected as petitioning under the First Amendment, but tax law draws distinctions for non-profit organisations: under the Internal Revenue Code, 501(c)(3) charities may devote only an insubstantial part of their activities to lobbying, and grassroots lobbying is treated as a separate, more tightly limited category under the expenditure test election of section 501(h). Disclosure laws in many jurisdictions focus mainly on direct lobbying, leaving grassroots campaigns less regulated.

Astroturfing

A manufactured campaign designed to look like spontaneous grassroots activity is called astroturfing, after the artificial turf brand AstroTurf. Astroturf campaigns are typically funded by corporations or advocacy groups and may use front organisations with citizen-sounding names, paid participants or automated messages. The practice is criticised as deceptive because it misrepresents the breadth and spontaneity of public support for a position.

Effectiveness

Research suggests that personalised, localised constituent contacts are considerably more effective than identical form messages, which legislative staff can identify and discount. Genuine grassroots movements — from the civil rights movement to campaigns for environmental regulation — have repeatedly shaped legislation, usually in combination with direct lobbying and public relations work by the sponsoring organisations.
