Audio plug-in

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audio plug-in is a software component that adds audio processing or sound generation to a host application, most commonly a digital audio workstation (DAW). Plug-ins take two broad forms: effects, which process existing audio (equalizers, compressors, reverbs, delays), and instruments, which generate sound from MIDI input (synthesizers, samplers, drum machines).

Formats

Plug-ins are built to standardized formats so they can run inside different hosts. Steinberg's VST (Virtual Studio Technology, introduced 1996; VST3 in 2008) is the most widespread format across Windows and macOS. Apple's Audio Units (AU) is native to macOS and iOS, Avid's AAX serves Pro Tools (succeeding RTAS and TDM), and the open-source LV2 and newer CLAP formats are used mainly on Linux and cross-platform software. Some plug-ins run partly on dedicated DSP hardware, as in Universal Audio's UAD platform.

Use in production

Plug-ins displaced racks of hardware processors in most studios by offering instant recall, automation of every parameter, multiple instances per project, and far lower cost. Entire mixes and film scores are now produced "in the box" with plug-in instruments and effects, including detailed emulations of vintage analog gear and orchestral sample libraries. Plug-in parameters can be automated over time and controlled from hardware control surfaces.
