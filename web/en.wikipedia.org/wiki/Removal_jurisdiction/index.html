In the United States, removal jurisdiction allows a defendant in a civil action filed in state court to transfer ("remove") the case to the federal district court for the district in which the state court sits, provided the federal court would have had original jurisdiction over the action. Removal is governed principally by 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441–1455 and is a purely statutory right; it does not exist in criminal cases brought by a state, except in narrow categories such as cases against federal officers.

Bases for removal

The most common grounds are federal question jurisdiction — claims arising under the Constitution, laws or treaties of the United States — and diversity of citizenship jurisdiction, where the parties are citizens of different states and the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000. Supplemental claims forming part of the same case or controversy may be removed together with a removable claim (28 U.S.C. § 1441(c)). Special removal statutes exist for, among others, federal officers (§ 1442), members of the armed forces, civil rights cases (§ 1443), and certain class actions under the Class Action Fairness Act.

Procedure

A defendant must file a notice of removal in the federal court within 30 days after service of the initial pleading (or an amended pleading making the case removable), and ordinarily all properly joined and served defendants must consent. A copy is filed with the state court, which then proceeds no further. The plaintiff may move to remand the case to state court; defects in removal procedure must be raised within 30 days, while lack of subject-matter jurisdiction can be raised at any time. The "forum defendant rule" (§ 1441(b)(2)) bars diversity removal if any properly joined defendant is a citizen of the forum state.

Rationale and debate

Removal, dating to the Judiciary Act of 1789, was designed partly to protect out-of-state and federal interests from local bias in state courts. Its scope remains debated in federalism and forum-shopping discussions, particularly over "snap removal" and fraudulent joinder doctrines.
