Bouguer anomaly

In geodesy and geophysics, the Bouguer anomaly (named after Pierre Bouguer) is a gravity anomaly corrected for the height at which it is measured and for the attraction of the terrain and of an idealised slab of rock between the measurement point and a reference level. After these corrections, remaining variations in measured gravity reflect lateral differences in the density of the Earth's crust and upper mantle below the station.

Corrections

To compute a Bouguer anomaly, the observed gravity value is reduced by applying, in sequence: a latitude correction for the reference ellipsoid; a free-air correction for elevation above sea level (about −0.3086 mGal per metre); a Bouguer correction for the gravitational attraction of the rock slab between the station and sea level, modelled as an infinite slab of typical crustal density 2.67 g/cm³; and a terrain correction accounting for nearby topography. The result is expressed in milligals (mGal).

Interpretation

Positive Bouguer anomalies indicate excess mass at depth, such as dense intrusive bodies or uplifted mantle beneath mid-ocean ridges, while strongly negative anomalies characterise mountain ranges, where low-density crustal "roots" extend into the mantle in accordance with isostasy. Bouguer anomaly maps are a standard tool in mineral and oil exploration, regional geology and the study of crustal structure, and are contrasted with free-air anomalies, which omit the slab correction and are preferred for satellite-altimetry studies of the ocean floor.
