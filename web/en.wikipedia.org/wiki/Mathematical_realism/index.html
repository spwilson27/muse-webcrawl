Mathematical realism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mathematical realism is the philosophical view that mathematical entities — numbers, sets, functions, spaces and so on — exist independently of human minds, languages and practices, and that mathematical statements are true or false according to how those entities are. It is a form of realism about abstract objects, and its most traditional version is mathematical Platonism, which holds that mathematical objects inhabit a non-spatiotemporal realm and that mathematicians discover, rather than invent, mathematical truths.

Arguments for realism

Realists commonly appeal to the indispensability argument, associated with W. V. O. Quine and Hilary Putnam: mathematics is indispensable to our best scientific theories, so we ought to believe in the mathematical entities those theories quantify over just as we believe in the physical entities they posit. Realists also point to the apparent objectivity of mathematics — its statements seem to have determinate truth values that constrain us — and to the convergence of mathematicians working independently on the same results, as evidence that mathematics describes something independent of us.

Problems and objections

The classic objection is the epistemological problem pressed by Paul Benacerraf: if mathematical objects are abstract and causally inert, it is hard to explain how embodied knowers could acquire knowledge of them. Anti-realists also question whether truth in mathematics needs reference to objects at all. Positions opposed to realism include nominalism (there are no abstract objects), formalism (mathematics is the manipulation of symbols according to rules), intuitionism/constructivism (mathematical truth is provability by construction), fictionalism (mathematical statements are like statements about fictional characters — useful but not literally true), and structuralism, which shifts attention from objects to the structures they instantiate and exists in both realist and anti-realist variants.

Notable realists

Kurt Gödel famously defended a Platonist view, comparing mathematical intuition to perception of a mind-independent reality. Many practising mathematicians describe themselves informally as Platonists in their working practice, whatever their considered philosophical position; Roger Penrose is a prominent contemporary defender of mathematical Platonism.
