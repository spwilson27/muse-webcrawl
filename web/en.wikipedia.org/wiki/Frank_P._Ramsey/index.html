Frank Plumpton Ramsey (22 February 1903 – 19 January 1930) was a British philosopher, mathematician and economist who made fundamental contributions to philosophy, mathematical logic, economics and the foundations of probability despite dying at the age of 26. He was a fellow of King's College, Cambridge, and a close friend of Ludwig Wittgenstein and John Maynard Keynes.

Early life and education

Ramsey was born in Cambridge, the son of Arthur Stanley Ramsey, a mathematician and president of Magdalene College. He was educated at Winchester College and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he graduated as Senior Wrangler in the Mathematical Tripos in 1923. His brilliance was evident early: at eighteen he had translated Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (with C. K. Ogden) and published a critical notice of it that impressed Wittgenstein himself.

Philosophy and logic

In "The Foundations of Mathematics" (1925), Ramsey simplified the theory of types of Principia Mathematica, distinguishing logical from semantic paradoxes and defending a version of logicism. His 1929 paper "On a Problem of Formal Logic" proved what is now called Ramsey's theorem, founding the branch of combinatorics known as Ramsey theory. Ramsey sentences, developed in his posthumous work, became an important tool in the philosophy of science for describing the content of scientific theories.

Probability

In "Truth and Probability" (written 1926, published posthumously), Ramsey criticised Keynes's logical theory of probability and developed the first subjective, Bayesian account: degrees of belief are measured by betting behaviour, and coherence requires that they obey the probability axioms — an early form of the Dutch book argument. This work anticipated modern decision theory and Bayesian statistics by decades.

Economics

Ramsey's two economics papers are classics. "A Contribution to the Theory of Taxation" (1927) derived the Ramsey rule for optimal commodity taxes, and "A Mathematical Theory of Saving" (1928) founded the modern theory of optimal economic growth, later developed as the Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans model. Keynes called the latter "one of the most remarkable contributions to mathematical economics ever made".

Death and legacy

Ramsey died in January 1930 following an abdominal operation, possibly complicated by jaundice contracted while swimming in the River Cam. His notes and unpublished manuscripts, edited by R. B. Braithwaite as The Foundations of Mathematics and Other Logical Essays (1931), influenced generations of philosophers. The Frank P. Ramsey Medal and the Ramsey Prize in economics commemorate his name.
