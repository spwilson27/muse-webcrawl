Competitive authoritarianism

Competitive authoritarianism is a type of hybrid regime in which formal democratic institutions exist and are widely viewed as the primary means of gaining power, but in which incumbents' abuse of the state places them at a significant advantage against their opponents. The term was developed by political scientists Steven Levitsky and Lucan A. Way, most fully in their 2010 book Competitive Authoritarianism: Hybrid Regimes After the Cold War.

Definition

In competitive authoritarian regimes, elections are regularly held and opposition parties compete — often vigorously — but the playing field is systematically uneven. Levitsky and Way identify four arenas of democratic contestation that incumbents skew in their favour:

  • The electoral arena — access to media and finance is unequal, electoral administration may be partisan, and fraud or voter suppression may occur, though elections remain real contests that incumbents can lose.
  • The legislative arena — legislatures exist and may debate and obstruct, but executives can often bypass or dominate them.
  • The judicial arena — courts are packed, pressured, or bypassed, limiting their ability to check the executive or protect the opposition.
  • The media — independent outlets exist but face harassment, ownership pressure, libel suits, and restricted access to state advertising and broadcasting licences.

The concept differs from full authoritarianism, where democratic institutions are façades and opposition cannot meaningfully compete, and from flawed democracies, where the playing field, while imperfect, is not systematically tilted by incumbent abuse.

Origins and outcomes

Levitsky and Way studied 35 regimes that were competitive authoritarian at some point between 1990 and 2008. They argued that outcomes — democratisation, stable competitive authoritarianism, or regression to full authoritarianism — depended chiefly on the strength of a country's linkage to the West (economic, social, and organisational ties that raise the cost of repression) and the incumbent's organisational power (the cohesion of the ruling party and coercive apparatus). Strong state and party organisations tended to sustain such regimes even under pressure, while high Western linkage with weak organisational power favoured democratisation.

Examples

Regimes frequently analysed as competitive authoritarian include Russia under Vladimir Putin in its earlier period, Venezuela under Hugo Chávez, Peru under Alberto Fujimori (often termed the concept's prototype), Zimbabwe under Robert Mugabe, and, in later Levitsky and Way writings, Hungary under Viktor Orbán. Scholars debate individual cases and whether some have crossed into full authoritarianism or electoral autocracy.
