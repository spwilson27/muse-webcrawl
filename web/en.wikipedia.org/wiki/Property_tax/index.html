Property tax

A property tax (or millage rate) is an ad valorem tax on the value of a property, usually levied on real estate by local governments. The tax is typically determined by multiplying the assessed value of the property by the applicable rate, and it is a principal source of revenue for municipalities, counties, and school districts in many countries, most prominently the United States, where it funds schools, police and fire services, roads, and other local public goods.

Property tax is the mainstay of local government finance in the US

Assessment

Tax authorities assess property values periodically, ideally at market value, though assessment ratios and caps vary widely. Owners may appeal assessments they consider inaccurate. Many jurisdictions grant exemptions or reduced rates for owner-occupied homes (homestead exemptions), agricultural land, places of worship, charities, and elderly or disabled owners. In the United States, rates are often expressed in mills, where one mill equals one dollar of tax per thousand dollars of assessed value.

Types of property

Property taxes may apply to:

  • Real property: land and buildings — the most common base.
  • Tangible personal property: vehicles, business equipment, and inventory, taxed in some jurisdictions.
  • Intangible property: stocks, bonds, and other financial assets, now rarely taxed.

Economics and criticism

Economists often regard property taxes, especially on land value, as relatively efficient because the supply of land is fixed and the tax does not discourage production; Henry George famously proposed a single tax on land. Criticisms include regressivity in practice (housing costs consume a larger share of low incomes), burdens on asset-rich but cash-poor owners such as retirees, volatility when assessments lag market swings, and — in the US — the reinforcement of local inequality because school funding tied to local property wealth varies enormously between districts. Similar recurrent taxes exist elsewhere under names such as council tax (United Kingdom, based on 1991 banded values), taxe foncière (France), and rates (parts of the Commonwealth).
