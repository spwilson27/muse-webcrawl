Alexander II of Russia

Alexander II (29 April 1818 – 13 March 1881) was Emperor of Russia from 1855 until his assassination in 1881. Known as Alexander the Liberator for his emancipation of the serfs in 1861 — the largest single act of liberation in nineteenth-century Europe — he carried through the Great Reforms of the judiciary, local government and army, expanded the empire in Central Asia and the Far East, sold Alaska to the United States (1867), and won the Russo-Turkish War (1877–1878) that freed Bulgaria. The failure of reform to satisfy either conservatives or revolutionaries ended with his murder by the People's Will after several attempts.

The Great Reforms

Defeat in the Crimean War exposed Russia's backwardness. Alexander told Moscow's nobles in 1856 that it was "better to abolish serfdom from above than to wait until it begins to abolish itself from below". The Emancipation Manifesto of 3 March 1861 freed some 23 million privately held serfs — who received land allotments but had to pay redemption dues for decades, through the commune. There followed elected zemstvos (1864), an independent judiciary with jury trials (1864), municipal reform, relaxed censorship and, under Dmitry Milyutin, universal conscription (1874).

Expansion and reaction

Abroad, the empire absorbed the Caucasus, Tashkent and Samarkand in Central Asia, and the Amur lands from China (1858–60; Vladivostok founded 1860), while selling distant Alaska. The Polish January Uprising (1863) was crushed, and after Karakozov's attempt on his life (1866) the reign turned conservative — yet revolutionary populism grew, and the People's Will (Narodnaya Volya) resolved to kill the Tsar.

Assassination

On 13 March 1881, returning to the Winter Palace, Alexander's carriage was bombed on the Catherine Canal embankment in Saint Petersburg; unhurt, he stepped out — and a second bomber, Ignacy Hryniewiecki, threw the fatal bomb. He died hours later. Ironically, that morning he had approved a proposal (the Loris-Melikov plan) for consultative national representation. His son Alexander III buried the plan and the reform era with him. The Church of the Saviour on Spilled Blood marks the spot.
