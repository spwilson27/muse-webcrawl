Search engine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A search engine results page with a query box and a list of results]

A web search engine is a software system designed to carry out web searches: it crawls the web, indexes content, and returns ranked results matching users' queries. Google Search, launched in 1998, dominates globally with around 90% market share; competitors include Microsoft Bing, Baidu (China), Yandex (Russia), and privacy-oriented DuckDuckGo.

Search engines operate in three stages: crawling (following links to discover pages), indexing (analysing and storing content in huge inverted indexes), and ranking (ordering results by relevance signals — keywords, link structure, freshness, location, and machine-learned quality estimates).

Ranking history

Early engines (Archie 1990 for FTP files; AltaVista 1995, Yahoo's directory) ranked mainly by keyword matching and were easily spammed. Google's PageRank (1998, Larry Page and Sergey Brin) scored pages by the links pointing to them — authority by citation — and, with cleaner design, displaced rivals within a few years.

Search engine optimisation (SEO) is the practice of tuning pages for ranking; engines counter manipulation with algorithm updates (Google's Panda 2011, Penguin 2012) and increasingly answer queries directly (featured snippets, AI-generated overviews from 2024).

Business

Search advertising — sponsored results sold by keyword auction — is among the most profitable businesses in history and funds the free consumer services of Google and Microsoft. Its market power is contested in antitrust cases, including the 2024 US ruling that Google illegally maintained a search monopoly, largely through default-placement payments.
