Base rate fallacy

The base rate fallacy, also called base rate neglect, is a cognitive error in which a person judges the probability of an outcome while ignoring the prior probability (base rate) of that outcome in the relevant population, relying instead on case-specific information.

Classic examples

If a disease affects 1 in 1,000 people and a test for it has a 5% false-positive rate, most people overestimate what a positive result means: testing 1,000 healthy people yields roughly 50 false positives, so a positive result is far more likely to be false than true. Similar reasoning errors appear in the famous "Linda problem" and in the Harvard Medical School study in which most physicians badly overestimated the predictive value of a positive screening test.

Psychology

Psychologists including Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman documented base-rate neglect in the 1970s, showing that vivid individuating information (such as a personality sketch) swamps statistical priors. Presenting information in natural frequencies ("10 out of 1,000") rather than percentages substantially reduces the error. Related courtroom errors include the prosecutor's fallacy, which confuses the probability of evidence given innocence with the probability of innocence given the evidence.
