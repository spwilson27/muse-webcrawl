A herding dog, also known as a stock dog or working dog, is a type of dog that is trained to herd livestock such as sheep, cattle and goats, or that belongs to a breed developed for that purpose. Herding breeds typically display a modified form of the predatory sequence of wild canids — eye, stalk and chase — with the final killing bite bred out or tightly controlled, allowing the dog to move animals by presence, posture and movement rather than by attack.

Herding styles

Two broad styles are recognised. Driving dogs work behind stock and push them forward, historically used to move animals along roads to market; many drovers' breeds also "heel" — nipping at the heels of cattle — and duck below kicks, a trait preserved in the low-slung corgis. Gathering dogs, typified by the Border Collie, run out wide around the flock, collect the animals and bring them back toward the handler, controlled at a distance by whistles and voice commands. The collie's intense fixed stare, called "eye", pressures sheep to move away.

Training

Herding ability is strongly heritable, and puppies from working lines often show instinctive interest in stock. Training builds on this instinct, teaching directional commands (such as "come-bye" and "away to me" in British sheepdog work), a reliable stop, and the ability to shed (separate) individual animals. Sheepdog trials test these skills competitively and remain a major venue for selecting breeding stock.

Breeds

Most kennel clubs group herding breeds in a pastoral or herding group. Notable herding breeds include the Border Collie, Australian Cattle Dog, Australian Shepherd, German Shepherd (originally a tending dog), Belgian Shepherd, Shetland Sheepdog, rough and smooth Collies, Bearded Collie, Old English Sheepdog, and the Pembroke and Cardigan Welsh Corgis. Livestock guardian dogs, by contrast, live full-time with the flock and protect it from predators; they are not herding dogs, though the two types often work alongside each other.
