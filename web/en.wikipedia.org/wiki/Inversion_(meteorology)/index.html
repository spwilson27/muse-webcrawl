Inversion (meteorology)

In meteorology, an inversion is a layer of the atmosphere in which temperature increases with height, deviating from the normal decrease of temperature with altitude. Because warm air overlies cooler, denser air, an inversion acts as a stable lid that suppresses vertical mixing and traps pollutants, fog, and sound near the ground.

Types

Radiation inversions form on clear, calm nights when the ground cools rapidly by radiating heat to space. Subsidence inversions occur in high-pressure systems where descending air warms as it is compressed, capping a cooler layer below. Frontal inversions arise where warm air overrides a wedge of cold air at a weather front, and marine inversions form where cool sea air undercuts warmer air over land.

Effects

Inversions can produce dense fog, frost, and severe air-pollution episodes by preventing smoke and exhaust from dispersing; notorious smogs in the Meuse Valley (1930), Donora (1948), and London (1952) were associated with strong inversions. They also cause anomalous radio propagation (ducting), mirage effects, and can make distant sounds unusually audible while distorting the apparent position of the setting sun.
