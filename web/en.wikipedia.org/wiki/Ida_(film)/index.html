Ida (film)

Ida is a 2013 Polish drama film directed by Paweł Pawlikowski and written by Pawlikowski and Rebecca Lenkiewicz. Set in Poland in 1962, it follows Anna, a young novice nun, who — before taking her vows — is told by her Mother Superior to visit her only surviving relative, her aunt Wanda, a former state prosecutor. Together they uncover the fate of Anna's parents, Polish Jews murdered during the German occupation. The film won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film, becoming the first Polish film to do so.

Plot

Anna, raised in a convent after being orphaned in infancy, learns from her aunt Wanda Gruz — a chain-smoking, hard-drinking judge once known as "Red Wanda" for her role in Stalinist show trials — that she was born Ida Lebenstein, into a Jewish family. The two women travel to the village where the family hid during the war and confront the Polish tenant who sheltered, then murdered, them. They recover the remains of Ida's parents and her infant brother and rebury them in the family plot in Lublin. Exposed briefly to the world — jazz, alcohol, and a saxophonist's affection — Ida returns to the convent and, in the film's final shots, walks toward her vows.

Production and style

The film was shot in black and white in the 4:3 Academy ratio, with characters frequently placed low in the frame beneath large expanses of empty space, a style influenced by the Polish cinema of the 1960s and by still photography. Newcomer Agata Trzebuchowska was cast as Ida after being spotted in a Warsaw café; Agata Kulesza's performance as Wanda received wide acclaim.

Reception

Ida premiered at the Gdynia Film Festival, where it won the Golden Lions, and went on to win the BAFTA for Best Film Not in the English Language, five European Film Awards, and the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film at the 87th Academy Awards. Critics praised its austere cinematography and moral gravity. In Poland, the film also sparked political controversy, with some commentators objecting to its portrayal of Polish complicity in the Holocaust.
