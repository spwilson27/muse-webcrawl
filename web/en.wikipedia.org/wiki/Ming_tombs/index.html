Ming tombs

The Ming tombs are a collection of mausoleums built by the emperors of the Ming dynasty of China. The first Ming emperor's tomb is the Xiaoling Mausoleum near Nanjing, the dynasty's first capital; the main group of tombs, known as the Thirteen Tombs of the Ming dynasty, lies in a valley at the foot of the Tianshou Mountains in Changping District, about 42 kilometres north of central Beijing.

History

The site was chosen by the Yongle Emperor, who moved the capital to Beijing, following principles of feng shui: an arc of mountains protects the valley from malign winds from the north. Construction began in 1409 with the emperor's own tomb, the Changling. Thirteen of the sixteen Ming emperors were buried in the valley; the Jingtai Emperor was buried elsewhere after being deposed, and the dynasty's first emperor, Hongwu, and last, Chongzhen, were buried outside the main cluster (Chongzhen was interred in the tomb of his concubine within the valley).

Layout

The tombs share a common approach, the Sacred Way (Spirit Way), a ceremonial avenue lined with stone statues of officials, warriors, and animals real and mythical, leading from a memorial archway and the Great Red Gate to the individual tomb complexes. Each tomb consists of surface structures — gates, halls and a soul tower — fronting a circular burial mound beneath which lies an underground palace containing the coffins and burial goods.

Excavation and preservation

The Dingling, tomb of the Wanli Emperor, was excavated in 1956–1958, the only Ming tomb to have been scientifically excavated; thousands of artifacts were recovered, though many organic objects deteriorated after exposure. The tombs were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2003 as part of the "Imperial Tombs of the Ming and Qing Dynasties." Three tombs — Changling, Dingling and Zhaoling — are open to the public.
