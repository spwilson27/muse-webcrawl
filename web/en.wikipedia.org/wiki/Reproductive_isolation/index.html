Reproductive isolation

Reproductive isolation is the set of evolutionary mechanisms, behaviours and physiological processes that prevent members of different species from interbreeding, or ensure that any hybrid offspring are sterile or inviable. It is central to the biological species concept and to speciation, since it is the accumulation of isolating barriers that allows diverging populations to become separate species.

Prezygotic barriers

Barriers acting before fertilisation include: habitat isolation (populations occupy different habitats); temporal isolation (breeding at different times); behavioural (ethological) isolation (differences in courtship signals, as in the songs of crickets or the calls of frogs); mechanical isolation (incompatible reproductive structures); and gametic isolation (sperm and egg fail to fuse, common in externally fertilising marine organisms).

Postzygotic barriers

Barriers acting after a hybrid zygote forms include hybrid inviability (the hybrid fails to develop), hybrid sterility (as in the mule, the sterile offspring of a horse and a donkey) and hybrid breakdown (later generations lose fitness). Many postzygotic barriers arise from Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities between genes that diverged in separate lineages.

Evolution of isolation

Isolating barriers typically evolve as by-products of genetic divergence in allopatry, but selection can directly strengthen prezygotic barriers where hybrids are unfit — a process called reinforcement. Theodosius Dobzhansky and Ernst Mayr made reproductive isolation central to the modern evolutionary synthesis.
