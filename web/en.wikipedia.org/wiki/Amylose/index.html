Amylose is a polysaccharide made of α-D-glucose units, bonded to each other through α(1→4) glycosidic bonds. It is one of the two components of starch, making up approximately 20–30% of its structure, the other being amylopectin. Because of its tightly packed helical structure, amylose is more resistant to digestion than other starch molecules, and is therefore an important form of resistant starch.

Structure

Amylose consists of long, essentially unbranched chains of glucose residues, typically containing 300 to 3,000 units. In aqueous solution the chain adopts a helical conformation, with about six glucose residues per turn. Iodine molecules fit inside the helix, producing the characteristic deep blue-black colour used in the iodine test for starch.

Properties and uses

Amylose is less readily broken down by enzymes than the highly branched amylopectin, so high-amylose starches digest more slowly and have a lower glycaemic impact. On cooling, amylose chains re-associate (retrogradation), which is a major cause of bread staling. Industrially, amylose has been used in the production of biodegradable films and coatings because of its ability to form strong, flexible films.
