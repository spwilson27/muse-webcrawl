Douglas Hyde (Irish: Dubhghlas de hÍde; 17 January 1860 – 12 July 1949), known by the pen name An Craoibhín Aoibhinn ("the pleasant little branch"), was an Irish academic, linguist, scholar of the Irish language and politician who served as the first President of Ireland from June 1938 to June 1945. He was a leading figure in the Gaelic revival, and the first president of Conradh na Gaeilge (the Gaelic League), one of the most influential organisations of the Irish cultural revival.

Early life and scholarship

Hyde was born at Longford House, Castlerea, County Roscommon, the son of a Church of Ireland rector, and grew up at Frenchpark, where he learned Irish from local speakers and developed a lifelong passion for the language and its folklore. He studied at Trinity College Dublin, graduating in modern languages and later law, and became professor of modern languages at the University of New Brunswick in Canada before returning to Ireland. His collection Beside the Fire (1890) and his pioneering Love Songs of Connacht (1893), with facing Irish text and English translation, established his reputation as a folklorist and poet.

Gaelic League

In 1892 Hyde delivered the influential lecture "The Necessity for De-Anglicising Ireland", arguing that Ireland should resist the abandonment of its own language and customs. In 1893 he co-founded the Gaelic League with Eoin MacNeill and others, and served as its president until 1915, when he resigned over the organisation's increasing politicisation. He became the first professor of Modern Irish at University College Dublin in 1909.

Presidency

Chosen as an agreed, cross-party candidate, Hyde was inaugurated as the first President of Ireland on 25 June 1938 under the new Constitution of Ireland. He set many precedents for the office, remained scrupulously non-partisan, and suffered a severe stroke in 1940 from which he partly recovered. He retired to Ratra House in Frenchpark — his former family home, provided to him by the government — and died in Dublin in 1949.

Legacy

Hyde's work in collecting folklore and championing the Irish language made him a central figure of the Celtic Revival. His legacy includes the Douglas Hyde Gallery in Dublin and numerous schools and cultural institutions named after him.
