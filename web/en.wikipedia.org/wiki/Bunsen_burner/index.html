Bunsen burner

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Bunsen burner

A Bunsen burner, named after Robert Bunsen, is a kind of ambient air gas burner used as laboratory equipment; it produces a single open gas flame, and is used for heating, sterilisation, and combustion. The gas can be natural gas (mainly methane) or a liquefied petroleum gas such as propane or butane. The burner consists of a metal tube on a flat base with a gas inlet at the bottom, connected by rubber tubing to a gas tap.

History

The device was developed around 1855 at the University of Heidelberg, where Bunsen, the professor of chemistry, wanted a hot, clean, soot-free flame for his laboratory. His technician Peter Desaga built the burners to Bunsen's design, drawing on earlier lamps by Michael Faraday and others. Bunsen famously did not patent the design. The new flame made possible systematic flame tests for identifying elements, and Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff went on to use flame spectroscopy to discover the elements caesium (1860) and rubidium (1861).

Operation

Gas from a small jet draws in air through adjustable holes at the base of the tube by the Venturi effect. With the air holes closed, the gas mixes with air only at the top of the barrel, producing a cooler, luminous yellow "safety flame" of about 1,000 °C that deposits soot. With the holes open, the premixed flame burns with a pale blue, nearly invisible pair of cones and can reach roughly 1,500 °C at the tip of the inner cone, suitable for heating and for sterilising instruments. A needle valve or the collar over the air holes regulates the flame.

Variants and use

Related burners include the hotter Meker burner, with a grid at the top of the tube, and the Teclu burner, which adjusts gas flow with a screw. In modern teaching laboratories, electric heating mantles and hotplates have replaced Bunsen burners for many tasks, but the burner remains a standard tool for sterilisation in microbiology, glassworking, and flame tests.
