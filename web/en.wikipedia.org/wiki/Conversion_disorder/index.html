Conversion disorder

Conversion disorder, known in the DSM-5 as functional neurological symptom disorder, is a mental disorder in which a person experiences neurological symptoms — such as paralysis, blindness, numbness, or seizures — that cannot be explained by a medical or neurological condition and are inconsistent with known patterns of nervous system disease.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms may involve motor function (weakness, abnormal movements, gait disturbance, non-epileptic seizures) or the senses (loss of vision, hearing, or touch). The term "conversion" derives from Freud's theory that psychological anxiety is "converted" into physical symptoms; modern accounts instead frame the disorder in terms of abnormal brain network function without structural damage. Symptoms are real and involuntary, distinguishing the disorder from malingering and factitious disorder.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on positive clinical findings of internal inconsistency — for example, Hoover's sign in functional leg weakness, or a seizure with features incompatible with epilepsy — rather than merely on the absence of disease. The DSM-5 no longer requires identifying a preceding psychological stressor, though stress or trauma commonly precedes onset.

Treatment

Explanation of the diagnosis, physical therapy, and cognitive behavioral therapy are mainstays of treatment; many patients improve once the diagnosis is accepted and rehabilitation begins. The condition was historically called hysteria, a term now abandoned.
