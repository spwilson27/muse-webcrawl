Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures

Video-EEG monitoring, the gold standard for diagnosis

Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES), also known as dissociative seizures or functional seizures, are episodes resembling epileptic seizures but not caused by abnormal electrical discharges in the brain. They are a manifestation of conversion disorder (functional neurological symptom disorder) and are understood as involuntary responses to psychological distress rather than deliberately produced events.

Signs and symptoms

Events may involve convulsions, trembling, pelvic thrusting, side-to-side head movements, loss of responsiveness, or collapse. Features that suggest PNES over epilepsy include long duration, fluctuating course, closed eyes during the event, retained awareness during bilateral motor activity, and absence of postictal confusion, though none is individually decisive. PNES frequently coexists with anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and, in a minority of patients, with epilepsy itself.

Diagnosis

The gold standard is capturing a typical event on video-electroencephalography (video-EEG), showing no epileptiform activity before, during, or after the episode. Diagnosis is often delayed by years, during which patients may receive antiseizure medications that do not help and carry side effects. An estimated 20 to 30 percent of patients referred to epilepsy centres for drug-resistant seizures are ultimately found to have PNES.

Treatment

Treatment centres on clear, empathetic communication of the diagnosis, withdrawal of unnecessary antiseizure medication, and psychological therapy — cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence base. Prognosis varies; a substantial minority become event-free, particularly with early diagnosis and engagement in treatment.
