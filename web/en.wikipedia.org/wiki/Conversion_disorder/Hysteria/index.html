Hysteria

A patient photographed during Charcot's studies at the Salpêtrière

Hysteria is a historically used medical label for a set of symptoms that included emotional excess, and physical symptoms such as paralysis, convulsions, blindness, and loss of sensation without an identified organic cause. The term derives from the Greek hystera (ὑστέρα), meaning uterus, reflecting the ancient theory that the condition — long framed as afflicting women — was caused by disturbances of the womb.

History

Descriptions resembling hysteria appear in ancient Egyptian and Greek medicine. In the nineteenth century, Jean-Martin Charcot studied hysteria systematically at the Salpêtrière Hospital in Paris, treating it as a neurological disorder affecting both sexes. His student Sigmund Freud, with Josef Breuer, developed the theory that hysterical symptoms expressed repressed psychological trauma — published in Studies on Hysteria (1895) and foundational to psychoanalysis.

Modern understanding

The diagnosis of hysteria was removed from the DSM in 1980. Symptoms once gathered under it are now classified separately, principally as functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder), somatic symptom disorder, and dissociative disorders. The term survives mainly in historical and colloquial contexts, where it can denote uncontrollable emotion or excitement, including in "mass hysteria" (mass psychogenic illness).

Criticism

Historians and feminists have criticised the hysteria diagnosis as a vehicle for pathologising women's behaviour and distress, noting the gendered assumptions in its theories and treatments, from the ancient wandering womb to nineteenth-century rest cures criticised in Charlotte Perkins Gilman's "The Yellow Wallpaper" (1892).
