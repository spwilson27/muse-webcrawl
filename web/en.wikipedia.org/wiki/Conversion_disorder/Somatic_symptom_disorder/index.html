Somatic symptom disorder

Somatic symptom disorder, also known as somatoform disorder or somatization disorder, is a mental disorder characterized by significant focus on physical symptoms — such as pain, weakness, or shortness of breath — to such a degree that it causes major emotional distress and problems functioning. The individual has excessive thoughts, feelings and behaviors relating to the physical symptoms. The symptoms themselves may or may not be associated with another medical condition, and the diagnosis does not require that the symptoms be medically unexplained.

The diagnosis replaced several older categories in the DSM-5, including somatization disorder, undifferentiated somatoform disorder, hypochondriasis (in part), and pain disorder. It is related to, but distinct from, conversion disorder, in which neurological symptoms such as paralysis or blindness arise without a neurological cause.

Signs and symptoms

People with somatic symptom disorder have one or more chronic physical symptoms together with disproportionate thoughts about their seriousness, persistently high anxiety about health, and excessive time and energy devoted to the symptoms. Common complaints include pain, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms and dizziness. The symptoms cause significant distress or disruption of daily life and typically persist for more than six months.

Causes

The exact cause is not fully understood. Risk factors include a heightened sensitivity to bodily sensations, a tendency to interpret sensations as signs of illness, childhood adversity, and co-occurring anxiety or depression. Genetic and environmental factors are both thought to contribute.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on the presence of distressing somatic symptoms plus abnormal thoughts, feelings or behaviors in response to them. Unlike earlier definitions, the symptoms do not need to be medically unexplained; what matters is the person's excessive response. Clinicians must still rule out medical conditions that could account for the symptoms.

Treatment

Treatment centers on regular, reassuring contact with a trusted clinician, avoidance of unnecessary tests and procedures, and psychotherapy — particularly cognitive behavioral therapy — aimed at changing unhelpful thoughts and behaviors around symptoms. Antidepressant medication may help when anxiety or depression is present.
