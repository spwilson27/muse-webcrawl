Bhagavata Purana

The Bhagavata Purana (Sanskrit: भागवतपुराण), also known as the Srimad Bhagavatam, is one of Hinduism's eighteen Mahapuranas and the most celebrated and widely studied of them. Composed in Sanskrit, probably between the 8th and 10th centuries CE, it promotes bhakti (devotion) toward Vishnu in his avatar as Krishna. Its tenth book, recounting Krishna's childhood and youth in Vrindavan, is among the most beloved texts of Hindu devotional literature and has inspired centuries of poetry, dance-drama, painting and temple ritual.

Content

The text is framed as a narration by the sage Shuka to the dying King Parikshit over seven days. It covers cosmology, genealogy, the avatars of Vishnu, and the stories of devotees such as Prahlada and Dhruva. Book ten's accounts of Krishna's birth, his childhood pranks, the lifting of Govardhan Hill, and his dance with the gopis (the rasa lila) became foundational for Krishna bhakti traditions including Gaudiya Vaishnavism, in which the Bhagavatam holds a status comparable to scripture revealed directly by God.

Influence

The Bhagavata Purana has been translated into most Indian languages and repeatedly into English. It shaped the Bhakti movement, the theology of Vallabha and Chaitanya, performance traditions such as Kathakali, Manipuri dance and Bhagavata storytelling (Bhagavatam recitation weeks remain common in Kerala and elsewhere), and a vast corpus of miniature painting illustrating its episodes.
