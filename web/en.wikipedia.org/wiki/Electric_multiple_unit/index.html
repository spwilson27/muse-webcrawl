Electric multiple unit

An electric multiple unit or EMU is a multiple-unit train consisting of self-propelled carriages using electricity as the motive power. An EMU requires no separate locomotive, as electric traction motors are incorporated within one or a number of the carriages. An EMU is usually formed of two or more semi-permanently coupled carriages, but electrically powered single-unit railcars are also generally classed as EMUs. The great majority of EMUs are passenger trains, but there are also specialised carriages for carrying mail and parcels.

History

Multiple-unit control was pioneered in the 1890s, notably by Frank J. Sprague, whose system allowed several powered cars to be controlled from a single driving cab. This made possible the long, high-capacity trains of urban rapid-transit systems such as the New York City Subway and the elevated lines of Chicago, as well as intensive suburban services such as those of the Southern Railway in London.

Advantages

Because traction motors are distributed among several cars, EMUs accelerate quickly and give a smoother ride than locomotive-hauled trains. They need no locomotive run-round at termini, can be lengthened or shortened easily, and their electric motors require less maintenance than diesel engines. Regenerative braking is common on modern designs.

Types

EMUs are described by the number of cars per set and their formation: motor cars carry traction equipment, while trailer cars do not. Suburban EMUs typically have high-density seating and wide doors; intercity EMUs prioritise comfort and higher top speeds, as in Japan's Shinkansen, Germany's ICE and France's TGV-derived sets. Some EMUs are powered from overhead lines, others from a third rail; some are dual-voltage to cope with different supply systems.
