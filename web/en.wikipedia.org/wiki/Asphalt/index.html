Asphalt, also known as bitumen, is a sticky, black, highly viscous liquid or semi-solid form of petroleum. It may be found in natural deposits or obtained as the residue of petroleum refining. The primary use of asphalt is in road construction, where it is used as the glue or binder mixed with aggregate particles to create asphalt concrete; other main uses are for bituminous waterproofing products, including roofing felt and the sealing of flat roofs.

Composition and properties

Asphalt consists mainly of complex hydrocarbons, including polycyclic aromatic compounds, together with small amounts of sulphur, nitrogen and metals. It softens when heated and hardens as it cools, and it is practically insoluble in water. In British English "bitumen" is the standard term for the binder, while "asphalt" commonly refers to the mixture with aggregate; in American usage the terms are often interchanged.

History

Natural asphalt was used in the ancient Near East as mortar and waterproofing — Babylonian builders employed it, and the Dead Sea was known in antiquity as Lake Asphaltites for the blocks of bitumen that floated to its surface. Modern road asphalt dates from the 19th century: John Loudon McAdam's macadam and, later, the patented tarmac of E. Purnell Hooley (1901) paved the way for today's hot-mix asphalt, produced in vast quantities worldwide and extensively recycled.
