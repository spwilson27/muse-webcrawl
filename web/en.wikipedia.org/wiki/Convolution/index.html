Convolution

In mathematics (in particular, functional analysis), convolution is a mathematical operation on two functions (f and g) that produces a third function expressing how the shape of one is modified by the other. The term convolution refers to both the result function and to the process of computing it. It is defined as the integral of the product of the two functions after one is reversed and shifted:

(f ∗ g)(t) = ∫−∞∞ f(τ) g(t − τ) dτ

The convolution of f and g is written f ∗ g, using an asterisk or star. If one of the functions is regarded as an input signal and the other as the impulse response of a linear time-invariant system, the convolution gives the output of that system.

Properties

Convolution is commutative, associative, and distributive over addition, and it interacts simply with differentiation: the derivative of a convolution is the convolution of either function with the derivative of the other. The convolution theorem states that under suitable conditions the Fourier transform of a convolution of two functions is the pointwise product of their Fourier transforms. This makes convolution efficiently computable via the fast Fourier transform, and it underlies the fact that filtering in the time domain corresponds to multiplication in the frequency domain.

Discrete convolution

For sequences, the discrete convolution is defined by summing products of indexed terms. Discrete convolution is fundamental in digital signal processing, where it implements finite impulse response filters, and in probability, where the distribution of a sum of independent random variables is the convolution of their distributions.

Applications

Convolution is used in signal and image processing (blurring, sharpening, and edge detection by kernel convolution), in probability theory, in statistics (smoothing), in optics and acoustics, and in machine learning, where convolutional neural networks apply learned convolution kernels to images and other grid-like data.
