Conus medullaris

The conus medullaris (from Latin, "medullary cone") or conus terminalis is the tapered, lower end of the spinal cord. It occurs near the lumbar vertebral levels L1 and L2, tapering into the filum terminale, a delicate filament of connective tissue that anchors the cord to the coccyx.

Above the conus medullaris, the spinal cord continues as the lumbar enlargement (intumescentia lumbosacralis), which supplies nerves to the lower limbs. Below the conus, the spinal nerves branch off and form a bundle of nerve roots known as the cauda equina ("horse's tail"), which float within the cerebrospinal fluid of the lumbar cistern.

Clinical significance

Damage to the conus medullaris causes conus medullaris syndrome, characterized by symmetric saddle anesthesia, early and prominent bladder and bowel dysfunction, impotence, and relatively mild motor weakness of the lower limbs with preserved reflexes in some cases. It is distinguished from cauda equina syndrome, which tends to be asymmetric, with severe radicular pain and areflexia in affected roots.

Because the conus terminates above the level at which a lumbar puncture is typically performed (usually between L3 and L4, or L4 and L5), the procedure avoids direct injury to the spinal cord in adults; the needle passes between the mobile nerve roots of the cauda equina instead.

Development

In newborns, the conus medullaris terminates at a lower level, around L2–L3, and ascends relative to the vertebral column during growth as the spine elongates faster than the cord. In children, the level of termination is variable, which is considered when performing lumbar puncture in pediatric patients.
