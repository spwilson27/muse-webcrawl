Underground press

The underground press refers to independent, alternative newspapers and magazines produced outside mainstream publishing, most famously the network of radical papers that flourished in the United States, Britain and other Western countries during the counterculture of the 1960s and early 1970s.

History

The American movement is usually dated from the founding of the Los Angeles Free Press in 1964 and the Berkeley Barb and New York's East Village Other in 1965. Cheap offset printing let activists produce papers covering civil rights, opposition to the Vietnam War, drugs, rock music and sexual liberation in a style unbound by journalistic convention. The Underground Press Syndicate (1966, later the Liberation News Service milieu) let member papers freely reprint each other's material, and at their peak hundreds of papers reached millions of readers. In Britain the leading titles were International Times (1966) and Oz magazine, both targets of celebrated obscenity prosecutions.

Decline and legacy

Most underground papers folded by the mid-1970s as the counterculture waned and advertising and staff migrated to successor publications; some, like Boston's The Phoenix, evolved into the alternative weeklies. The underground press pioneered the aesthetics of psychedelic design and comics, nurtured writers and photographers, and is regarded as a forerunner of zines and independent media.
