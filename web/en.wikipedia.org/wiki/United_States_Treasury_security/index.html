United States Treasury securities are marketable and non-marketable debt obligations issued by the United States Department of the Treasury to finance government spending. Backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. government, they are widely regarded as among the safest investments in the world, and the market for them — with tens of trillions of dollars outstanding — is the largest and most liquid securities market globally. Treasury yields serve as benchmark interest rates throughout the financial system.

Marketable securities

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term securities with maturities of one year or less (4 to 52 weeks), sold at a discount and paying no periodic interest. Treasury notes pay interest semiannually and mature in 2, 3, 5, 7, or 10 years. Treasury bonds are long-term, maturing in 20 or 30 years. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) adjust principal with the Consumer Price Index. Floating Rate Notes (FRNs), introduced in 2014, pay interest that resets weekly against the 13-week bill rate.

Non-marketable securities

These include U.S. savings bonds (Series EE and I), State and Local Government Series securities, and Government Account Series held by federal trust funds such as Social Security.

Auctions and trading

Securities are sold through regular auctions conducted by the Treasury and the Federal Reserve Banks, in which primary dealers are obliged to bid. They trade actively in the secondary market, and interest is exempt from state and local (but not federal) income tax.
