Auto-da-fé

An auto-da-fé (from Portuguese auto da fé, "act of faith") was the public ceremony of the Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions at which the judgments of the tribunal were read and the penances or punishments of convicted heretics were announced and carried out. The phrase came popularly — though somewhat inaccurately — to denote the burning at the stake of condemned heretics, which in fact was executed by the secular authorities after the ceremony of "relaxation" to the secular arm.

The ceremony

Autos-da-fé were elaborate public spectacles, held in a town's main square on a feast day. They began with a procession of officials, clergy, and the penitents — who wore the sanbenito, a penitential garment painted with flames or devils according to the sentence — followed by Mass, sermons, the reading of the sentences one by one, abjurations, and the reconciliation of penitents. Those condemned to death were handed over to the secular authorities and executed, usually by burning, outside the ceremony itself; relapsed or unrepentant heretics who converted at the last moment might be garrotted before burning.

History

The first Spanish auto-da-fé was held in Seville in 1481, shortly after the founding of the Spanish Inquisition in 1478. The institution spread to Spanish America and Portuguese territories including Goa. Grand autos, such as that in Madrid's Plaza Mayor in 1680, became major state occasions, attended by royalty and depicted in famous paintings by Francisco Rizi. The practice declined through the 18th century; the last person executed following an auto-da-fé is generally taken to be Cayetano Ripoll, hanged in Valencia in 1826 for deism, and the Inquisition itself was abolished in 1834.

Legacy

The auto-da-fé has become a symbol of religious persecution and intolerance, and the term survives metaphorically in several languages for public acts of denunciation and punishment.
