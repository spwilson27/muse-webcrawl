Ecological restoration

Native planting on a restored prairie site

Ecological restoration is the practice of assisting the recovery of an ecosystem that has been degraded, damaged or destroyed. It aims to re-establish the structure, species composition and ecological functions of a historical or reference ecosystem, and is carried out in habitats ranging from prairies and wetlands to forests, rivers and coral reefs. The discipline emerged in the 20th century, with early landmark projects such as the University of Wisconsin–Madison Arboretum's prairie restorations begun in the 1930s under the influence of Aldo Leopold.

Practice

Restoration projects typically begin by removing the causes of degradation and invasive species, then may involve reshaping landforms, restoring hydrology, reintroducing native plants and animals, and long-term monitoring and adaptive management. Techniques range from passive natural regeneration — simply protecting a site — to intensive interventions such as prescribed burning, seeding, and rewilding with large herbivores or predators. The related concept of rewilding emphasises restoring self-sustaining ecological processes with minimal ongoing management.

Rationale and policy

Restoration is undertaken for biodiversity conservation, ecosystem services (such as water purification, pollination and flood control), carbon sequestration, and cultural values. The United Nations declared 2021–2030 the Decade on Ecosystem Restoration, and international targets under the Convention on Biological Diversity call for large areas of degraded land and water to be brought under restoration. Restoration ecology, the scientific study of restoration, debates questions such as the choice of reference states under climate change and whether restored sites can ever match intact ecosystems.
