Henry James

Henry James (15 April 1843 – 28 February 1916) was an American novelist and critic, regarded as one of the greatest novelists in the English language. The brother of philosopher William James, he spent most of his life in England, becoming a British subject in 1915, and his work bridges literary realism and early modernism through its intense focus on consciousness and point of view.

Work

James's fiction repeatedly stages the clash between innocent, democratic America and sophisticated, corrupt Europe — the "international theme" of Daisy Miller (1878), The Portrait of a Lady (1881), and the late masterpieces The Wings of the Dove (1902), The Ambassadors (1903), and The Golden Bowl (1904). Other major works include Washington Square, the novella The Turn of the Screw (1898), and The Bostonians. His prefaces to the New York Edition of his collected works remain foundational texts of the theory of the novel, and his dramatic late style, dense and oblique, profoundly influenced modernist writers.

Legacy

James died in London in 1916. He is celebrated as "the Master" for his innovations in narrative perspective and psychological depth.
