Medical malpractice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Medical malpractice is a legal cause of action that occurs when a medical or health-care professional, through a negligent act or omission, deviates from standards in their profession, thereby causing injury to a patient. The plaintiff must establish duty (the treatment relationship), breach (failure to meet the standard of care, usually proven by expert testimony), causation and damages. The standard of care is classically that of a reasonably competent practitioner in the field — in England the Bolam test (1957: practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion), tempered by Bolitho (1998: the opinion must withstand logical analysis), and by informed-consent doctrines (Canterbury v. Spence, US, 1972; Montgomery, UK, 2015).

Malpractice systems are perennially debated: studies estimate that only a small fraction of negligent injuries produce claims, while physicians complain of defensive medicine and insurance crises; reforms include caps on non-economic damages (California's MICRA, 1975, long the model), pre-suit screening panels, apology laws and no-fault alternatives (New Zealand's ACC; Nordic patient-compensation schemes; birth-injury funds). Empirical consensus: the liability system compensates unevenly but the threat of suit is a weak proxy for quality — safety research shifted focus to systems errors and disclosure-and-offer programmes.
