Automobile safety

Automobile safety is the study and practice of design, construction, equipment and regulation to minimise the occurrence and consequences of traffic collisions involving motor vehicles. Road traffic safety more broadly includes roadway design. One of the first formal academic studies into improving motor vehicle safety was by Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory of Buffalo, New York. The main conclusion of their extensive report is that the crucial difference in survival is the design of the interior — what came to be called the "second collision" between occupants and the vehicle interior.

Active and passive safety

Active safety systems help the driver avoid a crash: anti-lock braking systems (ABS), electronic stability control, traction control, good visibility, and increasingly driver-assistance features such as automatic emergency braking and lane-keeping assistance. Passive safety systems protect occupants when a crash happens: seat belts, airbags, crumple zones, safety cages, head restraints and laminated glass.

History

Early automobiles had few safety provisions. Milestones include the introduction of hydraulic brakes and safety glass in the 1920s and 1930s, Béla Barényi's crumple-zone patent and padded interiors in the 1950s, Volvo engineer Nils Bohlin's three-point seat belt in 1959 (made freely available to other manufacturers), and Ralph Nader's 1965 book Unsafe at Any Speed, which spurred US federal safety regulation and the creation of the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) in 1970.

Regulation and testing

Vehicle safety is governed by regulations such as the US Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards and UN/ECE regulations in Europe. Independent consumer programmes — Euro NCAP, the US NCAP and the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety — conduct crash tests and publish ratings that have driven improvements beyond legal minimums. Crash test dummies, instrumented anthropomorphic devices, measure the forces occupants would experience.

Statistics

Worldwide, around 1.19 million people die in road traffic crashes each year according to the World Health Organization, making them a leading cause of death for young people. Fatality rates per distance travelled have fallen dramatically in developed countries since the 1970s, owing to safer vehicles, road engineering, seat-belt and drink-driving laws, and improved trauma care.
