Transfer RNA

TypeNon-coding RNA
LengthTypically 76–90 nucleotides
FunctionDelivers amino acids to the ribosome during translation

Transfer ribonucleic acid (tRNA), formerly referred to as soluble RNA (sRNA), is an adaptor molecule composed of RNA, typically 76 to 90 nucleotides in length. It serves as the physical link between the genetic code in messenger RNA (mRNA) and the amino acid sequence of proteins: each tRNA carries a specific amino acid and recognises the corresponding codon on the mRNA.

Structure

tRNA folds, through internal base pairing, into a cloverleaf secondary structure with several arms, which in three dimensions folds further into an L shape. Key features are the acceptor stem ending in the conserved 3′ CCA tail, to which the amino acid is attached; the anticodon loop, whose three nucleotides pair with the mRNA codon; and modified bases such as pseudouridine and inosine, which fine-tune decoding. The amino acid is attached to its tRNA ("charging") by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, an enzyme whose accuracy is essential to faithful protein synthesis — the process Francis Crick anticipated with his "adaptor hypothesis".

Function in translation

At the ribosome, charged tRNAs enter the A site, where codon–anticodon matching is checked; the growing polypeptide is transferred to the new amino acid, and the ribosome translocates, moving the emptied tRNA through the P and E sites. Some anticodons pair flexibly with third-position codon bases — Crick's wobble hypothesis — so fewer tRNA species are needed than there are codons. Beyond translation, tRNAs also give rise to small regulatory fragments in many organisms.
