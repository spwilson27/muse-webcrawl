Passage tomb

A passage tomb or passage grave is a type of Neolithic burial monument consisting of one or more burial chambers covered by a mound of earth or stones (a cairn or tumulus), reached from the outside by a narrow, distinct access passage. Passage tombs are among the oldest surviving stone buildings in the world and are distributed around the Atlantic seaboard of Europe, especially in Ireland, Britain, Brittany, Iberia, and Scandinavia.

Characteristics

The chamber is usually built of large upright stones (orthostats) roofed with slabs or a corbelled vault, and the passage is long enough that mourners could enter without disturbing the mound. Many chambers are cruciform or polygonal in plan. Passage tombs are frequently decorated with megalithic art — spirals, lozenges, and cup marks pecked into the stones — as at Newgrange and Knowth in Ireland's Boyne Valley. Several famous examples are aligned on astronomical events: at Newgrange (c. 3200 BC), a roof-box admits the light of the rising sun around the winter solstice to illuminate the chamber floor; Maeshowe in Orkney is aligned on the setting sun at the solstice.

Passage tombs were built roughly between 4000 and 2500 BC, often in cemeteries on prominent hilltops. Excavated chambers typically contain the cremated or disarticulated remains of multiple individuals, accompanied by grave goods such as pottery, stone balls, and bone pins, suggesting use as communal or ancestral tombs over long periods. Their construction implies organised labour and considerable astronomical and engineering knowledge in early farming societies.
