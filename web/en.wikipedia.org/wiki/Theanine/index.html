Theanine

Theanine

Chemical name: γ-L-glutamylethylamide

Formula: C7H14N2O3

Molar mass: 174.20 g/mol

CAS number: 3081-61-6

Sources: Tea (Camellia sinensis), Xerocomus badius

Theanine, also known as L-theanine, is an amino acid analogue of the proteinogenic amino acids L-glutamate and L-glutamine. It is found primarily and uniquely in tea plants (Camellia sinensis) and in certain fungi, notably the bay bolete (Xerocomus badius). It was isolated from green tea leaves in 1949 by Yajiro Sakato, and in 1950 was identified as the constituent chiefly responsible for the savoury, brothy taste — a form of umami — of green tea, especially shade-grown teas such as gyokuro, which are rich in theanine.

Chemistry and biosynthesis

Theanine is structurally similar to glutamate, with an ethyl group replacing the hydroxyl of the side-chain amide. In the tea plant it is synthesised in the roots from glutamic acid and ethylamine by theanine synthetase, then transported to the leaves, where it can make up roughly 1–2% of the dry weight of tea. Sunlight converts theanine into catechins, which is why shading tea bushes before harvest increases theanine content.

Effects

Because of its structural similarity to glutamate, theanine interacts with glutamate receptors and is widely sold as a dietary supplement marketed for relaxation and "calm focus", often combined with caffeine. Studies have reported effects on alpha brain-wave activity and modest reductions in perceived stress, though health authorities such as the European Food Safety Authority have rejected several proposed health claims for insufficient evidence. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration treats theanine as generally recognised as safe (GRAS) for use in foods and beverages at limited levels.
