Child neglect is a form of child maltreatment in which a person or caregiver fails to provide the care, supervision, affection, and basic necessities needed for a child's health, safety, and well-being. It is the most common form of reported child abuse in many countries, including the United States, where it accounts for the majority of substantiated maltreatment cases.

Neglect involves a persistent failure to meet a child's basic physical and emotional needs.

Types

Neglect is commonly divided into categories: physical neglect (failure to provide food, clothing, shelter, or hygiene), medical neglect (failure to obtain needed medical or dental care), educational neglect (failure to enrol a child in school or attend to special educational needs), supervisory neglect (inadequate supervision for the child's age), and emotional neglect (failure to provide affection, attention, and psychological support). Abandonment is generally treated as a severe form of neglect.

Causes

Neglect rarely results from a single cause. Contributing factors include poverty and material hardship, caregiver depression and other mental-health problems, substance abuse, domestic violence, social isolation, and a caregiver's own history of maltreatment. Poverty alone is not neglect, but chronic deprivation increases risk, and researchers debate how far neglect statistics reflect poverty itself rather than harmful caregiving.

Effects

Chronic neglect, particularly in early childhood, is associated with impaired brain development, attachment disorders, cognitive and language delays, poor school performance, and long-term physical and mental-health problems. Severe neglect can cause failure to thrive and, in extreme cases, death. Studies of children raised in deprived institutions, such as the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, demonstrated profound developmental effects of early neglect and substantial recovery when children were placed in quality foster care early enough.

Prevention and intervention

Responses range from family-support services, home-visiting programmes such as the Nurse-Family Partnership, and parenting education, to child-protective-services investigation and, where a child cannot remain safely at home, foster care or kinship care. Mandatory reporting laws in many jurisdictions require professionals who work with children to report suspected neglect.
