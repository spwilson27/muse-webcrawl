Overview

A gluten-free diet is a diet that strictly excludes gluten, the protein composite in wheat (including spelt, durum and semolina), barley, rye and their hybrids. It is the only established treatment for coeliac disease, in which gluten triggers autoimmune damage to the small intestine, and is also required in wheat allergy and used by some with non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (a contested entity).

Practice and issues

Naturally gluten-free foods include rice, maize, potatoes, quinoa, buckwheat (despite the name), legumes, meat, fish, eggs, fruit and vegetables; manufactured substitutes replace bread, pasta and beer. The diet demands vigilance against cross-contamination and hidden gluten in sauces and processed foods, carries higher cost, and can be low in fibre and B-vitamins if built on refined substitutes. From the 2010s the diet became a mass consumer trend far beyond medical need — a market of billions of dollars — drawing both praise for awareness and criticism from dietitians. The Dutch physician Willem-Karel Dicke's wartime observations (the Dutch famine of 1944–45) founded the modern understanding of the diet's role in coeliac disease.
