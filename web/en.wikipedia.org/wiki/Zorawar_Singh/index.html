Zorawar Singh (1784 – 12 December 1841) was a military general of Dogra Rajput origin who served under Gulab Singh of Jammu, within the Sikh Empire. Sometimes called the "Napoleon of India" for his daring high-altitude campaigns, Zorawar Singh conquered Ladakh and Baltistan and led an invasion of western Tibet, greatly extending the frontiers of the Dogra state that later formed the core of the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir.

Early life

Zorawar Singh was born into a Hindu Dogra Rajput family in the village of Ansar in the Kangra hills. After a quarrel at home he left as a teenager and eventually entered the service of Gulab Singh, the Raja of Jammu, who recognised his military talent and entrusted him with expanding commands, eventually appointing him governor of frontier districts.

Conquest of Ladakh and Baltistan

Between 1834 and 1840, Zorawar Singh led a series of campaigns across the Himalayas into Ladakh, defeating the Ladakhi king's forces and annexing the region to Jammu. In 1840 his armies crossed difficult passes into Baltistan, defeating the ruler of Skardu and bringing the Balti country under Dogra control. His troops campaigned at altitudes of 4,000 metres and above, in winter temperatures far below freezing — remarkable feats of logistics and endurance.

Tibet campaign and death

In 1841 Zorawar Singh invaded western Tibet with several thousand men, overrunning the Tibetan garrisons at Rudok and Gartok and advancing toward Lake Manasarovar and the Taklakot area. A Tibetan-Qing counteroffensive met his depleted, cold-stricken army at the Battle of Toyo in December 1841, where Zorawar Singh was killed. His army retreated, and the subsequent war ended with the Treaty of Chushul (1842), which restored the traditional frontier. He is remembered as one of India's great mountain generals, and his legend endures in Ladakh and Jammu; a light tank developed by India in the 2020s was named "Zorawar" in his honour.
