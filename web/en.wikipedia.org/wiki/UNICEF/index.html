UNICEF

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

UNICEF, originally the United Nations International Children's Emergency Fund, is an agency of the United Nations responsible for providing humanitarian and developmental aid to children worldwide. Created by the General Assembly on 11 December 1946 to provide emergency food and healthcare to children in countries devastated by the Second World War, made permanent in 1953, it now works in over 190 countries and territories — the world's most recognised children's organisation, awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1965.

Work

UNICEF's programmes span immunisation (it procures roughly half the world's childhood vaccines by volume through its Copenhagen supply division), nutrition, water and sanitation, education in emergencies, child protection and social policy, funded almost entirely by voluntary contributions from governments and a mass individual-donor base cultivated through national committees and greeting-card-era brand fame. Its data operation (Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys; the State of the World's Children reports) supplies many of the child-mortality and development statistics the world argues with. Advocacy and operations coexist deliberately: the Convention on the Rights of the Child, whose implementation UNICEF supports and monitors, is both its mandate and its measuring rod, and shortfalls (funding appeals routinely half-met; access blocked in active wars) are reported in its own publications.
