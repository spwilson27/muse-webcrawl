Legnica (German: Liegnitz) is a city in southwestern Poland, in the Lower Silesian Voivodeship, situated on the Kaczawa River. It is one of the oldest and largest cities of Silesia, known for the Piast Castle, its medieval cathedral and its role in the Battle of Legnica of 1241.

History

A fortified settlement existed at Legnica from the early Middle Ages; it is first mentioned in written sources in 1004. The city was the seat of a line of Silesian Piast dukes. On 9 April 1241, at the nearby village of Legnickie Pole, a Polish and allied army under Duke Henry II the Pious was defeated by the invading Mongols; Henry was killed, and the battle became a celebrated episode of Polish and European history, though the Mongols withdrew from Europe soon afterwards.

Legnica received city rights in the 13th century and developed as a centre of gold mining, weaving and trade. It passed from the Polish crown to Bohemia, and its Piast ducal line survived until 1675, when the last duke, George William, died — the last male member of the entire Piast dynasty. Under the German name Liegnitz, the city became part of Prussia in 1742 after the Silesian Wars of Frederick the Great; in 1760 Frederick won the Battle of Liegnitz nearby during the Seven Years' War. After the Second World War, Silesia was transferred to Poland under the Potsdam Agreement, the German population was expelled, and the city was repopulated with Poles, many displaced from Poland's former eastern territories.

Economy and landmarks

Modern Legnica lies in Poland's copper belt; a major copper smelter (Huta Miedzi Legnica) anchors its industry. Landmarks include the early Gothic Piast Castle — one of Poland's oldest brick castles — the Cathedral of Saints Peter and Paul, the Mausoleum of the Silesian Piasts, and the Knights' Academy. During the Cold War, Legnica hosted the headquarters of the Soviet Northern Group of Forces, earning it the nickname "Little Moscow".
