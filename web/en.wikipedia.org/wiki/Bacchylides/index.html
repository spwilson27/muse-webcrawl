Bacchylides

Bacchylides (Greek: Βακχυλίδης; c. 518 – c. 451 BCE) was a Greek lyric poet from Ceos, nephew of the poet Simonides and a younger contemporary and rival of Pindar. He was included among the canonical nine lyric poets of ancient Greece. Almost all of his work was lost until 1896, when papyri recovered from Egypt restored fourteen victory odes (epinicia) and six dithyrambs, making him, after Pindar, the best-preserved of the epinician poets.

Life and work

Bacchylides worked for patrons across the Greek world, including Hiero I of Syracuse — for whom Pindar also wrote — and composed odes celebrating victories at the Olympic, Pythian, Nemean and Isthmian games. His Ode 5 (for Hiero's horse Pherenikos, 476 BCE) covers the same victory as Pindar's Olympian 1. Compared with Pindar's dense, abrupt manner, Bacchylides writes with clarity and narrative smoothness, retelling myths at length within his odes; his dithyrambs, such as the dialogue poem on Theseus, approach miniature drama.

Recovery

Known in antiquity through quotations, the poems themselves were recovered when two papyrus rolls reached the British Museum in 1896 and were edited by Frederic G. Kenyon in 1897. The find transformed understanding of the epinician genre by providing a contemporary counterweight to Pindar.
