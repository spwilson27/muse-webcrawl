Hans Hahn (mathematician)

Born27 September 1879, Vienna, Austria-Hungary
Died24 July 1934 (aged 54), Vienna, Austria
FieldsFunctional analysis, topology, measure theory, philosophy of science
InstitutionsUniversity of Vienna, University of Bonn, University of Czernowitz
Known forHahn–Banach theorem, Hahn decomposition theorem, uniform boundedness principle

Hans Hahn (27 September 1879 – 24 July 1934) was an Austrian mathematician and philosopher who made fundamental contributions to functional analysis, topology, set theory, measure theory, and the calculus of variations. He was also a founding member of the Vienna Circle of logical positivists. His name is attached above all to the Hahn–Banach theorem, one of the cornerstones of functional analysis.

Life

Hahn studied in Vienna, Strasbourg, Munich, and Göttingen, completing his doctorate in Vienna in 1902. After posts at Innsbruck, Czernowitz, and Bonn, he returned to the University of Vienna in 1921 as professor of mathematics, where he remained until his death. His students included Karl Menger, and Kurt Gödel attended his lectures and seminars. Hahn served as president of the German Mathematical Society in 1926; his health, weakened by diabetes, declined in the early 1930s, and he died in 1934 following surgery.

Mathematical work

The Hahn–Banach theorem (published by Hahn in 1927, and independently by Stefan Banach) states that a bounded linear functional defined on a subspace can be extended to the whole space without increasing its norm; it underlies much of modern analysis. Hahn also proved the Hahn decomposition theorem for signed measures (the basis of the Jordan decomposition), contributed to the uniform boundedness principle (Banach–Steinhaus theorem), worked on curve theory — "Hahn–Mazurkiewicz curves" generalise Peano's space-filling curves — and wrote an influential treatise on the theory of functions of a real variable. The Hahn series and Hahn embedding theorem in ordered algebra also bear his name.

Philosophy

Hahn was, with Moritz Schlick and Rudolf Carnap, one of the organisers of the Vienna Circle, the group of philosophers and scientists that developed logical empiricism. He promoted the public discussion sessions from which the Circle grew and helped bring Carnap to Vienna, contributing to the movement's 1929 manifesto.
