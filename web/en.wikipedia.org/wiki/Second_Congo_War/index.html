Second Congo War

Date2 August 1998 – 18 July 2003
LocationDemocratic Republic of the Congo
BelligerentsDRC government with Angola, Zimbabwe, Namibia and others vs. Rwanda, Uganda, Burundi and Congolese rebel movements
DeathsEstimates of excess deaths range from about 3 to 5.4 million, mostly from disease and malnutrition

The Second Congo War, also known as the Great War of Africa, began in August 1998 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and drew in nine African nations and some 25 armed groups, making it the largest interstate war in modern African history. It was the deadliest conflict worldwide since the Second World War.

Origins

The war grew out of the First Congo War (1996–1997), in which a Rwandan- and Ugandan-backed rebellion led by Laurent-Désiré Kabila overthrew Mobutu Sese Seko. When Kabila, seeking to assert independence, ordered Rwandan and Ugandan forces to leave in July 1998, his former patrons backed a new rebellion by the Rally for Congolese Democracy (RCD) in the east. Angola, Zimbabwe, and Namibia intervened to support Kabila, saving Kinshasa from a rapid rebel offensive in August 1998, while the Uganda-backed Movement for the Liberation of the Congo (MLC) opened a northern front.

Course of the war

The country split into government-held west and south and rebel-held east and north, with front lines stabilizing into a war of attrition accompanied by widespread looting of Congo's mineral wealth — coltan, gold, diamonds, and timber — by all parties. Kabila was assassinated in January 2001 and succeeded by his son Joseph Kabila, who pursued negotiations. The Lusaka Ceasefire Agreement (1999) had little immediate effect, but the Sun City Agreement (2002) and the Pretoria Accord with Rwanda led to foreign troop withdrawals and a transitional government in 2003.

Human cost and legacy

Epidemiological studies estimated millions of excess deaths, overwhelmingly from disease and malnutrition in war-disrupted regions rather than direct violence. Sexual violence was used on a massive scale. Although the war formally ended in 2003, armed conflict — including the Kivu conflicts involving the FDLR and later the M23 movement — continued in the eastern provinces for decades afterward.
