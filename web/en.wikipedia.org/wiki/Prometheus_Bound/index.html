Prometheus Bound

Prometheus Bound (Ancient Greek: Προμηθεὺς Δεσμώτης, Promētheús Desmṓtēs) is an ancient Greek tragedy traditionally attributed to Aeschylus, though many scholars since the 19th century have questioned his authorship on stylistic and technical grounds. The play dramatises the punishment of the Titan Prometheus, chained to a rock in the Caucasus by order of Zeus for stealing fire and giving it to humanity and for withholding a prophecy about Zeus's possible downfall.

Plot

The play opens with Kratos (Power), Bia (Force), and the reluctant Hephaestus chaining Prometheus to a mountain crag. Left alone, Prometheus recounts his gifts to mankind — fire, numbers, writing, animal husbandry, medicine, prophecy, and metalworking — which saved mortals from destruction. The Oceanids arrive to sympathise; Oceanus advises submission and is rebuffed. The wandering Io, persecuted by Zeus's desire and Hera's jealousy, visits, and Prometheus prophesies her long journey and her descendant Heracles, who will one day free him; he also reveals that he alone knows of a marriage that could produce a son mightier than Zeus. In the final scene Hermes demands the secret on Zeus's behalf; Prometheus refuses, and amid earthquake and storm he is swallowed into the abyss, defiant.

Context and authorship

The play is thought to be the first of a trilogy (followed by the lost Prometheus Unbound, in which Prometheus was reconciled with Zeus and released by Heracles, and Prometheus the Fire-Bringer). Hesiod's Theogony and Works and Days provide the mythic background. The case against Aeschylean authorship — advanced influentially by Mark Griffith in 1977 — rests on metrical, linguistic, dramaturgical, and theological anomalies; other scholars defend the attribution, and the debate continues.

Legacy

The defiant, suffering benefactor of humanity became a central Romantic symbol: Shelley answered the play with his lyrical drama Prometheus Unbound (1820), and the figure influenced Byron, Goethe, Marx (who praised Prometheus as "the most eminent saint and martyr in the philosophical calendar"), and later dramatists and composers.
