Skaldic poetry

Skaldic poetry is the Old Norse court poetry composed by skalds (poets) from the late Viking Age into the later Middle Ages, chiefly in Norway and Iceland. Skaldic verse is distinguished from eddic poetry by its named authorship, occasional subject matter — above all praise poems (drápur) for kings and chieftains — and its extremely complex metres and diction, including elaborate kennings (compressed figurative periphrases such as "whale-road" for the sea).

Forms and metre

The most prestigious metre is dróttkvætt ("court metre"), an eight-line stanza with strict syllable counts, internal rhyme, and interlaced word order so intricate that lines must be disentangled to be parsed. Other forms include the simpler fornyrðislag and narrative kviðuháttr. Skalds were professional, rewarded artists — among the earliest named poets in Northern Europe — and their verses were quoted as historical authority in the kings' sagas (konungasögur) because their metrical rigidity preserved them unusually faithfully in oral transmission.

Notable skalds and poems

The earliest known skald is Bragi Boddason (9th century), credited with Ragnarsdrápa, a shield poem describing mythological scenes. Other celebrated skalds include Þorbjörn Hornklofi, Eyvindr skáldaspillir (Háleygjatal, Hákonarmál), Einarr skálaglamm, the Icelandic Egill Skallagrímsson — hero of Egils saga and author of Sonatorrek and Höfuðlausn — Sigvatr Þórðarson, and the prolific Óttarr svarti. In the 13th century Snorri Sturluson wrote his Prose Edda largely to preserve skaldic art for contemporary poets, and his Háttatal is a versified catalogue of metres.

Preservation

Skaldic stanzas survive mainly as quotations embedded in sagas, treatises, and Snorri's works. The modern Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages project has re-edited the entire corpus, which comprises thousands of stanzas attributed to several hundred named poets.
