Apprenticeship

Apprenticeship combines paid work with structured training under a skilled practitioner.

Apprenticeship is a system for training a new generation of practitioners of a trade or profession with on-the-job training and often accompanying study. Apprenticeships can enable practitioners to gain a licence to practise in a regulated occupation. Most of the training is done whilst working for an employer who helps the apprentices learn their trade in exchange for their continued labour for an agreed period after they have achieved measurable competencies.

History

The system developed in the late Middle Ages under the guilds, which regulated entry to crafts such as masonry, weaving, metalwork and printing. A master craftsman took on a young apprentice, usually bound by indenture for a term of years (seven was conventional in England and was codified in the Statute of Artificers 1563), providing board, lodging and instruction in return for labour. On completion the apprentice became a journeyman, and might eventually qualify as a master with a "masterpiece." Industrialisation eroded the guild system, but apprenticeship survived in the skilled trades and was adapted into national vocational systems.

Modern systems

Germany and Switzerland are noted for their "dual system," in which apprentices split time between a company and a vocational school, qualifying in several hundred recognised occupations. In the United Kingdom, apprenticeship frameworks and standards have been repeatedly reformed, with an apprenticeship levy on large employers introduced in 2017. In the United States, registered apprenticeships are overseen by the Department of Labor and are concentrated in construction and manufacturing, though they have expanded into healthcare, finance and information technology. Degree apprenticeships combine employment with university study leading to a full bachelor's or master's degree.
