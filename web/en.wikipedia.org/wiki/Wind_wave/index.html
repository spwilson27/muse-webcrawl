In fluid dynamics, a wind wave, or wind-generated water wave, is a surface wave that occurs on the free surface of bodies of water as a result of the wind blowing over the surface. The contact distance in the direction of the wind is known as the fetch. Waves in the oceans can travel thousands of kilometres before reaching land. Wind waves on Earth range in size from small ripples to waves over 30 m (100 ft) high, being limited by wind speed, duration, fetch, and water depth.

Formation

When wind blows over a calm water surface, friction and pressure fluctuations create small ripples (capillary waves). If the wind persists, the ripples grow into gravity waves: the wind pushes on the upwind face of each wave and the moving pressure pattern transfers energy to the water. Growth continues until limited by the wind's speed and duration, the available fetch, and ultimately by breaking (whitecapping) in a fully developed sea. After leaving the generation area, long waves sort themselves by speed into the regular, low, rounded undulations known as swell, which can cross entire ocean basins.

Characteristics

A wave field is described statistically, notably by the significant wave height—the average height of the highest third of waves—which approximates the height an experienced observer would report. Wave motion is largely orbital: individual water particles move in circles, with the motion decaying rapidly with depth, so waves "feel bottom" only in shallow water, where they steepen, slow, and finally break in the surf zone. Wave behaviour is modelled by theories ranging from linear (Airy) wave theory for small waves to Stokes and cnoidal theories for steep and shallow-water waves.

Rogue waves

Occasionally, constructive interference and focusing produce a single wave far larger than its neighbours—a rogue wave, long reported by mariners and first reliably measured by the Draupner wave (about 26 m) in the North Sea in 1995. Rogue waves pose serious risks to shipping and offshore structures.

Effects

Wind waves drive coastal erosion and sediment transport, mix the upper ocean, power surfing and wave-energy devices, and are a major design consideration for ships and offshore platforms. Ocean wave forecasting, using numerical models such as WAVEWATCH III, is a routine part of marine meteorology.
