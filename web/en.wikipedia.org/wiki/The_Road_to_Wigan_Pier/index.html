The Road to Wigan Pier is a book by the English writer George Orwell, first published on 8 March 1937. Commissioned by publisher Victor Gollancz, who issued it as a selection of his Left Book Club, the book records Orwell's journey in early 1936 through the depressed industrial districts of northern England — Barnsley, Sheffield, and above all the Lancashire coal town of Wigan — during the height of the Great Depression. It is divided into two sharply different halves: a piece of documentary reportage on working-class life, and a long, partly autobiographical essay arguing for socialism while attacking the failings Orwell saw in the contemporary socialist movement.

Part One: the industrial north

The first part describes, in unsparing detail, the conditions Orwell encountered: life in the squalid lodging-house of the Brookers over a tripe shop in Wigan; the slum housing of the coal districts, with its back-to-back terraces, overflowing middens and polluted canals; the working day of coal miners, whose descent into the pits and crawl to the coal face Orwell renders in some of the most famous reportage in English; and the mathematics of unemployment, as he works through a miner's family budget to show what the dole actually bought. The "pier" of the title was a joke in itself — a short coal jetty on the Leeds and Liverpool Canal, demolished before Orwell's visit, which had become a music-hall byword for the town.

Part Two: the argument for socialism

The second half turns inward and outward at once: an account of Orwell's own class formation as the child of "lower-upper-middle class" imperial servants, educated at Eton and employed in the Burma police, and an explanation of why he concluded that socialism was the only answer to poverty and approaching fascism. It is equally scathing about socialism's own adherents — "every fruit-juice drinker, nudist, sandal-wearer, sex-maniac, Quaker, 'Nature Cure' quack, pacifist, and feminist in England" — whom Orwell blamed for repelling the ordinary people socialism needed. Gollancz, dismayed, prefixed the Left Book Club edition with a foreword partially disavowing the second half, a fact noted in most later reprints.

Reception

The book sold well for the Left Book Club (some 44,000 copies) and established Orwell's reputation as a political writer. It has remained in print ever since, praised for the documentary power of Part One and argued over for the provocations of Part Two; Orwell himself moved on almost immediately to Spain, whose civil war produced Homage to Catalonia (1938).
