Shout (music)

In music, a shout is a vigorous vocal style and rhythmic religious practice rooted in African-American gospel and church traditions. As a vocal technique, the shout is a loud, impassioned, often rhythmically punctuated delivery — sometimes called "shout singing" — used by gospel, blues, soul, and R&B singers to convey ecstatic emotion, and distinct from both melodic singing and preaching. Preachers in the Black church tradition likewise use a heightened, musical, sometimes shouted cadence known as "whooping".

Ring shout

The term is also associated with the ring shout, one of the oldest surviving African-American religious practices, in which worshippers move in a circle, shuffling and stomping, accompanied by call-and-response singing, hand clapping, and stick beating, without lifting the feet or crossing them (which would constitute dancing in stricter interpretations). The ring shout survived notably among the Gullah-Geechee people of the Sea Islands of Georgia and South Carolina, where groups such as the McIntosh County Shouters preserved it; it is recognized as a foundational influence on African-American music.

Gospel shout music

In Pentecostal and Holiness churches, "shout music" refers to the fast, driving instrumental music — typically featuring organ, drums, bass, and brass — that accompanies congregational dancing in the Spirit ("shouting" or "getting happy"). Trombone shout bands of the United House of Prayer for All People, exemplified by groups in the tradition of Madison's Lively Stones and the Kings of Harmony, developed a distinctive, exuberant brass-driven style that influenced soul and funk horn sections.

In popular music

Shouted and call-and-response vocals carried into rhythm and blues, rock and roll, and soul — the Isley Brothers' 1959 hit "Shout" being a famous adaptation of gospel shout dynamics to secular music. Gospel shout vocalists such as Mahalia Jackson, Sister Rosetta Tharpe, and later soul singers built climaxes around repeated, shouted phrases over vamp sections.
