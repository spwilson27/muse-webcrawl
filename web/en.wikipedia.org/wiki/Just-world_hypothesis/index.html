Just-world hypothesis

The just-world hypothesis is the cognitive bias that assumes that "people get what they deserve" – that actions will have morally fair and fitting consequences for the actor. For example, the assumptions that noble actions will eventually be rewarded and evil actions will eventually be punished fall under this hypothesis. In other words, the just-world hypothesis is the tendency to attribute consequences to – or expect consequences as the result of – either a universal force that restores moral balance or a universal connection between the nature of actions and their results.

This belief generally implies the existence of cosmic justice, destiny, divine providence, desert, stability, and order. It is often associated with a variety of fundamental fallacies, especially in regard to rationalizing suffering on the grounds that the sufferers "deserve" it.

Origins

The hypothesis was popularized in the 1960s by American social psychologist Melvin Lerner, who is known as the founder of the study of justice beliefs and "just-world" theory. Lerner observed in his studies on rewards that students paradoxically devalued the actual work of others who were randomly rewarded, and that participants treated victims unfairly even when the victims were clearly innocent of any wrongdoing.

Philosophers have long reflected on the idea. The ancient Greek concept of hubris, and the related doctrine that the gods punish arrogance, embodies an early form of the belief. The Book of Job in the Hebrew Bible can be read as a critique of the doctrine that suffering always reflects desert.

Evidence

In a classic 1966 experiment by Lerner and Simmons, observers watched a woman apparently receive electric shocks as punishment for incorrect answers in a learning task. Observers who could not compensate the victim or stop the shocks tended to derogate her, rating her as less attractive and less worthy, in order to reconcile her suffering with a belief in a just world.

Subsequent research has connected belief in a just world with religiosity, authoritarianism, and conservative political attitudes, as well as with victim blaming in cases of poverty, illness, and crime. Researchers distinguish between belief in a just world for the self, which is associated with well-being and adaptive coping, and belief in a just world for others, which correlates more strongly with harsh social attitudes.

Criticism and implications

The just-world hypothesis helps explain phenomena such as blaming victims of assault or misfortune, indifference to poverty, and the rationalization of inequality. Critics note that while the belief may provide a sense of security, predictability, and motivation to behave fairly oneself, it can also obstruct empathy and social change. Cross-cultural studies suggest the strength of the belief varies across societies and is not universal.
