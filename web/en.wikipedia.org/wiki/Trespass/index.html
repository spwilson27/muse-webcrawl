Trespass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trespass is an area of tort law broadly divided into three varieties: trespass to the person (assault, battery and false imprisonment), trespass to chattels (interference with goods), and trespass to land (unauthorised entry onto another's land). Trespass is actionable per se — without proof of damage — because it vindicates the right itself: the landowner's exclusive possession, the individual's bodily integrity.

Trespass to land includes entering, remaining after permission ends, placing objects on land, and (within limits) interfering with airspace and subsoil. It is generally a civil wrong, though some entries are criminal (aggravated trespass, squatting in residential buildings in England since 2012, and many jurisdictions' burglary and unlawful-entry offences). Defences include licence, necessity and legal authority. The tort must be distinguished from nuisance, which is indirect interference with use and enjoyment and requires proof of damage, and from the protest tactic of “mass trespass” (such as the Kinder Scout trespass of 1932), which invokes customary access claims later partly vindicated by right-to-roam legislation.
