The Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) is a species of ray-finned fish in the family Salmonidae, the third largest of the salmonids and the type species of the genus Salmo. It is found in the northern Atlantic Ocean and in rivers flowing into it, from North America to northern Russia.

Life cycle

Atlantic salmon are anadromous: they hatch in freshwater streams as fry, develop through the parr stage, and migrate to sea as silvery smolts. After one to four winters feeding in the ocean — where they grow rapidly on crustaceans and small fish — adults return, usually to their natal river, to spawn in gravel nests called redds. Unlike Pacific salmon, Atlantic salmon do not necessarily die after spawning and may return to sea and spawn again.

Fisheries and aquaculture

The species has long been prized by anglers and commercial fishers, earning the nickname "king of fish". Wild stocks have declined severely because of overfishing, dams, pollution, habitat loss and interactions with farmed fish. Commercial wild fisheries are now small, while salmon farming — centred in Norway, Chile, Scotland and Canada — produces millions of tonnes annually and dominates the retail market. Escaped farmed salmon and sea lice are ongoing conservation concerns.

Conservation

Many national populations are protected under endangered-species legislation, and restoration efforts include dam removal, fish passages and hatchery supplementation. In 2023 the IUCN assessed the global species as Near Threatened, with the British population listed as Endangered.
