Silver bromide (AgBr) is a soft, pale-yellow, water-insoluble salt well known for its unusual sensitivity to light. It is the principal light-sensitive material used in traditional photographic film and paper, a role it has held since the late nineteenth century. Silver bromide occurs naturally as the mineral bromargyrite.

Preparation

Silver bromide is prepared by reacting silver nitrate with an alkali bromide such as potassium bromide: AgNO3 + KBr → AgBr + KNO3. Although simple, the preparation for photographic use is performed under carefully controlled conditions, because the temperature, concentrations, and presence of gelatin during precipitation determine the size and shape of the AgBr grains, and hence the speed and contrast of the resulting emulsion.

Photographic application

When a photon strikes a grain of silver bromide, it can liberate an electron that reduces an interstitial silver ion to a silver atom. Clusters of a few silver atoms form a latent image; during chemical development, grains bearing such clusters are reduced entirely to black metallic silver, amplifying the image by many orders of magnitude. Upon heating, the salt decomposes into metallic silver and bromine vapour above about 250 °C.

Its crystal lattice — the same rock-salt structure as sodium chloride — has an indirect band gap of about 2.6 eV. Silver bromide has a greater photosensitivity and a broader spectral response than silver chloride, making it the preferred halide for camera films, while silver chloride was favoured for printing papers.
