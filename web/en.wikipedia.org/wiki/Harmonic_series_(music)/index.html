Harmonic series (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The harmonic series on C: harmonics 1–16 approximate increasingly fine divisions of the octave.

The harmonic series (also called the overtone series) is the sequence of frequencies, musical tones or pitches produced by a vibrating string or air column, consisting of the fundamental frequency and its integer multiples. Each component is a harmonic (or partial/overtone); their relative strengths determine the timbre of an instrument's sound. The harmonic series is the physical foundation of consonance, of brass and string playing technique, and of much of Western harmony.

Above the eighth harmonic the partials come at ever smaller intervals, and several (the 7th, 11th, 13th and 14th) deviate audibly from equal-tempered pitches — a feature exploited by natural-horn players and by overtone singing traditions such as Tuvan throat singing, in which a singer amplifies selected upper harmonics into a melody.

Musical consequences

Brass instruments without valves or slides (natural trumpet, natural horn, alphorn, bugle) can play only notes of the harmonic series of their tube length; string players produce harmonics by lightly touching nodes of the string. The low harmonics spell out a major triad, a fact long invoked to justify the "naturalness" of major harmony, from Rameau's corps sonore onward. Fourier analysis shows any periodic tone can be decomposed into harmonic partials, underpinning both acoustic theory and electronic sound synthesis.
