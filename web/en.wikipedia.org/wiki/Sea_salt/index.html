Sea salt

Sea salt is salt produced by the evaporation of seawater. It is used as a seasoning in foods, in cooking, in cosmetics, and historically for preserving food. Commercial production typically involves shallow evaporation ponds (salt pans or salterns) in which seawater is concentrated by sun and wind until sodium chloride crystallises and is harvested.

Composition

Although chiefly sodium chloride, sea salt contains trace minerals from seawater — including potassium, calcium, magnesium and sulfates — which can give it a more complex flavour and varied crystal shapes than refined table salt, which is mined from underground deposits, heavily processed and often iodised. Unrefined sea salts may be grey (such as sel gris from Brittany) or tinted by clays and algae; flaky finishing salts such as Maldon salt and fleur de sel are prized by chefs for texture.

Production and history

Solar evaporation of seawater is an ancient industry dating back millennia, practised around the Mediterranean, in China, and in pre-Columbian America. Sea salt production shaped trade routes and economies, and salt works often used windmills or pumps to move brine between ponds of increasing salinity. Today major producers include coastal regions of France, Spain, Australia, Mexico and the United States.

Health

From a health perspective, sea salt and table salt contain comparable amounts of sodium by weight, and the trace minerals in sea salt are present in quantities too small to offer significant nutritional benefit. Because sea salt is usually not iodised, exclusive use can contribute to iodine deficiency where that is a concern.
