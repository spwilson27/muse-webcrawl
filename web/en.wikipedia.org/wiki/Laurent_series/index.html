Laurent series

In mathematics, the Laurent series of a complex function f(z) is a representation of that function as a power series which includes terms of negative degree. It may be used to express complex functions in cases where a Taylor series expansion cannot be applied. The Laurent series was named after and first published by Pierre Alphonse Laurent in 1843. Karl Weierstrass may have discovered it earlier, in a paper of 1841 that was not published until later.

Laurent series
A Laurent series converges on an annulus

Definition

The Laurent series for a complex function f(z) about a point c is given by

f(z) = Σn=−∞∞ an (z − c)n

where the coefficients an are given by a line integral that generalises Cauchy's integral formula:

an = (1/2πi) ∮ f(z) / (z − c)n+1 dz

The part of the series with nonnegative powers is called the analytic part (or regular part), and the part with negative powers is the principal part. Unlike a Taylor series, a Laurent series can be used to represent a function on an annulus around a singularity.

Convergence

A Laurent series converges on an annulus r < |z − c| < R, where the inner radius is set by the nearest singularity inside and the outer radius by the nearest singularity outside. On the annulus the series converges to a holomorphic function; conversely, every function holomorphic on an annulus has a unique Laurent expansion there.

Classification of singularities

The principal part of the Laurent expansion about a point classifies an isolated singularity: if the principal part is zero, the singularity is removable; if it has finitely many nonzero terms, the point is a pole, whose order is the magnitude of the most negative exponent; if it has infinitely many nonzero terms, the point is an essential singularity, near which the function behaves wildly by the Casorati–Weierstrass and Picard theorems. The coefficient a−1 is the residue, the key quantity in the residue theorem for evaluating contour integrals.

Example

The function f(z) = e1/z expanded about 0 has infinitely many negative-power terms, displaying an essential singularity at the origin. By contrast, 1/((z−1)(z−2)) has different Laurent expansions in the annuli |z| < 1, 1 < |z| < 2, and |z| > 2, illustrating that the annulus is part of the data of the series.
