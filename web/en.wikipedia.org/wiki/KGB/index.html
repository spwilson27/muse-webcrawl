The KGB (Committee for State Security) was the principal security agency of the Soviet Union from 1954 until the dissolution of the USSR in 1991. It combined the functions of an internal security police, an intelligence service and a border guard, and enforced Communist Party control over Soviet society while running espionage operations worldwide. Its headquarters were the Lubyanka Building in Moscow, whose basement prison became a byword for political repression.

History

The KGB was created on 13 March 1954, in the reorganisation that followed Stalin's death and the fall of Lavrentiy Beria, by downgrading the Ministry of State Security (MGB) to a committee attached to the Council of Ministers. It inherited the lineage of the Cheka (1917), GPU, NKVD and MGB. Under chairmen such as Aleksandr Shelepin, and above all Yuri Andropov (1967–1982), it refined a system of surveillance, censorship and "prophylaxis" of dissent. The KGB backed the suppression of the Hungarian uprising of 1956 and the Prague Spring of 1968, infiltrated dissident and religious groups at home, and ran vast foreign intelligence networks. After the failed August 1991 coup, in which its chairman Vladimir Kryuchkov was a leading plotter, the KGB was broken up; its functions passed mainly to Russia's FSB (domestic) and SVR (foreign intelligence).

Organisation

The KGB's vast remit was divided among chief directorates: the First Chief Directorate ran foreign intelligence; the Second counter-intelligence inside the USSR; the Fifth (created 1967) combated "ideological subversion" and policed dissidents; others controlled the border troops, military counter-intelligence, signals intelligence and government communications, surveillance, and the guarding of party leaders. At its height the organisation employed several hundred thousand staff plus border forces and an enormous informant network.

Foreign operations

KGB foreign intelligence, directed from Moscow Centre, recruited agents across the West — heirs to coups such as the Cambridge Five in Britain — stole atomic and technological secrets, planted disinformation ("active measures"), supported allied movements, and conducted occasional assassinations, most notoriously that of the Ukrainian nationalist Stepan Bandera in 1959. Its rivalry with the CIA defined the intelligence dimension of the Cold War.

Legacy

The KGB's archives remain largely closed, and its legacy is contested: for many it symbolises the terror and conformism of the Soviet system; its personnel networks nevertheless retained great influence in post-Soviet Russia, where former KGB officer Vladimir Putin rose to the presidency.
