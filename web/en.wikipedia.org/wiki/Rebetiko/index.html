Rebetiko (Greek: ρεμπέτικο), plural rebetika, is a term for a genre of Greek urban popular music that emerged from the underworld and working-class culture of Greek cities and the refugee communities of Asia Minor in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Its songs deal with love, poverty, prison, hashish, exile, and defiance of authority, and its central instrument is the long-necked bouzouki. Long stigmatised and censored, rebetiko was later embraced as a cornerstone of Greek national culture, and in 2017 it was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The bouzouki is the emblematic sound of rebetiko.

History

The genre coalesced in the cafés and hashish dens (tekedes) of Piraeus, Athens, and Thessaloniki, and in the musical cafés of Smyrna and Constantinople. The population exchange of 1923 brought hundreds of thousands of Greek refugees from Asia Minor, whose refined "Smyrna school" singing — exemplified by Roza Eskenazi — merged with the rougher Piraeus style of Markos Vamvakaris and his famed Piraeus Quartet. Early recordings, including many made in the United States by the Greek diaspora, spread the music widely. Under the Metaxas dictatorship (1936–1941) rebetiko lyrics were censored for their underworld and drug themes; after the Second World War, Vassilis Tsitsanis broadened the genre's audience and musical sophistication.

Revival and legacy

Composers such as Manos Hadjidakis and Mikis Theodorakis championed rebetiko from the 1940s–60s, bringing the bouzouki into art music and the mainstream. A revival in the 1970s and 1980s — aided by scholarship, reissues, and Costas Ferris's 1983 film Rembetiko — restored the reputations of the classic rebetes. The associated dance is the solo, improvisational zeibekiko.
