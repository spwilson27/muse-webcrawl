Inverse function

In mathematics, an inverse function of a function f (also called the inverse of f) is a function that undoes the operation of f. The inverse of f exists if and only if f is bijective (both injective and surjective), and it is then denoted f−1. If f maps x to y, then f−1 maps y back to x.

Properties

A function and its inverse satisfy f−1(f(x)) = x for every x in the domain of f, and f(f−1(y)) = y for every y in the codomain. The graph of f−1 is the reflection of the graph of f across the line y = x. A non-bijective function can be made invertible by restricting its domain; for example, the square function on non-negative reals has the square root as its inverse.

Examples

Familiar inverse pairs include exponential and logarithmic functions, the trigonometric functions and their inverses (arcsine, arccosine, arctangent), and conversions such as Fahrenheit to Celsius and its reverse. In calculus, the derivative of an inverse function at a point equals the reciprocal of the derivative of the original function at the corresponding point (the inverse function theorem).
