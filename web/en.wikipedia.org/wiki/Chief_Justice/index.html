Chief justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A chief justice is the presiding member of a supreme court or other high court, and typically its administrative head. In the United States the office is styled Chief Justice of the United States: the holder presides over the Supreme Court, assigns majority opinions when in the majority, administers the presidential oath of office by tradition, presides over presidential impeachment trials, and heads the federal judiciary's administrative bodies. The office carries one vote like any associate justice; its influence is procedural, administrative and symbolic, magnified by long tenure (John Marshall served 1801–1835 and shaped the institution).

Similar offices exist worldwide: the Chief Justice of India heads the Supreme Court and the collegium that recommends judicial appointments; in Canada and Australia the chief justice heads the national high court and is a central figure in its public standing; in the United Kingdom the Lord Chief Justice heads the judiciary of England and Wales (distinct from the President of the UK Supreme Court). Appointment methods vary — executive nomination with legislative confirmation in the US, seniority or collegium systems elsewhere — as does tenure, life in the US and fixed retirement ages in most other systems.
