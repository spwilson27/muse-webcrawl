Health insurance in the United States is provided through a mixed system of private insurers and public programmes. Most Americans obtain coverage through employer-sponsored group plans, while government programmes cover the elderly and disabled (Medicare), low-income people (Medicaid), children (CHIP), and veterans. Unlike most other developed nations, the United States does not guarantee universal coverage, and a significant minority of the population remains uninsured.

History

Employer-based insurance expanded during the Second World War, when wage controls led employers to compete for workers with fringe benefits, and favourable tax treatment of premiums entrenched the system. Medicare and Medicaid were created in 1965 under President Lyndon B. Johnson. The Affordable Care Act (ACA) of 2010 expanded Medicaid, created regulated insurance marketplaces with income-based subsidies, barred denial of coverage for pre-existing conditions, and — until its penalty was zeroed in 2019 — required most individuals to obtain coverage.

Structure and costs

Plans commonly involve premiums, deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance, with networks such as HMOs and PPOs. US health spending is by far the highest in the world, exceeding $4.5 trillion annually in the early 2020s (roughly 17–18% of GDP), driven by high prices for services, drugs, and administration. Medical bills are a leading cause of personal bankruptcy, and cost-sharing leaves many insured people "underinsured."

Coverage and debate

The uninsured rate fell from about 16% before the ACA to roughly 8% in the 2020s. Reform remains politically contentious, with proposals ranging from a public option and Medicare expansion to "Medicare for All" single-payer models and market-based deregulation.
