Seiðr

A depiction of a Norse seeress (völva), the typical practitioner of seiðr

Seiðr (sometimes anglicised as seid or seith) is a form of magic related to shamanism that was practised in Old Norse society. Its practitioners, most often women known as völvur (seeresses), performed rituals for divination and for influencing events — foretelling destinies, altering weather, and blessing or cursing individuals.

Attestations

Seiðr is described in several sagas and poems. In the Saga of Erik the Red, the völva Þorbjörg, visiting a farm in Greenland during a famine, sits upon a raised platform (a seiðhjallr) while women sing the ritual songs (varðlokkur) needed to summon spirits, after which she delivers prophecies. The mythological record associates seiðr with the goddess Freyja, who is said to have taught it to the Æsir, and with Odin, whose practice of it Loki mocks as unmanly in the poem Lokasenna.

Gender and social standing

Seiðr carried an ambivalent status: its power was respected and sought after, yet for men its performance was associated with ergi — unmanliness or effeminacy — and could bring social stigma. Male practitioners (seiðmenn) existed, but the archetypal practitioner of the sources is the itinerant female seeress with her staff, cat-skin gloves and distinctive dress.

Interpretation

Scholars have compared seiðr with the shamanic practices of the neighbouring Sámi people, noting elements such as trance, spirit helpers, drumming or singing, and the ritual platform. The subject remains debated, as does the degree to which saga descriptions reflect genuine pre-Christian practice. In modern times, seiðr has been revived and reinterpreted within contemporary Norse pagan movements.
