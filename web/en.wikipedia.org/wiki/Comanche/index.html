Comanche

The Comanche (Comanche: Nʉmʉnʉʉ, "the People") are a Native American people of the Great Plains whose historic territory, Comancheria, stretched across present-day northwestern Texas, eastern New Mexico, southeastern Colorado, southwestern Kansas, western Oklahoma, and northern Chihuahua. Renowned as superb horsemen, the Comanche dominated the southern plains during the 18th and early 19th centuries and were a major power in the history of the American Southwest.

The Comanche became the pre-eminent mounted power of the southern Great Plains.

History

The Comanche split from the Shoshone people of Wyoming around the late 17th century and moved south onto the plains, acquiring horses — descendants of animals brought by the Spanish — and reorganising their society around mounted bison hunting. By the mid-18th century they had displaced the Apache from the southern plains and built a far-reaching system of raiding and trading, exchanging bison products, horses, and captives with New Mexico, Texas, and French Louisiana. The Comanche fought wars with Spain, Mexico, the Republic of Texas, and the United States.

In the later 19th century, U.S. military campaigns, the destruction of the bison herds, and diseases reduced Comanche independence. Following the Red River War of 1874–75, the last free bands, including that of Quanah Parker, surrendered and moved to a reservation in Indian Territory (Oklahoma), where the Comanche Nation is headquartered today near Lawton, Oklahoma.

Culture and language

Comanche society was organised into loosely affiliated bands rather than a single unified tribe, with leadership resting on merit and consensus. Their language, a Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family closely related to Shoshone, is severely endangered but is the subject of revitalisation efforts. The Comanche Code Talkers of the Second World War famously used the language for secure communications in the European theatre.
