Trio sonata

The trio sonata is a musical form and ensemble texture that was among the most important chamber music genres of the Baroque era. Despite its name, a trio sonata is written for three melodic lines but usually performed by four players: two treble instruments (typically two violins, or violin and oboe or flute) playing the two upper parts, and a basso continuo group — cello or bassoon plus a chordal instrument such as harpsichord or organ — realizing the bass line.

History

The form emerged in Italy in the early 17th century and was brought to its classical form by Arcangelo Corelli, whose four sets of trio sonatas (opp. 1–4, published 1681–1694) set the standard. Corelli distinguished the sonata da chiesa (church sonata, typically four movements in slow–fast–slow–fast order with abstract character) from the sonata da camera (chamber sonata, a suite of dances). Other major composers of trio sonatas include Henry Purcell, Georg Philipp Telemann, George Frideric Handel, and Johann Sebastian Bach, whose six organ trio sonatas (BWV 525–530) transfer the texture to a single instrument.

Decline

By the mid-18th century the trio sonata gave way to newer Classical forms such as the string quartet and the accompanied keyboard sonata.
