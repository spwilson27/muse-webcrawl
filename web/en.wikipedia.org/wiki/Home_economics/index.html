Home economics, also called domestic science or family and consumer sciences, is a subject concerning human development, personal and family finances, consumer issues, housing and interior design, nutrition and food preparation, and textiles and apparel.

Home economics courses have been taught in schools and universities since the 19th century and remain part of curricula in many countries, though course content has broadened from cooking and sewing to encompass consumer education, sustainability, and family studies.

History

The field was professionalized in the United States through the Lake Placid Conferences beginning in 1899, led by Ellen Swallow Richards, the first woman admitted to MIT and a pioneer of sanitary science. The American Home Economics Association was founded in 1909. Federal support came through the Morrill Land-Grant Acts and the Smith–Hughes Act of 1917, which funded vocational home economics education.

Scope

Modern family and consumer sciences address nutrition and wellness, early childhood development, personal finance and consumer decision-making, clothing construction and textile science, and housing and resource management. In many school systems the subject is now elective and open to all genders, having historically been directed primarily at girls.

Criticism and evolution

Twentieth-century feminists sometimes criticized home economics for channeling women into domestic roles, while defenders noted it opened scientific and academic careers to women and professionalized household knowledge. The discipline was renamed "family and consumer sciences" in the United States in 1994 to reflect its wider scope.
