Caliban is a fictional character in William Shakespeare's play The Tempest. He is the subjugated slave of the magician Prospero and the son of the witch Sycorax, who was banished to the island before Prospero's arrival. Caliban is described as a "savage and deformed slave" and is one of the play's most discussed figures.

Role in the play

Caliban claims the island as his birthright: "This island's mine, by Sycorax my mother, / Which thou tak'st from me." Prospero accuses him of attempting to violate Miranda, and keeps him enslaved, fetching wood and water under threat of magical punishment. Caliban plots with the drunken butler Stephano and the jester Trinculo to murder Prospero, worshipping Stephano as a god. The conspiracy fails, and at the play's end Caliban resolves to "be wise hereafter, / And seek for grace."

Although enslaved and often grotesque, Caliban is given some of the play's most beautiful verse, notably "Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises, / Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt not."

Name and origins

The name is often linked to "cannibal" (an anagram, roughly) and to Cariban, a term for the peoples of the Caribbean. Sources proposed for the character include travel accounts of the New World, such as Montaigne's essay "Of the Cannibals".

Interpretation

Since the 20th century, Caliban has frequently been read through postcolonial criticism as a colonised subject dispossessed by a European settler, a reading taken up in retellings such as Aimé Césaire's Une Tempête (1969). In science fiction, the character inspired the half-human monster in Forbidden Planet and other adaptations.
