Oxidative stress

Oxidative stress reflects an imbalance between the systemic manifestation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and a biological system's ability to readily detoxify the reactive intermediates or to repair the resulting damage. Disturbances in the normal redox state of cells can cause toxic effects through the production of peroxides and free radicals that damage all components of the cell, including proteins, lipids, and DNA.

Oxidative stress from oxidative metabolism causes base damage, as well as strand breaks in DNA. Base damage is mostly indirect and caused by the reactive oxygen species generated, e.g. superoxide radical (O2•−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and hydroxyl radical (•OH).

Sources and defences

ROS arise as by-products of mitochondrial respiration, from enzymes such as NADPH oxidases, and from external sources including ionising radiation, pollutants and certain drugs. Cells defend themselves with enzymatic antioxidants—superoxide dismutase, catalase and glutathione peroxidase—and small molecules such as glutathione, vitamin C and vitamin E. Signalling at low ROS levels is also physiological (redox signalling); pathology arises when production overwhelms repair.

Effects in disease

Oxidative stress is implicated in ageing and in the development of many conditions: cardiovascular disease and atherosclerosis, neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, diabetes, cancer, chronic inflammation and ischemia–reperfusion injury. Whether it is a cause or a consequence in each case remains debated, and large trials of antioxidant supplementation have generally failed to show benefit—and sometimes shown harm—a finding attributed to ROS's dual signalling role.
