Waters of the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Waters subject to federal jurisdiction under the Clean Water Act.

Waters of the United States is a regulatory term used in United States environmental law, principally in the Clean Water Act of 1972 (formally the Federal Water Pollution Control Act Amendments), to define the scope of federal jurisdiction over surface waters. The phrase determines which rivers, streams, lakes, wetlands, and other water bodies are protected by federal permitting requirements, water quality standards, and pollution discharge controls.

The precise boundaries of the term have been disputed for decades. Because the Clean Water Act prohibits the discharge of pollutants into "navigable waters," defined in the statute as "the waters of the United States, including the territorial seas," agencies and courts have repeatedly had to decide whether the term extends beyond traditionally navigable waterways to tributaries, adjacent wetlands, ponds, ephemeral streams, and ditches.

Regulatory history

The Army Corps of Engineers and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) jointly administer the definition. Early regulations in the 1970s adopted an expansive reading, covering waters used in interstate commerce and wetlands adjacent to them. In United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes (1985), the Supreme Court upheld jurisdiction over wetlands adjacent to navigable waters.

Later decisions narrowed the reach. In Solid Waste Agency of Northern Cook County v. Army Corps of Engineers (2001), the Court rejected jurisdiction over isolated ponds based solely on use by migratory birds. In Rapanos v. United States (2006), a fractured Court produced competing tests: Justice Scalia's plurality would limit jurisdiction to relatively permanent waters and wetlands with a continuous surface connection, while Justice Kennedy's concurrence asked whether a water has a "significant nexus" to traditionally navigable waters.

Rulemaking and the 2023 decision

The Obama administration's 2015 Clean Water Rule attempted to codify the significant-nexus approach; the Trump administration's 2020 Navigable Waters Protection Rule replaced it with a narrower definition excluding most ephemeral streams and non-adjacent wetlands. In Sackett v. EPA (2023), the Supreme Court held that the Act covers wetlands only when they have a continuous surface connection to relatively permanent bodies of water connected to traditional navigable waters, substantially adopting the Rapanos plurality view. The Biden administration subsequently revised its rule to conform to Sackett.

Significance

The definition controls the reach of Section 402 discharge permits, Section 404 dredge-and-fill permits for wetlands, oil spill liability, and state water quality certification. Its shifting scope has major consequences for agriculture, real estate development, mining, and conservation, and it remains one of the most litigated phrases in American environmental law.
