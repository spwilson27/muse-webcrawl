Synchronicity

Synchronicity (German: Synchronizität) is a concept first introduced by the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung, which holds that events are "meaningful coincidences" if they occur with no causal relationship yet seem to be meaningfully related. Jung developed the idea in collaboration with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli and published it in his 1952 essay Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle.

Concept

Jung proposed that, just as events may be connected by causality, they may also be connected by meaning — an "acausal connecting principle" linking a person's inner psychological state with an outer event. His classic example involved a patient describing a dream of a golden scarab, at which moment a scarab-like beetle appeared at the window of the consulting room.

Criticism

Mainstream science regards synchronicity as an example of apophenia and confirmation bias: given the vast number of events in a life, striking coincidences are statistically expected (sometimes framed by Littlewood's law). The concept lacks empirical testability, though it remains influential in analytical psychology and popular culture.
