Irish Civil War

The Irish Civil War (Irish: Cogadh Cathartha na hÉireann; 28 June 1922 – 24 May 1923) was a conflict that followed the Irish War of Independence and accompanied the establishment of the Irish Free State, an entity independent from the United Kingdom but within the British Empire. It was fought between the provisional government of the Free State, which supported the Anglo-Irish Treaty, and the anti-Treaty IRA, which opposed the Treaty and the state's oath of allegiance to the British Crown.

Background

The Anglo-Irish Treaty of December 1921 ended the War of Independence but split the republican movement: it granted dominion status, partitioned Ireland, and required an oath to the Crown. The Dáil approved the Treaty by a narrow margin in January 1922. Anti-Treaty forces under Rory O'Connor occupied the Four Courts in Dublin in April, and after British pressure the provisional government under Michael Collins bombarded the building on 28 June 1922, opening the war.

Course of the war

Free State forces, expanded into a National Army with British-supplied artillery and armoured cars, quickly took Dublin and the major towns. Collins was killed in an ambush at Béal na Bláth, County Cork, in August 1922. The anti-Treaty side reverted to guerrilla warfare under Liam Lynch. The Free State government, led by W. T. Cosgrave, responded with severe measures: 77 anti-Treaty prisoners were officially executed, including reprisal executions after assassinations of deputies. After Lynch's death in April 1923, his successor Frank Aiken ordered a ceasefire and the dumping of arms in May 1923.

Consequences

Estimates of the dead range from roughly 1,500 to 4,000; more people may have died than in the War of Independence, and the conflict left deep bitterness in Irish politics. The main political parties of twentieth-century Ireland — Fianna Fáil (founded by Éamon de Valera in 1926) and Fine Gael (successor to the pro-Treaty party) — descend directly from the two sides of the Civil War.
