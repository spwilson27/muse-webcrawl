Teleconnection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In meteorology and climatology, a teleconnection is a persistent, large-scale pattern of pressure and circulation anomalies that spans vast geographical areas, linking weather and climate anomalies in widely separated regions of the globe. Teleconnections typically persist for weeks to decades and recur with characteristic spatial structures.

Examples

  • The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), which shifts rainfall and temperature patterns worldwide.
  • The North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO), governing winter weather in Europe and eastern North America.
  • The Pacific–North American (PNA) pattern, affecting North American temperature and precipitation.
  • The Indian Ocean Dipole and the Arctic Oscillation.

Mechanisms

Teleconnections are often produced by Rossby wave trains propagating through the atmosphere from tropical heating anomalies, and by coupled ocean–atmosphere interactions. The concept emerged from work by Gilbert Walker in the early 20th century on the Southern Oscillation and was formalized with the recognition of stationary wave patterns in the 1980s.

Applications

Because teleconnection indices (such as the Niño 3.4 index) evolve slowly, they are central to seasonal climate forecasting, agriculture planning, and water resource management.
