Transactional leadership

Transactional leadership (or managerial leadership) is a style of leadership in which leaders focus on supervising, organising, and performance. It is based on a system of exchange: followers comply with the leader's instructions in return for rewards, and are corrected or punished when performance falls short. The concept was described by sociologist Max Weber in 1947 and developed by James MacGregor Burns in his 1978 work on leadership.

Components

Researchers, notably Bernard Bass, identify three components: contingent reward — clarifying goals and recognising achievement; management by exception (active) — monitoring for deviations and correcting them; and management by exception (passive) — intervening only when problems become serious. A related absence of leadership, laissez-faire, sits at the inactive end of the range.

Comparison with transformational leadership

Transactional leadership is usually contrasted with transformational leadership, in which leaders inspire followers through vision, intellectual stimulation and individual consideration. Bass argued the styles are complementary rather than opposites: effective leaders combine transactional clarity with transformational motivation, an idea measured by the Multifactor Leadership Questionnaire.

Applications and criticism

The style is common in sales management, manufacturing and the military, where goals are measurable and short-term. Critics note it can discourage innovation and intrinsic motivation because it frames work purely as an exchange of effort for reward.
