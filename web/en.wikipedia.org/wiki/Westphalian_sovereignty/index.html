Westphalian sovereignty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Westphalian sovereignty is the principle in international relations that each state has exclusive sovereignty over its territory and domestic affairs, to the exclusion of external actors, and that states are legally equal and must not intervene in one another's internal affairs. It is named for the Peace of Westphalia (1648) — the treaties of Osnabrück and Münster that ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic.

Historians of international relations long treated 1648 as the birthday of the modern states-system: thereafter, in the textbook story, rulers' authority no longer derived from pope or emperor, and the society of sovereign states was born. Revisionist scholarship (Andreas Osiander, Benno Teschke and others) cautions that the treaties were imperial constitutional settlements full of exceptions, and that “Westphalian sovereignty” as a coherent doctrine is largely a later construction — yet the term remains the standard shorthand for the norms of territorial integrity, legal equality and non-intervention codified in the UN Charter.

Challenges

The principle is pressured from many directions: human-rights law and the Responsibility to protect doctrine qualify non-intervention; the European Union pools sovereignty; global finance, climate and cyberspace overflow borders; and powerful states intervene while invoking the principle for themselves. Krasner's term “organised hypocrisy” captures the coexistence of the norm with its frequent violation.
