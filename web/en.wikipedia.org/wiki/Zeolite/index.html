Zeolite

Zeolites are a family of microporous, crystalline aluminosilicate minerals and synthetic materials whose structures contain regular channels and cages of molecular dimensions. Their name, coined by Swedish mineralogist Axel Fredrik Cronstedt in 1756, comes from the Greek zeo ("to boil") and lithos ("stone"), because zeolite crystals visibly release steam when heated.

Structure

Zeolite frameworks are built from corner-sharing TO4 tetrahedra, where T is silicon or aluminium. Substituting Al³⁺ for Si⁴⁺ introduces negative framework charge, balanced by exchangeable cations (such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺) that sit in the pores along with water molecules. Pore openings typically range from about 3 to 10 ångströms, allowing zeolites to adsorb molecules selectively by size — hence their use as molecular sieves.

Natural occurrence

Natural zeolites form where volcanic rocks and ash react with alkaline groundwater. Common species include clinoptilolite, mordenite, chabazite, erionite and phillipsite. More than 40 naturally occurring framework types are known, while over 200 synthetic frameworks have been characterised.

Applications

  • Catalysis — zeolites such as ZSM-5 and faujasite are key catalysts in petroleum refining (fluid catalytic cracking) and petrochemical production.
  • Ion exchange — used in water softening and in laundry detergents as builders replacing phosphates.
  • Adsorption and separation — drying of gases, air separation, and removal of contaminants.
  • Agriculture and medicine — soil conditioning, animal feed additives, and removal of radioactive caesium and strontium from contaminated water.

Safety

Most zeolites are innocuous, but the fibrous zeolite erionite is a potent carcinogen when inhaled, comparable to asbestos, and is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC.
