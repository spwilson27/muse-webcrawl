Swiss neutrality is one of the main principles of Switzerland's foreign policy, dictating that Switzerland is not to be involved in armed conflicts between other states. This policy is self-imposed and designed to ensure external security and promote peace. Switzerland has the oldest policy of military neutrality in the world; it has not participated in a foreign war since its neutrality was recognised by the Treaty of Paris in 1815, and it did not join the United Nations until 2002.

Origins

The roots of Swiss neutrality lie in the aftermath of the Battle of Marignano in 1515, after which the Swiss Confederates largely abandoned expansionist warfare. The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 formally recognised Swiss independence from the Holy Roman Empire. At the Congress of Vienna in 1815, the great powers of Europe signed a declaration affirming Switzerland's "perpetual neutrality", partly to create a stable buffer state between France and Austria.

The world wars

Switzerland maintained its neutrality through both world wars, mobilising its militia army and fortifying its alpine borders. Its conduct in the Second World War — including trade with Nazi Germany and the handling of refugees and Jewish assets — was later examined critically by the Bergier Commission, which reported in 2002.

Armed neutrality

Swiss neutrality is armed: Switzerland maintains a large militia-based army and, during the Cold War, prepared extensive fortifications and demolitions known as the National Redoubt. Neutrality forbids joining military alliances (Switzerland stayed out of NATO, though it joined NATO's Partnership for Peace in 1996) and prohibits foreign military bases on Swiss soil, but it does not prevent participation in international peacekeeping, economic sanctions, or humanitarian "good offices" — Geneva hosts numerous international organisations and peace talks.

Modern debates

Switzerland joined the United Nations in 2002 after a referendum, and adopted EU and UN sanctions against Russia following the 2022 invasion of Ukraine, prompting debate about the compatibility of sanctions and arms-re-export restrictions with strict neutrality. Successive popular initiatives have sought either to entrench neutrality more deeply in the constitution or to interpret it more flexibly.
