Robert Fogel

Robert William Fogel
Born: 1 July 1926, New York City, United States
Died: 11 June 2013, Oak Lawn, Illinois, United States
Field: Economic history
Institutions: University of Chicago, Harvard University
Awards: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (1993)

Robert William Fogel (1 July 1926 – 11 June 2013) was an American economic historian and scientist, best known for pioneering "cliometrics" — the application of economic theory and quantitative methods to economic history. He shared the 1993 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Douglass North for renewing research in economic history.

Work

Fogel's first major work, Railroads and American Economic Growth (1964), used counterfactual analysis to argue that railroads, while important, accounted for only a few percent of American national income in 1890, since canals and roads could have substituted for them — challenging the assumption that railways were indispensable to growth. His most controversial work, Time on the Cross (1974, with Stanley Engerman), applied quantitative methods to American slavery, arguing that slavery was profitable and that enslaved people were materially better treated than had been assumed; the book provoked intense criticism over its methods and moral framing, which Fogel addressed in later work including Without Consent or Contract (1989), where he emphasised slavery's moral iniquity.

Later research

In later decades Fogel studied long-run changes in health, nutrition, and the human body — his concept of "technophysio evolution" — arguing that improvements in nutrition and physiology explain much of modern economic growth and mortality decline. He directed a large research programme at the University of Chicago on early-life conditions and later-life health.
