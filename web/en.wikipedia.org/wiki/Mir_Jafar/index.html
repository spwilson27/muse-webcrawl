Mir Jafar (c. 1691 – 5 February 1765), born Sayyid Mir Muhammad Jafar Khan, was a military commander and the Nawab of Bengal under the suzerainty of the British East India Company. He is notorious in South Asian historiography for his betrayal of Nawab Siraj ud-Daulah at the Battle of Plassey in 1757, an act that opened the way to British rule in Bengal and, ultimately, India. In Bengali and Urdu idiom, his name became a byword for treachery, comparable to "quisling" or "Benedict Arnold" in English.

Role in the Battle of Plassey

Jafar was commander-in-chief (Mir Bakhshi) of the army of Siraj ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Bengal. Dissatisfied with his position and ambitious for the throne, he entered into a secret conspiracy with Robert Clive and the East India Company, together with the Jagat Seth banking family and other disaffected courtiers. At Plassey on 23 June 1757, Jafar held back the greater part of the Nawab's army from the battle, ensuring the Company's victory over Siraj ud-Daulah, who was captured and killed shortly afterwards.

Nawab under the Company

Installed as Nawab, Jafar paid enormous sums to the Company and its officers as "restitution" and presents, which strained Bengal's treasury. Finding him insufficiently compliant — and grown wealthy from his private trade — the Company deposed him in 1760 in favour of his son-in-law Mir Qasim. When Qasim in turn rebelled and was defeated, Jafar was restored in July 1763 and remained a figurehead Nawab until his death in 1765, when he was succeeded by his son Najmuddin Ali Khan. Under his rule the Company consolidated its grip on Bengal, acquiring the diwani (revenue administration) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765.

Legacy

Mir Jafar's name is remembered across the Indian subcontinent as a synonym for a traitor who sells his country to a foreign power; "Mir Jafar" and "Mir Sadiq" are used together as a stock phrase for betrayal in Urdu and Bengali political rhetoric.
