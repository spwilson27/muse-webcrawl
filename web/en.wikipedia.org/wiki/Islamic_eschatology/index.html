Islamic eschatology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Islamic eschatology (Arabic: علم الآخرة) is the aspect of Islamic theology incorporating the events of the afterlife and the end of the world. It concerns the signs of the Hour (ʿAlāmāt al-Sāʿa), death and the intermediate state (Barzakh), resurrection (Qiyāma), the final judgment, and the eternal abodes of paradise (Jannah) and hell (Jahannam). The primary sources are the Quran and the hadith literature.

The afterlife

After death, the soul enters Barzakh, an intermediate state lasting until the Day of Resurrection. Tradition describes questioning in the grave by the angels Munkar and Nakir. On the Day of Judgment (Yawm al-Qiyāma), the dead are raised, deeds are weighed on the scales (mīzān), and each person receives the record of their deeds — in the right hand for the saved, the left for the damned. Souls then cross the bridge al-Ṣirāṭ, finer than a hair and sharper than a sword, stretched over hell; the righteous pass to paradise, while the wicked fall.

Signs of the Hour

Hadith collections enumerate minor and major signs preceding the end. The major signs include the appearance of the Dajjal (a false messiah figure), the return of Jesus (ʿĪsā), who in Islamic tradition will defeat the Dajjal; the coming of the Mahdi, a messianic redeemer — a doctrine especially central in Shia Islam, where the Mahdi is identified with the Hidden Imam; the emergence of Gog and Magog (Yaʾjūj and Maʾjūj); and cosmic portents such as the sun rising from the west.

Paradise and hell

Jannah is depicted in the Quran as gardens beneath which rivers flow, with abundant food, drink, and companionship, the greatest reward being the vision of God (in Sunni theology). Jahannam is a place of fire and punishment; scholars have debated whether punishments are eternal for all, whether hell will eventually be emptied, and the fate of non-Muslims, producing a range of views from strict literalism to universalist readings.

Schools and interpretations

Sunni, Shia, Sufi, and philosophical traditions interpret eschatological texts differently: the Muʿtazila emphasised rational and allegorical readings; Sufis often spiritualised the signs as interior states; modern movements have sometimes politicised Mahdist expectation. Eschatology remains a live theme in preaching, apocalyptic literature, and contemporary politics.
