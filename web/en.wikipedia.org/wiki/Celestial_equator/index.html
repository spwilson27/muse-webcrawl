Celestial equator

TypeGreat circle on the celestial sphere
Declination0° by definition
RelationProjection of Earth's equator onto the sky
Crosses ecliptic atThe March and September equinoxes

The celestial equator is the great circle of the imaginary celestial sphere on the same plane as the equator of Earth. It is an abstract projection of the terrestrial equator out into space. Due to Earth's axial tilt, the celestial equator is inclined by about 23.44° with respect to the ecliptic plane. An observer standing on Earth's equator visualises the celestial equator as a semicircle passing through the zenith directly overhead.

Properties

The celestial equator divides the sky into the northern and southern celestial hemispheres. Objects on it have a declination of 0° in the equatorial coordinate system, which uses the celestial equator as its fundamental plane; positions are measured in declination north or south of it and in right ascension eastward from the March equinox. The Sun crosses the celestial equator twice a year, at the equinoxes, and at those times day and night are of nearly equal length everywhere on Earth.

Observation

From mid-northern latitudes the celestial equator arcs across the southern sky; from the southern hemisphere it arcs across the northern sky. It passes through a well-known sequence of constellations, including Pisces, Cetus, Taurus, Eridanus, Orion — where the three stars of Orion's Belt lie almost exactly on it — Monoceros, Hydra, Virgo, Ophiuchus, Aquila and Aquarius. Stars near the celestial equator are visible from nearly the entire surface of the Earth at some time of year.

Precession

Because the direction of Earth's axis slowly precesses with a period of about 25,800 years, the celestial equator and the positions of the equinoxes shift gradually against the background stars; equatorial coordinates are therefore always quoted for a specific epoch, such as J2000.0.
