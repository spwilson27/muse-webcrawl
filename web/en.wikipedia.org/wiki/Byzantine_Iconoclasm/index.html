Byzantine Iconoclasm

Byzantine Iconoclasm (Greek: Εἰκονομαχία, "image struggle" or "war on icons") refers to two periods in the history of the Byzantine Empire, in the 8th and 9th centuries, when the religious use of images (icons) was opposed and suppressed by imperial authority, amid theological dispute between iconoclasts and iconophiles (iconodules), who defended veneration of images.

First Iconoclasm

Tradition holds that Emperor Leo III ordered the removal of the icon of Christ from the Chalke gate of the palace in 726 and formally banned religious images around 730, deposing Patriarch Germanos. His son Constantine V convened the Council of Hieria (754), which condemned icon veneration. Iconophile monks were persecuted, and much early Byzantine religious art was destroyed or whitewashed.

Restoration and second Iconoclasm

Empress Irene restored icons through the Second Council of Nicaea (787), which distinguished veneration (proskynesis) of images from worship (latreia) due to God alone. In 814 Leo V revived iconoclasm, which continued under Michael II and Theophilos. After Theophilos's death, his widow Theodora, regent for Michael III, definitively restored the icons in 843 — an event still celebrated in Eastern Orthodoxy as the Triumph of Orthodoxy on the first Sunday of Lent.

Consequences

The controversy deepened the estrangement between Constantinople and the papacy, contributed to the loss of Byzantine influence in Italy, destroyed an incalculable amount of early medieval art, and shaped Orthodox theology of images, articulated by writers such as John of Damascus and Theodore the Studite.
