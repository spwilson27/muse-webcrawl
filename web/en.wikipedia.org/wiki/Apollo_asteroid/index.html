Apollo asteroid

The Apollo asteroids are a group of near-Earth asteroids named after 1862 Apollo, the first such object recognised, discovered by Karl Reinmuth in 1932 but lost until 1973. Apollo asteroids are Earth-crossing asteroids: they have orbital semi-major axes greater than that of Earth (a > 1.0 AU) and perihelion distances small enough (q < 1.017 AU) to cross Earth's orbit.

Characteristics

Because their orbits cross Earth's, Apollo asteroids can in principle collide with our planet, and the largest are classed as potentially hazardous asteroids. Their orbital periods are longer than one year, and gravitational perturbations from the planets continually reshape their paths over timescales of thousands to millions of years. Thousands of Apollos are known, and the population is continually replenished from the main asteroid belt.

Notable members

The largest known Apollo asteroid is 1866 Sisyphus, about 8 km across. 1566 Icarus, discovered in 1949, passes closer to the Sun than Mercury at perihelion. The Chelyabinsk meteor of 2013 — a roughly 20-metre body that exploded over Russia, injuring more than 1,500 people — was an Apollo asteroid, as very likely was the Tunguska event object of 1908. Several Apollo asteroids have been visited or targeted by spacecraft, and asteroid 162173 Ryugu and 101955 Bennu, sampled by the Hayabusa2 and OSIRIS-REx missions, belong to the Apollo group.

Related groups

Apollo asteroids are distinguished from the Aten asteroids, whose semi-major axes are less than 1 AU but which also cross Earth's orbit, and from the Amor asteroids, which approach but do not cross Earth's orbit.
