Hapax legomenon

EtymologyGreek ἅπαξ λεγόμενον, "(something) said only once"
FieldLinguistics, corpus linguistics, philology
Related termsDis legomenon, nonce word

In corpus linguistics, a hapax legomenon (plural hapax legomena; from Greek ἅπαξ, "once", and λεγόμενον, "being said") is a word or expression that occurs only once within a given body of text — a work, an author's surviving writings, or an entire language's attested literature. Words occurring twice are called dis legomena, three times tris legomena. The term is important in classical and biblical philology, where single attestations can be crucial and puzzling evidence for a word's meaning.

Characteristics

By Zipf's law, in any large corpus a great many words occur only once: typically around 40–60 percent of the distinct vocabulary of a text sample consists of hapax legomena. Their frequency grows predictably with corpus size, a regularity used in stylometry and authorship attribution. Many hapaxes are proper names, rare technical terms, compounds coined for a single occasion, or scribal errors and ghost words.

In ancient texts

Homeric Greek contains numerous hapaxes — such as asaminthos, a kind of bathtub — whose interpretation exercised ancient and modern scholars, since the single context often gives only a partial clue. The Hebrew Bible and New Testament likewise contain many words found nowhere else in scripture; some, like the New Testament's epiousios in the Lord's Prayer ("daily" bread, or perhaps "for the coming day"), have generated centuries of translation debate. In small or fragmentary literatures such as Hittite or Ugaritic, hapaxes can make up a large share of the vocabulary and limit how fully the language can be understood.

Use in scholarship

Lexicographers flag hapaxes in dictionaries, and textual critics weigh them when judging variant readings, since copyists were likelier to garble rare words than common ones. In computational linguistics, hapax counts are used to estimate a system's unseen vocabulary and to smooth probability estimates in language models.
