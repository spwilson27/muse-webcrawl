Cryptobiosis

Cryptobiosis (from Greek kryptos, "hidden", and biosis, "life") is a metabolic state in which an organism's metabolic activity comes to a reversible standstill in response to adverse environmental conditions such as desiccation, freezing, oxygen deficiency or extreme salinity. In this ametabolic state the organism shows no visible signs of life, yet it can survive for very long periods — in some cases decades — and revive when conditions improve. The phenomenon was first described by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in 1702, who observed dried "animalcules" (rotifers) reviving in water.

Forms of cryptobiosis

  • Anhydrobiosis — survival of extreme desiccation; the best-known form, seen in tardigrades, bdelloid rotifers, brine shrimp (Artemia) cysts, some nematodes and the resurrection plants. Water is replaced or cells protected by sugars such as trehalose and by intrinsically disordered proteins.
  • Cryobiosis — tolerance of very low temperatures.
  • Anoxybiosis — survival without oxygen.
  • Osmobiosis — response to extreme osmotic conditions.
  • Chemobiosis — response to high levels of environmental toxins.

Mechanisms

On drying, tardigrades contract into a shrunken "tun", lose over 97% of their body water, and their metabolism falls to a tiny fraction of normal. Protective molecules vitrify the cell contents into a glass-like state, stabilising membranes and proteins until rehydration. In 2007 tardigrades in cryptobiosis survived direct exposure to the vacuum and radiation of open space during the ESA FOTON-M3 mission — the first animals known to do so.

Significance

Cryptobiosis informs research on astrobiology, preservation of biological materials, seed banking and the limits of life on Earth and elsewhere.
