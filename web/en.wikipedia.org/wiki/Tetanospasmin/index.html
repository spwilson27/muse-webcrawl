Tetanospasmin

Tetanospasmin, also called tetanus toxin or TeNT, is an extremely potent neurotoxin produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani and is responsible for the clinical manifestations of tetanus. It is one of the most powerful toxins known: the estimated lethal human dose is less than 2.5 nanograms per kilogram of body weight. The toxin is produced as a single 150 kDa polypeptide that is cleaved into a heavy chain (100 kDa) and a light chain (50 kDa) linked by a disulfide bond.

Mechanism of action

Tetanospasmin binds to peripheral motor nerve terminals, is internalized, and travels retrogradely up the axon to the spinal cord and brainstem (retrograde axonal transport). There it crosses into inhibitory interneurons and its light chain — a zinc-dependent protease — cleaves the synaptic vesicle protein synaptobrevin (VAMP), preventing release of the inhibitory neurotransmitters GABA and glycine. Loss of inhibition leaves motor neurons unopposed, producing the spastic paralysis characteristic of tetanus: rigidity, trismus ("lockjaw"), and painful spasms triggered by minor stimuli.

Comparison with botulinum toxin

Tetanospasmin is structurally and mechanistically closely related to the botulinum neurotoxins. The difference in clinical effect arises from trafficking: botulinum toxin remains at the neuromuscular junction and blocks acetylcholine release, causing flaccid paralysis, whereas tetanospasmin migrates to the central nervous system and blocks inhibition, causing spastic paralysis.
