Superluminal motion

Superluminal motion is the apparently faster-than-light motion of certain astronomical objects, especially the relativistic jets of distant active galaxies and quasars. Despite the name, the effect does not involve anything actually travelling faster than light: it is an optical illusion caused by objects moving at speeds close to the speed of light at a small angle toward the observer, combined with relativistic effects on the arrival times of emitted light.

Explanation

Consider a blob of plasma ejected from a quasar jet at speed v close to c, at an angle θ to the line of sight. Between two emissions, the blob moves partly toward the observer, so the light from the second emission has a shorter distance to travel. The observer therefore measures a compressed time interval, while the transverse displacement on the sky is unchanged in comparison. The apparent transverse speed can then exceed c by a factor related to the Lorentz factor of the jet; apparent speeds of several times — occasionally tens of times — the speed of light have been recorded. The effect is maximised when the jet points almost, but not exactly, at the observer.

Observations

Superluminal motion was first observed in the early 1970s in the quasars 3C 279 and 3C 273 using very-long-baseline interferometry (VLBI), which tracks compact radio components moving apart over months and years. It is now routinely measured in blazars and radio galaxies, and also in microquasars within the Milky Way, such as GRS 1915+105. The phenomenon is strong evidence that quasar jets are relativistic and oriented close to the line of sight, a cornerstone of the unified model of active galactic nuclei.

Relation to special relativity

Because no information or matter exceeds the speed of light in any inertial frame, superluminal motion does not violate special relativity. It is a projection effect of light-travel time, anticipated theoretically before its discovery.
