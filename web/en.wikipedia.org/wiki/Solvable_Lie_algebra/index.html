Solvable Lie algebra

In mathematics, a Lie algebra is solvable if its derived series terminates in the zero subalgebra. The derived Lie algebra of the Lie algebra g is the subalgebra generated by all commutators [x, y] for x, y in g; the derived series is the sequence of subalgebras obtained by repeatedly taking derived algebras. A Lie algebra is solvable precisely when this series eventually reaches {0}.

Definitions and examples

Every abelian Lie algebra is solvable, as is the algebra of upper triangular matrices, whose derived series steps down through matrices vanishing on successive diagonals. The Lie algebra of all upper triangular n×n matrices is the standard example of a solvable, non-nilpotent Lie algebra. Every nilpotent Lie algebra is solvable.

Properties

Subalgebras, quotients, and extensions of solvable Lie algebras are solvable. Every finite-dimensional Lie algebra contains a unique maximal solvable ideal, called its radical. Lie's theorem states that over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, a solvable Lie algebra of linear transformations has a common eigenvector, so it can be represented by upper triangular matrices. Cartan's criterion characterizes solvability in terms of the Killing form: a Lie algebra is solvable if and only if its Killing form vanishes on the derived algebra.

Relation to solvable groups

The terminology is borrowed from Galois theory via Lie groups: a connected Lie group is solvable as an abstract group if and only if its Lie algebra is solvable. The Levi decomposition expresses any finite-dimensional Lie algebra as a semidirect sum of its solvable radical and a semisimple subalgebra.
