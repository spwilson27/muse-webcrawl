Slater's rules

Shielding of an electron by inner-shell electrons in an atom

In quantum chemistry, Slater's rules, proposed by John C. Slater in 1930, provide a way to estimate the effective nuclear charge (Zeff) experienced by an electron in a multi-electron atom, accounting for the shielding (screening) of the nuclear charge by the other electrons.

The rules

The effective nuclear charge is computed as Zeff = Z − S, where Z is the atomic number and S is the shielding constant. Slater's rules assign shielding contributions as follows:

  1. Write the electron configuration in grouped form: (1s) (2s, 2p) (3s, 3p) (3d) (4s, 4p) (4d) (4f) ...
  2. Electrons in groups to the right of the electron of interest contribute nothing.
  3. For an ns or np electron: each other electron in the same group contributes 0.35 (except 1s electrons, which contribute 0.30 to each other); each electron in the (n−1) shell contributes 0.85; each electron in shells (n−2) and below contributes 1.00.
  4. For an nd or nf electron: each other electron in the same group contributes 0.35; each electron in any group to the left contributes 1.00.

Use and limitations

The rules give estimates accurate enough for qualitative discussions of atomic size, ionization energy trends, and Slater-type orbitals, which Slater introduced in the same work. They are empirical and approximate: they treat s and p electrons in the same shell as equivalent and do not capture detailed radial behavior. Later refinements, such as the Clementi–Raimondi effective nuclear charges derived from Hartree–Fock calculations, provide more accurate values.
