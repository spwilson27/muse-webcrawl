Adverse drug reaction

Image placeholder: a drug-induced rash

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is a harmful, unintended response to a medication that occurs at doses normally used in humans. Adverse drug reactions are a significant cause of illness and death: studies in hospital settings commonly attribute roughly 5% of admissions to ADRs, and they rank among the leading causes of mortality in developed countries.

Classification

The classic classification divides reactions into Type A (augmented) reactions — predictable extensions of a drug's known pharmacology, dose-related and common, such as bleeding on anticoagulants — and Type B (bizarre) reactions — unpredictable, often immunological, not clearly dose-related, such as anaphylaxis to penicillin. Extended schemes add types for chronic use (Type C), delayed effects such as carcinogenesis (Type D), withdrawal effects (Type E) and failure of therapy (Type F).

Causes and risk factors

Risk factors include old age, polypharmacy, multiple illnesses, impaired kidney or liver function, and genetic variation in drug-metabolising enzymes (pharmacogenomics). Drug interactions, both pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic, account for many preventable reactions.

Detection and reporting

The science of detecting, assessing and preventing ADRs is pharmacovigilance. Spontaneous reporting systems include the FDA's MedWatch and Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) in the United States, the Yellow Card scheme in the United Kingdom, and EudraVigilance in Europe; reports feed into international monitoring coordinated by the WHO Uppsala Monitoring Centre. Notable historical episodes that reshaped drug regulation include the thalidomide disaster and the withdrawal of rofecoxib (Vioxx).
