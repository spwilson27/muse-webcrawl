Armenians in Turkey

Armenians in Turkey (Turkish: Türkiye Ermenileri) are one of the indigenous peoples of Anatolia and an officially recognised minority in the Republic of Turkey. The community today numbers roughly 40,000–70,000 citizens, concentrated overwhelmingly in Istanbul, plus several thousand recent immigrants from Armenia, and the small remaining population of Vakifli, the last Armenian village in Turkey, in Hatay Province. In the final years of the Ottoman Empire the Armenian population of Anatolia had numbered well over a million before the Armenian genocide of 1915–1917 destroyed or exiled it.

An Armenian church in Istanbul, centre of the modern community.

Ottoman period

Under the Ottoman millet system, Armenians were organised as the Armenian millet under the Armenian Patriarch of Constantinople. Armenians were prominent in trade, finance, crafts, and the imperial bureaucracy; by the nineteenth century, Istanbul Armenians (the amira class) included the sultan's bankers, architects of imperial palaces (the Balyan family), and directors of the imperial mint and gunpowder works. In the eastern provinces, Armenian peasants suffered under taxation and Kurdish tribal raids, and the massacres of 1894–96 under Sultan Abdul Hamid II killed tens of thousands. The Adana massacre of 1909 and then the genocide of 1915 effectively ended Armenian life in Anatolia.

Republic of Turkey

The 1923 Treaty of Lausanne recognised Armenians (with Greeks and Jews) as a protected non-Muslim minority entitled to schools, foundations, and religious freedom. In practice, the community faced the 1942 Wealth Tax (Varlık Vergisi), which ruined many minority businesses, the 1955 Istanbul pogrom (directed mainly at Greeks but also affecting Armenians), property confiscations from Armenian foundations later partially remedied by a 2011 decree, and an informal exclusion from public service. Istanbul Armenians maintain dozens of churches, schools, hospitals (Surp Pırgiç), newspapers such as Agos (founded by Hrant Dink, assassinated in 2007), and the Patriarchate in Kumkapı.

Hidden Armenians

Descendants of Armenians — often women and children — who were Islamised or adopted into Muslim families during the genocide are sometimes called "hidden" or "crypto" Armenians; estimates of their number vary widely, and since the 2000s some have publicly reclaimed Armenian identity, language, and baptism.
