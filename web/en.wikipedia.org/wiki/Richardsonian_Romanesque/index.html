Richardsonian Romanesque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Richardsonian Romanesque is a style of Romanesque Revival architecture named after the American architect Henry Hobson Richardson (1838–1886), whose masterpiece is Trinity Church, Boston (1872–1877). The style was widely adopted across the United States and Canada in the 1880s and 1890s, particularly for courthouses, city halls, libraries, railway stations, universities and churches.

Characteristics

The style is distinguished by massive, rock-faced stone walls, often in contrasting colours; broad round arches springing from short, squat columns; deep window and door recesses; cylindrical towers capped with conical roofs; asymmetric massing; and a strong sense of weight and permanence. Richardson drew on southern French, Spanish and Syrian Romanesque sources rather than copying the more delicate Norman forms used by earlier revivalists, and he suppressed ornament in favour of bold, simple forms and rich surface texture.

Spread and influence

Richardson's early death at 47 made his style a national phenomenon disseminated by his former assistants and imitators. Hundreds of public buildings in the "Romanesque" manner rose in American and Canadian cities, among them the Allegheny County Courthouse in Pittsburgh, Cincinnati City Hall, Buffalo's New York Central station buildings and many Carnegie libraries. The style fell from favour after about 1900, displaced by Beaux-Arts classicism for public architecture, but it influenced the young Louis Sullivan and Frank Lloyd Wright and remains one of the most recognisable American architectural idioms.
