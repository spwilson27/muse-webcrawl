Magic in the Greco-Roman world

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the Greco-Roman world of classical antiquity, magic (Greek: μαγεία, mageia; Latin: magia) was a widespread practice encompassing spells, curses, amulets, divination and ritual techniques aimed at influencing supernatural powers, deities, daimones and the dead for personal ends. The word derives from the Magi, Persian priests whose religious expertise Greeks regarded as foreign and sometimes suspect.

Attitudes and law

Greek and Roman attitudes toward magic were ambivalent. Magic could be distinguished from legitimate religion by its private, coercive and self-interested character, yet the boundary was porous: healing charms, protective amulets and agricultural rites shaded into everyday piety. Roman law increasingly penalised harmful magic; the Lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficiis (81 BCE) covered poisoning and harmful spells, and later imperial legislation, especially under Christian emperors, broadened prohibitions.

Practices

Common practices included:

  • Curse tablets (defixiones, κατάδεσμοι): thin lead sheets inscribed with curses against rivals in love, litigation, business and athletics, often deposited in graves, wells or sanctuaries.
  • Love spells (agōgai): rites designed to draw a desired person to the practitioner, abundantly documented in the Greek Magical Papyri.
  • Amulets and phylacteries: inscribed gems, metals and papyri worn for protection against illness and demons.
  • Divination: astrology, dream interpretation, necromancy and oracles.
  • Voces magicae: strings of unintelligible "powerful names", palindromes such as ablanathanalba, and character signs.

Sources

The major literary sources include the Greek Magical Papyri (2nd century BCE – 5th century CE), a body of spell handbooks from Roman Egypt; works by Pliny the Elder, Apuleius (whose Apology defends him against a charge of magic), Lucan and Lucian; and thousands of surviving curse tablets and amulets. Scholars such as Fritz Graf and Christopher Faraone have emphasised that "magic" was as much a label of social disapproval as a coherent category of ritual practice.
