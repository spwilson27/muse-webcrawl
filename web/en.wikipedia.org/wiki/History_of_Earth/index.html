History of Earth

The history of Earth spans approximately 4.54 billion years, from the accretion of the planet out of the solar nebula to the present day. Geologists divide this span into eons, eras, periods, and epochs: the Hadean, Archaean, and Proterozoic eons (collectively the Precambrian, nearly 90% of Earth's history) and the Phanerozoic eon, the "time of visible life," beginning about 541 million years ago.

Formation and the Hadean

Earth formed by accretion of dust and planetesimals; a collision with a Mars-sized body, Theia, is thought to have produced the Moon about 4.5 billion years ago. The early planet had a magma ocean, heavy bombardment, and a reducing atmosphere. As it cooled, a crust formed and oceans condensed. The oldest dated minerals, Jack Hills zircons from Australia (~4.4 billion years), indicate liquid water existed surprisingly early.

Origin and early evolution of life

Life — single-celled prokaryotes — appeared by at least 3.7–4 billion years ago. Photosynthetic cyanobacteria eventually oxygenated the atmosphere in the Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 billion years ago), causing a mass extinction of anaerobic life and possibly triggering global glaciations. Eukaryotic cells, arising through symbiosis, enabled sexual reproduction and, in the late Proterozoic and Ediacaran Period, the first large multicellular organisms.

The Phanerozoic

The Cambrian explosion (~541 million years ago) saw the rapid diversification of animal body plans. Life colonised land in the Ordovician–Devonian; forests, tetrapods, and amniotes followed. The Phanerozoic was punctuated by five major mass extinctions, including the end-Permian "Great Dying" (252 million years ago) and the end-Cretaceous event 66 million years ago, when an asteroid impact ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs and opened the way for mammalian dominance. Continents drifted through supercontinent cycles — Rodinia, Pangaea — reshaping climate and evolution. Hominins appeared in the last few million years; anatomically modern humans about 300,000 years ago.
