Articles of Confederation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Articles of Confederation and Perpetual Union was the first constitution of the United States, drafted by the Continental Congress in 1777 and ratified in 1781, governing the young nation until the current Constitution took effect in 1789. It created a confederation of sovereign states — a "firm league of friendship" — with a weak central Congress that could declare war, conduct diplomacy, coin money and requisition funds and troops from the states, but could not tax, regulate commerce or compel compliance.

Failure and replacement

The 1780s exposed the design: unpaid war debts and worthless requisitions, interstate tariff quarrels, inability to respond to Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) or to British and Spanish pressure, and an amendment rule requiring unanimity that blocked repair (the impost proposals failed on single-state vetoes). Yet the Confederation Congress also achieved the Land Ordinance of 1785 and Northwest Ordinance of 1787, organising western expansion and barring slavery from the Northwest Territory. The Philadelphia Convention of 1787, summoned to revise the Articles, instead drafted a new federal constitution — legally audacious, defended in The Federalist as necessary to create "a more perfect Union". The Articles remain the standing American lesson in the costs of too-weak union, and the confederal model still studied wherever integration is shallow by design.
