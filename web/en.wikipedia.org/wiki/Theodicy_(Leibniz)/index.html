Theodicy (Leibniz)

The Theodicy (French: Essais de Théodicée sur la bonté de Dieu, la liberté de l'homme et l'origine du mal, "Essays of Theodicy on the Goodness of God, the Freedom of Man and the Origin of Evil"), published in 1710, is the only book-length philosophical work of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz published in his lifetime. It coined the term theodicy — the attempt to reconcile the existence of evil with the goodness and omnipotence of God.

Background and content

The work grew out of Leibniz's conversations with Sophie Charlotte of Hanover, Queen of Prussia, about Pierre Bayle's Historical and Critical Dictionary, which had argued that faith and reason conflict over the problem of evil. Leibniz replies that this world is the best of all possible worlds: among infinitely many possible worlds in God's understanding, God — bound by his own wisdom and goodness — chose the one with the greatest perfection, in which evils are permitted as necessary consequences of greater goods and of creaturely finitude. He distinguishes metaphysical, physical and moral evil, defends human freedom through his doctrine of the complete individual concept, and maintains that apparent evil contributes to the harmony of the whole, as dissonance does in music.

Reception

The Theodicy influenced German Enlightenment philosophy (Wolff and his school) and, by reaction, provoked Voltaire's satire Candide (1759), whose Dr Pangloss parodies Leibnizian optimism with the refrain that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds" — mocked in the face of the Lisbon earthquake and human cruelty. Later philosophers and theologians have continued to engage its arguments, which remain a reference point in the philosophy of religion.
