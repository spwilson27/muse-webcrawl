Wind speed is a fundamental atmospheric quantity caused by air moving from high to low pressure, usually due to changes in temperature. Wind speed is now commonly measured with an anemometer, historically also estimated from visual effects using the Beaufort scale. Wind speed affects weather forecasting, aircraft and maritime operations, the design of structures, and the growth rates of many plants and animals.

Measurement

The World Meteorological Organization standard is a 10-minute average wind measured 10 metres (33 ft) above ground over open terrain. Instruments include cup and propeller anemometers, sonic anemometers (which time ultrasonic pulses), pressure-tube anemometers, and remote-sensing devices such as Doppler lidar and radar. Short-period maxima within the averaging interval are reported as gusts; a gust is typically defined as a peak lasting a few seconds.

Records

The fastest non-tornadic surface wind gust directly measured was 408 km/h (253 mph) at Barrow Island, Australia, on 10 April 1996 during Tropical Cyclone Olivia, recognised by the World Meteorological Organization. The previous record of 372 km/h (231 mph) was set at Mount Washington Observatory, New Hampshire, in 1934. Winds inside tornadoes, estimated by mobile Doppler radar, have exceeded 480 km/h (300 mph) in exceptional cases such as the 1999 Bridge Creek–Moore tornado.

Factors affecting wind speed

Wind speed increases with the pressure gradient and decreases near the surface because of friction with terrain, vegetation, and buildings—the reason speeds are greater over the sea and increase with height above ground. Local effects such as valley funnelling, katabatic drainage flows, and thunderstorm outflows can produce extreme local speeds.
