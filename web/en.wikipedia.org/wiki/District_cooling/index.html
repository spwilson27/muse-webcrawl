District cooling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A district cooling plant

District cooling is the cooling equivalent of district heating. Working on broadly similar principles to district heating, district cooling delivers chilled water to buildings like offices and factories needing cooling. In winter, the source for the cooling can often be sea water, so it is a cheaper resource than using electricity to run compressors for cooling. Alternatively, district cooling can be provided by a central chiller plant, which may use electricity, natural gas, or waste heat from industry or power generation.

How it works

A central plant produces chilled water, typically at around 4-7 degrees Celsius, which is pumped through an insulated underground pipe network to customer buildings. There, heat exchangers transfer the cooling to the building's own air-conditioning system, and the warmed water is returned to the plant to be cooled again.

Advantages

Centralized cooling allows the use of large, highly efficient industrial chillers and thermal energy storage, reduces the need for individual building chillers and their maintenance, frees up roof and plant-room space, and can lower overall emissions, particularly where free cooling from seawater, lakes, or rivers, or surplus waste heat driven absorption chillers, are available. District cooling is widely used in the Middle East, Scandinavia, and parts of North America and Asia.
