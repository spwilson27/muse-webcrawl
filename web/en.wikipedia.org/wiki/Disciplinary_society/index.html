Disciplinary society

A disciplinary society is a concept in political and social theory, developed principally by the French philosopher Michel Foucault, describing a form of society in which power is exercised through institutions that observe, normalise and train individuals — schools, hospitals, barracks, factories, workshops and prisons — rather than primarily through the sovereign's threat of violence. Foucault set out the concept in his 1975 book Discipline and Punish: The Birth of the Prison.

Concept

Foucault argued that from the 17th and 18th centuries a new technology of power spread through Western societies. Where sovereign power had operated by spectacular punishment of the body, disciplinary power works quietly and continuously: it partitions space, timetables activities, drills bodies through exercises, and subjects individuals to hierarchical observation, normalising judgement and the examination. Its aim is to produce "docile bodies" that are simultaneously more obedient and more useful — economically productive and politically compliant. The disciplines are not confined to prisons; the same techniques recur across the school, the clinic, the army and the factory, so that society as a whole takes on the character of a "carceral archipelago".

The panopticon

Foucault's emblem of this power is Jeremy Bentham's panopticon, a prison design in which a single guard in a central tower can observe every inmate, while the inmates cannot tell whether they are being watched at any moment. Uncertainty induces the prisoners to police themselves: power becomes automatic and internalised, and the same principle, Foucault argued, generalises to the whole disciplinary society.

Criticism and development

Gilles Deleuze's 1992 essay "Postscript on the Societies of Control" proposed that disciplinary societies were giving way to societies of control, in which enclosures are replaced by continuous modulation — codes and passwords rather than moulds — as in corporations, electronic monitoring and data tracking. Later thinkers have extended, revised or criticised Foucault's account, applying it to digital surveillance and algorithmic governance.
