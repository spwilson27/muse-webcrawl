Cabinet of curiosities

Frontispiece of Ferrante Imperato's Dell'Historia Naturale (1599), one of the earliest depictions of a cabinet of curiosities.

Cabinets of curiosities — also known as cabinets of wonders, and in German Kunstkammer or Wunderkammer — were collections of notable objects, encompassing a wide variety of fields, that formed the precursors of modern museums. Beginning in the sixteenth century, cabinets were assembled by princes, scholars, merchants and apothecaries across Europe, and were often arranged to display the collector's wealth, learning and command of the world.

Contents

Objects in a cabinet were conventionally grouped into categories such as naturalia (specimens from nature: shells, horns, taxidermy, minerals, fossils), artificialia (artefacts made by human craft, including works in ivory, goldsmith work and automatons), scientifica (instruments such as clocks, globes and telescopes), and exotica (objects from distant lands reached through new trade and exploration routes). Unusual or monstrous specimens — real or fabricated, such as "dragons" made from altered rays — were prized.

Famous collections

Notable cabinets included those of Ferrante Imperato in Naples, Ole Worm in Copenhagen, John Tradescant the Elder and Younger in London (whose collection formed the nucleus of the Ashmolean Museum), the Habsburg imperial collections in Vienna and Prague under Rudolf II, and Peter the Great's Kunstkamera in St. Petersburg.

From cabinets to museums

As scientific method advanced in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, private wonder-cabinets gave way to systematic, specialised collections open to scholars and the public. The encyclopaedic ambition of the cabinet nonetheless survives in the founding concept of institutions such as the British Museum.
