The Balfour Declaration of 1926 — issued as the Balfour Report of the Inter-Imperial Relations Committee at the Imperial Conference of 1926 — declared the United Kingdom and its Dominions to be equal in status and united by allegiance to the Crown. Not to be confused with the Balfour Declaration of 1917 concerning a Jewish national home in Palestine, the 1926 declaration was a foundational step in transforming the British Empire into the Commonwealth of Nations.

Background

After the First World War, the self-governing Dominions — Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Irish Free State and Newfoundland — chafed at residual British control: Canada had refused automatic support in the Chanak Crisis of 1922 and signed the Halibut Treaty with the United States on its own authority in 1923. At the 1926 conference the committee chaired by former prime minister Arthur Balfour was charged with defining Dominion status.

Text and meaning

The report's famous formula described Britain and the Dominions as:

"...autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another in any aspect of their domestic or external affairs, though united by a common allegiance to the Crown, and freely associated as members of the British Commonwealth of Nations."

The declaration also redefined the office of governor-general: henceforth the governor-general was the representative of the sovereign alone, not of the British government, and Dominion governments gained the right to advise the Crown directly. It affirmed that each Dominion's parliament was independent and that Britain would not legislate for a Dominion except at its request and with its consent.

Consequences

The declaration was a statement of principle rather than law; its legal implementation came with the Statute of Westminster 1931, which removed the Colonial Laws Validity Act's restrictions and confirmed the legislative independence of the Dominions that adopted it. The Balfour formula of free association under a common Crown underlies the modern Commonwealth realms, in which the same person is separately king or queen of each realm.
