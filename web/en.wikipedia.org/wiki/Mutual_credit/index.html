Mutual credit

Mutual credit is a type of alternative currency in which a community of users extends credit to one another on a zero-sum basis. In a mutual credit system, currency is created at the moment of a transaction: the buyer's account goes into debit by the amount of the purchase, and the seller's account is credited by the same amount. Because every credit is matched by an equal debit, the total money supply within the system is always zero; money is created by the participants themselves rather than issued by a central authority.

In mutual credit, each transaction creates a matching credit and debit, so the net money supply remains zero.

History

Ideas resembling mutual credit date to the 19th century, when reformers such as Pierre-Joseph Proudhon proposed a "Bank of the People" based on reciprocal exchange, and Josiah Warren experimented with labour notes at his Cincinnati Time Store. In the 20th century, E. C. Riegel and others developed theories of private, participant-issued money. The modern movement began in earnest with Michael Linton's design of the Local Exchange Trading System (LETS) in the Comox Valley, British Columbia, in 1983, which spread widely during the 1980s and 1990s.

How it works

Members of a mutual credit network hold accounts denominated in a unit of account — often equivalent to the national currency or to hours of labour, as in time banks. When a member makes a purchase, their balance falls (possibly below zero, representing a commitment to provide goods or services to the community in the future), while the seller's balance rises. Systems usually impose debit and credit limits to manage trust and prevent abuse. Accounting is transparent to members, and administration is typically handled by a small fee levied in the mutual credit unit itself.

Examples

  • LETS (Local Exchange Trading Systems) — community networks, especially in the UK, Australia, and New Zealand.
  • Time banks — all services are valued at one credit per hour.
  • WIR Bank — a large Swiss mutual credit clearing system for businesses, founded in 1934.
  • Sardex — a business-to-business mutual credit network in Sardinia, founded in 2009.
  • Commercial barter exchanges — trade exchanges operating on mutual credit principles.

Advantages and criticisms

Advocates argue that mutual credit provides liquidity where conventional money is scarce, keeps spending within the local community, and is interest-free. Critics note that such systems depend heavily on trust, can struggle to scale, and may face regulatory and tax complications; participants can also accumulate debits and then leave the network without reciprocating.
