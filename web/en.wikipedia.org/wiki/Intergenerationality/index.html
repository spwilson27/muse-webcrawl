Intergenerationality

Intergenerationality refers to interactions, relationships and exchanges between people of different generations — that is, between different age cohorts within families, communities and societies. The term is used across sociology, demography, public policy, economics and care work to describe both the bonds that link generations and the tensions that can arise between them.

In families

Within families, intergenerational relations include the transmission of material resources (such as inheritance and financial support), care (grandparents caring for grandchildren, adult children caring for elderly parents), values, language and cultural practices. Sociologists describe these flows with concepts such as intergenerational solidarity and intergenerational ambivalence. Rising life expectancy has lengthened the period during which three or even four generations are alive simultaneously, sometimes called the "beanpole family" structure.

In public policy

Intergenerational equity is a central concern in debates over pensions, public debt, housing and climate change. Policies made today can distribute costs and benefits unevenly across age groups: pay-as-you-go pension systems transfer income from current workers to current retirees, while government borrowing and environmental degradation may impose burdens on future generations. The concept of intergenerational justice asks what obligations the present generation owes to those who come after.

Intergenerational programs

Intergenerational programs deliberately bring together younger and older people for mutual benefit. Examples include mentoring schemes pairing retirees with schoolchildren, shared-site facilities combining nurseries with care homes, and oral-history projects. Research suggests such programs can reduce loneliness and ageism among older participants and improve social skills and attitudes toward ageing among the young.

Intergenerational conflict

Media and political discussion sometimes frames generations — such as Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials and Generation Z — as groups with competing interests over housing, employment and welfare spending. Sociologists caution that differences within generations are often larger than differences between them, and that generational labels can obscure class, regional and cultural variation.
