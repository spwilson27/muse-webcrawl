The calculus ratiocinator is a theoretical universal logical calculation framework described by the German philosopher and mathematician Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, usually paired with his related concept of a characteristica universalis, a universal formal language. The two form the twin pillars of Leibniz's project for a universal science.

Concept

Leibniz envisioned that if concepts were encoded in a formal symbolic language — the characteristica universalis — then disputes could be settled by mechanical calculation. When controversies arose, philosophers would no longer need to argue; instead they would sit down and say, "Calculemus" ("Let us calculate").

The calculus ratiocinator was to be embodied in a machine that would manipulate the symbols according to logical rules. Leibniz's own stepped reckoner, which could perform the four arithmetic operations, showed his interest in mechanising calculation, though the logical calculus itself was never built.

Legacy

Leibniz's manuscripts on the calculus lay largely unpublished for two centuries. When they came to light, scholars recognised anticipations of modern symbolic logic: George Boole's algebra of logic, Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift, and ultimately formal systems, automated reasoning and computer science have all been viewed as partial realisations of the programme.
