Coulomb's law

Coulomb's inverse-square law, or simply Coulomb's law, is an experimental law of physics that calculates the amount of force between two electrically charged particles at rest. The electric force between charged bodies at rest is conventionally called the electrostatic force or Coulomb force.

The law was first published in 1785 by French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, who measured the force using a torsion balance. In scalar form:

F = ke |q1q2| / r2

where ke is Coulomb's constant (approximately 8.988 × 109 N·m²/C²), q1 and q2 are the signed magnitudes of the charges, and r is the distance between them. The force is attractive if the charges have opposite signs and repulsive if they have the same sign. In vector form the force acts along the line joining the two charges.

Relation to other laws

Coulomb's law is analogous to Newton's law of universal gravitation, also an inverse-square law, but electrical forces are far stronger than gravitational ones for elementary particles, and charges — unlike masses — come in two signs. The law is a special case (electrostatics) of the Lorentz force law and underlies the definition of the electric field: the field due to a point charge is E = keq/r² radial. Within quantum electrodynamics, it is understood as arising from the exchange of virtual photons.

Limitations

The law strictly applies to point charges at rest in vacuum. For charges in a dielectric medium the force is reduced by the relative permittivity; at atomic scales quantum effects and, for very large fields, vacuum polarisation modify the simple picture.
