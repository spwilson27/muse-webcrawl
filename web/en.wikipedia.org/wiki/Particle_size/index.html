Particle size

Particle size is a notion introduced for comparing dimensions of solid particles (grains), liquid particles (droplets), or gaseous particles (bubbles). Because real particles are rarely spherical, "size" is expressed through an equivalent diameter — the diameter of a sphere that has the same measured property, such as volume, projected area, settling velocity or sieving behaviour, as the particle in question.

Definitions

Different measurement techniques give different equivalent diameters:

  • Sieve diameter — the width of the smallest square aperture through which the particle will pass.
  • Stokes diameter — the diameter of a sphere with the same density and settling velocity in a fluid.
  • Volume-equivalent diameter — from the particle's measured volume.
  • Feret and Martin diameters — statistical measures taken from microscope images.

Collections of particles are described by a particle size distribution, summarised by statistics such as the median diameter D50 and the percentiles D10 and D90.

Measurement

Methods include sieving, sedimentation, microscopy and image analysis, electrical sensing zone (Coulter) counting, and laser diffraction and dynamic light scattering, the latter being standard for sub-micrometre suspensions and nanoparticles. The international standard ISO 9276 governs the representation of results.

Applications

Particle size controls the behaviour of powders and suspensions across industries: the dissolution rate of pharmaceuticals, the texture of foods and cosmetics, the strength of cement and ceramics, soil classification (sand, silt and clay), and air quality regulation, where particulate matter is classed as PM10 and PM2.5 by aerodynamic diameter.
