Montgomery bus boycott

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Montgomery bus boycott was a civil-rights protest during which African Americans refused to ride city buses in Montgomery, Alabama, to protest segregated seating. It began on 5 December 1955, four days after Rosa Parks, a seamstress and NAACP activist, was arrested for refusing to give up her seat to a white passenger, and it lasted 381 days, sustained by an extraordinary community car-pool and walking network organised through Black churches and the Montgomery Improvement Association, whose young president, the 26-year-old pastor Martin Luther King Jr., became the movement's voice.

The boycott ended when the US Supreme Court affirmed Browder v. Gayle (1956), holding bus segregation unconstitutional; Montgomery's buses were integrated on 21 December 1956. Its significance far exceeded the city: it demonstrated mass nonviolent direct action as a workable strategy (building on earlier boycotts and the theory of Jo Ann Robinson's Women's Political Council, which had prepared the action), fused church organisation with constitutional litigation, created the template (and leadership) for the Civil rights movement, and showed the economic leverage of a community that supplied the majority of the bus system's riders.
