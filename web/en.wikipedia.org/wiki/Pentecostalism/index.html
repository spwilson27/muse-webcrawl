Pentecostalism is a form of Christianity that emphasises a direct personal experience of God through baptism with the Holy Spirit, evidenced, in its classical forms, by speaking in tongues (glossolalia). Named for the day of Pentecost (Acts 2), it emerged from the Holiness movement in the United States — with key beginnings at Topeka, Kansas (1901) and the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles (1906–1909) led by William J. Seymour — and has become one of the largest and fastest-growing movements in world Christianity.

Beliefs and practice

Pentecostals are generally evangelical in doctrine, adding the distinctive "second blessing" of Spirit baptism, gifts of the Spirit (healing, prophecy, tongues, interpretation), and an expectation of Christ's imminent return. Worship is expressive and participatory. Major denominations include the Assemblies of God (founded 1914), the Church of God in Christ, and numerous Oneness Pentecostal bodies, which baptise in Jesus' name and hold a non-trinitarian doctrine of God. The related charismatic movement from the 1960s carried Pentecostal practices into mainline Protestant and Catholic churches.

Global growth

Pentecostalism has grown explosively in Latin America (notably Brazil), sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Asia, often through independent "neo-Pentecostal" megachurches that also teach forms of prosperity theology — the belief that faith yields health and wealth — which is celebrated by adherents and criticised by other Christians. Scholars estimate Pentecostals and charismatics together at several hundred million, roughly a quarter of all Christians, making the movement central to any account of contemporary Christianity's shifting centre of gravity toward the Global South.
