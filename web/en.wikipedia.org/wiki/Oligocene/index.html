The Oligocene is a geologic epoch of the Paleogene Period that extends from about 33.9 million to 23.03 million years before the present (Ma). It follows the Eocene and is followed by the Miocene, the first epoch of the Neogene. The name, coined by Heinrich Ernst Beyrich in 1854, comes from the Greek oligos ("few") and kainos ("new"), referring to the paucity of additional modern mollusc species compared with earlier strata.

The Oligocene began with a major cooling event, the Eocene–Oligocene transition, when the Antarctic ice sheet formed and global sea levels fell. The epoch saw the spread of grasslands, the decline of tropical forests, and continued diversification of modern mammal groups.

Climate

Climates cooled markedly at the start of the epoch (the Oi-1 glaciation event, around 33.5 Ma) as the Drake Passage and Tasmanian Gateway opened, allowing the Antarctic Circumpolar Current to develop and thermally isolating Antarctica. A further cooling step occurred in the late Oligocene, partly offset by warming near the Oligocene–Miocene boundary.

Oceans and continents

India continued its collision with Asia, uplifting the Himalayas, while the Tethys Ocean narrowed. Australia separated fully from Antarctica, and South America remained an island continent with a distinctive endemic fauna.

Life

The epoch opened with the Grande Coupure ("great break") in Europe, a wave of extinctions and immigrations of Asian mammal groups. Faunas became recognisably modern: early horses, camels and ruminants diversified in North America, and giant forms such as Paraceratherium, among the largest land mammals ever, roamed Asia. The first anthropoid primates and early elephants appeared in Africa.

Subdivisions

The Oligocene is divided into two stages: the Rupelian (33.9–27.8 Ma) and the Chattian (27.8–23.03 Ma).
