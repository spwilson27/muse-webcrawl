Picard–Lindelöf theorem

In mathematics, specifically the study of differential equations, the Picard–Lindelöf theorem gives conditions under which an initial value problem has a unique solution. It is also known as Picard's existence theorem, the Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem, Lindelöf's theorem, and the existence and uniqueness theorem for first-order ordinary differential equations. The theorem is named after Émile Picard, Ernst Lindelöf, Rudolf Lipschitz and Augustin-Louis Cauchy.


Picard iterates converging to the unique solution of an initial value problem.

Statement

Consider the initial value problem

dy/dt = f(t, y),   y(t0) = y0,

where f is uniformly Lipschitz continuous in y (with Lipschitz constant L) and continuous in t on a rectangle containing (t0, y0). Then, for some value ε > 0, there exists a unique solution y(t) to the problem on the interval [t0 − ε, t0 + ε].

Proof sketch

The proof converts the differential equation into an equivalent integral equation and applies the Banach fixed-point theorem to the integral operator

(Tφ)(t) = y0 + ∫t0t f(s, φ(s)) ds.

Successive applications of T starting from the constant function φ0(t) = y0 produce the Picard iterates, which converge uniformly to the unique fixed point — the solution. Gronwall's inequality can be used to show uniqueness directly.

Remarks

The theorem guarantees only local existence: solutions may fail to extend to the whole domain if they "blow up" in finite time (as with dy/dt = y²). Continuity of f alone guarantees existence (Peano existence theorem) but not uniqueness; the classic example dy/dt = y2/3, y(0) = 0, has infinitely many solutions. The result generalises to systems of equations, to complex variables, and to certain partial differential equations through the Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem.

History

Cauchy gave an early existence proof in the 1820s under differentiability assumptions. Lipschitz's condition appeared in the work of Rudolf Lipschitz; Émile Picard developed the method of successive approximations in the 1890s, and Ernst Lindelöf published further refinements in 1894.
