Scopes Trial


The trial in Dayton, Tennessee, July 1925.
State of Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes
CourtCriminal Court of Rhea County, Tennessee
Date10–21 July 1925
DefendantJohn Thomas Scopes
ChargeTeaching evolution in violation of the Butler Act
ProsecutionWilliam Jennings Bryan (special prosecutor)
DefenceClarence Darrow, Dudley Field Malone, Arthur Garfield Hays
VerdictGuilty; $100 fine (later overturned on a technicality)

The Scopes Trial, formally The State of Tennessee v. John Thomas Scopes and popularly known as the Scopes Monkey Trial, was a legal case from 10 to 21 July 1925 in which a high school teacher, John T. Scopes, was accused of violating Tennessee's Butler Act, which had made it illegal for public school teachers to teach human evolution. The trial was deliberately staged to attract publicity to the small town of Dayton, Tennessee, and became a national spectacle pitting modern science against biblical literalism.

Background

The Butler Act, passed in March 1925 and signed by Governor Austin Peay, prohibited teaching "any theory that denies the Story of the Divine Creation of man as taught in the Bible, and to teach instead that man has descended from a lower order of animals." The American Civil Liberties Union offered to finance a test case; Dayton civic leaders, hoping to boost their town, persuaded Scopes — a 24-year-old teacher and football coach who had substituted in a biology class — to stand accused. Three-time Democratic presidential nominee and fundamentalist leader William Jennings Bryan volunteered to assist the prosecution; celebrity defence attorney Clarence Darrow volunteered for the defence.

Trial

Hundreds of reporters, including H. L. Mencken, descended on Dayton, and proceedings were broadcast live on radio — a first for an American trial. The judge excluded scientific testimony on evolution itself, so the defence's climax came outdoors on 20 July, when Darrow called Bryan to the stand as a Bible expert and subjected him to a celebrated cross-examination on the literal truth of Genesis, which Bryan endured while insisting on the Bible's inerrancy. The next day the jury convicted Scopes after nine minutes of deliberation; he was fined $100. Bryan died five days after the trial ended.

Aftermath and legacy

The Tennessee Supreme Court upheld the Butler Act in 1927 but overturned Scopes's conviction because the judge, not the jury, had set the fine, and the prosecution was dropped. The Act remained on the books until 1967; the U.S. Supreme Court struck down such bans in Epperson v. Arkansas (1968). The trial — fictionalised in the 1955 play and 1960 film Inherit the Wind — remains an emblem of the American culture wars over evolution in public schools, though historians note that contemporary coverage and the play distorted the Dayton community and the trial's legal issues, and that the textbook Scopes used actually endorsed evolution.
