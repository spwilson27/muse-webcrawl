Phoebus cartel

Phoebus cartel
Founded23 December 1924, Geneva, Switzerland
Dissolved1939
MembersOsram, Philips, General Electric, Compagnie des Lampes and others
PurposeStandardisation and market control of incandescent light bulbs

The Phoebus cartel was an organisation that controlled the manufacture and sale of incandescent light bulbs worldwide from 1924 to 1939. Its members included Osram, Philips, General Electric, Associated Electrical Industries and the Compagnie des Lampes. The cartel is one of the most famous examples of planned obsolescence.

Activities

Founded in Geneva in 1924 as "Phoebus S.A. Compagnie Industrielle pour le Développement de l'Éclairage", the cartel standardised bulbs — including the Edison screw base and standard voltages — divided world markets among members, and set quotas. Its most notorious activity was the agreement to limit the standard lifetime of bulbs to 1,000 hours, shorter than the 1,500–2,000 hours many bulbs of the era could achieve. Members' bulbs were tested in a laboratory in Switzerland, and companies whose bulbs lasted too long were fined. Internal documents later confirmed the policy explicitly: bulb life was to be deliberately shortened to increase replacement sales.

End and legacy

The cartel effectively collapsed with the outbreak of the Second World War in 1939, and its practices were later litigated in the United States; in 1949 a US court found General Electric's related arrangements violated the Sherman Antitrust Act. The 1,000-hour standard nevertheless persisted in the industry for decades. The cartel remains the classic case study in planned obsolescence, frequently contrasted with the Centennial Light in Livermore, California, which has burned almost continuously since 1901.
