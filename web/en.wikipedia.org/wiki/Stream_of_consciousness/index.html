Stream of consciousness

Stream of consciousness
Term coined byWilliam James (psychology); applied to literature by May Sinclair
Key practitionersJames Joyce, Virginia Woolf, William Faulkner, Marcel Proust

Stream of consciousness is a narrative mode or method of narration that depicts the multitudinous thoughts and feelings which pass through the mind of a narrator or character. The term was coined by the psychologist William James in The Principles of Psychology (1890) and was applied to literature by the critic May Sinclair in 1918.

Technique

Stream-of-consciousness writing typically renders interior monologue with loose, associative syntax, minimal punctuation, abrupt shifts of topic, and a blending of memory, sensation, and reflection, in an attempt to mimic the actual flow of thought rather than a tidy summary of it. It is a hallmark of literary modernism.

Major works

  • James Joyce, Ulysses (1922) and Finnegans Wake (1939)
  • Virginia Woolf, Mrs Dalloway (1925) and To the Lighthouse (1927)
  • William Faulkner, The Sound and the Fury (1929)
  • Marcel Proust, In Search of Lost Time
