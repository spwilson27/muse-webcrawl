African-American Vernacular English

African-American Vernacular English
Also called: AAVE, African American English, Ebonics
Region: United States
Type: Dialect of English
Features: Habitual "be", copula deletion, negative concord

African-American Vernacular English (AAVE) is a variety of American English spoken principally by working- and middle-class African Americans. Linguists regard it as a fully systematic dialect with its own phonological and grammatical rules.

Features

Characteristic features include habitual be to mark recurring actions ("He be working"), deletion of the copula where standard English contracts it, negative concord ("ain't nobody"), and distinctive phonology such as final consonant cluster reduction. Its tense–aspect system marks distinctions, such as stressed BIN for remote past, not expressed in standard varieties.

History and study

Debate continues over AAVE's origins — in English dialects, African languages, or creole formation. William Labov's sociolinguistic research established its systematicity. In 1996 the Oakland school board's "Ebonics" resolution brought AAVE to national attention.
