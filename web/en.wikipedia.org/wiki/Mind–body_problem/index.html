Mind–body problem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The mind–body problem is a central problem in the philosophy of mind concerning the relationship between thought and consciousness in the human mind and the body. It addresses questions such as: Are the mind and body separate substances, or one and the same? How can mental states — beliefs, desires, sensations — cause physical events, and vice versa? And what is the place of mind in a physical world?

Descartes proposed that mind and body interact at the pineal gland.

Historical background

Plato held that the soul is immortal and distinct from the body, while Aristotle treated the soul as the "form" of a living body. The problem took its modern form with René Descartes, who argued in the Meditations (1641) that mind (res cogitans, a thinking, unextended substance) and body (res extensa, an extended, unthinking substance) are really distinct — a view known as substance dualism. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed Descartes on the obvious difficulty: how can an immaterial mind causally interact with a material body?

Dualist responses

  • Interactionism — mind and body are distinct but causally influence each other (Descartes; later Karl Popper and John Eccles).
  • Occasionalism — God produces the appropriate effect on each occasion (Nicolas Malebranche).
  • Parallelism / pre-established harmony — mind and body run in divinely coordinated parallel without interaction (Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz).
  • Epiphenomenalism — the physical causes the mental, but mental states have no physical effects.
  • Property dualism — there is one substance but two kinds of properties, physical and mental; David Chalmers defends a form of this, and argues consciousness poses a "hard problem" not explained by physical processes alone.

Monist responses

  • Physicalism / materialism — only the physical exists; mental states are identical to, or realised by, brain states. Variants include the identity theory (Ullin Place, J. J. C. Smart), functionalism, and eliminative materialism.
  • Idealism — reality is fundamentally mental (George Berkeley).
  • Neutral monism — the basic stuff of the world is neither mental nor physical (Bertrand Russell, William James).
  • Dual-aspect theory — mind and body are two aspects of a single underlying reality, associated with Spinoza.

Modern discussion

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century debates involve thought experiments such as Frank Jackson's "Mary's room" (the knowledge argument), Chalmers' philosophical zombies, Thomas Nagel's "What is it like to be a bat?", and Joseph Levine's "explanatory gap". Many philosophers and neuroscientists adopt some form of physicalism, while disagreeing over whether the subjective character of experience — qualia — can be fully explained in physical terms.
