Akinesia

Akinesia is the absence or loss of the ability to initiate voluntary movement. It is a cardinal symptom of Parkinson's disease and other parkinsonian syndromes, and sits at the severe end of the spectrum of bradykinesia (slowness of movement) and hypokinesia (reduced amplitude of movement).

Features

Patients with akinesia experience difficulty starting movements such as rising from a chair, taking the first step, or beginning to write. Tasks requiring sequential or simultaneous movements are particularly affected. Sudden, transient episodes of akinesia known as "freezing" may occur, classically when passing through doorways or turning. In severe form, as in the akinetic crisis of advanced Parkinson's disease or neuroleptic malignant syndrome, the patient may become almost completely immobile.

Causes and mechanism

Akinesia in Parkinson's disease results from the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra and consequent dysfunction of the basal ganglia motor circuits. It may also be produced by dopamine-blocking drugs (antipsychotics), and occurs in other neurodegenerative conditions such as multiple system atrophy and progressive supranuclear palsy.

Treatment

Management is directed at the underlying cause, principally dopaminergic therapy with levodopa and dopamine agonists, which improves initiation of movement, though freezing episodes may respond less well. Physiotherapy, cueing strategies (visual or auditory), and deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus can help selected patients.
