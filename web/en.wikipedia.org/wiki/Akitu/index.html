Akitu

Akitu (Sumerian: á-ki-ti; Akkadian: akītu) was a spring festival in ancient Mesopotamia, the most important religious celebration of the Babylonian year. Held in the month of Nisanu (March–April), around the spring equinox, it marked the New Year and the renewal of kingship and cosmic order.

The Babylonian festival

In Babylon the festival lasted up to twelve days. The creation epic Enūma Eliš was recited before the statue of Marduk, recounting his defeat of the chaos goddess Tiamat. Statues of the gods were brought in procession from their cities to the Akitu house (bīt akītu) outside the city walls. A striking rite involved the high priest ritually humbling the king: the king's regalia were removed, his cheek was slapped, and he knelt to declare that he had not sinned against the god or the city — a ceremony believed to secure Marduk's renewed mandate for the king's reign and the land's fertility.

History

The Akitu originated in Sumerian agricultural festivals connected with the barley harvest and was celebrated in several Mesopotamian cities, each honouring its own patron deity. The Babylonian version rose to prominence with Marduk's supremacy and continued into the Hellenistic period. Elements of the New Year festival survive in the modern Assyrian celebration of Kha b-Nisan and are echoed in the timing of other Near Eastern spring festivals.
