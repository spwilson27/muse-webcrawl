Gossypium barbadense is one of several species of cotton. It is in the mallow family and has been cultivated since ancient times, particularly in coastal regions of South America. It produces extra-long staple (ELS) fibres and is the source of premium cottons sold under names such as Pima cotton, Egyptian cotton, and Sea Island cotton.

Description

G. barbadense is a tropical, frost-sensitive perennial shrub reaching 1-3 metres in height. It bears yellow flowers with a red or purple centre spot. Its fibres are notably long and silky compared with the more widely grown Gossypium hirsutum (upland cotton), making them suited to fine, strong yarns.

History

The species was domesticated in the coastal regions of present-day Ecuador and Peru at least 4,000-5,000 years ago. It spread through the Caribbean — the species epithet refers to Barbados — and in the 19th century, long-staple varieties grown on the Sea Islands of the southeastern United States commanded high prices. A variety developed in the American Southwest in the early 20th century was named Pima, in honour of the Pima people who assisted in its development.

Uses

ELS cotton is used for high-quality shirts, towels, and luxury textiles. The seed yields an oil used in cooking and cosmetics, and the plant has traditional medicinal uses in its regions of origin.
