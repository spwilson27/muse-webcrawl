Standard Average European

Standard Average European (SAE) is a concept introduced by the American linguist Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897–1941) to describe a group of features shared by many European languages. Whorf used the term to contrast the "average" Western European language with languages of radically different structure that he had studied, such as Hopi, in developing his ideas on linguistic relativity.

Features

Whorf listed grammatical features typical of SAE languages, including:

  • a distinction between nouns and verbs as basic word classes;
  • plural marking and classifiers of quantity tied to a strict count/mass distinction;
  • a tense system dividing time linearly into past, present and future;
  • subject–predicate sentence structure with agreement;
  • metaphors of time as a measurable, spatialised substance — time that can be saved, wasted, counted in units.

Later research in linguistic typology developed the idea into the notion of a European Sprachbund (linguistic area): languages such as French, German, Spanish, Italian, English, Dutch and their neighbours share features acquired through millennia of contact, including definite and indefinite articles, a "have"-perfect, relative clauses introduced by relative pronouns, participial passive constructions, and a nominative–accusative alignment with flexible word order. In the EUROTYP project, Martin Haspelmath enumerated a set of such areal features and identified a core area (roughly French, German, Dutch and northern Italian) surrounded by a periphery, with languages such as Basque, Celtic, and the eastern European languages participating to a lesser degree.

Significance

The concept remains influential in typology and in critiques of linguistic practice: much of what textbooks treat as "normal" grammar is simply SAE grammar, and categories distilled from European languages may distort the description of languages elsewhere. The term is also invoked in debates over the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, since Whorf's point was that SAE speakers mistake the categories of their own languages for universal features of reality.
