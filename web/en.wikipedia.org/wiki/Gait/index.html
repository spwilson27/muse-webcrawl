Gait

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gait is the pattern of movement of the limbs of animals, including humans, during locomotion over a solid surface. Most animals use a variety of gaits, choosing a speed-appropriate pattern; humans' natural gaits are walking and running, which emerge in early childhood without instruction. The study of gait — gait analysis — is used in medicine to diagnose neurological and musculoskeletal disorders, in sports science, in the design of prostheses and robots, and in biometrics, since an individual's gait is highly distinctive.

The human gait cycle: stance and swing phases alternate for each leg.

The human gait cycle

One gait cycle runs from one heel strike of a foot to the next heel strike of the same foot. It divides into a stance phase (about 60 per cent of the cycle in walking, when the foot is on the ground: heel strike, foot flat, midstance, heel-off, toe-off) and a swing phase (about 40 per cent, when the foot swings forward). Walking keeps at least one foot on the ground at all times, with periods of double support; running abolishes double support and adds an aerial phase. Key measurements include cadence, step and stride length, walking speed and the vertical oscillation of the centre of mass, which behaves like an inverted pendulum in efficient walking.

Animal gaits

Quadrupeds use gaits such as the walk, trot, canter, gallop, pace and bound, classifiable by footfall sequence and symmetry; horses change gait at characteristic speeds. Eadweard Muybridge's pioneering 1870s photographs of a galloping horse — proving all four feet leave the ground — founded both gait science and motion pictures. Bipeds, arthropods and swimmers show equally diverse patterns, and gait transitions are a classic subject in biomechanics.

Clinical significance

Characteristic abnormal gaits help localise disease: the shuffling, festinating gait of Parkinson's disease; the hemiplegic gait after stroke; the waddling gait of muscular dystrophy; antalgic (pain-avoiding), ataxic and Trendelenburg gaits. Instrumented gait laboratories use force plates, motion capture and electromyography to quantify movement for diagnosis, rehabilitation and orthopaedic planning.
