A nature reserve (also known as a wildlife refuge, wildlife sanctuary, biosphere reserve, or natural reserve) is a protected area of importance for flora, fauna, or features of geological or other special interest, which is reserved and managed for purposes of conservation and to provide special opportunities for study or research. It may be designated by government institutions in some countries, or by private landowners, such as charities and research institutions.

History

The idea of protecting areas for nature has ancient roots in hunting reserves and sacred groves, but the modern nature reserve dates to the 19th century. Drachenfels in Germany was protected in 1836 when the Prussian government purchased the mountain to prevent quarrying; in Britain, Charles Waterton established what is often called the world's first nature reserve at Walton Hall in the 1820s. The first American refuges followed with the federal bird reservations of Theodore Roosevelt, beginning with Pelican Island, Florida, in 1903.

Purposes

Nature reserves protect biodiversity by safeguarding habitats from development, agriculture, and exploitation. They serve as refuges for endangered species, as sites for ecological research and monitoring, as reservoirs of genetic diversity, and — where public access is permitted — for environmental education and recreation. Reserves are often categorised by the IUCN protected-area system; Category Ia strict nature reserves are set aside primarily for science with minimal human visitation, while Category IV areas are actively managed for particular species or habitats.

Designations by country

In the United Kingdom, National Nature Reserves are declared by the statutory nature conservation bodies under the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 and the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981; local nature reserves are designated by local authorities. The United States maintains the National Wildlife Refuge System, administered by the Fish and Wildlife Service, encompassing more than 560 refuges. Other countries use designations such as zapovednik (Russia, strict scientific reserves), parque natural (Spain), and national nature reserves in China. UNESCO designates biosphere reserves under its Man and the Biosphere Programme, combining core protected areas with zones of sustainable use.

Strict nature reserves

Strict nature reserves exclude most human activity to allow ecosystems to function undisturbed and to serve as scientific reference sites. Entry may be limited to authorised researchers. Examples include the Kronotsky and Barguzin zapovedniks in Russia and various IUCN Category Ia reserves worldwide. Such strict protection complements more accessible reserves and national parks in global conservation strategy, including the international "30 by 30" goal of protecting 30% of land and sea by 2030.
