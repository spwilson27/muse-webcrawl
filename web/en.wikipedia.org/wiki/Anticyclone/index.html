Anticyclone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An anticyclone is a large-scale circulation of winds around a central region of high atmospheric pressure — clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, the opposite of a cyclone. Anticyclones are associated with calm, settled weather: air descends and warms, suppressing cloud formation, so they bring clear skies — hot weather in summer and cold, frosty weather in winter, sometimes with fog.

Structure and behaviour

Surface anticyclones form where air aloft converges and sinks (subsidence), warming adiabatically and creating a temperature inversion that traps pollution and fog near the ground. The largest and most persistent anticyclones are the semi-permanent subtropical highs over the oceans — such as the Azores High — and the intense, cold Siberian High of winter. Slow-moving "blocking" anticyclones can deflect storm tracks for days or weeks, causing droughts and heat waves, as in the European heat waves of 2003 and 2022.

Anticyclones on other planets

Jupiter's Great Red Spot is an enormous anticyclonic storm that has persisted for centuries, and similar long-lived high-pressure vortices occur on Neptune.
