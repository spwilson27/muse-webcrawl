Malapropism

A malapropism (also called a malaprop, acyrologia, or Dogberryism) is the erroneous use of a word in place of a similar-sounding one, often with an unintentionally amusing effect. Malapropisms are usually made by speakers who are reaching for a word beyond their vocabulary and substitute one that sounds similar but means something quite different.

Origin of the term

The term derives from the character Mrs. Malaprop in Richard Brinsley Sheridan's 1775 play The Rivals, whose name comes from the French mal à propos ("inappropriate"). Mrs. Malaprop's famous errors include describing someone as "as headstrong as an allegory on the banks of the Nile" (for alligator) and praising "a nice derangement of epitaphs" (for arrangement of epithets). The earlier character Dogberry in Shakespeare's Much Ado About Nothing commits similar errors, giving rise to the alternative name "Dogberryism."

Examples and analysis

Examples include saying "a vast suppository of information" for repository, or "the pineapple of politeness" for pinnacle. Linguistically, malapropisms reveal how words are stored in the mind by sound and meaning: the substituted word typically matches the intended word in stress pattern, number of syllables, and part of speech. Malapropisms differ from spoonerisms (transposed sounds) and from eggcorns, in which the substituted phrase makes a kind of sense to the speaker.
