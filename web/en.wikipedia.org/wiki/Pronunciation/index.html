Pronunciation

Pronunciation is the way in which a word or a language is spoken. This may refer to generally agreed-upon sequences of sounds used in speaking a given word or language in a specific dialect ("correct" or "standard" pronunciation) or simply the way a particular individual speaks a word or language. Pronunciation is studied within the fields of phonetics and phonology.

Aspects of pronunciation

Pronunciation involves several layers of structure:

  • Segmental features: the individual sounds — consonants and vowels — that make up words. Languages differ in which sounds they use and in which contrasts are meaningful (phonemes).
  • Suprasegmental features: properties extending over more than one sound, including stress, intonation, rhythm, tone, and length. In tone languages such as Mandarin Chinese, pitch distinguishes word meanings.
  • Connected speech processes: assimilation, elision, linking, and reduction, which alter sounds when words are spoken in sequence rather than in isolation.

Notation

Linguists record pronunciation using the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), a standardised system in which each symbol represents one sound. A broad (phonemic) transcription is written between slashes / /, while a narrow (phonetic) transcription appears in square brackets [ ]. Dictionaries often supplement IPA with respelling systems based on ordinary orthography.

Variation and change

Pronunciation varies by region, social class, age, and context; such patterned variation is a principal object of study in sociolinguistics. Prestige varieties — for example Received Pronunciation in Britain or General American in the United States — are social conventions rather than inherently "correct" forms. Over time, pronunciations change: the Great Vowel Shift, for instance, systematically altered the long vowels of English between roughly 1400 and 1700. Individuals may also adjust their pronunciation in a given interaction, a phenomenon known as accommodation.

Teaching and learning

Second-language learners typically transfer the sound patterns of their first language, producing a foreign accent. Pronunciation instruction aims at intelligibility rather than native-like perfection, focusing on phonemic contrasts, word stress, and intonation patterns that most affect comprehension.
