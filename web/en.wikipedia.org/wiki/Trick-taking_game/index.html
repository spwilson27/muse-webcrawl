Trick-taking game

TypeCard game family
PlayersTypically 2–8
ExamplesBridge, Whist, Hearts, Spades, Euchre, Skat, Tarot games

A trick-taking game is a card game in which play is centred on a series of finite rounds called tricks. In each trick, every player plays one card face up to the table; the cards are compared under the rules of the game, and the trick is won ("taken") by the player of the card judged strongest. The winner of a trick usually leads the first card of the next trick. Objectives vary: players may aim to win as many tricks as possible, to win tricks containing valuable cards, to avoid taking certain tricks or cards, or to win exactly a predicted number of tricks.

Basic structure

Most trick-taking games share a common framework:

  • The lead: the player to lead plays a card of some suit, called the led suit.
  • Following suit: the other players in turn must, if able, play a card of the led suit. A player who cannot follow suit may play any card — discarding an unwanted card or playing a trump.
  • Winning the trick: if no trump has been played, the trick is won by the highest card of the led suit. If one or more trumps were played, the highest trump wins.
  • Trump: a suit (or, in some games, particular cards) designated as ranking above all plain suits for the duration of a hand.

Classification

Game historian David Parlett classifies trick-taking games by their objectives into several broad families:

  • Plain-trick games — value lies in the number of tricks won, not their contents; e.g. Whist and Contract bridge.
  • Point-trick games — cards taken in tricks carry point values; e.g. Skat, Tarot games, and Manille.
  • Trick-avoidance games — players try not to take certain cards or tricks; e.g. Hearts, where hearts and the queen of spades carry penalty points.
  • Auction and prediction games — players bid or predict the number of tricks they will take, as in Spades and Oh Hell.

History

Trick-taking is one of the oldest and most widespread mechanisms in card play. Games recognisably based on tricks were played in Europe by the fifteenth century — the Tarot pack, created in Italy in the 1440s, introduced the idea of a permanent trump suit — and related games developed independently elsewhere, such as the Chinese game later popularised worldwide. Whist, enormously popular in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, gave rise to Bridge, which became the dominant trick-taking game of the twentieth century and an internationally organised mind sport.

Terminology

  • Lead — to play the first card of a trick.
  • Follow suit — to play a card of the suit led.
  • Ruff / trump in — to play a trump when unable (or not required) to follow suit.
  • Discard / slough — to play a plain-suit card of another suit, neither following nor trumping.
  • Revoke / renege — to fail to follow suit when able, usually penalised.
  • Void — to hold no cards of a given suit.
