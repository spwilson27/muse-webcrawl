A fortune cookie is a crisp and sugary cookie wafer made from flour, sugar, vanilla, and sesame seed oil with a piece of paper inside, a "fortune", on which is an aphorism or a vague prophecy. The message inside may also include a list of lucky numbers or a Chinese phrase with translation.

Fortune cookies are often served as a dessert in Chinese restaurants in the United States and other Western countries, but are not a tradition in China itself.

History

The precise origin is contested. Japanese tsujiura senbei — larger, darker crackers containing fortunes, made near Kyoto — predate the American cookie, and Japanese immigrants in California, notably Makoto Hagiwara of San Francisco's Japanese Tea Garden, are credited with serving an adapted version from around the 1890s–1910s. A rival claim credits David Jung, a Cantonese immigrant in Los Angeles, around 1918. A mock court trial in San Francisco in 1983 "ruled" in favour of the San Francisco claim. During the Second World War, with Japanese-American bakers interned, production passed largely to Chinese-American businesses, cementing the cookie's association with Chinese restaurants.

Production

Modern cookies are baked as flat discs, folded around the paper slip while still warm and pliable, and harden as they cool. The largest manufacturers produce millions of cookies per day, and the writing of fortunes is a small literary industry in itself.
