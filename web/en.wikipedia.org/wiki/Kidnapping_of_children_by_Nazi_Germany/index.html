Kidnapping of children by Nazi Germany

A Lebensborn children's home during the war
Period1939–1945
VictimsChildren from occupied countries, chiefly Poland
Estimated numbersUp to 200,000 from Poland alone (estimates vary widely)
OrganisationsSS, Lebensborn, RuSHA
PurposeForced "Germanisation" of children judged racially valuable

During the Second World War, the kidnapping of children by Nazi Germany (Polish: rabunek dzieci) formed part of the regime's programme to seize and forcibly "Germanise" children from occupied countries — above all Poland — whom SS racial experts judged to possess supposedly Nordic or "Aryan" features. The children were taken from their parents, orphanages or the streets, given new German names and identities, and placed with SS families, German foster parents or institutions of the Lebensborn programme.

The programme

The policy was driven by Heinrich Himmler, who reasoned that children of "good blood" in the conquered East should be recovered for the German nation rather than left to strengthen enemy populations. Selection was carried out by officials of the SS Race and Settlement Main Office (RuSHA) using pseudo-scientific racial examinations — measuring skulls, photographing profiles, and grading eye and hair colour. Children deemed racially valuable were sent to transit camps and Lebensborn homes, where their birth records were falsified, their native language forbidden, and many were told their parents were dead. Those who failed selection could be sent back, consigned to children's camps, or murdered; some older children ended in concentration camps.

Scale

The programme operated across occupied Europe — in Poland, Yugoslavia (Slovenia), Czechoslovakia, the Soviet territories, Norway and elsewhere. Polish historians estimate that around 200,000 Polish children were deported for Germanisation, of whom only a fraction — perhaps 15–20 per cent — were recovered after the war. Estimates differ considerably between national historiographies, and many records were deliberately destroyed.

After the war

After 1945, Allied and Polish search services traced some children through surviving Lebensborn files, but tracing was obstructed by falsified documents and by foster families who concealed children's origins; many children, raised believing themselves German, never learned the truth. The kidnapping programme featured in the RuSHA trial at Nuremberg, where Lebensborn officials were tried — though the court treated the organisation's kidnapping activities leniently, a judgement later criticised. In postwar memory, kidnapped children (in Poland called dzieci rabowane) became an enduring symbol of German crimes, and survivors' associations have campaigned for recognition and compensation.
