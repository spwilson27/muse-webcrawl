Dopamine receptors are a class of G protein-coupled receptors that bind the neurotransmitter dopamine. Five subtypes, D1 through D5, are divided into two families: the D1-like family (D1 and D5), which typically raises cyclic AMP through Gs proteins, and the D2-like family (D2, D3 and D4), which lowers it through Gi proteins. They mediate dopamine's roles in movement, reward, motivation, learning, and hormonal control.

Distribution and function

D1 and D2 receptors are abundant in the striatum, where they mark the two principal populations of medium spiny neurons of the direct and indirect basal-ganglia pathways. D2 receptors on pituitary lactotrophs inhibit prolactin release; D3 receptors concentrate in limbic areas such as the nucleus accumbens; D4 receptors in the prefrontal cortex have been linked to attention.

Clinical significance

Almost all antipsychotic drugs block D2 receptors, the basis of the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia; conversely, dopamine agonists stimulating these receptors treat Parkinson's disease and hyperprolactinaemia. Variants in the D4 receptor gene have been studied in relation to novelty-seeking and ADHD. Dopamine receptor signalling is also central to addiction research.
