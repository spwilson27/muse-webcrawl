Winston cone

For the inventor, see Roland Winston.

A Winston cone is a non-imaging light concentrator consisting of an off-axis paraboloidal reflector, designed by Roland Winston in 1966 and named after him. It is a type of compound parabolic concentrator (CPC). Radiation entering the wide entrance aperture within an accepted range of angles is reflected down the cone and emerges from a much smaller exit aperture, achieving concentration close to the maximum permitted by the laws of thermodynamics (conservation of étendue) for a given acceptance angle.

Design

The profile of the reflector is formed from parabolic segments whose axes are tilted relative to the cone axis. A three-dimensional Winston cone is made by rotating this profile, producing a trumpet-shaped mirror. Because the device forms no image, the geometry can be tailored purely to maximize throughput: light outside the acceptance half-angle is rejected, while light inside it eventually reaches the exit after one or more reflections. Truncated versions sacrifice a little concentration for large savings in mirror depth and material.

Applications

Winston cones are widely used to concentrate sunlight in solar thermal collectors, including evacuated-tube heaters, and to gather Cherenkov light in particle and gamma-ray detectors — a notable example being their use on the photomultiplier arrays of imaging atmospheric Cherenkov telescopes. They are also used in infrared instrumentation and for coupling light to small photovoltaic cells and photodetectors.
