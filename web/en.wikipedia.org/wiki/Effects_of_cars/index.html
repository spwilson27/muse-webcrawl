Effects of cars

The effects of cars in the developed and developing worlds since the twentieth century include environmental, social, and economic consequences, both positive and negative. Widespread car use has reshaped cities, patterns of work and leisure, and the environment, and the growth of global vehicle numbers has raised questions about sustainability, safety, and public health.

Environmental effects

Cars powered by internal combustion engines emit carbon dioxide and air pollutants including nitrogen oxides and particulates, contributing to climate change and urban air pollution. Road transport accounts for a substantial share of global greenhouse gas emissions. Manufacturing vehicles and building road infrastructure consume large quantities of energy and materials.

Safety

Road traffic crashes kill more than a million people each year worldwide and are a leading cause of death for young people. Improvements in vehicle design, road engineering, and traffic law have reduced death rates per kilometre travelled in many developed countries, even as total traffic has grown.

Social and economic effects

The car greatly increased personal mobility and flexibility of travel, enabling suburbanisation and new patterns of commuting, shopping, and leisure. At the same time, car-dependent development has been criticised for urban sprawl, traffic congestion, the decline of public transport, reduced physical activity contributing to obesity, and the severance of communities by major roads. Economically, the automobile industry is a major employer, while households and governments spend large sums on vehicles, fuel, parking, and road infrastructure.

Alternatives

Responses to the negative effects of cars include investment in public transport, cycling and walking infrastructure, electric vehicles, car sharing, congestion pricing, and land-use planning aimed at reducing the need to travel by car.
