Mission Revival architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mission Revival is an architectural style that drew inspiration from the Spanish colonial missions of California, flourishing in the United States from about 1890 to 1930. It was one of several revival styles celebrating regional Spanish heritage, alongside the related Spanish Colonial Revival.

Characteristics

Mission Revival buildings feature smooth stucco walls, red clay-tile roofs, wide eaves, round-arched windows and doorways arranged in arcades, and shaped mission-style parapets (espadañas) sometimes crowned with a small bell tower, or campanario. Interiors often include tiled floors and courtyards. The style was deliberately simple and picturesque rather than archaeologically exact, quoting the Alta California missions such as San Carlos de Borromeo and Santa Barbara.

History

The style emerged in California in the 1890s as the state romanticised its Spanish past — a trend boosted by Helen Hunt Jackson's novel Ramona (1884) and by the restoration of the ruined missions. Railways adopted it enthusiastically: the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe and Southern Pacific built dozens of Mission Revival depots across the Southwest, and architect A. Page Brown's California Building at the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition helped popularise it nationally. Architects such as Lester S. Moore and G. Stanley Wilson applied it to hotels, schools, churches, theatres and houses, most famously at the Mission Inn in Riverside, California — a fantastical, ever-expanding complex. After the 1915 Panama–California Exposition in San Diego, the more ornate Spanish Colonial Revival largely superseded it.
