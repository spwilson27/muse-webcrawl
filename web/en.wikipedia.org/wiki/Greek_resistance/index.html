The Greek resistance (Greek: Εθνική Αντίσταση, Ethnikí Antístasi, "National Resistance") is the name for the armed and political movements that resisted the Axis occupation of Greece from 1941 to 1944 during World War II. After the German, Italian, and Bulgarian occupation forces divided the country in April–May 1941, resistance organizations emerged ranging from guerrilla armies in the mountains to intelligence networks, sabotage groups, and illegal presses in the cities. The largest were the communist-led National Liberation Front (EAM) and its army ELAS; the republican National Democratic Greek League (EDES) under Napoleon Zervas; and the smaller EKKA. At its height the resistance liberated and administered large mountainous areas of the interior — "Free Greece" — tying down significant Axis forces and carrying out celebrated sabotage operations with British support.

Occupation and first acts of resistance

Greece entered the war in October 1940, repelling the Italian invasion from Albania before German forces overran the country in April 1941. The occupation regime brought catastrophic hardship, above all the Great Famine of the winter of 1941–42, in which hundreds of thousands died. Among the first symbolic acts of defiance was the lowering of the swastika flag from the Acropolis by Manolis Glezos and Apostolos Santas on the night of 30–31 May 1941. Strikes, demonstrations, and clandestine newspapers appeared despite mass arrests and executions.

The main organizations

EAM and ELAS

The National Liberation Front (EAM) was founded on 27 September 1941, principally at the initiative of the Communist Party of Greece (KKE), and built a mass movement numbering perhaps two million members, with affiliated youth (EPON), labour, and solidarity organizations. Its military arm, the Greek People's Liberation Army (ELAS), founded in 1942 and commanded in the field by Aris Velouchiotis with the political leadership of Stefanos Sarafis, became by 1943 the strongest guerrilla force in the country, controlling most of mountainous mainland Greece.

EDES and EKKA

The National Democratic Greek League (EDES), led by the republican officer Napoleon Zervas, operated mainly in Epirus and was the second-largest guerrilla army. EKKA, under Colonel Dimitrios Psarros, fielded the 5/42 Evzone Regiment in central Greece until it was destroyed by ELAS in April 1944 and Psarros killed.

Major operations and Free Greece

On the night of 25–26 November 1942, a combined force of ELAS and EDES guerrillas with British SOE commandos blew up the Gorgopotamos railway viaduct — Operation Harling — severing a key supply line during the North African campaign; it remains the most famous act of the Greek resistance. In 1943, after Italy's surrender, the resistance acquired large stocks of Italian weapons, and EAM established in the mountains a rudimentary state with schools, courts, and the March 1944 Political Committee of National Liberation (PEEA), the "Mountain Government".

Cooperation with the British-brokered National Bands Agreement (July 1943) periodically broke down into fighting between ELAS and rival groups — a "first round" of civil conflict in the winter of 1943–44 that foreshadowed the Greek Civil War. Agreements at Plaka (February 1944) and the Lebanon Conference (May 1944) brought EAM into a government of national unity under George Papandreou.

Liberation and aftermath

German forces withdrew from Athens on 12 October 1944, and the government-in-exile returned days later with British troops. Within weeks, disputes over the disarmament of ELAS erupted into the Dekemvriana fighting in Athens (December 1944–January 1945) between EAM/ELAS and government and British forces. The Varkiza Agreement of February 1945 ended the fighting but left deep divisions; persecution of former resistance members followed, and full-scale civil war broke out in 1946. The resistance was officially recognized by the Greek state only in 1982, when the PASOK government legislated the recognition of the "National Resistance" as a whole.
