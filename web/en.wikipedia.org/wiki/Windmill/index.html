Windmill

A windmill is a structure that converts wind power into rotational energy by means of vanes called sails or blades, especially to mill grain (gristmills). Historically, windmills were also used for pumping water, draining land, sawing timber, and other tasks. The term has since extended to the modern wind turbine used for generating electricity.

History

Vertical-axis windmills were described in Persia by the ninth century and were used for grinding grain and drawing water. Horizontal-axis windmills appeared in northwestern Europe in the late twelfth century. Dutch engineers greatly refined the technology, building tower mills, smock mills, and the drainage mills that reclaimed the polders of the Netherlands. By the nineteenth century there were tens of thousands of windmills in Europe before steam and internal-combustion engines displaced them.

Types

European mills are classified by construction: the post mill, whose body rotates around a central post; the tower mill, a fixed stone or brick tower with a rotating cap; and the smock mill, a tapered wooden structure. Multi-bladed farm windmills, developed in the United States from the 1850s, pumped water for farms, ranches, and railways across the Great Plains and the Australian outback.

Mechanics

Wind pressure on the sails turns the wind shaft, whose brake wheel drives a wallower and great spur wheel, transmitting power through gearing to millstones or machinery. Millers controlled power by reefing or — after the invention of spring and patent sails — by adjusting shutters on the sails.
