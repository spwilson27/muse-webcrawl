Pulmonary fibrosis

Pulmonary fibrosis is a chronic, progressive lung disease in which the interstitium — the tissue between the air sacs (alveoli) — becomes scarred and stiffened, impairing gas exchange. It is the end pattern of many interstitial lung diseases. The most common form, with no identifiable cause, is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).

Causes

Known causes include occupational and environmental exposures (asbestos, silica, coal dust, moulds and bird proteins causing hypersensitivity pneumonitis), connective-tissue diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and scleroderma, drugs including amiodarone, methotrexate and bleomycin, and radiation. Genetic factors contribute: mutations in telomere-maintenance genes (TERT, TERC) and the MUC5B promoter variant are associated with both familial and sporadic disease.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms develop gradually: exertional breathlessness, a dry cough, fatigue and weight loss. Examination often finds fine "Velcro" crackles at the lung bases and clubbing of the fingers. Pulmonary function tests show a restrictive pattern with reduced diffusing capacity (DLCO). High-resolution CT typically shows subpleural reticulation, traction bronchiectasis and honeycombing — the usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern.

Treatment and prognosis

Scarring is irreversible, but the antifibrotic drugs pirfenidone and nintedanib slow the decline in forced vital capacity, as shown in the ASCEND and INPULSIS trials. Management includes supplemental oxygen, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination and treatment of reflux and other comorbidities; lung transplantation is the only curative option for suitable patients. Median survival in IPF is about three to five years from diagnosis, and acute exacerbations carry high mortality.
