Māori language

Māori (te reo Māori)
Region: New Zealand
Family: Austronesian → Polynesian → Tahitic
Speakers: about 200,000 with some knowledge (2020s estimates)
Status: Official language of New Zealand (1987)
ISO 639-1: mi

The Māori language, known in Māori as te reo Māori or simply te reo ("the language"), is an Eastern Polynesian language spoken by the Māori people, the Indigenous population of mainland New Zealand. It is closely related to Cook Islands Māori and Tahitian, and became one of New Zealand's official languages in 1987.

Structure

Māori has a small phoneme inventory — ten consonants and five vowels, with vowel length distinctive and marked by macrons (ā, ē, ī, ō, ū) — and open syllables. Its grammar uses particles to mark tense, aspect, and case, and word order is typically verb–subject–object.

Decline and revitalisation

During the twentieth century the language declined sharply as English dominated schooling and urban life; by the 1970s fluent speakers were a shrinking minority. A major revitalisation movement followed: Te Wiki o te Reo Māori (Māori Language Week) began in 1975, kōhanga reo immersion preschools opened from 1982, followed by immersion primary and secondary schools (kura kaupapa Māori), Māori television, and official recognition.

Status today

Census figures show that a few percent of New Zealanders can converse in Māori, with higher proportions among Māori themselves and growing numbers of young speakers thanks to immersion education, though the language remains vulnerable.
