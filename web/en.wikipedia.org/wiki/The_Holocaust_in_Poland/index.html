The Holocaust in Poland was the systematic persecution and murder of Jews in German-occupied Poland during the Second World War. Poland had Europe's largest Jewish population before the war — about 3.3 million people — and Nazi Germany built its major extermination camps on occupied Polish soil. By 1945 roughly 90 percent of Polish Jewry had been murdered, the highest proportion of any country.

Occupation and ghettoisation

Following the German invasion of Poland in September 1939, Jews were subjected to forced labour, confiscation, and concentration into sealed ghettos such as those in Warsaw (the largest, holding some 400,000 people) and Łódź. Starvation and disease killed hundreds of thousands before deportations to the camps began.

Extermination

Under Operation Reinhard (1942–1943), the extermination camps at Bełżec, Sobibór, and Treblinka murdered approximately 1.7 million Jews, most of them Polish. Auschwitz-Birkenau, Chełmno, and Majdanek also operated in occupied Poland. Resistance included the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943 and revolts at Treblinka, Sobibór, and Auschwitz. Many Poles risked death to aid Jews — Poles form the largest national group among the Righteous Among the Nations — while others denounced or harmed them; the subject remains historically sensitive.

Aftermath

Only a few hundred thousand Polish Jews survived, most in the Soviet Union. Post-war violence, including the Kielce pogrom of 1946, drove most survivors to emigrate.
