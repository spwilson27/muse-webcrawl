Robben Island

Robben Island (Afrikaans: Robbeneiland) is an island in Table Bay, about 6.9 kilometres west of the coast of Bloubergstrand, Cape Town, South Africa. Its name is Dutch for "seal island". Roughly oval in shape and about 3.3 kilometres long, the island is best known as the site of the maximum-security prison where Nelson Mandela was imprisoned for 18 of his 27 years in captivity under the apartheid regime. It is now a museum and a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Since the Dutch colonised the Cape in the seventeenth century, Robben Island has been used to isolate political prisoners, exiles, and the socially marginal. Dutch and later British authorities banished resistance leaders from the Cape and from colonies such as the Dutch East Indies to the island. Among its early prisoners were Muslim religious leaders from the East Indies, and later Xhosa chiefs such as Makhanda, who drowned attempting to escape in 1820.

During the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the island also served as a hospital and quarantine station for people with leprosy, chronic illnesses, and mental illness. During the Second World War it was fortified as part of the defences of Cape Town, and a lighthouse on the island dates from 1865.

The apartheid prison

From 1961 the South African government used Robben Island as a maximum-security prison for political prisoners, and from 1963 it held prominent anti-apartheid activists. Nelson Mandela arrived in 1964 after the Rivonia Trial and spent 18 years there in a small cell, performing hard labour in the island's limestone quarry, before being transferred to Pollsmoor Prison in 1982. Other notable prisoners included Walter Sisulu, Govan Mbeki, Ahmed Kathrada, and Jacob Zuma, who later became President of South Africa.

Despite harsh conditions — censored letters, limited visits, punitive labour, and racially differentiated rations — prisoners organised education for one another, and the prison became known informally as "Mandela University" or "the University of Robben Island" for the political and academic study that took place there.

Museum and heritage site

The last political prisoners left in 1991 and the prison closed in 1996. Robben Island Museum opened in 1997, and in 1999 UNESCO declared the island a World Heritage Site, recognising it as a symbol of the triumph of the human spirit, freedom, and democracy over oppression. Ferries carry visitors from the Nelson Mandela Gateway at the Victoria & Alfred Waterfront in Cape Town, and tours are often led by former political prisoners.

Environment

The island supports a colony of African penguins, introduced and native bird species, springbok, ostriches, and other wildlife. Its surrounding waters form part of a marine protected area.
