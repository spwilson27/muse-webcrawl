EROEI

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the study of energy economics, energy return on investment (EROI or EROEI, for energy returned on energy invested) is the ratio of the amount of usable energy delivered by an energy source to the amount of energy expended to obtain that energy. When the EROEI of a source falls below or near 1, that source becomes an "energy sink" — it cannot be considered a viable primary source of energy. The concept was developed within the field of biophysical economics and ecological energetics, notably by Charles A. S. Hall and his colleagues building on earlier work by Howard T. Odum.

Representative EROEI ranges for selected energy sources.

Definition and calculation

EROEI is calculated as:

EROEI = Energy delivered ÷ Energy invested

The result depends heavily on the system boundary chosen: whether one counts only the energy used at the point of extraction (EROEI at the wellhead or mine mouth), or also refining, transport, infrastructure construction and decommissioning (an "extended" EROEI). Comparing published values therefore requires care, and estimates for the same technology can vary by an order of magnitude between studies.

The energy cliff

Because net energy is the difference between gross output and the energy consumed in obtaining it, net energy declines slowly at high EROEI but collapses rapidly as EROEI approaches 1 — a phenomenon known as the net energy cliff. A resource with an EROEI of 10 delivers 90% of its gross energy to society; at an EROEI of 2, only 50% is delivered; at 1.1, only about 9%. Researchers such as Hall argue that a modern industrial society requires a minimum EROEI at the point of use of roughly 3:1 merely to sustain basic infrastructure, and considerably more — perhaps 12:1 or higher — to support healthcare, education and the arts.

Historical trends

The EROEI of United States oil and gas discovery peaked around the 1930s at roughly 100:1 and has declined steadily as the most accessible deposits were depleted, reaching the range of 10–20:1 for production in recent decades. A similar declining trend has been documented globally. Defenders of unconventional fossil fuels point out that even modest-EROEI sources remain useful when energy carriers differ in quality (for example, liquid fuels for transport), while critics argue that low EROEI ultimately constrains economic growth regardless of price signals, since money is ultimately a claim on energy.

Relevance to energy transitions

EROEI analysis is widely used in debates over renewable energy. Early solar photovoltaic systems had EROEI values near or below 1 in cloudy climates, but manufacturing improvements and higher-efficiency modules have raised modern estimates substantially. A related concern is "energy cannibalism": if a low-EROEI technology is deployed too rapidly, the energy consumed in building new capacity can temporarily consume much of the energy the existing fleet produces, slowing net additions of usable energy during the transition.
