Fen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fen is a type of peat-accumulating wetland fed by mineral-rich ground or surface water. It is one of the main types of wetland, along with marshes, swamps and bogs. Whereas bogs are acidic and fed only by rainwater (ombrotrophic), fens receive water that has flowed through mineral soil or rock, making them typically neutral to alkaline and relatively rich in dissolved minerals.

Characteristics

Fens support a distinctive flora of sedges, rushes, grasses and brown mosses, and "rich fens" are among the most species-diverse habitats in temperate regions, home to many specialised orchids and other calcicole plants. The water table is at or just below the surface for most of the year, and peat accumulates where production of plant material exceeds decomposition.

Types

Hydrologists and ecologists distinguish poor fens (slightly acidic, closer to bogs in character) from rich fens (alkaline, mineral-rich), and classify fens by landscape position: valley fens, basin fens, sloping fens and spring fens, among others. The Fens of eastern England are a well-known lowland region of drained fen peatland, now largely agricultural.

Conservation

Like other peatlands, fens store substantial carbon, and they are threatened worldwide by drainage, peat extraction, eutrophication and conversion to agriculture. Many surviving fens are protected in nature reserves and under frameworks such as the Ramsar Convention and the EU Habitats Directive.
