Democracy in America

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Democracy in America (De la démocratie en Amérique) is a classic French text by Alexis de Tocqueville, published in two volumes (1835 and 1840), based on his nine-month journey through the United States in 1831–32 (officially to study prisons). Part travelogue, part comparative political science and part prophecy, it examines democracy — equality of conditions — as a providential and irresistible fact reshaping society, and asks what kinds of freedom equality can sustain.

Themes

The first volume analyses American institutions (federalism, townships, parties, press, judiciary) and coins the warning of the "tyranny of the majority"; the second, more philosophical volume explores equality's effects on thought, feeling and mores — individualism, the restless pursuit of material well-being, and the danger of a soft, tutelary despotism ("an immense and tutelary power" shepherding citizens who no longer exist, politically, except to choose their shepherds periodically). Tocqueville's counterweights — local self-government, voluntary associations ("the mother science" of democracy), religion, and the legal profession — became the standard vocabulary of civil-society theory. He also wrote penetratingly, and pessimistically, on race in America (the "three races" chapter) and on Native American dispossession. The work is perennially re-read in American political argument from every side.
