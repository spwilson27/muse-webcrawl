Mod (subculture)

Mod, from modernist, is a subculture that began in late-1950s London and spread throughout Great Britain, eventually influencing fashions and trends in other countries. Focused on music and fashion, the subculture has its roots in a small group of stylish London-based young men and women in the late 1950s who were termed modernists because they listened to modern jazz.

Characteristics

Mods were famous for their tailored suits in Italian styles, fishtail parkas worn to protect the suit beneath, button-down shirts, and polished shoes; for their love of scooters — typically Lambretta or Vespa machines decorated with many mirrors and lights; and for all-night dancing in clubs to American soul, R&B, Motown, ska and British beat music. Amphetamines were widely associated with sustaining the all-night club scene. The target symbol (roundel) of the Royal Air Force became an iconic mod emblem, later popularised worldwide by The Who.

Mods and rockers

The mass media contrasted mods with rockers — leather-jacketed bikers who favoured 1950s rock and roll — and widely reported clashes between the two groups at seaside resorts such as Clacton, Brighton and Margate during the bank holidays of 1964. The sociologist Stanley Cohen studied these events in his classic account of moral panics, Folk Devils and Moral Panics (1972), which argued that the press had greatly exaggerated the scale of the disorder.

Decline and revivals

The original mod scene fragmented in the mid-to-late 1960s into psychedelia, skinhead and other styles. A major mod revival in the late 1970s, inspired partly by the film Quadrophenia (1979, based on The Who's 1973 album), produced bands such as The Jam, whose Paul Weller was nicknamed "the Modfather." Mod fashion and music were again echoed in 1990s Britpop. Scooter rallies and mod weekends continue to draw devotees internationally.
