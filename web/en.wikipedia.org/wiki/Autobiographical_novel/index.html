Autobiographical novel

An autobiographical novel, also known as a semi-autobiographical novel, is a form of novel using autofiction techniques, or the merging of autobiographical and fictive elements. The literary technique is distinguished from an autobiography or memoir by the stipulation of being fiction. Because an autobiographical novel is partially fiction, the author does not ask the reader to expect the text to fulfill the "autobiographical pact". Names and locations are often changed and events are recreated to make them more dramatic — but the story still bears a close resemblance to the author's life.

Characteristics

Autobiographical novels tend to feature a protagonist whose experiences mirror the author's, often under a thinly disguised name (the author's alter ego), and a loose, episodic narrative built from real episodes. The bildungsroman (coming-of-age novel) is frequently autobiographical. Because the author may mix fact, imagination, and truth, much care is taken to keep the elements subjective.

Notable examples

  • Jane Eyre (1847) by Charlotte Brontë — draws on her experiences at boarding school and as a governess.
  • David Copperfield (1850) by Charles Dickens — regarded as Dickens's most autobiographical work.
  • A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man (1916) by James Joyce — Stephen Dedalus as the author's alter ego.
  • Sons and Lovers (1913) by D. H. Lawrence.
  • Of Human Bondage (1915) by W. Somerset Maugham.
  • The Bell Jar (1963) by Sylvia Plath — published under a pseudonym, mirroring her breakdown and treatment.
  • On the Road (1957) by Jack Kerouac — roman à clef of the Beat generation.
  • I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings (1969) by Maya Angelou — published as autobiography but employing novelistic techniques.

Related forms

Close relatives include the roman à clef, in which real people appear under fictional names; autofiction, a term coined by Serge Doubrovsky for works that deliberately fuse the two; and the fictionalised memoir. Distinguishing the genres matters to criticism and marketing, as the Jayson Blair- and James Frey-style controversies over "memoirs" containing fabrications illustrate.
