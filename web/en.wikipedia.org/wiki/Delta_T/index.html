Delta T

In precise timekeeping, Delta T (ΔT) is a measure of the cumulative effect of the departure of the Earth's rotation period from the fixed-length day of atomic time. Formally, it is the difference between Terrestrial Time (TT) and Universal Time (UT):

ΔT = TT − UT

Delta T is needed to convert between the uniform time scales used in celestial mechanics and the Earth-rotation-based time used for observing the sky from the rotating Earth. It is essential for computing the circumstances of eclipses, occultations, and the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets as seen from a particular location.

Causes

The Earth's rotation is gradually slowing, chiefly because of tidal friction from the Moon, which lengthens the day by roughly 1.7 milliseconds per century. Superimposed on this long-term trend are irregular fluctuations caused by movements of mass within the Earth, including changes in the core, mantle, oceans, and atmosphere, as well as post-glacial rebound.

Values

Delta T is determined from observations, today principally by analyses of lunar occultations and by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service (IERS). In the early 2020s its value was about 69 seconds. For ancient dates it must be extrapolated from records of historical eclipses; for 500 BCE it is estimated at roughly 16,000 seconds (about 4.5 hours), with considerable uncertainty.

Historical determinations

Records of solar and lunar eclipses from Babylonian, Chinese, Arab, and European sources allow astronomers to reconstruct Delta T back over roughly 2,500 years. Telescopic observations since the 17th century provide progressively more accurate values, and since the mid-20th century atomic time has permitted direct measurement.
