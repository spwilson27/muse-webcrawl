Carronade

A 68-pounder carronade, a short-range naval smasher.

A carronade is a short, smoothbore, cast-iron naval cannon developed by the Carron Company of Scotland in the late 18th century. It fired a very large shot for its weight and barrel length, using a smaller powder charge than a long gun of comparable calibre. Carronades were devastating at close range but ineffective at long distances, earning names such as “smashers” in British service.

Design

The weapon’s short barrel and large bore reduced weight and allowed ships to carry heavy firepower on upper decks. Instead of trunnions in the earliest naval mounting arrangements, many carronades were mounted on sliding carriages that absorbed recoil. Their windage—the gap between shot and bore—was reduced compared with many contemporary guns, improving efficiency despite the modest charge.

Use

The Royal Navy initially resisted the type, but its performance in the American Revolutionary War and later conflicts led to widespread adoption. Carronades were used by several navies and by merchant vessels. They were especially effective in the close-action tactics of the Napoleonic Wars, where a broadside of large shot could splinter timbers and disable crews.

Decline

As naval gunnery shifted toward longer ranges, shell guns, rifled artillery, and steam propulsion, the carronade’s short range became a decisive weakness. By the mid-19th century it had largely disappeared from front-line warships.
