Revolution Controversy

The Revolution Controversy was a British debate over the French Revolution, lasting from 1789 to 1795. A pamphlet war began with Richard Price's sermon in 1789 entitled "A Discourse on the Love of Our Country," which sparked the debate.

Notable writers in the controversy include Edmund Burke, Thomas Paine, Mary Wollstonecraft, James Mackintosh, William Godwin, and the radical pamphleteers and journalists of the 1790s. At stake were questions about representative government, the legitimacy of hereditary rule, natural rights, and the lessons of the Glorious Revolution of 1688.

Origins

On 4 November 1789, the dissenting minister Richard Price delivered a sermon, A Discourse on the Love of Our Country, to the London Revolution Society, commemorating the Glorious Revolution. Price drew parallels between the events of 1688 and the French Revolution of 1789, arguing that the principles of both justified popular resistance to tyranny and affirmed the people's right to choose their governors.

Burke's Reflections

Stung by Price's argument, Edmund Burke responded a year later with Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790), a foundational text of modern conservatism. Burke defended inherited tradition, prescription, and gradual reform over abstract rights and revolutionary rupture, and he famously lamented the passing of the age of chivalry in his defence of Marie Antoinette.

Paine's Rights of Man

Thomas Paine's Rights of Man (1791–92) answered Burke point by point, defending natural rights, popular sovereignty, and the French Revolution. It sold in enormous numbers and was prosecuted for seditious libel, with Paine convicted in absentia after fleeing to France.

Wollstonecraft and the radical response

Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Men (1790) was one of the earliest responses to Burke, and her A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) extended the arguments of the controversy to women's education and status. James Mackintosh's Vindiciae Gallicae (1791) offered a more moderate Whig defence of the Revolution.

Suppression and end

As the Revolution in France radicalised and Britain went to war with revolutionary France in 1793, the British government of William Pitt the Younger cracked down on radical publishing through the Treason Trials of 1794, the "Gagging Acts" of 1795, and prosecutions for sedition. By about 1795 the pamphlet war had subsided, though its arguments shaped British political thought for decades.
