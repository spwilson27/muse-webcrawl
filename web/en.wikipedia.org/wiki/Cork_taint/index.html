Cork taint

Cork taint is a term referring to a wine fault characterized by a set of undesirable smells or tastes found in a bottle of wine, especially musty, moldy, wet-cardboard or wet-dog aromas. The chief cause is the chemical compound 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), though related haloanisoles such as 2,4,6-tribromoanisole (TBA) and other compounds can produce similar taints. A wine affected by this fault is described as "corked" or "corky", even when the source is not the cork itself.

Cause

TCA forms when naturally occurring fungi, in the presence of chlorophenol compounds, convert those compounds into chloroanisoles as a defense mechanism. Chlorophenols historically entered cork production through chlorinated pesticides used in cork oak forests and chlorine bleaching of corks during manufacture. TCA is detectable by the human nose at extraordinarily low concentrations — a few parts per trillion — and it also suppresses the perception of fruit aromas even below the threshold at which the musty note is consciously identified. Cork taint can also arise from contaminated barrels, winery water systems, cardboard packaging, and other cellar materials.

Prevalence and prevention

Estimates of the proportion of natural-cork-sealed wines affected have commonly ranged from about 1 to 7 percent, though rates have fallen as the cork industry adopted steam cleaning, peroxide rather than chlorine treatments, and screening technologies including individual-cork gas chromatography testing. Dissatisfaction with cork taint was a major driver of the wine industry's adoption of alternative closures, including synthetic corks and screw caps (Stelvin closures), especially in Australia and New Zealand.
