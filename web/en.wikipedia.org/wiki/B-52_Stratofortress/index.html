The Boeing B-52 Stratofortress is an American long-range, subsonic, jet-powered strategic bomber. The B-52 was designed and built by Boeing, which has continued to provide support and upgrades. It has been operated by the United States Air Force since the 1950s. The bomber can carry up to 70,000 pounds (32,000 kg) of weapons and has a typical combat range of around 8,800 miles (14,200 km) without aerial refueling.

Development

The B-52 was developed from a 1946 requirement for a successor to the B-36 Peacemaker. Boeing's design evolved from a straight-wing turboprop proposal into an eight-engined jet with a 35° swept wing, drawing on the B-47 Stratojet. Of the 744 aircraft built, only the B-52H variant — 102 delivered between 1961 and 1962 — remains in service.

Operational history

The Stratofortress formed the airborne leg of the U.S. nuclear triad during the Cold War, flying airborne alert missions with thermonuclear weapons. It dropped enormous tonnages of conventional bombs in the Vietnam War (notably Operation Arc Light and the 1972 Linebacker operations), and later served in the Gulf War, the Balkans, Afghanistan, and Iraq, increasingly as a standoff platform launching cruise missiles and dropping precision-guided munitions.

Longevity

Successive upgrades to avionics, weapons, and communications — together with a program to re-engine the fleet with Rolls-Royce F130 turbofans and fit a new radar — are expected to keep the B-52J in service into the 2050s, giving the type a service life of roughly a century from its introduction. Its aircrews affectionately call it the "BUFF" (Big Ugly Fat Fellow).
