Cognitive linguistics

Cognitive linguistics is an interdisciplinary branch of linguistics that investigates the relationship between language and the mind, treating language as an integral part of general cognition rather than an autonomous mental faculty. It emerged in the 1970s and 1980s from the work of scholars including George Lakoff, Ronald Langacker, and Leonard Talmy, partly in reaction against the formalist assumptions of generative grammar.

Principles

Cognitive linguists hold three central commitments. First, language is not an autonomous faculty: the same cognitive abilities — categorisation, attention, memory, imagery — underlie both language and other thought. Second, grammar is conceptualisation: grammatical structure reflects how speakers construe situations, so meaning is encyclopedic rather than a narrow dictionary entry. Third, language knowledge emerges from usage: frequency, entrenchment, and exemplars shape the grammar, a view called the usage-based model.

Major topics

The field is known for conceptual metaphor theory, which argues that abstract thought is structured by mappings from bodily experience (for example, understanding argument as war, or time as motion); for Langacker's cognitive grammar, in which the basic unit is the symbolic assembly of form and meaning; for construction grammar, which treats idioms and abstract sentence patterns alike as learned form–meaning pairs; and for research into image schemas, mental spaces, and conceptual blending. Category structure is analysed in terms of prototypes, following Eleanor Rosch's psychology of categorisation.

Criticism and influence

Detractors, particularly from the generative tradition, have argued that cognitive linguistics lacks predictive precision and that some metaphor analyses are difficult to falsify. Nevertheless, its ideas have influenced language teaching, lexicography, stylistics, and natural language processing, and it remains one of the principal functional approaches to language.
