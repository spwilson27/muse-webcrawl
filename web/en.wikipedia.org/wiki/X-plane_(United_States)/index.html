X-plane (United States)

The X-planes are a series of experimental United States aircraft (and spacecraft) used to test and evaluate new technologies and aerodynamic concepts. Designated under the joint-service aircraft designation system, in which X stands for "special research", the X-planes have been operated by agencies including the U.S. Air Force, NASA (and its predecessor NACA), the U.S. Navy, DARPA and private contractors since the mid-1940s. Many X-planes are among the most famous research aircraft in history.

Notable X-planes

  • Bell X-1 (1946): the first crewed aircraft to exceed the speed of sound in level flight, flown by Chuck Yeager on 14 October 1947.
  • Douglas D-558-2 Skyrocket and other early jets explored transonic flight.
  • North American X-15 (1959–1968): a rocket-powered hypersonic research aircraft that reached Mach 6.7 and altitudes above 100 km, earning some of its pilots astronaut wings; Neil Armstrong flew it before joining NASA's astronaut corps.
  • Lifting bodies (X-24, M2-F2 and others) demonstrated wingless re-entry concepts used by the Space Shuttle.
  • Grumman X-29 (1984) and Rockwell-MBB X-31 tested forward-swept wings and thrust-vectoring supermanoeuvrability.
  • Boeing X-32 and Lockheed Martin X-35 competed in the Joint Strike Fighter programme; the X-35 led to the F-35 Lightning II.
  • Boeing X-37: an uncrewed reusable orbital spaceplane operated by the U.S. Space Force.
  • Lockheed Martin X-59 Quesst: a NASA experimental aircraft designed to demonstrate quiet supersonic flight over land, reducing the sonic boom to a gentle "thump".

Administration

X-designations are assigned sequentially but not every number corresponds to a flown aircraft; some projects were cancelled, redesignated, or kept classified for years. Most X-plane flight testing has been conducted from Edwards Air Force Base in California and the surrounding Rogers Dry Lake, with NASA's Armstrong Flight Research Center as a principal operator.
