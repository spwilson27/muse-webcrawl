Jean Nicolas Arthur Rimbaud (20 October 1854 – 10 November 1891) was a French poet known for his transgressive and surreal themes and for his influence on modern literature and arts, which prefigured surrealism. Born in Charleville, he started writing poems at a very young age and was a prodigy; he completely stopped writing literature at age 20, after assembling his last major work, the Illuminations. In his famous "Lettres du voyant" (1871) he proclaimed that the poet must become a voyant, a seer, through "a long, immense and reasoned derangement of all the senses".

Life

Rimbaud grew up in Charleville in the Ardennes, the son of an army captain who abandoned the family and a strict mother. A brilliant schoolboy, he began running away to Paris during the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune, about which he wrote with sympathy. In September 1871 he arrived in Paris at the invitation of the poet Paul Verlaine, to whom he had sent his poems, including the celebrated sonnet "Voyelles" and the visionary prose-poem sequence that became Une saison en enfer.

Relationship with Verlaine

Rimbaud and Verlaine began a stormy, absinthe-fuelled affair, scandalising the Paris literary world. They travelled to London and Belgium. In July 1873, in Brussels, Verlaine, drunk and jealous, shot Rimbaud in the wrist and was imprisoned for two years. Rimbaud returned to Charleville and finished Une saison en enfer (A Season in Hell, 1873), the only work he published himself. His prose poems, the Illuminations, written around 1872–1875, were published by Verlaine in 1886, after Rimbaud had vanished from literature.

Later years

From 1875 Rimbaud wandered Europe, worked as a labourer, tutor and soldier (briefly enlisting in the Dutch colonial army in Java, from which he deserted), and in 1880 settled in Aden and then Harar, in present-day Ethiopia, where he worked for a trading company dealing in coffee and, controversially, firearms. His letters home concern business and exploration, never poetry. In 1891 he developed what proved to be bone cancer in his right knee; the leg was amputated in Marseille, but the disease spread and he died on 10 November 1891, aged 37.

Legacy

Rediscovered by the Symbolists and championed by Verlaine in Les Poètes maudits (1884), Rimbaud became the archetype of the rebellious poetic genius. His work influenced the Dadaists and Surrealists, the Beat writers, and musicians including Bob Dylan, Jim Morrison and Patti Smith.
