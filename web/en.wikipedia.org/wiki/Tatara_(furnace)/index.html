Tatara (furnace)

A tatara (Japanese: 鑪) is a traditional Japanese clay furnace used for smelting iron and producing steel. The word later came to denote the whole building housing the furnace. The tatara process, refined over centuries in Japan — especially in the Chūgoku region — produces tamahagane, the high-carbon steel traditionally used for Japanese swords.

Process

The tatara is a rectangular clay hearth, roughly 3–4 metres long, built new for each smelting run and demolished afterwards to extract the product. A typical operation lasts about three days and nights, during which alternating layers of iron sand (satetsu) and charcoal are fed into the furnace while bellows supply a continuous blast of air, reaching temperatures around 1,200–1,500 °C. Because the furnace never fully melts the iron, the product is a heterogeneous bloom (kera) of steel and iron from which the smith selects the best pieces of tamahagane, judged by fracture and colour.

Operation and organization

A tatara run is directed by a master smelter, the murage, who judges the flames and sounds of the furnace. The work was historically embedded in Shinto ritual, with offerings and a shrine to the furnace deity. Regions such as Izumo (modern Shimane Prefecture), endowed with good iron sand and forests for charcoal, dominated production, and the tatara system underpinned the great iron-and-sword economy of early modern Japan.

Modern revival

Traditional tatara operation declined with the introduction of Western steelmaking in the Meiji era. In 1977 the Society for Preservation of Japanese Art Swords (NBTHK) built the Nittoho Tatara in Shimane Prefecture to supply tamahagane to contemporary swordsmiths, where runs continue each winter. The furnace and its lore reached a worldwide audience through the Studio Ghibli film Princess Mononoke, whose Irontown is modelled on a tatara community.
