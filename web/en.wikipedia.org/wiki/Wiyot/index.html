The Wiyot (Wiyot: Soulatluk, "the language") are an indigenous people of California living near Humboldt Bay, California, and a small surrounding area. Since the 19th century their culture, like that of the neighbouring Yurok, has been markedly distinct from most other California indigenous cultures, their society having a patrilineal system of laws and property rights influenced by the cultures of the Pacific Northwest Coast. Their language, Wiyot, is one of the two Algic languages that, together with Yurok, are distantly related to the Algonquian languages of the Great Lakes and eastern North America.

History

Historically the Wiyot lived in villages along Humboldt Bay (Wigi in Wiyot), the lower Mad River, and the lower Eel River. Their population before European contact is estimated at between 1,000 and 3,000. The California Gold Rush brought settlers to Humboldt Bay beginning in 1850, leading to violent conflict over land and resources.

Wiyot massacre of 1860

On 26 February 1860, during the World Renewal Ceremony — the most important ritual in Wiyot religion — a group of white settlers attacked unarmed Wiyot men, women and children on Indian Island (Tuluwat) in Humboldt Bay, killing between 80 and 250 or more people. Simultaneous attacks took place at other Wiyot sites. The massacre devastated the tribe, and survivors were removed to reservations. The journalist and author Bret Harte, then living in the area, wrote an editorial condemning the massacre, for which he received death threats and left California.

Modern Wiyot Tribe

The federally recognized Wiyot Tribe is based at the Table Bluff Reservation in Loleta, California. In 2000 the tribe purchased land on Tuluwat Island and, after an extensive environmental cleanup of a contaminated boatyard site, the City of Eureka returned the remainder of the island to the Wiyot Tribe in 2019 — a landmark act of Indigenous land return in the United States. In March 2014, the tribe held the World Renewal Ceremony on the island for the first time in more than 154 years.

Efforts to revive the Wiyot language are ongoing, using archival recordings and materials recorded from the last fluent speakers in the mid-20th century.
