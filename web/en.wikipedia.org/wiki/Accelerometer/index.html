Accelerometer

An accelerometer is a device that measures proper acceleration — the acceleration experienced by an object relative to free fall. Single- and multi-axis accelerometers detect the magnitude and direction of acceleration as a vector quantity and are used to sense orientation, vibration, shock and motion.

Principles

Most accelerometers work by sensing the displacement of a small proof mass suspended by springs: acceleration causes the mass to deflect in proportion to the force, and the deflection is converted to an electrical signal. Common transduction methods include piezoresistive strain gauges, piezoelectric crystals, and differential capacitance. Microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) devices, fabricated on silicon, made accelerometers small and cheap enough for consumer electronics.

Applications

  • Inertial navigation systems in aircraft, spacecraft and missiles
  • Airbag deployment and stability control in automobiles
  • Screen orientation, step counting and motion input in smartphones and game controllers
  • Structural health monitoring, seismology and vibration analysis
  • Hard-drive free-fall protection in laptops

By the equivalence principle, an accelerometer at rest on Earth's surface reads approximately 1 g upward, and reads zero in free fall — a fact exploited in gravimetry and in Einstein's thought experiments on gravity.
