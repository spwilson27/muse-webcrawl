Emitter-coupled logic

In electronics, emitter-coupled logic (ECL) is a high-speed integrated circuit bipolar transistor logic family. ECL uses a differential amplifier configuration with single-ended input, and uses emitter followers for its outputs to achieve very short propagation delays (originally around two nanoseconds, and as low as tens of picoseconds in modern implementations). Because the transistors never operate in saturation, ECL avoids the storage-time delays that limit other bipolar families, making it for decades the fastest logic family available.

Operation

An ECL gate steers a roughly constant tail current between two branches of a long-tailed pair. Depending on the input voltages, current flows either through the reference branch or through the input branch; emitter followers then shift the collector voltages to produce complementary logic levels, typically around −0.85 V (high) and −1.7 V (low) relative to a positive ground in the classic −5.2 V supply scheme. The basic gate naturally provides both OR and NOR outputs.

Characteristics

ECL's advantages are speed, high fan-out capability, and well-controlled impedance suited to transmission-line interconnections. Its disadvantages are substantial: gates draw current continuously, so static power consumption is high (tens of milliwatts per gate), the small noise margin and non-standard negative logic levels complicate interfacing, and the low integration density compares poorly with CMOS. Positive emitter-coupled logic (PECL) runs the same circuitry from a positive supply for easier interfacing with other families.

History and applications

ECL, first described as "current-steering logic," was invented at IBM in the late 1950s by Hannon S. Yourke. Motorola's MECL series made it a standard commercial family from the 1960s onward. Because of its speed, ECL was used in high-performance mainframes and supercomputers, including the Cray-1 (which used ECL gate arrays), IBM System/360 and System/370 high-end models, and the Amdahl 470 series. As CMOS speeds improved, ECL retreated to niches such as clock distribution, high-speed data communications, and test equipment.
