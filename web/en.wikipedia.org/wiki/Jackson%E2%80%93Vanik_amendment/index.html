Jackson–Vanik amendment

The Jackson–Vanik amendment to the Trade Act of 1974 was a provision of United States federal law intended to affect US trade relations with countries with non-market economies — originally the Soviet Union and other communist states — that restricted freedom of emigration and other human rights. Sponsored by Senator Henry M. "Scoop" Jackson and Representative Charles Vanik, it was enacted in 1975.

Provisions

The amendment denied most favoured nation (normal trade relations) status and US government credits to non-market-economy countries that denied their citizens the right to emigrate, imposed heavy taxes on emigration, or otherwise violated emigration rights. Normal trade status could be restored through a presidential waiver, subject to congressional review, where the president determined that the waiver would substantially promote free emigration.

Application and history

The amendment arose largely in response to the Soviet Union's restrictions on Jewish emigration, supported by advocates for refuseniks such as Natan Sharansky. It became a prominent instrument of Cold War human-rights pressure. Waivers were granted to Romania, Hungary, and China at various times. After the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, Russia and other post-Soviet states remained formally subject to the amendment, and annual compliance certifications continued until the amendment was repealed for Russia and Moldova in 2012, when it was replaced in Russia's case by the Magnitsky Act, which imposed targeted sanctions for human-rights abuses.

Legacy

The Jackson–Vanik amendment is remembered as a landmark example of linking trade policy to human rights, and its repeal debate in 2012 shaped the adoption of Magnitsky-style sanctions legislation in the United States and other countries.
