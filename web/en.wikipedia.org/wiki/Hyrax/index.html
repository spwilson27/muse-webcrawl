Hyraxes are small, thickset, herbivorous mammals in the order Hyracoidea, native to Africa and the Middle East. Superficially they resemble rodents or pikas, with short tails, rounded ears and ever-growing incisors, but they belong to the Paenungulata — the group that also includes elephants and manatees, their closest living relatives. Five species are generally recognised, including the rock hyrax, yellow-spotted rock hyrax and the tree hyraxes.

Characteristics

Hyraxes weigh 2–5 kg and have rubbery, sweat-gland-bearing foot pads that act like suction cups for climbing rock faces and trees. They thermoregulate poorly, so they bask in the sun and huddle together. Their stomach is a complex, multi-chambered structure for fermenting plant matter, and they have a distinctive repertoire of vocalisations — rock hyrax songs follow syntactic rules, with regional dialects. A gland on the back produces secretions used in social signalling, and crystallised hyrax urine and dung (hyraceum) was historically used in perfumery and traditional medicine.

Behaviour and ecology

Rock hyraxes live in colonies of up to 80 individuals on rocky outcrops (kopjes), with sentries posting warning calls for predators such as Verreaux's eagles, leopards and snakes. Tree hyraxes are nocturnal and largely solitary forest-dwellers known for their loud nocturnal calls. Gestation is long for so small an animal — about seven months — and young are born fully furred and active.

Evolution

Fossil hyraxes were far more diverse and larger than today's species: the Eocene–Miocene genus Titanohyrax reached the size of a small horse, and hyraxes were among the dominant herbivores of early Cenozoic Africa. The Hebrew Bible's "coney" (shaphan) is generally identified as the rock hyrax.
