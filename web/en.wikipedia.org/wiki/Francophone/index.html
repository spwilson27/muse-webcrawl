Francophone

A francophone is a person who speaks French, especially as a native language. The word also describes a country, region, or institution in which French is widely spoken or has official status. In a broader cultural and political sense, "Francophone" refers to the worldwide community of French-speaking peoples, often organized through la Francophonie, the international community of French-speaking nations and governments.

Usage and definitions

The term derives from the French francophone, coined around 1880 by the geographer Onésime Reclus to describe speakers of French worldwide. Statistics on the number of francophones vary with the definition used: estimates by the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie count more than 300 million French speakers across five continents, including native speakers, those who use French daily as a second language, and learners. French is an official language in 29 countries and is widely spoken in many others in Europe, Africa, the Americas, and Oceania.

The Francophone world

Major francophone populations are found in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Luxembourg, and Monaco in Europe; Quebec, New Brunswick, and other parts of Canada, as well as Haiti and French Guiana, in the Americas; and across West, Central, and North Africa, where French serves as an official or administrative language in countries such as Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire, Cameroon, Mali, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo — now the most populous francophone country. Demographic projections suggest that population growth in francophone Africa could make French one of the most spoken languages in the world by the middle of the twenty-first century.

Institutions

The principal political institution of the francophone world is the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie (OIF), founded in 1970, which counts 88 member states and governments and promotes the French language, cultural diversity, education, and cooperation among its members.
