Thurston norm

In mathematics, the Thurston norm is a function on the second homology group of an oriented 3-manifold that measures the topological complexity of surfaces representing homology classes. It was introduced by William Thurston in his work on foliations and the geometry of 3-manifolds, and it plays a central role in the study of fibered 3-manifolds and foliations.

Definition

For an embedded oriented surface S in a 3-manifold M, define its complexity as the sum over its components of the negative part of the Euler characteristic, discarding sphere and disc components. The Thurston norm of a homology class is the minimum of this complexity over all embedded surfaces representing the class. Thurston showed that this function extends to a seminorm on the real second homology (or cohomology) group, and that its unit ball is a finite-sided polyhedron.

Properties and applications

The norm is closely related to foliations: a class lies in the cone over a top-dimensional face of the unit ball precisely when it can be represented by the leaves of a foliation, and a 3-manifold fibers over the circle with fiber of a given topological type exactly when the corresponding cohomology class lies in the interior of such a fibered face. The Thurston norm also gives lower bounds for the genus of surfaces and is connected to the Alexander polynomial: the norm can be bounded using the degrees of the multivariable Alexander polynomial, and for fibered manifolds the two are closely related. It has been generalized and applied in the theory of taut foliations, Heegaard Floer homology, and the virtual fibering of hyperbolic 3-manifolds.
