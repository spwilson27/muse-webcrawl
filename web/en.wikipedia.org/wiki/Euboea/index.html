Euboea

Euboea (Greek: Εύβοια, Evvia) is the second-largest Greek island after Crete, lying in the Aegean Sea off the eastern coast of Central Greece. Long and narrow — about 180 km (110 mi) in length and between 7 and 50 km wide — it is separated from the mainland by the narrow Euripus Strait at Chalcis, its capital, where a bridge now connects island and mainland. The island forms the regional unit of Euboea, with a population of roughly 190,000.

Geography

A mountainous spine runs the length of Euboea; its highest peak is Mount Dirfys at 1,743 m (5,719 ft). The north is green and forested, while the south is drier. The island is prone to wildfires in summer — severe fires in August 2021 devastated large areas of northern Euboea and forced evacuations by ferry from resort beaches.

History

In antiquity Euboea's two great cities, Chalcis and Eretria, were leading maritime and colonising powers; Euboean settlers founded colonies in Italy (including Cumae and Pithekoussai) and in northern Greece (Chalcidice is named for Chalcis). The rivalry between the two cities produced the Lelantine War, one of the earliest recorded Greek conflicts. Eretria was destroyed by the Persians in 490 BC in retaliation for its support of the Ionian Revolt. The island later passed under Athenian, Macedonian, Roman, Byzantine, Venetian and Ottoman rule — the Venetians called it Negroponte, a name also given to Chalcis. Euboea became part of the modern Greek state in 1830.

The island's name, from Greek eu ("well") and bous ("ox"), means "rich in cattle", reflecting its fertile plains in antiquity.
