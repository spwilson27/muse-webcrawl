Adaptation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology, adaptation has several interrelated meanings: the trait that makes an organism more fit for its environment and lifestyle; the process by which populations acquire such traits through natural selection; and the state of being so fitted. Adaptations range from the molecular (antifreeze proteins in polar fish) to the anatomical (the giraffe's neck, the eye), physiological (altitude acclimatisation) and behavioural (migration, hibernation).

Adaptation is constrained by history: evolution tinkers with what exists, so structures are repurposed rather than designed from scratch — feathers likely insulating before they flew, the vertebrate eye carrying a blind spot, the human back and knee reflecting a quadruped past. Not every trait is an adaptation: some are by-products ('spandrels'), drift, or ancestral baggage.

Levels and trade-offs

Because environments vary and traits interact, adaptation is always a compromise — allocation to reproduction versus survival, speed versus manoeuvrability, resistance versus growth. Co-adaptation and co-evolution (flowers and pollinators, predators and prey, hosts and parasites) produce arms races and mutual dependencies.

Acclimation vs adaptation

Individuals also adjust within their lifetimes — tanning, altitude blood changes, muscle training — which is phenotypic plasticity, not genetic adaptation, though the capacity to adjust is itself inherited and selected.
