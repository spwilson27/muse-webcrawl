The Laz people (Laz: Lazepe) are a Kartvelian ethnic group native to the Black Sea coastal regions of modern-day Turkey and Georgia. They speak the Laz language, a member of the Zan branch of the Kartvelian family alongside Mingrelian. Their traditional homeland, known historically as Lazica or Lazistan, stretches along the south-eastern shore of the Black Sea.

History

The ancestors of the Laz are associated with the ancient kingdom of Colchis and later with the kingdom of Lazica (Egrisi), a client state contested between the Byzantine and Sasanian empires during the Lazic War of the sixth century. The region was gradually incorporated into the Ottoman Empire from the late fifteenth century, and most Laz in the Ottoman lands converted to Sunni Islam, while those under Georgian rule remained Orthodox Christian. After the Russo-Turkish wars and the First World War, the Laz found themselves divided between Turkey and Soviet Georgia.

Culture and language

Laz culture is shaped by the sea and the steep tea-growing mountains behind the coast. Traditional music features the kemençe fiddle and the tulum bagpipe, and the horon circle dance is shared with neighbouring peoples. The Laz language, primarily oral, is classified by UNESCO as endangered; it is written in Latin script in Turkey and in Georgian script in Georgia. Most Laz today are bilingual or have shifted to Turkish or Georgian.

Population

Estimates of the Laz population vary widely, from roughly 150,000 to over a million, depending on whether ethnic origin or active language use is counted. The largest concentrations are in Turkey's Rize and Artvin provinces and in the Georgian region of Adjara, with a significant diaspora in Istanbul and other cities.
