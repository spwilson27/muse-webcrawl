Akari (Japanese: あかり, "light"), known before launch as ASTRO-F, was an infrared astronomy satellite developed by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) in cooperation with institutes in Europe and Korea. It was launched on 21 February 2006 aboard an M-V rocket from the Uchinoura Space Center into a polar Sun-synchronous orbit, and performed the first all-sky infrared survey since the IRAS mission of 1983, with greatly improved sensitivity and spatial resolution.

Instruments

Akari carried a 68.5 cm telescope cooled to about 6 kelvin by liquid helium and mechanical coolers, feeding two instruments:

  • Far-Infrared Surveyor (FIS) – imaged the sky in four bands between roughly 50 and 180 micrometres.
  • Infrared Camera (IRC) – covered the near- and mid-infrared (about 2–26 micrometres) in nine bands for deep pointed observations and a mid-infrared all-sky survey.

Mission

The all-sky survey phase lasted about 16 months, until the liquid helium cryogen was exhausted in August 2007; by then more than 94 percent of the sky had been surveyed at least twice. A "warm" phase followed using the near-infrared channel with mechanical cooling alone. Electrical problems in 2011 ended observations, and the satellite was decommissioned on 24 November 2011.

Results

The Akari catalogues contain over a million infrared sources and have been used to study star formation, evolved stars, galaxy evolution, and the interstellar medium. Notable results include detailed far-infrared maps of star-forming regions and observations of debris discs and asteroids, including thermal measurements used to estimate asteroid sizes and albedos.
