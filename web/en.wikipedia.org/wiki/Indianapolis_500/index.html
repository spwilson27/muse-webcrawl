The Indianapolis 500, formally known as the Indianapolis 500-Mile Race, or simply the Indy 500, is an annual automobile race held at Indianapolis Motor Speedway (IMS) in Speedway, Indiana, United States, an enclave suburb of Indianapolis. The event is traditionally held over Memorial Day weekend, usually the last weekend in May. It is contested as part of the IndyCar Series, and is one of the oldest automobile races in the world, first run in 1911.

The race is often billed as "The Greatest Spectacle in Racing". Thirty-three drivers start in eleven rows of three on the 2.5-mile (4.0 km) rectangular oval, completing 200 laps. Average speeds now exceed 230 mph (370 km/h) in qualifying.

History

The speedway was built in 1909 as a testing ground for Indiana's automobile industry. Ray Harroun won the inaugural 500 in 1911 in a Marmon Wasp, famously using a rear-view mirror. The race was suspended during the two World Wars. In the postwar era, roadster-era cars gave way to rear-engined designs after Jack Brabham's Cooper in 1961 and Jim Clark's victory for Lotus in 1965. A. J. Foyt, Al Unser Sr., Rick Mears, and Hélio Castroneves share the record of four victories each.

Traditions

The event is rich in ceremony: the winner drinks milk in Victory Lane, a practice dating to Louis Meyer in 1936; the winner's likeness is sculpted on the Borg-Warner Trophy; and pre-race rituals include the singing of "Back Home Again in Indiana" and the command to start engines. The winning driver also traditionally kisses the yard of bricks at the start/finish line, a remnant of the track's original brick surface.

Qualifying

Qualifying is held the weekend before the race and determines not only the 33-car field but pole position through a shootout among the fastest cars. Bump Day drama — faster cars displacing slower ones from the field — has been a feature for most of the race's history.
