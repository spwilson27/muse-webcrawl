The war on poverty is the unofficial name for legislation first introduced by United States President Lyndon B. Johnson during his State of the Union address on 8 January 1964, when he declared "unconditional war on poverty in America". The legislation was passed as part of Johnson's Great Society domestic agenda and generated the Office of Economic Opportunity, which administered programs intended to curb poverty by improving living conditions and increasing access to education, healthcare and employment.

Programs

The Economic Opportunity Act of 1964 created the Job Corps, VISTA (Volunteers in Service to America), community action programs, work-study and adult basic education. Other Great Society measures closely associated with the war on poverty included the Food Stamp Act, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (1965), the Head Start preschool program, and the creation of Medicare and Medicaid in 1965. The initiative also expanded Social Security benefits and food assistance.

Effects and criticism

Between 1964 and 1973 the official US poverty rate fell from about 19 percent to about 11 percent, reaching historic lows, though the extent to which the programs (rather than economic growth) caused the decline remains debated. Conservatives such as Ronald Reagan argued the war had been lost — Reagan famously remarked in 1988 that "poverty won" — while liberal historians contend the programs substantially reduced poverty, especially among the elderly, and that funding was cut back too soon as spending shifted to the Vietnam War. The phrase "war on poverty" was popularised in the press rather than by Johnson himself, and debate over the effectiveness and cost of the programs remains a fixture of American politics.
