Gustav Line

The Gustav Line was the main German defensive line of the Winter Line in Italy during the Italian Campaign of World War II. It stretched from the Tyrrhenian Sea north of the Garigliano River, across the Apennine Mountains to the mouth of the Sangro River on the Adriatic coast. The line was fortified with gun pits, concrete bunkers, turreted machine-gun emplacements, barbed wire and minefields.

The centre of the line, where it crossed the main route to Rome (Highway 6) through the Liri Valley, was anchored by the mountains around Monte Cassino and the abbey that crowned it. Field Marshal Albert Kesselring, the German commander in Italy, made the Gustav Line the strongest of his defensive positions south of Rome.

Battle of Monte Cassino

Between January and May 1944 the Allies fought four major battles to break through at Cassino. The first assault by the U.S. Fifth Army failed with heavy losses, including the disastrous Rapido River crossing by the 36th Infantry Division. The second battle saw the controversial bombing of Monte Cassino Abbey on 15 February 1944, which reduced the historic monastery to rubble without dislodging the defenders. A third assault in March also failed.

Operation Diadem

The line was finally breached in May 1944 during Operation Diadem, the fourth battle, when the British Eighth Army and French Expeditionary Corps — including Moroccan Goumiers who crossed terrain considered impassable in the Aurunci Mountains — outflanked the position, while Polish II Corps captured the abbey ruins on 18 May. The way to Rome opened; the city fell on 4 June 1944, and the Germans fell back to the Gothic Line north of Florence.
