Tradition

A tradition is a belief, behaviour, or cultural practice with significance within a group or society, passed down within the group or society from one generation to another with symbolism or special meaning connecting it with their past. Examples include holidays, ceremonies, dress, foodways, folklore, and forms of greeting. Traditions may persist for thousands of years or arise rapidly, and they serve to reinforce group identity, transmit values, and mark continuity between past and present.

A traditional ceremony: practices handed down across generations.

Etymology

The English word derives from the Latin traditio, "a handing over, delivery", from tradere, "to hand over, to betray". The term originally had legal and religious connotations — the handing down of doctrine or custom — and acquired its general cultural sense in English over the 18th and 19th centuries.

Functions

Anthropologists and sociologists note that traditions justify present arrangements by reference to the past, encode collective memory, and create solidarity. Philosophers such as Alasdair MacIntyre have argued that moral reasoning itself takes place within living traditions. In religions, tradition may be a formal source of authority alongside scripture, as in the Catholic and Orthodox concept of Sacred Tradition, or in Judaism's Oral Torah.

Invented traditions

In their influential 1983 volume The Invention of Tradition, Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger showed that many practices claiming ancient origins were in fact codified quite recently — often in the 19th century — for purposes of nation-building or social cohesion; examples include elements of Scottish Highland dress and various royal ceremonies. The concept does not imply that such traditions are inauthentic to their practitioners, but that their claimed antiquity may be constructed.

Tradition and change

Traditions are not static: they are continually reinterpreted, and scholars distinguish "traditional" societies from modern ones only with caution, since every tradition was once an innovation. Debates over preserving, reviving, or abandoning traditions — in language, ritual, dress, and law — recur in many societies, and folklorists study how traditional practices adapt to new media and migration.
