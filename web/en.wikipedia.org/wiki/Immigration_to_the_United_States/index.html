Immigration to the United States

Immigration to the United States
Foreign-born population: ~47–48 million (2023)
Share of population: ~14%
Top origins: Mexico, India, China, Philippines

Immigration to the United States has been a major source of population growth and cultural change throughout American history. The United States has more immigrants than any other country — around 47 million in recent counts, roughly 14% of the population — and its annual flow reshaped its economy and politics from the colonial era onward.

Waves

Early settlers were mainly English, with enslaved Africans brought forcibly and Germans and Scots-Irish arriving in the 18th century. Mid-19th-century waves brought Irish fleeing the Great Famine and Germans after 1848. Between 1880 and 1924, more than 20 million arrived, increasingly from southern and eastern Europe and Asia, processed at Ellis Island (opened 1892) and Angel Island. Nativist reaction produced the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) and the quota system of the Immigration Act of 1924.

Modern system

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 abolished national-origins quotas, favouring family reunification and skilled employment, which shifted origins toward Latin America and Asia. The 1986 IRCA legalised about 3 million unauthorised immigrants while sanctioning employers. Today lawful routes include family and employment green cards, the diversity lottery, refugee and asylum programmes; unauthorised immigration — about 11 million people — remains politically contentious, as do border enforcement, DACA and temporary protected status.
