Lectio divina

Lectio divina (Latin for "divine reading") is a traditional monastic practice of scriptural reading, meditation, and prayer intended to promote communion with God and to increase knowledge of God's word. It does not treat Scripture as texts to be studied, but as the living word. Rooted in the Rule of Saint Benedict and earlier monastic tradition, the practice was classically described in four movements: lectio (reading), meditatio (meditation), oratio (prayer), and contemplatio (contemplation).

History

The roots of lectio divina lie in the prayerful reading of Scripture practised by the Desert Fathers and described by Origen and Saint Ambrose. The Rule of Saint Benedict (6th century) prescribed daily periods of lectio divina for monks, and Benedictine and Cistercian monasteries preserved the practice through the Middle Ages. The Carthusian prior Guigo II systematised the four stages in his Scala Claustralium ("The Ladder of Monks", c. 1150), comparing the steps to rungs of a ladder by which monks ascend to God: reading seeks, meditation finds, prayer asks, and contemplation tastes.

The four movements

Lectio (reading)

The practitioner reads a short passage of Scripture slowly and repeatedly, attentively listening for a word or phrase that stands out.

Meditatio (meditation)

The reader "ruminates" on the text — reflecting on how the word speaks to his or her life, in the traditional image of chewing the cud.

Oratio (prayer)

The reflection turns into prayer: a dialogue in which the reader responds to God with petition, thanksgiving, repentance, or adoration arising from the text.

Contemplatio (contemplation)

Finally the practitioner rests silently in God's presence, beyond words and thoughts — regarded as the goal of the practice, a gift of grace rather than an achievement.

Modern practice

The Second Vatican Council's constitution Dei Verbum (1965) recommended lectio divina to all the faithful, and Pope Benedict XVI repeatedly commended it as a means of renewal, predicting it could bring "a new spiritual springtime" to the Church. In modern usage the practice is taught widely beyond monasteries, to lay Catholics, other Christians, and in adapted forms in retreats and small groups; some contemporary guides add a fifth step, actio — putting the word into practice in daily life.
