Communications satellite

A communications satellite is an artificial satellite that relays and amplifies radio telecommunication signals via a transponder; it creates a communication channel between a source transmitter and a receiver at different locations on Earth. Communications satellites are used for television, telephone, radio, internet, and military applications.

History

The idea was popularised by Arthur C. Clarke in 1945, proposing geostationary orbit for relay stations. Early experiments included Project SCORE (1958) and Echo (1960). Telstar 1, launched in 1962, relayed the first transatlantic television signals. Syncom 3 (1964) was the first geostationary communications satellite, broadcasting the Tokyo Olympics.

Orbits

Geostationary orbit (GEO, about 35,786 km) keeps a satellite fixed over the equator, ideal for television broadcasting. Medium Earth orbit (MEO) is used by navigation and some communications systems, while low Earth orbit (LEO) constellations such as Starlink and Iridium provide low-latency data and voice services but require many satellites for continuous coverage.

Applications and structure

A typical satellite carries multiple transponders operating in C, Ku, Ka and other frequency bands, solar arrays for power, batteries for eclipse periods, and station-keeping thrusters. Services include direct-to-home television, satellite radio, broadband internet, mobile telephony backhaul, and secure government communications.
