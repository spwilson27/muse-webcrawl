Volcanism of Iceland

Volcanism in Iceland occurs frequently because of the country's particular geology. Iceland sits astride the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the divergent boundary between the Eurasian and North American tectonic plates, and also over a mantle plume or hotspot. The combination of rifting and hotspot activity makes Iceland one of the most volcanically active regions on Earth, with eruptions occurring on average every four to five years.

Volcanic systems

Iceland has around 30 active volcanic systems, including Katla, Hekla, Grímsvötn, Bárðarbunga, Krafla, Askja, and the Reykjanes systems. Many are partly covered by glaciers, so eruptions can produce meltwater floods called jökulhlaup.

Notable eruptions

  • Eldgjá (c. 939) – the largest flood basalt eruption in recorded history.
  • Laki (1783–84) – a fissure eruption whose gases caused a global climate anomaly and famine in Iceland and beyond.
  • Askja (1875) – explosive eruption that deposited ash across Scandinavia.
  • Eldfell (1973) – eruption on Heimaey that threatened the harbour town of Vestmannaeyjar; lava flows were slowed by seawater cooling.
  • Eyjafjallajökull (2010) – ash plume disrupted European air travel for weeks.
  • Holuhraun (2014–15) – Iceland's largest effusive eruption since Laki.
  • Fagradalsfjall / Sundhnúkur (2021– ) – renewed eruptions on the Reykjanes Peninsula after some 800 years of quiet.

Monitoring

The Icelandic Meteorological Office monitors seismic activity, ground deformation and gas emissions to warn of impending eruptions, working with the Department of Civil Protection.
