Media freedom in Russia

Journalists covering a press event in Moscow

Media freedom in Russia concerns the constitutional rights of Russian citizens to freedom of speech and of the press, and how those rights are exercised in practice. The Russian constitution guarantees freedom of the press and prohibits censorship, but international observers such as Reporters Without Borders and Freedom House have long assessed the Russian media environment as heavily restricted, with most major television and print outlets under direct or indirect state control. Russia ranked near the bottom of the World Press Freedom Index in the 2020s.

History

After the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the 1990s saw an explosion of pluralistic media under Boris Yeltsin, although major outlets were captured by oligarchs who used them as political instruments. Under Vladimir Putin from 2000 onward, the state reasserted control over national television — the dominant news source — beginning with the takeover of Vladimir Gusinsky's NTV. Independent newspapers such as Novaya Gazeta continued publishing critical reporting but under increasing pressure.

Legal framework and restrictions

A series of laws expanded state leverage over the media: the "foreign agents" law of 2012 and its extensions required media receiving foreign funding to register and label all output, and was later applied to individual journalists. Laws against "extremism" and "fake news" have been used to sanction reporting. Following the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, legislation criminalising "false information" about the armed forces — punishable by up to 15 years in prison — led nearly all remaining independent outlets to suspend operations, leave the country, or be blocked; access to foreign broadcasters and social media platforms was also restricted.

Violence against journalists

Russia has been one of the more dangerous countries for journalists. High-profile murders include those of Anna Politkovskaya of Novaya Gazeta in 2006, Natalya Estemirova in 2009, and many others, frequently with impunity for those who ordered the killings. Novaya Gazeta editor Dmitry Muratov received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2021; the paper suspended publication in Russia in 2022 and relaunched in exile as Novaya Gazeta Europe.

Exile media

Since 2022, much of Russian independent journalism has operated from abroad, with outlets such as Meduza (based in Riga), TV Rain (Dozhd), and The Insider broadcasting and publishing online to Russian audiences through websites, YouTube, and Telegram despite blocking and "foreign agent" or "undesirable organisation" designations.
