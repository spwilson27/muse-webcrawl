Proteoglycan

Structure of a proteoglycan aggregate in cartilage.

Proteoglycans are proteins that are heavily glycosylated. The basic proteoglycan unit consists of a "core protein" with one or more covalently attached glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains. The chains are long, linear carbohydrate polymers that are negatively charged under physiological conditions due to the presence of sulfate and uronic acid groups.

Structure

Proteoglycans occur in connective tissue. In cartilage, the proteoglycan aggrecan binds, via a link protein, to a long filament of hyaluronic acid, forming huge aggregates; the entrapped water makes cartilage resilient under compressive load. Depending on their GAG chains, proteoglycans carry chondroitin sulfate, dermatan sulfate, heparan sulfate, or keratan sulfate.

Types

They are categorised by size and localisation: large aggregating proteoglycans (aggrecan, versican), small leucine-rich proteoglycans (decorin, biglycan, lumican), cell-surface proteoglycans (syndecans, glypicans) and basement-membrane proteoglycans (perlecan, agrin). Heparan sulfate proteoglycans act as co-receptors for growth factors such as FGF.

Function and disease

Proteoglycans fill the extracellular matrix, regulate its hydration and mechanical properties, bind growth factors and cytokines, and guide cell migration. Genetic defects in GAG synthesis or sulfation cause skeletal dysplasias and connective-tissue disorders, and loss of aggrecan from articular cartilage is an early event in osteoarthritis.
