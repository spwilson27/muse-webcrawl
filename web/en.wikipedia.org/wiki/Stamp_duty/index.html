Stamp duty

Stamp duty is a tax levied on legal documents and transactions, historically evidenced by a physical stamp impressed on the document to show the duty had been paid. It originated in the Netherlands in 1624, was adopted in England in 1694 under William and Mary, and spread throughout the British Empire. A document not properly stamped was generally inadmissible in court — historically the chief means of enforcement.

History

The British Stamp Act 1765, which extended stamp duties to newspapers, pamphlets, and legal papers in the American colonies, provoked widespread protest under the slogan "no taxation without representation" and was a major grievance leading to the American Revolution. In Britain, duties on newspapers ("taxes on knowledge") survived until the 19th century.

Modern forms

In the United Kingdom, Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT), introduced in 2003, applies to purchases of land and property in England and Northern Ireland at progressive rates above price thresholds; Scotland levies the Land and Buildings Transaction Tax and Wales the Land Transaction Tax instead. Stamp duty reserve tax and stamp duty at 0.5% apply to purchases of shares in UK companies. Other jurisdictions with stamp duties include Ireland, Australia (state transfer duties), Hong Kong and Singapore (notably on property), India, and South Africa.

Because stamp duties tax transactions rather than income or wealth, economists frequently criticise them for impeding mobility and efficient reallocation of assets, while governments value them as simple to collect and difficult to avoid.
