Eisenstein prime

In mathematics, an Eisenstein prime is an irreducible element of the ring of Eisenstein integers ℤ[ω]. An Eisenstein integer z = a + bω is an Eisenstein prime if and only if it is irreducible in ℤ[ω], that is, it cannot be written as a product of non-unit Eisenstein integers.

Characterisation

An Eisenstein integer a + bω is prime precisely in the following cases:

  • It is equal to a rational prime p ≡ 2 (mod 3) multiplied by a unit — rational primes congruent to 2 mod 3 remain prime in ℤ[ω].
  • It is, up to a unit, a factor of a rational prime p ≡ 1 (mod 3); such primes split as p = π·π̄ with π = a + bω of norm p.
  • It is the prime 1 − ω (up to a unit), whose norm is 3; the rational prime 3 ramifies, with 3 = −(1 − ω)² up to a unit.

Equivalently, a non-zero Eisenstein integer is prime if its norm is a rational prime, or if it is a rational prime p ≡ 2 (mod 3) (times a unit), a fact closely parallel to the characterisation of Gaussian primes.

Examples

The smallest Eisenstein primes include 2 and 5 (rational primes ≡ 2 mod 3), 1 − ω (above 3), and 2 − ω and its conjugate (of norm 7). The first several Eisenstein primes that are not rational primes occur for rational primes 7, 13, 19, 31, … — the primes congruent to 1 modulo 3.
