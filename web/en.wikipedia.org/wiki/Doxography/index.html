Doxography

Doxography (Greek: δόξα, doxa, "opinion, belief" + γραφία, "writing") is a term used in classical scholarship for works that collect and report the opinions (doxai) of earlier philosophers and scientists, especially in ancient Greek philosophy.

Doxography
Field: Classical philology, history of philosophy
Key figures: Theophrastus, Aëtius, Diogenes Laertius
Key edition: Diels, Doxographi Graeci (1879)

History

The genre is traced to Theophrastus, whose lost Opinions of the Natural Philosophers underlies much later reporting. Surviving doxographical sources include the Placita attributed to Aëtius (preserved via pseudo-Plutarch and Stobaeus), the Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers by Diogenes Laertius, and summaries in Cicero, Sextus Empiricus and Hippolytus.

Method and importance

Hermann Diels coined the modern study of the tradition in Doxographi Graeci (1879) and, with Walther Kranz, collected the fragments of the Presocratics. Because most early Greek philosophy survives only through doxography, scholars must read such reports critically: doxographers often systematized, simplified or refracted older views through later schools' categories.
