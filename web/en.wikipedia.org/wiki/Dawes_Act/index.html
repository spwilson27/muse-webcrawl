Dawes Act

The Dawes Act of 1887 (also known as the General Allotment Act) regulated land rights on tribal territories in the United States. Named after Senator Henry L. Dawes of Massachusetts, it authorized the President of the United States to subdivide Native American tribal communal landholdings into allotments for Native American heads of families and individuals. The aim of the act was to assimilate Native Americans into mainstream US society by encouraging individual farming, breaking up tribal government and communal landholding.

Provisions

The act allotted plots — typically 160 acres to a head of family, 80 acres to single adults — held in trust by the federal government for 25 years before full title passed, together with US citizenship. Land remaining after allotment was declared "surplus" and sold to non-Native settlers. Amendments, notably the Burke Act of 1906, strengthened federal control over allottees deemed "incompetent".

Effects

Between 1887 and 1934 Native Americans lost control of about 90 million acres — nearly two-thirds of the land held in 1887 — through surplus sales, fraud, and the sale of allotted land after trust periods expired. Allotment also fractionated holdings among heirs into unusably small interests. The policy was repudiated by the Meriam Report (1928) and ended by the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934, though its legacy of fractionation persists.
