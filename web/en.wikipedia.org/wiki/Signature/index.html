Signature

A signature (from Latin signare, "to sign") is a handwritten — and often stylised — depiction of someone's name, nickname or other mark that a person writes on documents as evidence of identity and intent. A signature attests that the person wrote, approved or agreed to be bound by the contents of a document. Signatures have been used on contracts, cheques, wills, petitions and official acts for centuries, and their study and forgery have given rise to forensic document examination. In the digital age, the functional equivalents include electronic signatures and cryptographic digital signatures.

Function and legal status

Legally, a signature generally serves three purposes: identifying the signer, showing that the signer adopts the document, and binding the signer to its terms. Most legal systems do not require any particular form: an initials mark, an "X" (historically common for illiterate signers, often witnessed), or even a printed or stamped name may be valid if made with signing intent. Statutes such as the Statute of Frauds (1677) in England required signed writing for certain contracts, a rule echoed in many jurisdictions today. Signatures may be witnessed or notarised to raise their evidentiary weight.

History

Precursors include seals pressed into clay, wax and ink — used from ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt through medieval Europe and, in East Asia, the personal seal or dojang/hanko still used in Korea, Japan and China. Handwritten name signatures became standard in Europe as literacy spread in the early modern period. Elaborate flourishes (paraphs) developed in part to deter forgery. Famous historical signatures, such as John Hancock's oversized signature on the United States Declaration of Independence, have become cultural symbols; autographs of notable people are also collected as memorabilia.

Verification and forgery

Banks and institutions traditionally compared signatures against specimen cards; modern systems use automated handwriting verification. Forgery of signatures is a crime in most jurisdictions. Forensic document examiners compare questioned signatures with exemplars, examining letterforms, speed, pressure and pen lifts, though disguised and traced forgeries can be difficult to detect.

Electronic and digital signatures

An electronic signature is any electronic indication of assent — a typed name, a click, or a scanned handwritten image — recognised by laws such as the US ESIGN Act and UETA and the EU's eIDAS Regulation. A digital signature, by contrast, is a specific cryptographic construction: the signer hashes the document and encrypts the hash with a private key, allowing anyone with the corresponding public key to verify both integrity and origin. Qualified digital signatures under eIDAS carry the same legal effect as handwritten signatures throughout the EU.
