Irish banking crisis

The Irish banking crisis was the near-collapse of Ireland's banking system during the post-2008 financial crisis. Irish banks, swollen by years of reckless property lending during the Celtic Tiger boom, faced insolvency when the property bubble burst in 2007–2008 and credit markets froze.

Bank guarantee

On 30 September 2008, the Irish government issued a blanket guarantee of the deposits and most liabilities of the six main Irish banks — a commitment of roughly €440 billion, about twice national GDP. The guarantee, intended to prevent a run, instead bound the state's finances to the banks' losses and is widely regarded as a pivotal and costly decision.

Anglo Irish Bank and nationalisation

Anglo Irish Bank, the most exposed lender to property developers, was nationalised in January 2009 amid scandals over concealed directors' loans. Allied Irish Banks and Bank of Ireland were progressively recapitalised and brought under majority state control. In 2009 the state created the National Asset Management Agency (NAMA) to acquire toxic property loans from the banks.

Bailout

By November 2010, the cost of bank rescues — ultimately around €64 billion, some 40% of GDP — forced Ireland into an EU–IMF programme of €85 billion in loans, accompanied by austerity budgets, tax rises and emigration. Ireland exited the bailout in December 2013. Subsequent official inquiries, including the 2016 banking inquiry, criticised bank boards, auditors, regulators and government for failures of supervision and risk management.
