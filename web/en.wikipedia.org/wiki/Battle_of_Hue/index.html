The Battle of Huế (31 January – 2 March 1968) was one of the longest and bloodiest battles of the Vietnam War. During the Tet Offensive, North Vietnamese Army (PAVN) and Viet Cong forces seized most of Huế, the former imperial capital of Vietnam, and held it for 26 days before US Marines, US Army and South Vietnamese (ARVN) forces retook the city in gruelling house-to-house fighting.

Marines fighting in the Citadel district, February 1968.

The battle

Exploiting the Tet holiday ceasefire, some 10,000 PAVN and Viet Cong troops infiltrated Huế and captured the Citadel — the walled imperial city north of the Perfume River — and most of the modern city to the south, holding only the ARVN headquarters and the US MACV compound against them. Initial allied counterattacks, constrained by rules of engagement meant to spare the historic city and hampered by monsoon weather that grounded air support, failed with heavy losses. As restrictions were lifted, Marines fought block by block across the river and into the Citadel, using tear gas, recoilless rifles and eventually heavy firepower; the Citadel's flag was finally retaken on 24 February and the last pockets cleared by 2 March.

Civilian massacre and destruction

During the occupation, communist forces executed thousands of civilians — government officials, teachers, clergy and others on prepared lists — in what became known as the Massacre at Huế; mass graves containing roughly 2,800 bodies were later found, and total civilian deaths in the battle are commonly estimated between 2,800 and 6,000. Some 80% of the city was damaged or destroyed and about 116,000 civilians were made homeless.

Significance

Although a tactical allied victory, Huế — broadcast into American homes nightly — deepened US public disillusionment with the war and, with the wider Tet Offensive, marked a strategic turning point in American policy.
