Passivation (chemistry)

Passivation, in physical chemistry and engineering, refers to coating a material so that it becomes "passive", that is, less readily affected or corroded by the environment. Passivation involves the creation of an outer layer of shield material that is applied as a microcoating, formed by chemical reaction with the base material, or allowed to build up by spontaneous oxidation in the air.

Mechanism

Many metals naturally form a hard, relatively inert surface layer — usually an oxide — when exposed to air, which protects the material underneath from further corrosion. Aluminium, for example, spontaneously forms a thin layer of aluminium oxide (Al2O3) that prevents the bulk metal from oxidizing further. Iron, by contrast, forms rust, a porous oxide that flakes away and does not protect the underlying metal; iron only becomes passive under specific conditions or when alloyed, as in stainless steel, where chromium forms a self-repairing chromium(III) oxide layer.

Applications

Passivation treatments are used to improve the corrosion resistance of stainless steel parts by removing free iron from the surface using citric or nitric acid baths. Passivation is also central to semiconductor manufacturing, where the surface of silicon is passivated with silicon dioxide, and to the protection of aluminium and zinc coatings. The behaviour of passive layers can break down locally, leading to pitting corrosion — a major concern in marine and chemical-plant environments.

History

The phenomenon was studied in the 19th century by chemists including Michael Faraday and Christian Friedrich Schönbein, who observed that iron immersed in concentrated nitric acid became temporarily unreactive. The term "passive state" was later quantified using electrochemical methods and potential–pH (Pourbaix) diagrams.
