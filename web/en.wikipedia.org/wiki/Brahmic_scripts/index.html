Dozens of scripts descend from Brahmi, from Devanagari in the north to Tamil in the south and Thai and Khmer in Southeast Asia.

The Brahmic scripts, also known as the Indic scripts, are a family of abugida writing systems descended from the ancient Indian Brahmi script. They are used throughout South Asia and Southeast Asia — and were historically used in Central Asia as well — to write hundreds of languages, including most of the languages of India, Nepal, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, and the national languages of Myanmar (Burmese), Thailand (Thai), Cambodia (Khmer), and Laos (Lao), among many others. Several hundred million people write with Brahmic scripts daily, making the family one of the most widely used writing systems in the world.

In a Brahmic abugida, each basic character typically represents a consonant with an inherent vowel (usually /a/), modified by diacritical marks (matras) to indicate other vowels, suppressed by a virama/halant sign to show a bare consonant, and combined into ligatures for consonant clusters. The traditional ordering of characters — vowels first, then consonants arranged by place of articulation (velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, labial) — reflects the sophisticated phonetic science of ancient India.

History

Brahmi is securely attested in the rock and pillar edicts of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka (mid-3rd century BCE), inscribed across the Indian subcontinent; the origin of the script is debated, with proposals of derivation from a Semitic alphabet (such as Aramaic) alongside arguments for indigenous development, possibly with links to the still-undeciphered Indus script. Over the following centuries Brahmi diversified regionally. In the north, the Gupta script of the 4th–6th centuries CE gave rise to Nagari and the eastern scripts; in the south, Pallava, Kadamba, and related forms produced the Dravidian scripts and were carried to Southeast Asia with Indian trade and religion, where they parented the Mon–Burmese, Khmer, Thai, and Old Javanese scripts. Tibetan tradition credits Thonmi Sambhota with adapting an Indian script for Tibetan in the 7th century CE.

Characteristics

Brahmic scripts share a common abstract character inventory and phonetic ordering: independent vowel letters for word-initial vowels, consonant letters grouped into five vargas of five consonants each (stops at five places of articulation, each in voiceless, aspirated, voiced, voiced-aspirated, and nasal variants), plus semi-vowels, sibilants, and /h/. Conjunct consonants may stack vertically (as in Devanagari) or use special forms. Descendant scripts differ greatly in appearance — angular Devanagari with its headline (shirorekha), rounded southern scripts shaped by writing on palm leaves — while remaining structurally parallel, so that transliteration between them is largely mechanical. In Unicode, the Brahmic scripts are encoded following the ISCII model, so corresponding characters occupy corresponding code positions across blocks.

Major branches

  • Northern Brahmic — Devanagari (Hindi, Marathi, Nepali, Sanskrit), Bengali–Assamese, Gurmukhi (Punjabi), Gujarati, Odia, Tibetan, Meetei Mayek, and others.
  • Southern Brahmic — Tamil, Malayalam, Telugu, Kannada, Sinhala, and the Grantha script once used for Sanskrit in the south.
  • Southeast Asian — Burmese (from Mon/Pyu), Khmer, Thai and Lao (from Khmer), Javanese and Balinese (Hanacaraka), and the Philippine Baybayin, all tracing to southern Indian prototypes.
  • Central Asian (historical) — Tocharian and Khotanese scripts of the Tarim Basin, and the 'Phags-pa script devised for the Mongol Empire.
