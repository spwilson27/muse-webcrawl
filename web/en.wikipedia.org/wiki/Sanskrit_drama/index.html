Sanskrit drama

Sanskrit drama is the tradition of classical theatre in ancient India, written primarily in Sanskrit with portions in Prakrit languages. Regarded as an achievement of Sanskrit literature, it flourished from roughly the early centuries CE through the early medieval period.

Origins and theory

The theoretical foundation of Sanskrit drama is the Natya Shastra, attributed to Bharata Muni, a treatise on the performing arts covering stage design, acting, dance, music, and the theory of rasa (aesthetic flavour or sentiment). Later works such as Dhananjaya's Dasharupaka systematized dramatic genres.

Characteristics

Sanskrit plays are multilingual: kings and high-status men speak Sanskrit, while women and lower-status characters speak various Prakrits. Plays mix prose and verse, open with a benediction (nandi) and a prologue in which the stage-manager (sutradhara) introduces the play, and conventionally end happily; tragedy in the Western sense is largely absent. Stock characters include the hero (nayaka), heroine (nayika), and the vidushaka, a comic Brahmin companion.

Major playwrights

Important dramatists include Bhasa, whose plays such as Svapnavasavadatta were rediscovered in the early 20th century; Kalidasa, author of Abhijnana-Shakuntala (The Recognition of Shakuntala), Vikramorvasiyam, and Malavikagnimitram; Shudraka, credited with Mricchakatika (The Little Clay Cart); Vishakhadatta, author of the political drama Mudrarakshasa; Harsha; and Bhavabhuti.

Legacy

Sanskrit drama influenced later Indian performing traditions, including Kutiyattam of Kerala, recognized by UNESCO as a masterpiece of intangible heritage, and was rediscovered by European scholarship in the late 18th century, when Sir William Jones's translation of Shakuntala (1789) won wide admiration.
