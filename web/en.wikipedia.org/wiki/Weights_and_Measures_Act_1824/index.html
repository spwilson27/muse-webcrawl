Weights and Measures Act 1824

Citation: 5 Geo. 4. c. 74
Parliament: Parliament of the United Kingdom
Royal assent: 1824
Commencement: 1 January 1826
Status: Repealed

The Weights and Measures Act 1824 (5 Geo. 4. c. 74) was a statute of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that unified the system of weights and measures throughout the British Empire, establishing the imperial system of units. It came into force on 1 January 1826 and replaced the many local and customary units that had coexisted in Britain with a single standard system based on new physical standards.

Provisions

The Act defined the imperial yard and the imperial pound (the Troy pound was retained initially for precious metals; the avoirdupois pound became the general standard), and established the imperial gallon as the volume of ten pounds of distilled water under specified conditions. From the gallon were derived the quart, pint, bushel and other measures of capacity. It abolished many older units such as the Winchester measure and the Scots and Irish units in legal trade, requiring local standards to be compared with the Exchequer standards.

Standards

New standards were constructed under the supervision of the Royal Society and stored at the Exchequer. When the Palace of Westminster burned down in 1834, the primary standards were destroyed and had to be reconstructed from secondary copies — an episode that led to a scientific commission and, eventually, to more robust standards under the Weights and Measures Act 1878.

Legacy

The 1824 Act is the founding statute of the imperial system, variations of which spread throughout the British Empire and persisted in the United States as customary units (with notable differences, such as the US gallon). In the United Kingdom, imperial units were progressively supplemented and replaced by the metric system during the 20th century.
