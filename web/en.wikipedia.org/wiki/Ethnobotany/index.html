Ethnobotany is the scientific study of the relationships between peoples and plants — how particular human cultures make use of native flora for food, medicine, shelter, clothing, fuel, tools, dyes and ritual. Combining methods from botany and anthropology, it documents traditional ecological knowledge, often in collaboration with indigenous communities, and contributes to conservation, agriculture and the search for new medicines.

History

The term was coined in 1895 by the American botanist John William Harshberger, though systematic recording of plant lore is far older, running from Dioscorides' De materia medica through colonial-era herbals. The discipline took modern form in the twentieth century, above all through Richard Evans Schultes, whose fieldwork in the Amazon from 1941 documented thousands of medicinal and psychoactive plants and earned him recognition as the father of modern ethnobotany.

Methods and applications

Ethnobotanists combine herbarium collection and taxonomic identification with interviews, participant observation and quantitative techniques, such as use-value indices, to assess the cultural importance of species. Documented traditional remedies have led to important drugs and leads — the rosy periwinkle's alkaloids and the fever remedy artemisinin, drawn from Chinese herbal medicine, are frequently cited examples. The field also supports the conservation of crop genetic diversity and of the knowledge systems that sustain it.

Ethics

Ethnobotany has been criticised historically for extracting knowledge from indigenous peoples without consent or benefit-sharing — a practice labelled biopiracy when commercial products result. Modern codes of ethics, the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Nagoya Protocol require prior informed consent and fair sharing of benefits with source communities.
