Dacite is a volcanic rock formed by rapid solidification of lava that is high in silica and low in alkali metal oxides. It has a fine-grained (aphanitic) to porphyritic texture and is intermediate in composition between andesite and rhyolite. It is the extrusive equivalent of granodiorite. The name derives from Dacia, the Roman province between the Danube and the Carpathian Mountains where the rock was first described.

Composition and texture

Dacite consists largely of plagioclase feldspar and quartz with smaller amounts of hornblende and biotite in a glassy or fine-grained groundmass. Its silica content of roughly 63 to 69 percent makes dacitic magma viscous, favouring the formation of steep-sided lava domes and thick, blocky flows rather than fluid sheets.

Occurrence

Dacite is characteristic of subduction-zone volcanism. Famous dacitic eruptions include the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens in Washington, which produced a dacite dome in its crater, and eruptions of Mount Pinatubo. Dacitic magma, being viscous and gas-rich, is prone to explosive eruptions producing pyroclastic flows. See also Andesite, Rhyolite, Lava dome, and Volcanic rock.
