Horoscope

A horoscope (from Greek: ὥρα, "hour, time" and σκοπός, "watcher") is an astrological chart or diagram representing the positions of the Sun, Moon, planets, astrological aspects and sensitive angles at the time of an event, such as the moment of a person's birth. The word horoscope is derived from the Greek words hōra and scopos, meaning "time" and "observer" (horoskopos, pl. horoskopoi, or "marker(s) of the hour"). It is used as a method of divination regarding events relating to the point in time it represents, and forms the basis of the horoscopic traditions of astrology.

Construction

A natal horoscope is cast for the exact date, time, and place of birth. The astrologer computes the ascendant (the zodiac sign rising on the eastern horizon), the midheaven, the positions of the planets in the twelve signs and twelve houses, and the angular relationships (aspects) between them. Interpretation then proceeds from these placements, sign by sign and house by house.

History

Horoscopic astrology developed in the Hellenistic world, drawing on Babylonian omen astrology and Greek astronomy, and was codified in works such as Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos. It spread to India and the Islamic world, returning to medieval Europe, where casting horoscopes was a learned profession. In the twentieth century, simplified "sun-sign" horoscopes — based only on the Sun's sign — became a staple of newspapers and magazines.

Criticism

Scientific tests of natal astrology, including the widely cited double-blind test published by Shawn Carlson in Nature in 1985, have found astrologers unable to match charts to personality profiles better than chance. The popularity of general horoscope readings is often explained by the Barnum effect — the tendency to accept vague, generally applicable descriptions as personally meaningful. Astrology and horoscopes are regarded by the scientific community as pseudoscience, though they retain a large popular following.
