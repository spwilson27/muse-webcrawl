Impressment


A press gang in action, early 19th century

Impressment, colloquially "the press" or "press-ganging", is the taking of men into military or naval service by force, with or without notice. It was most famously practiced by the British Royal Navy during the 18th and early 19th centuries, when press gangs seized sailors from merchant vessels, ports, and coastal towns to man warships during wartime expansions.

Practice

Naval captains were issued press warrants authorizing them to compel service from "seamen, seafaring men and persons whose occupations and callings are to work in vessels and boats upon rivers." In theory, landsmen and foreign nationals were exempt, but in practice gangs often took men indiscriminately, and even legal protections such as apprenticeships or employment in protected trades were frequently ignored. Impressed men served indefinitely unless discharged, escaped, or were killed or disabled.

Anglo-American tensions

The impressment of sailors from American merchant ships, and Britain's claim to seize British-born deserters who had taken American citizenship, was a major grievance leading to the War of 1812. Incidents such as the Chesapeake–Leopard affair of 1807 inflamed American public opinion. The practice effectively ended after the Napoleonic Wars, as the navy reduced in size and moved toward long-service volunteer enlistment.
