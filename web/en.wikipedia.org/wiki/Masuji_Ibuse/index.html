Masuji Ibuse

Masuji Ibuse (Japanese: 井伏 鱒二; 15 February 1898 – 10 July 1993) was a Japanese novelist and short-story writer, celebrated for the gentle humor, plain style, and deep compassion of works such as John Manjirō, the Castaway and Black Rain (1965–66), the great novel of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima. A mentor to Osamu Dazai, he received the Order of Culture in 1966 and is regarded as one of the most humane voices of twentieth-century Japanese fiction.

Life

Ibuse was born in the mountain village of Kamo (now part of Fukuyama) in Hiroshima Prefecture, the son of a landowning family. He went to Tokyo intending to become a painter, then studied French literature at Waseda University, leaving without a degree to pursue writing. His debut story "Seizure" — later famous in its revised form as "Salamander" (1929), the fable of a salamander trapped in his rock pool — appeared in 1923. Through the 1930s he built a reputation for warm, deceptively simple stories of country people and small absurdities, and his historical novel John Manjirō, the Castaway, about the shipwrecked boy who became Japan's first student in America, won the Naoki Prize.

During the Pacific War Ibuse served as a propaganda-corps writer in Singapore and Malaya, experiences he later treated with quiet irony. After the war he settled in Tokyo, fished obsessively, and wrote steadily into his nineties.

Black Rain

Ibuse's masterpiece, Black Rain (Kuroi Ame, serialized 1965–66), is built from the diaries of a Hiroshima survivor, Shigematsu, who transcribes his family's experience of the bombing of 6 August 1945 and the radioactive "black rain" that fell afterward, while trying — years later — to arrange a marriage for his niece Yasuko, whose prospects are shadowed by rumors that she was exposed. Drawing on actual diaries and documents, the novel records catastrophe in an even, restrained voice that makes it one of the most powerful works of atomic-bomb literature. Shōhei Imamura's film adaptation (1989) won major international attention.

Style and legacy

Ibuse's prose — plain, humorous, oblique — hides moral weight beneath country anecdotes and fishing stories. He was famously a teacher: the young Osamu Dazai attached himself to him as a disciple, a fraught relationship retold in both writers' legends. Ibuse died in Tokyo in 1993 at the age of 95.
