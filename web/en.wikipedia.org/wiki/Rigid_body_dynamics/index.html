Rigid body dynamics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rigid-body dynamics studies the movement of systems of interconnected bodies under the action of external forces. The assumption that the bodies are rigid — that they do not deform under the action of applied forces — simplifies analysis by reducing the number of parameters needed to describe the configuration of the system to the translation and rotation of reference frames attached to each body. The dynamics of a rigid body system is described by the laws of kinematics and by the application of Newton's second law or their derivatives in Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics.

Kinematics

The configuration of a rigid body in space is specified by the position of its centre of mass (3 parameters) and its orientation (3 parameters, e.g. Euler angles, a rotation matrix or a unit quaternion). Velocities follow from the linear velocity of the centre of mass and the angular velocity vector ω; the velocity of any point of the body is v = vCM + ω × r.

Kinetics

Translation of the centre of mass obeys ΣF = maCM. Rotation about the centre of mass is governed by Euler's equations, expressed with the inertia tensor I:

I ω̇ + ω × (I ω) = τ

In principal axes these reduce to three scalar equations. Consequences include torque-free precession (the polhode motion of an asymmetric top) and gyroscopic stability. The total kinetic energy splits into translational and rotational parts: T = ½mvCM² + ½ω·I·ω.

Applications

Rigid-body dynamics underlies robotics, vehicle and spacecraft dynamics, biomechanics, and real-time physics engines in video games and animation, where fast approximations integrate the equations of motion under constraints and collisions.
