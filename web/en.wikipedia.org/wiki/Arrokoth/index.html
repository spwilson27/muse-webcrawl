486958 Arrokoth (provisional designation 2014 MU69) is a trans-Neptunian object in the classical Kuiper belt. It is a contact binary — two lobes gently merged — about 36 km long, and is the most distant and most primitive object ever visited by a spacecraft: NASA's New Horizons probe flew past it on 1 January 2019, after its 2015 Pluto flyby.

Name

Before its official naming, the object was nicknamed "Ultima Thule" following a public campaign. In November 2019 it was formally named Arrokoth, a word meaning "sky" in the Powhatan language of the Algonquian peoples of the Chesapeake Bay region, with the consent of Powhatan elders; the name was chosen in part because the discovery and mission teams were based in the region (Maryland and Washington, D.C.).

Discovery and flyby

Arrokoth was discovered on 26 June 2014 in images from the Hubble Space Telescope taken during a dedicated search for a post-Pluto target for New Horizons. Its orbit — nearly circular, low-inclination, at about 44.6 AU — identifies it as a "cold classical" Kuiper belt object, a population thought to have formed in place and remained largely undisturbed since the Solar System's formation.

New Horizons passed within about 3,500 km of Arrokoth at 05:33 UTC on 1 January 2019, at a distance of about 6.6 billion km (43.4 AU) from the Sun, returning images and spectra over the following months.

Geology and composition

The object consists of two flattened lobes — informally named Wenu (the larger) and Weeyo — joined at a narrow neck, resembling a snowman or two pancakes merged. Its surface is strikingly red, coloured by tholins (complex organic compounds), and relatively smooth with few craters, consistent with a pristine planetesimal formed by the gentle gravitational collapse of a pebble cloud (the streaming-instability model) rather than by violent collision. Surface features include bright patches in the neck and rolling terrain on Wenu. Methanol ice and complex organics were detected spectroscopically; water ice was surprisingly hard to detect on the surface.

Significance

Because cold classicals are thought to be the least evolved bodies accessible to exploration, Arrokoth provides direct evidence about how planetesimals — the building blocks of the planets — formed in the early Solar System.
