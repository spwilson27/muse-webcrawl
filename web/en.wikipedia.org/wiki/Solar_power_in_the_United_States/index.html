Solar power in the United States

Solar power in the United States includes utility-scale photovoltaic plants, distributed rooftop solar, and concentrated solar power. The U.S. is the world's second-largest solar market after China. By the mid-2020s total installed solar capacity exceeded 235 GW, and solar supplied roughly 5–7% of national electricity generation, with much higher shares in states such as California, Nevada, Arizona, and Hawaii. Solar has been the largest source of new generating capacity added to the U.S. grid in recent years.

History

Modern photovoltaics began in the U.S.: Bell Labs demonstrated the first practical silicon solar cell in 1954. Early markets were satellites and remote power. The Solar Energy Generating Systems (SEGS) trough plants in California's Mojave Desert (1984–1990) pioneered utility CSP, and the Ivanpah tower plant (2014) became the largest CSP facility of its kind. Falling module prices in the 2010s, driven by global manufacturing scale, made solar the cheapest new electricity source in much of the country.

Policy

Key drivers include the federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC), first enacted in 2005 and extended and expanded by the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022, which also added manufacturing credits to rebuild a domestic supply chain; state renewable portfolio standards; and net metering rules for rooftop customers, long the subject of regulatory debate. California's policies made it the longstanding state leader, though Texas has led in recent annual utility-scale additions.

Segments

  • Utility-scale — large ground-mounted plants selling power under contract; the majority of capacity.
  • Residential and commercial rooftop — millions of installations, concentrated in sunny and high-electricity-price states.
  • Community solar — shared projects allowing renters and others to subscribe.
  • Storage pairing — batteries increasingly co-located to shift solar energy into the evening peak.
