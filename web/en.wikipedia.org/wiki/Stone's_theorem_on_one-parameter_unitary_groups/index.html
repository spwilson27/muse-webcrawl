Stone's theorem on one-parameter unitary groups

Stone's theorem, proved by Marshall Harvey Stone in 1930–1932, is a fundamental result in functional analysis that establishes a correspondence between strongly continuous one-parameter unitary groups on a Hilbert space and self-adjoint operators. It is a cornerstone of the mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics, where it connects observables (self-adjoint operators) with the symmetries and dynamics they generate.

Statement

Theorem. Let {U(t) : t ∈ ℝ} be a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group on a Hilbert space H. Then there exists a unique self-adjoint operator A on H such that U(t) = eitA for all t, where the exponential is defined by the functional calculus. Conversely, for every self-adjoint operator A, the operators eitA form a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group whose infinitesimal generator is A.

The generator is recovered as the strong derivative at zero: Aψ = −i limt→0 (U(t)ψ − ψ)/t for precisely those vectors ψ for which the limit exists; this set is the domain of A.

Significance in quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics, time evolution is given by a one-parameter unitary group U(t) = e−itH/ℏ, and Stone's theorem guarantees that the Hamiltonian H is a self-adjoint operator, ensuring that the Schrödinger equation iℏ dψ/dt = Hψ is well posed. Similarly, spatial translations are generated by the momentum operator and rotations by angular momentum. Wigner's theorem and the Stone–von Neumann theorem build on this framework.

Generalizations

The theorem extends to unitary representations of locally compact abelian groups (the SNAG theorem — Stone–Naimark–Ambrose–Godement) and has an analogue for contraction semigroups in the Hille–Yosida theorem, where self-adjoint operators are replaced by generators of strongly continuous semigroups.
