HOMO and LUMO

The HOMO and LUMO are the highest-energy filled and lowest-energy empty molecular orbitals of a molecule, respectively. HOMO stands for highest occupied molecular orbital and LUMO for lowest unoccupied molecular orbital. Both are examples of frontier molecular orbitals, a concept introduced by Kenichi Fukui in frontier molecular orbital theory.

Energy gap

The energy difference between the HOMO and the LUMO, called the HOMO–LUMO gap, is an important measure of how easily a molecule can be excited, how it absorbs light, and how it behaves chemically. A large gap generally implies high kinetic stability and low chemical reactivity, because adding an electron to a high-lying LUMO or removing one from a low-lying HOMO is energetically costly. Conversely, a small gap implies that the molecule is easily polarised and reactive, and that it absorbs light at longer wavelengths.

Chemical behaviour

The HOMO can be thought of as the orbital that acts as an electron donor, analogous to the valence band in solids, while the LUMO acts as the electron acceptor, analogous to the conduction band. In interactions between two molecules, the strongest stabilising interactions generally occur between the HOMO of one reactant and the LUMO of the other, provided their energies are close and their symmetries match. Concepts of chemical hardness and softness in HSAB theory are closely related to the size of the HOMO–LUMO gap.

Measurement

The energy of the HOMO can be estimated from ionisation energies measured by photoelectron spectroscopy, while the LUMO energy is related to electron affinity. In practice, orbital energies are most often obtained from quantum chemical calculations, and the HOMO–LUMO gap is widely used in the design of organic semiconductors, dyes, and photovoltaic materials.
