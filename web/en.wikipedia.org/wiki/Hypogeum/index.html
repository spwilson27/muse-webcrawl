Hypogeum

A hypogeum (plural hypogea) is an underground temple or tomb. The word derives from the Greek hypo (under) and gaia (earth). Hypogea often contain niches for cremated human remains or loculi for buried remains, and were used in the ancient world for religious and funerary purposes as well as storage and habitation.

Notable examples

The most famous example is the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum in Paola, Malta, a Neolithic underground complex carved into limestone over several levels, dated to around 4000–2500 BC, used both as a sanctuary and a necropolis for more than 7,000 individuals. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site, notable for rooms imitating megalithic temple architecture above ground, red ochre paintings, and the "Sleeping Lady" figurine found there.

Other examples include the hypogea of ancient Egypt (such as the tombs in the Valley of the Kings), Etruscan hypogea in Italy, the Roman catacombs and hypogea of early Christian Rome, the hypogeum beneath the Colosseum — a network of tunnels and cages that held animals and gladiators before contests — and carved rock tombs across the Mediterranean and Near East.

Construction and use

Hypogea were excavated from living rock or built below ground and covered over. Their cool, dark interiors offered protection for burials and ritual seclusion. Archaeologists use hypogea to study funerary customs, diet and disease (from skeletal remains), and ancient engineering.
