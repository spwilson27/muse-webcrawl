High Holy Days

The High Holy Days, also called the High Holidays or Days of Awe (Hebrew: יָמִים נוֹרָאִים, Yamim Noraim), are the most solemn period of the Jewish religious year, comprising Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish New Year, and Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, together with the ten days between them, the Ten Days of Repentance.

Observance

The season begins on the first of Tishrei with Rosh Hashanah, marked by the sounding of the shofar (ram's horn), festive meals with symbolic foods such as apples and honey, and the tashlikh ceremony of casting sins into water. During the intervening days, Jews engage in repentance (teshuvah), prayer and charity, seeking forgiveness from God and from other people. Traditional belief holds that on Rosh Hashanah God inscribes each person's fate for the coming year in the Book of Life, and that the decree is sealed on Yom Kippur.

Yom Kippur

Yom Kippur, the tenth of Tishrei, is observed with a 25-hour fast, abstention from work, and extended synagogue services including the Kol Nidre prayer on the eve of the fast, the memorial service Yizkor, and the concluding Ne'ila service ending with a final shofar blast. Synagogue attendance reaches its annual peak during the High Holy Days, and many otherwise non-observant Jews attend services.
