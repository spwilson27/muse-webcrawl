Trypanosoma brucei

PhylumEuglenozoa
OrderTrypanosomatida
FamilyTrypanosomatidae
GenusTrypanosoma
SpeciesT. brucei

Trypanosoma brucei is a species of parasitic kinetoplastid protozoan that causes African trypanosomiasis, known as sleeping sickness in humans and nagana in animals, in sub-Saharan Africa. It is transmitted by the bite of infected tsetse flies (Glossina species). Two subspecies infect humans: T. b. gambiense, responsible for the chronic form of the disease in West and Central Africa, and T. b. rhodesiense, which causes the acute form in East and Southern Africa. A third subspecies, T. b. brucei, infects animals but not humans.

Life cycle

The parasite has a complex life cycle alternating between the mammalian bloodstream and the tsetse fly gut. In the mammal, slender bloodstream trypomastigotes multiply by binary fission in blood, lymph, and tissue fluids; some transform into short, stumpy forms that can infect feeding flies. In the fly's midgut they become procyclic trypomastigotes, migrate to the salivary glands, and mature into infective metacyclic forms.

Antigenic variation

T. brucei is famous for its ability to evade the host immune system by antigenic variation of its surface coat, which is composed of a dense layer of variant surface glycoprotein (VSG). The genome contains more than a thousand VSG genes and pseudogenes, of which only one is expressed at a time; periodic switching of the expressed gene produces successive waves of parasitemia. The parasite's unusual cell biology — including its single large mitochondrion with a concatenated DNA network called the kinetoplast, and RNA editing of mitochondrial transcripts — has made it an important model organism.
