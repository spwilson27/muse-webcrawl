Treasury stock

Treasury stock, or treasury shares, are shares that were originally issued by a company and subsequently reacquired by that same company through a share buyback. Treasury shares are issued but not outstanding: they carry no voting rights, receive no dividends, and are not counted in calculations of earnings per share.

Accounting treatment

On the balance sheet, treasury stock is recorded as a contra-equity account, reducing total shareholders' equity by the cost of the repurchased shares (the cost method, most common in the United States). Treasury shares may later be resold, used to satisfy employee share option and compensation plans, or retired, in which case they revert to the status of authorised but unissued capital.

Reasons for buybacks

Companies repurchase shares to return cash to shareholders, to signal that management believes the shares are undervalued, to offset dilution from employee equity awards, or to increase earnings per share by reducing the share count. In some jurisdictions, notably the United Kingdom, companies were historically much more restricted in holding their own shares; reforms have permitted treasury shares subject to limits on quantity and duration.
