Ragamala paintings

Placeholder: a Ragamala miniature gives visual form to the mood of a raga.

Ragamala paintings are a form of Indian miniature painting, a set of illustrations depicting the ragas — the melodic modes of Indian classical music. The Sanskrit word rāgamālā means "garland of ragas". Flourishing in the courts of Rajasthan and the Deccan, and in the Pahari kingdoms of the Punjab Hills, from the late fifteenth to the nineteenth century, these paintings translate sound into image: each raga and its feminine counterpart, the ragini, together with subsidiary "sons" (ragaputras), were personified as heroes, heroines, lovers, or deities in evocative scenes.

System and iconography

Standard systems group six principal (male) ragas — Bhairava, Malkauns, Hindola, Deepak, Megha, and Shri — each with five or six raginis and a retinue of sons, making sets of 36, 42, or more leaves. Each mode is bound to a season, a time of day, and a mood (rasa): Megha, the cloud raga, brings peacocks dancing in monsoon rain; Deepak, the fire raga, shows lamps and flames; the ragini Todi is pictured as a woman charming deer with her vina in a forest. Verses known as dhyana mantras, describing each figure, were often inscribed on the paintings themselves and served painters as iconographic prescriptions.

Schools and collections

Ragamala series were produced in the Mughal-influenced Deccan sultanates, in the Rajput courts of Mewar, Bundi, Kota, Malwa, and Bikaner, and in the Pahari centres of Basohli and Kangra, each with a distinctive palette and mood — from the bold, flat intensity of early Basohli to the lyrical delicacy of Kangra. Complete and partial sets are held by major museums including the Los Angeles County Museum of Art, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Victoria and Albert Museum.
