Hemispherectomy

Hemispherectomy is a neurosurgical procedure in which one cerebral hemisphere of the brain is removed, partially removed, or disconnected. It is a drastic but often highly effective treatment for severe, drug-resistant epilepsy arising from one damaged hemisphere, and is performed mostly in children, whose brains can often reorganise functions into the remaining hemisphere.

History

The operation was first performed in 1928 by Walter Dandy, who removed the right hemisphere of patients with malignant brain tumours. In the 1950s it was applied to epilepsy, notably by the Canadian neurosurgeon Theodore Rasmussen, but anatomical hemispherectomy fell out of favour because of delayed complications such as superficial cerebral haemosiderosis caused by blood breakdown products in the cavity.

Types

Anatomical hemispherectomy removes the entire affected hemisphere. Functional hemispherectomy, developed to reduce complications, removes only part of the hemisphere (usually the temporal lobe and central region) and severs the connecting white matter tracts, including the corpus callosum, leaving the disconnected tissue in place but isolated. Modern variants, such as hemispherotomy, disconnect the hemisphere through smaller openings.

Indications and outcomes

Candidate conditions include Rasmussen's encephalitis, Sturge–Weber syndrome, hemimegalencephaly, large prenatal strokes, and extensive cortical dysplasia. Around 70–80 per cent of appropriately selected patients become seizure-free. Expected deficits include weakness of the opposite hand and loss of the opposite visual half-field; remarkably, language usually recovers well when the operation is done early in childhood, even for the dominant hemisphere. The procedure illustrates the remarkable neuroplasticity of the young brain.
