Fuzzy logic

Fuzzy logic is a form of many-valued logic in which the truth value of variables may be any real number between 0 and 1, representing degrees of truth. It is employed to handle the concept of partial truth, where the truth value may range between completely true and completely false — in contrast to Boolean logic, where values are only the integers 0 or 1. The term was introduced with the 1965 proposal of fuzzy set theory by mathematician Lotfi Zadeh of UC Berkeley.

Fuzzy sets and membership

In fuzzy set theory, an element belongs to a set to a degree given by a membership function. Linguistic variables such as "temperature is hot" take fuzzy values, and reasoning proceeds with rules of the form "if temperature is hot, then fan speed is high". Logical operations are generalised, commonly using min for AND, max for OR, and 1−x for NOT (Zadeh operators), though t-norms give other options.

Applications

Fuzzy control systems became the first major commercial application: the Sendai subway's fuzzy train controller (1987) and Japanese consumer products of the late 1980s — washing machines, rice cookers, camcorder stabilisation, and air conditioners — popularised the approach. Fuzzy methods are also used in control engineering, pattern recognition, decision support, and data mining, and in consumer terms such as "fuzzy" washing cycles.

Relation to probability and criticism

Fuzzy logic is distinct from probability: fuzziness models vagueness of meaning (e.g., "tall"), whereas probability models uncertainty about well-defined events. Critics, including some statisticians, have argued that many fuzzy applications could be handled by probability or classical control; proponents respond that fuzzy systems are simple to design from expert knowledge and robust in nonlinear control.
