Richard Ainley Easterlin (January 12, 1926 – December 19, 2024) was an American economist and demographer, regarded as a founder of the economics of happiness. He is best known for the Easterlin paradox — the observation that rising national income does not necessarily produce rising reported happiness — and for his work on fertility, cohort size and long-run economic growth.

Career

Easterlin studied engineering at Stevens Institute of Technology and took his PhD in economics at the University of Pennsylvania in 1953. He taught at Penn for most of his early career before moving to the University of Southern California in 1982, where he remained as professor emeritus. He was a member of the National Academy of Sciences and a past president of the Population Association of America and the Economic History Association.

The Easterlin paradox

In his 1974 paper "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Some Empirical Evidence," Easterlin pointed out that although richer people report being happier than poorer people within a country at a given time, average happiness in a country does not rise with long-run economic growth, and richer and poorer countries can report similar average happiness. He explained this through relative income: people judge their well-being against social norms and their own rising aspirations, so general income growth "cancels out." The paper helped launch the modern field of happiness economics and remains a central reference in debates about growth and well-being.

Demographic work

Easterlin also proposed the "relative cohort size" hypothesis: members of unusually large birth cohorts (such as the baby boom) face crowded labour and housing markets and comparatively low relative incomes, and respond by marrying later and having fewer children, while small cohorts enjoy the opposite advantages — a mechanism he used to explain post-war fertility swings in the United States. His scholarly books include Population, Labor Force, and Long Swings in Economic Growth (1968) and Birth and Fortune (1980), and he edited Happiness in Economics (2002).
