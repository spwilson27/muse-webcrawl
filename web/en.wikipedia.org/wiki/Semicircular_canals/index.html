Semicircular canals

The semicircular canals are three semicircular, fluid-filled tubes in the inner ear of all jawed vertebrates. They are part of the vestibular system and detect rotational movements of the head, working together with the otolith organs, which detect linear acceleration and gravity.

Structure

The three canals — anterior (superior), posterior and lateral (horizontal) — are arranged roughly at right angles to one another, each lying in a different plane of rotation. Each canal is filled with endolymph and opens at one end into a swelling, the ampulla, which contains the crista ampullaris: a mound of hair cells whose stereocilia are embedded in a gelatinous cupula.

Function

When the head rotates, the endolymph lags behind the canal wall because of inertia, deflecting the cupula and bending the hair-cell bundles. This changes the firing rate of vestibular nerve fibres, signalling angular acceleration in the canal's plane. Signals from the two ears are compared, and the vestibulo-ocular reflex uses them to stabilise gaze by moving the eyes opposite to head rotation.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the canals causes vertigo. In benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, displaced otoconia enter a canal (usually the posterior) and produce brief spinning sensations with certain head positions, treated with repositioning manoeuvres such as the Epley manoeuvre. Superior canal dehiscence, in which bone over the anterior canal is missing, can cause sound- or pressure-induced vertigo.
