Pacifism

Pacifism is the opposition or resistance to war, militarism (including conscription and mandatory military service) or violence. The word pacifism was coined by the French peace campaigner Émile Arnaud and adopted by other peace activists at the tenth Universal Peace Congress in Glasgow in 1901. A related term is ahimsa (to do no harm), which is a core philosophy in Indian religions such as Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism.

Varieties

Pacifist positions range across a spectrum. Absolute pacifists hold that all violence—including in self-defence—is morally wrong. Conditional pacifists oppose war in practice while conceding some wars might be justified; "nuclear pacifists" specifically reject nuclear war. Others emphasise nonviolent resistance as a pragmatic strategy, as developed by Mohandas Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. Conscientious objection—the refusal of military service on grounds of conscience—grew from pacifist religious traditions such as the historic peace churches (Quakers, Mennonites, Brethren).

History

Nonviolent ideals appear in ancient sources—from Jain and Buddhist ahimsa to the teachings of Jesus and early Christian refusal of military service. Modern organised pacifism emerged in the 19th century with peace societies in Britain and America, grew after the slaughter of the First World War, and shaped campaigns for nuclear disarmament and opposition to the Vietnam War in the 20th century.

Criticism

Critics argue that pacifism can enable aggression, that violence may be necessary to stop atrocities, and that nonviolence succeeds only against opponents with a conscience. Pacifists reply that war's costs and escalatory dynamics consistently exceed predictions, and point to the documented effectiveness of civil resistance campaigns in the 20th and 21st centuries.
