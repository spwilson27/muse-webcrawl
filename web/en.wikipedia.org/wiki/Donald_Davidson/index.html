Donald Herbert Davidson (March 6, 1917 – August 30, 2003) was an American philosopher. He served as Slusser Professor of Philosophy at the University of California, Berkeley, from 1981 to 2003 after having also held chairs at Rockefeller University, Princeton University, and the University of Chicago. Davidson was known for his charismatic personality and the depth and difficulty of his thought. His work exerted a considerable influence in many areas of philosophy from the 1960s onward, particularly in philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, and action theory. While Davidson was an analytic philosopher, and most of his influence lies in that tradition, his work has also attracted attention in continental philosophy, particularly in literary theory and related areas.

Life and career

Davidson was born in Springfield, Massachusetts, and studied at Harvard University, where he initially pursued literature and classics before turning to philosophy. He earned his PhD in 1949 with a dissertation on Plato's Philebus. At Harvard he was taught by, among others, Willard Van Orman Quine, who became a major influence. During the Second World War he served in the United States Navy. Davidson held posts at Queen's College, Stanford University, Princeton, Rockefeller, the University of Chicago, and finally Berkeley.

Work

Radical interpretation and the principle of charity

Davidson's project of radical interpretation asks how a field linguist could come to understand a completely unknown language using only the evidence of speakers' assent and dissent to sentences in observable circumstances. Building on a Tarski-style truth theory as a theory of meaning, Davidson argued that interpretation must proceed under a principle of charity: the interpreter must attribute to speakers mostly true beliefs and rational patterns of thought, since massive error would undermine the very possibility of interpretation.

Anomalous monism

In his 1970 paper "Mental Events," Davidson defended anomalous monism, the view that mental events are identical with physical events (token identity) while there are no strict psychophysical laws connecting mental and physical descriptions. Mental events cause physical events, but causation holds between events however described, whereas laws are linguistic and the mental vocabulary is not reducible to the physical.

Action theory

In "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963), Davidson revived the view that reasons are causes of action, against the then-dominant Wittgensteinian orthodoxy. An action is intentional under a description when it is caused, in the right way, by a primary reason: a belief and a pro-attitude toward actions of a kind.

Truth and meaning, triangulation

Later work developed the idea of triangulation: thought and speech require interaction between at least two creatures and a shared world, so that each can correlate the other's responses with distal stimuli. Davidson also rejected the scheme–content distinction in "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme" (1974), arguing against conceptual relativism.
