Efficiency (statistics)

In statistics, efficiency is a measure of the quality of an estimator, of an experimental design, or of a hypothesis testing procedure. Essentially, a more efficient estimator, experiment or test needs fewer observations than a less efficient one to achieve a given performance. The concept was introduced by Ronald Fisher and is closely tied to the Cramér–Rao bound, which sets a lower limit on the variance of any unbiased estimator.

A more efficient estimator has a narrower sampling distribution.

Estimators

The efficiency of an unbiased estimator T of a parameter θ is defined as e(T) = 1/I(θ) ÷ Var(T), where I(θ) is the Fisher information of the sample. A value of 1 means the estimator attains the Cramér–Rao lower bound and cannot be improved upon (among unbiased estimators). Such an estimator is called efficient.

Relative efficiency

For two estimators, the relative efficiency compares their variances: the estimator with the smaller variance is more efficient. For example, for normally distributed data the sample mean is more efficient than the sample median; the asymptotic relative efficiency of the median to the mean is 2/π ≈ 0.637, meaning the median needs about 57 percent more observations to match the mean's precision. For heavy-tailed distributions, the ranking can reverse.

Asymptotic efficiency

Many estimators are only efficient asymptotically, as the sample size tends to infinity. Maximum likelihood estimators are, under regularity conditions, asymptotically efficient — one of the principal justifications for their widespread use.

Experimental design and tests

The same idea applies to designs (a more efficient design reaches a given precision with fewer runs) and to hypothesis tests (Pitman efficiency compares tests by the sample sizes they need to reach the same power against nearby alternatives).
