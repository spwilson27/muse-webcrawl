Holograph

A holograph is a document written entirely in the handwriting of the person whose signature it bears. The term derives from the Greek holos ("whole") and graphein ("to write"). Holograph documents are significant in law — most notably the holographic will — and in historical and literary scholarship, where an author's own manuscript can authenticate authorship and reveal the process of composition.

Holographic wills

A holographic will is a last will and testament written wholly in the testator's own hand and signed by the testator, typically without witnesses. Because the entire document is in the testator's handwriting, many jurisdictions that recognize them dispense with the usual formalities of attestation, reasoning that the handwriting itself provides strong evidence of authenticity and intent. Holographic wills are valid in roughly half of U.S. states, in a number of civil-law jurisdictions (for example under the French Civil Code), and in Canadian provinces such as Ontario, though rules vary: some jurisdictions require the material provisions to be entirely handwritten, and some admit holographic wills only in limited circumstances, such as for soldiers in the field or mariners at sea (the historical "nuncupative" and privileged wills).

In scholarship and collecting

Holograph manuscripts of letters, diaries, drafts, and literary works are prized by archives and collectors. Comparison of a holograph draft with the published text allows scholars to trace revisions; a holograph letter can resolve questions of attribution that a printed or secretarial copy cannot. Conversely, distinguishing a document that is merely signed from one written throughout by the author is an important task in authentication, since genuine holograph material generally commands far greater evidentiary and monetary value.
