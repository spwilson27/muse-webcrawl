Smokeless powder

Smokeless powder is a type of propellant used in firearms and artillery that produces far less smoke and fouling than black powder when fired. The name is nearly a misnomer, since the product is neither a true powder nor completely smokeless, but the term persists. Smokeless powder consists of nitrocellulose alone (single-base), nitrocellulose with nitroglycerin (double-base), or nitrocellulose, nitroglycerin and nitroguanidine (triple-base), formed into grains whose size and shape control the burning rate.

History

Black powder's dense white smoke obscured battlefields and revealed gun positions, and its fouling clogged breech mechanisms. In 1884 French chemist Paul Vieille invented Poudre B, made from gelatinised nitrocellulose, the first practical smokeless powder; France adopted it in the Lebel rifle, the first military firearm to use smokeless ammunition. Alfred Nobel patented ballistite (nitroglycerin and nitrocellulose) in 1887, and Frederick Abel and James Dewar developed the similar British cordite in 1889. By the 1890s smokeless powders had transformed infantry weapons, enabling smaller-calibre, higher-velocity, magazine-fed rifles and effective machine guns.

Characteristics

Smokeless powder contains roughly three times the energy of black powder per unit mass and much of its combustion product is gaseous, so it leaves little solid residue. Its burning is a surface phenomenon: manufacturers shape the grains — balls, flakes, cylinders, or perforated sticks — and apply deterrent coatings so that gas is generated at a controlled, progressive rate suited to the weapon, from fast powders for shotguns and pistols to slow powders for rifles and cannon.

Stability and safety

Nitrocellulose slowly decomposes, releasing nitrogen oxides that accelerate further breakdown; stabilisers such as diphenylamine are added to absorb these products, and stored powder is tested for stabiliser depletion. Old or heat-degraded smokeless powder can become hazardous and is a concern in museum ammunition stocks. Unconfined, smokeless powder burns vigorously rather than exploding, but under confinement it is a high explosive hazard and is regulated as an explosive in transport and storage.

Use

Virtually all modern small arms, most artillery, and many rocket and ejection-seat charges use smokeless propellants. Handloaders choose from hundreds of commercial grades differing in burn rate and energy content.
