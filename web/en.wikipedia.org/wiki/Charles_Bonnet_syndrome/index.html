Charles Bonnet syndrome

Charles Bonnet syndrome (CBS) is a condition in which people with significant vision loss experience vivid, complex visual hallucinations while remaining psychologically normal and fully aware that the images are not real. The hallucinations — which may include patterns, faces, costumed figures, animals, or miniature people ("Lilliputian" hallucinations) — arise because the visual cortex, deprived of normal input, becomes spontaneously active. The syndrome is named after the Swiss naturalist Charles Bonnet, who described the experiences of his grandfather in 1760.

Complex visual hallucinations with retained insight characterize the syndrome

Signs and symptoms

Hallucinations in CBS are purely visual, occur in the blind or dim parts of the visual field, and are not accompanied by delusions or hallucinations in other senses. Insight is retained: sufferers know the figures are unreal, though the images may be startling or distressing at first. Episodes typically last minutes, may recur for months or years, and often fade as the brain adapts. The condition most commonly affects older adults with macular degeneration, glaucoma, cataracts, or other causes of severe visual impairment; prevalence among people with low vision is often estimated around 10–15%, and it is thought to be underreported because patients fear being labelled mentally ill.

Mechanism

The leading explanation is the "release phenomenon": deafferentation of the visual cortex lowers its activation threshold, so spontaneous firing in specialized visual areas generates percepts corresponding to those areas' functions — faces from face-selective cortex, landscapes from place areas, and so on. The model is analogous to phantom limb sensations after amputation.

Management

There is no definitive drug treatment; management centres on explaining the condition, which greatly relieves anxiety, treating the underlying eye disease where possible, improving lighting and remaining vision, and teaching techniques such as rapid eye movements that can interrupt episodes. Anticonvulsant or antipsychotic medications are occasionally tried in severe cases.
