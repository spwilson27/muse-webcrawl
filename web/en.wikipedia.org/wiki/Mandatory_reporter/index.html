Mandated reporter

A mandated reporter (also called a mandatory reporter) is a person who is legally required by law to report reasonable suspicion of child abuse or neglect, and in some jurisdictions elder abuse or abuse of vulnerable adults, to designated authorities such as child protective services or law enforcement. Failure to report can result in criminal penalties.

Who is a mandated reporter

Categories vary by jurisdiction, but typically include teachers and school staff, childcare workers, medical and mental-health professionals, social workers, clergy (sometimes with confessional exceptions), law enforcement officers, foster parents, and film processors. In a number of U.S. states — including Indiana, New Jersey, and Wyoming — the duty applies to all adults regardless of profession.

History

Mandatory reporting laws spread in the United States during the 1960s and 1970s following the publication of research on the "battered child syndrome" and the passage of the federal Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act (CAPTA) of 1974, which made reporting laws a condition of federal child-protection funding. Similar laws exist in Canada, Australia, and many other countries.

Process and criticism

Reports are usually made by phone to a hotline, followed by a written report within a set period. Reporters generally receive legal immunity for reports made in good faith, and their identity is kept confidential. Critics argue that mandatory reporting can overwhelm child-protection systems with unsubstantiated reports and may deter families from seeking help, while supporters regard it as an essential safeguard for children unable to report abuse themselves.
