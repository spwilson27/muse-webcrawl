Dionysius of Phocaea

Dionysius of Phocaea (fl. 494 BC) was a Greek admiral from Phocaea in Ionia who commanded the combined Ionian fleet at the Battle of Lade during the Ionian Revolt against the Persian Empire. The revolt's decisive naval defeat at Lade in 494 BC led to the fall of Miletus and the effective end of the rebellion. Dionysius' story is told by the historian Herodotus in Book 6 of his Histories.

Battle of Lade

When the Persian fleet advanced on Miletus in 494 BC, the Ionian cities assembled a fleet of 353 triremes at the island of Lade, off the Milesian coast. The contingents chose Dionysius as their common commander. According to Herodotus, Dionysius told the Ionians that their fate rested "on a razor's edge" and that they must choose between freedom and slavery; he then subjected them to a rigorous regime of training, drilling them in the breakthrough manoeuvre (the diekplous), keeping the ships at anchor under arms and the crews working through the heat of the day.

After seven days the Ionians, unaccustomed to such discipline, refused to continue the training and went ashore to rest in the shade. When the battle came, the large Samian contingent — secretly persuaded by the Persians through their exiled tyrant Aeaces — deserted in the middle of the fighting, followed by the Lesbians and most of the other allies. Only the Chians fought on with distinction. The Ionian fleet was destroyed.

Later career

Dionysius, seeing the cause was lost, fled with three captured enemy ships. Rather than return to Phocaea, which he expected to be enslaved, he sailed to Phoenicia, sank several merchant vessels and seized considerable plunder, then made his way to Sicily. There he set himself up as a pirate — though Herodotus notes that he preyed only on Carthaginian and Tyrrhenian shipping, and never on Greeks — and accumulated great wealth.
