Loss mitigation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Loss mitigation is the process by which a mortgage servicer works with a borrower in default, or at risk of default, to modify the terms of a loan or otherwise resolve the delinquency in a way that reduces the loss to the lender or investor. Loss mitigation departments became prominent in the United States during the subprime mortgage crisis of 2007–2009, when soaring foreclosures made negotiated resolutions cheaper than repossession for many loans.

Options

Common loss mitigation options include:

Forbearance — a temporary reduction or suspension of payments while the borrower recovers from a short-term hardship. Loan modification — a permanent change to one or more loan terms, such as the interest rate, term or principal balance, to reduce the monthly payment. Repayment plans — spreading missed payments over subsequent months. Short sale — sale of the property for less than the balance owed, with the lender accepting the proceeds in settlement. Deed in lieu of foreclosure — the borrower voluntarily conveys title to the lender in exchange for release from the debt.

Regulation

In the United States, federal mortgage servicing rules administered by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (Regulation X, implementing the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act) require servicers to evaluate complete loss mitigation applications and generally prohibit "dual tracking" — pursuing foreclosure while a complete application is under review. Government programmes such as the Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP), launched in 2009, provided standardised modification waterfalls for eligible loans.
