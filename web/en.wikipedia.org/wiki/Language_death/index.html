Language death

In linguistics, language death occurs when a language loses its last native speaker. By extension, language extinction is when the language is no longer known by anyone, including second-language speakers. Of the roughly 7,000 living languages, a large fraction are endangered.

Types

Language death is usually gradual — communities shift to a dominant language over generations — but can be sudden when all speakers die. Stages are described by UNESCO's vitality scale from vulnerable to extinct. Examples of recently extinct languages include Eyak (Alaska, 2008) and Livonian (2013); Latin is “dead” in a different sense, having evolved into the Romance languages.

Causes

Colonisation, compulsory education in majority languages, urbanisation, media and economic pressure drive language shift. Loss entails loss of cultural knowledge encoded in the language.

Revitalisation

Language revitalisation has revived Hebrew as a spoken language and strengthened Welsh, Hawaiian, Māori and Irish through immersion schools and official support. Documentation by linguists preserves records of dying languages.
