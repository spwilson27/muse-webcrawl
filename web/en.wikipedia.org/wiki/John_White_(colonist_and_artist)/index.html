John White (colonist and artist)

[Image placeholder: watercolour of an Algonquian village in the style of John White]

John White (c. 1540 – c. 1593) was an English colonial governor, explorer, artist, and artist-cartographer. He was a pioneer settler of the English colony at Roanoke Island in present-day North Carolina, and served as governor of the second Roanoke expedition in 1587. His detailed watercolour drawings and maps of the lands and peoples of the North American coast are among the most important visual records of the New World made by a European in the sixteenth century.

Early life and the 1585 expedition

Little is known of White's early life. In 1585 he accompanied Sir Richard Grenville's expedition to the coast of North America, sponsored by Sir Walter Raleigh. During the voyage and the subsequent residence at Roanoke, White produced a remarkable series of watercolours depicting the Algonquian peoples, their villages, ceremonies, dress, and food, as well as the flora and fauna of the region. These images, later engraved by Theodor de Bry and published with Thomas Hariot's A Briefe and True Report of the New Found Land of Virginia (1590), shaped European perceptions of America for generations.

Governor of the 1587 colony

In 1587 White returned to Roanoke as governor of a new colony of more than one hundred men, women, and children, including his daughter Eleanor Dare. Eleanor's daughter, Virginia Dare, born in August 1587, was the first English child born in the Americas. Pressed by the colonists, White reluctantly returned to England later that year to press for supplies, but the Anglo-Spanish War and the Spanish Armada delayed his return until 1590. When he finally reached Roanoke, the settlement was deserted; the only clue was the word "CROATOAN" carved on a post. The fate of the "Lost Colony" remains unknown, and White never learned what became of his family.

Later life and legacy

White spent his final years in Ireland and England, writing an account of his voyages. His surviving watercolours, now in the British Museum, are celebrated both as works of art and as ethnographic documents. His map of the Virginia and Carolina coasts was among the most accurate of its era.
