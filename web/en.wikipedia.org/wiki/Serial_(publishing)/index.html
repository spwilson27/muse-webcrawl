Serial (publishing)

In publishing and library sciences, a serial is a publication issued in successive parts, usually (but not always) bearing numerical or chronological designations, and intended to be continued indefinitely. Serials include periodicals such as magazines and scholarly journals, newspapers, annuals (reports, yearbooks, and directories), monographic series, and numbered proceedings and transactions of societies.

The defining characteristic of a serial, as distinguished from a monograph, is its ongoing nature: each issue or part is published separately over time, and the publication as a whole has no predetermined conclusion. In library cataloguing, serials are typically identified by an International Standard Serial Number (ISSN).

Types of serials

Serials take many forms, including:

  • Periodicals — publications issued at regular intervals more frequently than annually, such as weekly magazines and quarterly journals.
  • Newspapers — serials issued daily or weekly reporting news.
  • Annuals — publications issued once a year, including yearbooks and annual reports.
  • Monographic series — groups of separate monographs issued under a collective title, each volume also bearing its own title.

History

Serial publication became a dominant literary form in the 19th century, when many novels — including most of the works of Charles Dickens — were first published in monthly or weekly installments in magazines before appearing in book form. This practice, sometimes called publication in parts, made literature affordable to a wider readership and shaped narrative techniques such as the cliffhanger.

Serials in libraries

Managing serials is a distinct specialty within librarianship, because libraries must track receipt of individual issues, claim missing numbers, bind completed volumes, and manage subscription renewals and title changes. The advent of electronic journals has transformed serials management, with most scholarly serials now distributed online.
