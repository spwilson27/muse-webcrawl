The Great Highland bagpipe (Scottish Gaelic: a' phìob mhòr, "the great pipe") is a type of bagpipe native to Scotland, and the Scottish analogue to the great Irish warpipes. It has acquired widespread recognition through its usage in the British military and in pipe bands throughout the world. The instrument is most famously associated with Highland regiments, ceremonial occasions, and competitions such as the Glenfiddich Piping Championship.

Design

Air is supplied to an animal-skin or synthetic bag, usually by a blowpipe (or bellows in some variants), and squeezed under the arm to give a continuous wind supply. The melody is played on the chanter, a conical-bore pipe with a double reed, giving a loud, penetrating sound with a range of nine notes and extensive grace-note ornamentation. Three drones — two tenors and a longer bass, each with a single reed — rest over the shoulder and sound constant notes one and two octaves below the chanter's low A.

Music

The classical repertoire is ceòl mòr ("great music"), or piobaireachd: extended, formally structured compositions built as variations on a ground (ùrlar), traditionally used as laments, salutes and battle tunes. Lighter music (ceòl beag) includes marches, strathspeys, reels, hornpipes and jigs. Music was historically transmitted by canntaireachd, a sung vocable notation.

History

Bagpipes existed in Scotland by the 1400s, and the Great Highland form became the instrument of hereditary piping families such as the MacCrimmons of Skye, pipers to the MacLeods of Dunvegan. Though often described as an instrument of war, after the Jacobite rising of 1745 the popular claim that the pipes were legally "banned" is a myth — they were not listed in the Disarming Acts. In the 19th century, competition, military adoption and the Scottish diaspora made the Highland pipe a worldwide emblem of Scotland; the massed pipes and drums of pipe bands are now found from Canada and Australia to Oman and Nepal.
