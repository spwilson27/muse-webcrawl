Croke Park (Irish: Páirc an Chrócaigh) is a Gaelic games stadium in Dublin, Ireland, and the headquarters of the Gaelic Athletic Association (GAA). With a capacity of 82,300, it is among the largest stadiums in Europe, and it hosts the All-Ireland finals in Gaelic football and hurling. It is named for Thomas Croke, Archbishop of Cashel and an early patron of the GAA.

History

The site was used for sport from 1884. The GAA bought the ground in 1913 with the help of Frank Dineen. On 21 November 1920, during the Irish War of Independence, British forces opened fire on the crowd at a Dublin–Tipperary football match, killing 14 people including the Tipperary player Michael Hogan — the original Bloody Sunday. The Hogan Stand commemorates him, and Hill 16, the great terrace, was traditionally said to have been built from the rubble of the 1916 Easter Rising.

The stadium was largely rebuilt between 1993 and 2005 in a four-phase redevelopment that left three sides fully seated under a sweeping roof, with Hill 16 remaining as terracing. During the redevelopment of Lansdowne Road, Croke Park controversially hosted rugby union and association football internationals (2007–2010) after the GAA temporarily relaxed its rule against "foreign games" — including Ireland's 2007 Six Nations rugby match against England, an occasion of great symbolic resonance. The stadium also hosts major concerts.

The stadium today

Croke Park houses the GAA Museum and offers stadium and "skyline" tours of the roof. Its playing surface is larger than that of soccer or rugby grounds, reflecting the dimensions of a Gaelic games pitch.
