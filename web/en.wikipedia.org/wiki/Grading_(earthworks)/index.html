Grading (earthworks)

A motor grader performing fine grading of a building pad.

In civil engineering and landscape construction, grading is the work of ensuring a level base, or a base with a specified slope, for a construction work such as a foundation, the base course for a road or railway, or landscape and garden improvements, or surface drainage. The earthworks created for such a purpose are often called the sub-grade or finished contouring.

Process

Grading typically proceeds in stages:

  • Clearing and grubbing — removal of vegetation, topsoil and obstructions from the site.
  • Rough grading — bulk earthmoving (cut and fill) that brings the site close to its designed elevations, using scrapers, bulldozers, excavators and dump trucks. Soil excavated from high areas ("cut") is deposited and compacted in low areas ("fill").
  • Fine (finish) grading — final shaping of surfaces to precise tolerances, commonly performed with a motor grader, skid-steer loader or laser-guided equipment; GPS and laser machine-control systems are now standard on larger projects.
  • Compaction and proof-rolling — verifying the subgrade can support the loads above it, to specified densities measured by field density tests.

Purposes

Grading is performed to:

  • Provide flat pads for buildings and pavements while keeping the correct elevation relative to foundations and utilities.
  • Direct stormwater away from structures and toward swales, inlets and detention facilities; finished grades around buildings typically fall away at a minimum slope (often 5% for the first few metres in residential codes, with lower minima for paved surfaces).
  • Create road subgrades, railway formations, airport runways, sports fields and golf courses to the profiles shown on the construction drawings.
  • Reshape land for landscaping, erosion control and reclamation of mined or degraded land.

Regulation and environment

Many jurisdictions require a grading permit for earthmoving above a certain volume, and grading plans are usually prepared and certified by a civil engineer or landscape architect. Grading operations are major sources of sediment runoff, so projects are commonly required to implement erosion and sediment controls (such as silt fences and sediment basins) under stormwater regulations. In archaeology-sensitive or protected habitats, grading may be restricted or require prior surveys.

Equipment

The characteristic machine of grading is the motor grader, a long-framed machine with a wide blade between front and rear axles capable of precise control of elevation and cross-slope. Other equipment includes bulldozers, wheel tractor-scrapers, excavators, compactors (sheepsfoot and smooth-drum rollers) and water trucks for moisture conditioning of fill.
