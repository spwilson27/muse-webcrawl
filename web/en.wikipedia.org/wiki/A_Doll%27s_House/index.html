A Doll's House (Danish and Bokmål: Et dukkehjem; also translated as A Doll House) is a three-act play written by the Norwegian playwright Henrik Ibsen. It premiered at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen on 21 December 1879, having been published earlier that month. The play is famous for its critical portrayal of the roles of men and women in 19th-century marriage, and for its ending: the protagonist, Nora Helmer, walks out on her husband and children — the slamming of the door that closes the play was described as "the slam heard around the world".

Plot

Nora Helmer, a seemingly carefree housewife, has secretly borrowed a large sum of money years earlier by forging her father's signature, to finance a trip to Italy that saved her husband Torvald's life. The lender, the bank clerk Nils Krogstad, now blackmails her as Torvald — recently promoted to bank manager — plans to dismiss him. When Torvald learns the truth, he berates Nora and vows to submit to Krogstad's demands only to protect his own reputation; although a reprieve arrives when Krogstad returns the incriminating bond, Nora has recognised that her marriage has been a performance in which she was treated first as her father's and then her husband's "doll". In the celebrated final scene she tells Torvald that she must leave to educate herself and discover who she is, and exits, slamming the door.

Characters

  • Nora Helmer – the protagonist; wife of Torvald.
  • Torvald Helmer – a lawyer and bank manager; Nora's husband.
  • Dr. Rank – a wealthy friend of the Helmers, secretly in love with Nora and dying of disease.
  • Kristine Linde – Nora's widowed school friend, seeking work and renewal.
  • Nils Krogstad – a bank employee and Nora's creditor.
  • Anne-Marie – the Helmers' nanny.

Reception and significance

The play's ending caused a sensation and controversy across Europe; for the first German production Ibsen was pressured into writing an alternative ending in which Nora stays, which he called a "barbaric outrage" against his own work. Ibsen insisted he wrote about humanity rather than feminism as such, but the play became a landmark of modern drama and a touchstone for debates about individual freedom and women's emancipation. UNESCO has included Ibsen's autograph notes for the play in the Memory of the World register, and A Doll's House was reported to be the most performed play in the world in its centenary years.
