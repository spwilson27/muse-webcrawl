Flex-fuel vehicle

Also calledFFV, dual-fuel vehicle
FuelsGasoline blended with ethanol (up to E85/E100) or methanol
Largest marketBrazil, followed by the United States

A flexible-fuel vehicle (FFV) or dual-fuel vehicle is an alternative-fuel vehicle with an internal combustion engine designed to run on more than one fuel, usually gasoline blended with either ethanol or methanol, with both fuels stored in the same common tank. Modern flex-fuel engines can run on any proportion of the mixture, sensed automatically by the engine management system.

Technology

Because ethanol contains oxygen and has a higher octane rating but lower energy density than gasoline, a flex-fuel engine uses a fuel-composition sensor (or infers composition from the exhaust oxygen sensor after refuelling) to adjust injection quantity and ignition timing. Ethanol-compatible materials are used throughout the fuel system to resist corrosion. Fuel economy falls roughly in proportion to the blend's lower energy content — on E85, typically by 25–30 per cent — partly offset where engines are optimised with higher compression ratios.

Markets

Brazil is the world's largest flex-fuel market: following the 1970s Proálcool programme, the first modern flex-fuel cars were launched there in 2003, and they came to dominate new-car sales, running on any mix of gasohol and hydrous ethanol (E100) from sugarcane. In the United States, millions of FFVs were sold — largely to earn manufacturers fuel-economy credits — but E85 fuelling stations remained scarce outside the Midwest corn belt. Sweden and other European markets promoted E85 during the 2000s before policy shifted toward electrification.

Debate

Supporters credit flex-fuel vehicles with cutting petroleum dependence and, where ethanol is made from sugarcane, reducing net carbon emissions. Critics point to food-versus-fuel land use, the modest climate benefit of corn ethanol, and the credit-driven U.S. fleet that rarely used E85 in practice.
