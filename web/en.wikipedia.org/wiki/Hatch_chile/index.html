Hatch chile

Hatch chile refers to chile pepper varieties of the species Capsicum annuum grown in the Hatch Valley of southern New Mexico, centred on the village of Hatch, which styles itself the "Chile Capital of the World". The name denotes the growing region rather than a single cultivar; peppers grown in the valley are marketed as New Mexico chile and prized for their earthy, sweet flavour attributed to the local soil and climate.

Hatch chiles roasting, a late-summer ritual in New Mexico.

Cultivation and varieties

Pepper farming in the Hatch Valley dates to the early 20th century, building on varieties developed at New Mexico State University by horticulturists beginning with Fabián García, whose 'New Mexico No. 9' of 1913 standardised the pod type. Modern named cultivars such as 'Big Jim', 'Joe E. Parker', and 'Sandia' are grown in the valley. Peppers ripen from green to red; green chile is picked unripe and usually roasted, while red chile is left to ripen and is commonly dried and ground into powder or rehydrated into sauce.

Cultural significance

Hatch hosts an annual Chile Festival over the Labor Day weekend, drawing tens of thousands of visitors. Chile is central to New Mexican cuisine — the state's official question, enacted in 1999, is "Red or green?", referring to the choice of chile sauce. In 2023, New Mexico made the smell of roasting green chile the first official state aroma in the United States.
