Track circuit

A track circuit is an electrical device used to prove the absence of a train on a section of railway track to control railway signals. An insulated section of rails is energised with a low-voltage current; when no train is present, the current reaches a relay at the far end and the section is reported clear. When a train enters the section, its metal wheels and axles short-circuit the two rails, the relay de-energises, and the section is reported occupied. Principles similar in function are also used in axle counters.

Basic track circuit: the train's axles shunt the rails and drop the relay.

Principles and history

The track circuit was invented by the American engineer William Robinson, who received a patent in 1872 and demonstrated the system on the Philadelphia and Erie Railroad. It made possible automatic block signalling, in which signals are controlled directly by the trains themselves rather than solely by operators, greatly improving railway safety and capacity.

The design is inherently fail-safe: a broken rail, a failed power supply, or a broken wire interrupts the circuit and causes the relay to drop, indicating "occupied" and setting signals to their most restrictive aspect. Track circuits also detect many broken rails because the break interrupts the return path.

Types

  • DC track circuits — the simplest form, used on non-electrified lines.
  • AC track circuits — used where electric traction return currents flow in the rails; impedance bonds allow traction current to pass while confining signalling current to the section.
  • Audio-frequency (jointless) track circuits — use tuned electrical separation instead of insulated joints, and can carry cab-signalling data to the train.
  • Coded track circuits — transmit pulse codes through the rails for both occupancy detection and signalling information.

Limitations

Track circuits can fail to detect a train when rust, leaf contamination, or sand on the railhead insulates the wheels from the rails — a loss of "shunt" that has contributed to accidents and level-crossing failures. Very light vehicles may also shunt poorly. Track circuits generally do not detect broken rails in all configurations, and they cannot distinguish the number of vehicles or their exact position within the section.
