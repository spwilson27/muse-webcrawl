Pierre-Simon, marquis de Laplace (23 March 1749 – 5 March 1827) was a French scholar and polymath whose work was important to the development of engineering, mathematics, statistics, physics, astronomy, and philosophy. He summarised and extended the work of his predecessors in his five-volume Mécanique céleste (Celestial Mechanics) (1799–1825). This work translated the geometric study of classical mechanics to one based on calculus, opening up a broader range of problems. In statistics, the Bayesian interpretation of probability was developed mainly by Laplace.

He formulated Laplace's equation, pioneered the Laplace transform and the least squares method, and was the first to discuss black holes (as objects from which light cannot escape under Newtonian gravity) and to propose the nebular hypothesis of the origin of the Solar System jointly with Kant's earlier idea. He is remembered as one of the greatest scientists of all time, sometimes referred to as the French Newton.

Work

Celestial mechanics

Laplace showed that the apparent irregularities in the motions of Jupiter and Saturn arise from their mutual perturbations and cancel over long periods, demonstrating the stability of the Solar System within Newtonian theory. His Mécanique céleste systematised gravitational astronomy, including the theory of tides, the figure of the Earth, and planetary perturbations; famously, when Napoleon asked why God did not appear in the work, Laplace is said to have replied, "I had no need of that hypothesis."

Probability and statistics

In his Théorie analytique des probabilités (1812) and the popular Essai philosophique sur les probabilités (1814), Laplace laid foundations of mathematical probability and statistics: the Bayesian interpretation, the method of least squares (also developed by Gauss), generating functions, and the central limit theorem. His image of an intellect — "Laplace's demon" — that knows all positions and forces and can therefore compute the entire past and future epitomises classical determinism.

Physics

With Lavoisier he worked on calorimetry; he corrected Newton's formula for the speed of sound by accounting for adiabatic compression; he contributed to capillarity and potential theory, whence Laplace's equation (∇²φ = 0) bears his name, as do the Laplace operator, the Laplace transform, and Laplace's law.

Life and honours

Born in Beaumont-en-Auge in Normandy and educated at Caen, Laplace was introduced by d'Alembert to a teaching post at the École Militaire in Paris. He survived the Revolution, served briefly as Minister of the Interior under Napoleon (1799), became a senator and count of the Empire, and was made a marquis under the restored Bourbons. He was elected to the Académie des sciences in 1773 and to the Académie française in 1816. He died in Paris in 1827.
