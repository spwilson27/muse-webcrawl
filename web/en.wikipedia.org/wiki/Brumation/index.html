Brumation

Brumation is a term coined by Wilbur "Bill" Walder Mayhew in 1965 for a state of dormancy exhibited by ectothermic animals — particularly reptiles — during cold weather. It is analogous to hibernation in endothermic mammals, but brumating animals, although very lethargic and often immobile for long periods, still wake periodically to drink, may feed opportunistically on warm days, and respond to their surroundings; their metabolic rate falls with body temperature because they cannot generate metabolic heat.

Physiology and behaviour

Before entering brumation, many reptiles stop feeding and empty their digestive tracts, since undigested food could rot in the stomach at low temperatures. During brumation the animal shelters in a burrow, rock crevice or other refuge below the frost line, where temperatures remain stable. Heart and breathing rates slow dramatically. Unlike true hibernation, which is driven by internal hormonal cycles and day length, brumation is largely temperature-driven, and animals may emerge whenever conditions warm temporarily. Aquatic turtles may brumate underwater, absorbing oxygen through the skin and cloaca.

In captivity

Reptile keepers often simulate brumation with a controlled cooling period to stimulate breeding in species such as bearded dragons, corn snakes and leopard geckos, in which a winter rest is followed by the reproductive cycle on rewarming.
