Rapid prototyping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rapid prototyping is a group of techniques used to quickly fabricate a scale model of a physical part or assembly using three-dimensional computer aided design (CAD) data. Construction of the part or assembly is usually done using 3D printing or "additive layer manufacturing" technology.

The first methods for rapid prototyping became available in the middle 1980s and were used to produce models and prototype parts. In 1986, Charles Hull founded 3D Systems and patented stereolithography, a process in which a vat of liquid ultraviolet-curable photopolymer resin is cured layer by layer by an ultraviolet laser. Other processes followed, including fused deposition modelling (FDM), developed by Scott Crump and commercialised by Stratasys, and selective laser sintering (SLS), developed at the University of Texas at Austin.

History

Rapid prototyping technology emerged from work in the 1970s on photo-hardening polymers and topographic modelling. In 1981, Hideo Kodama of the Nagoya Municipal Industrial Research Institute published an account of a functional rapid-prototyping system using photopolymers, and in 1984 French engineers filed a patent for the stereolithography process weeks before Hull. The expiry of key patents around 2009, particularly for FDM, opened the field to low-cost desktop machines and the RepRap open-source project, which aimed to build a self-replicating printer.

Applications

Rapid prototyping is widely used in the automotive, aerospace, medical and consumer-product industries. Engineers use physical prototypes to check form, fit and function, to test ergonomics, and to communicate design intent before committing to expensive tooling. In medicine, patient-specific models derived from CT or MRI scans are used for surgical planning and for fabricating custom implants and prostheses. Architects use the technology for scale models of buildings.

Technologies

  • Stereolithography (SLA) — a laser cures liquid photopolymer resin layer by layer.
  • Fused deposition modelling (FDM) — a heated nozzle extrudes thermoplastic filament.
  • Selective laser sintering (SLS) — a laser fuses powdered material such as nylon.
  • Binder jetting — a liquid binding agent is deposited onto a powder bed.
  • Material jetting — droplets of photopolymer are deposited and cured with UV light.

Rapid tooling and manufacturing

Beyond prototypes, additive processes are used for rapid tooling — the production of moulds, dies and patterns — and increasingly for direct digital manufacturing of end-use parts, particularly where production volumes are low or geometries are complex. The boundary between prototyping and production has blurred as machine speed, material range and part quality have improved.
