Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (1881 – 10 November 1938) was a Turkish field marshal, revolutionary statesman, and the founding father of the Republic of Turkey. He served as its first president from 1923 until his death in 1938.

Military career

An Ottoman army officer, Mustafa Kemal distinguished himself at the Battle of Gallipoli in 1915, where his leadership helped repel the Allied landings. After the Ottoman defeat in the First World War and the Allied occupation of Constantinople, he organised the Turkish national movement in Anatolia.

War of Independence and the Republic

From 1919 to 1922 he led the Turkish War of Independence, defeating Greek forces in the west and securing international recognition of Turkish sovereignty through the Treaty of Lausanne (1923). The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on 29 October 1923, and the Ottoman sultanate and caliphate were abolished.

Reforms

As president, Atatürk launched a sweeping programme of political, social, and cultural reforms — collectively known as Atatürk's Reforms — aimed at building a secular, modern nation-state. These included adopting a Swiss-based civil code, replacing the Arabic script with a Latin-based Turkish alphabet in 1928, extending political rights to women (who gained the vote in 1934), and promoting state-led industrialisation and education. In 1934 the Grand National Assembly granted him the surname Atatürk, meaning "Father of the Turks."

Legacy

Atatürk died at Dolmabahçe Palace in Istanbul in 1938. His mausoleum, Anıtkabir, stands in Ankara. His principles, known as Kemalism, remain embedded in the Turkish constitution, and he is widely regarded as one of the most significant state-builders of the twentieth century.
