Barrow Island is a 202-square-kilometre island lying about 50 kilometres northwest of the Pilbara coast of Western Australia. It is one of Australia's most important conservation reserves — a Class A nature reserve that remains free of introduced predators — while simultaneously hosting a major oil field in production since 1967 and the Gorgon liquefied natural gas project, one of the largest resource developments in Australian history.

Environment

The island is low, arid and covered in spinifex grassland, with limestone caves and surrounding coral reefs and seagrass beds. Because it was never connected to mainland predator populations and is protected by strict quarantine, Barrow Island preserves mammal species that have vanished or declined on the mainland, including the Barrow Island euro, spectacled hare-wallaby, golden bandicoot and a distinctive black-and-white form of the black-flanked rock-wallaby. Flatback turtles nest on its beaches, and its caves shelter endemic invertebrates and the blind cave fish. The waters around the island form part of the Montebello/Barrow Islands Marine Conservation Reserves.

History and industry

Aboriginal occupation of the island area dates back tens of thousands of years, when lower sea levels made it a hill on a coastal plain. The island was named in 1816 by Phillip Parker King for Sir John Barrow of the Admiralty. Oil was discovered in 1964 and production began in 1967; the field has yielded hundreds of millions of barrels. In 2009 construction began on the Gorgon project, operated by Chevron, which pipes gas from the offshore Greater Gorgon fields to LNG and domestic gas plants on the island and includes one of the world's largest carbon dioxide injection schemes, storing CO2 in a deep reservoir beneath the island. Industrial activity operates under an internationally noted quarantine system designed to keep the island free of invasive species.
