The Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, or BZ reaction, is one of a class of reactions that serve as a classical example of non-equilibrium thermodynamics, resulting in the establishment of a nonlinear chemical oscillator. It is the best-known example of an oscillating chemical reaction: a mixture of chemicals that changes colour periodically, and, when unstirred in a shallow dish, forms striking expanding patterns of concentric rings and spirals.

History

The reaction was discovered by the Soviet chemist Boris Pavlovich Belousov in 1951, while he was attempting to model the Krebs cycle in vitro using a solution of bromate, citric acid and cerium ions. He observed that the solution oscillated between colourless and yellow. His attempts to publish the finding were repeatedly rejected, editors insisting that such behaviour violated the second law of thermodynamics; the work appeared only in an obscure collected volume in 1959. In 1961 the graduate student Anatol Zhabotinsky took up the system, replacing citric acid with malonic acid and adding the redox indicator ferroin, and characterised the propagating waves. The reaction was brought to wide attention in the West after a 1968 conference in Prague.

Mechanism

The chemistry is complex and involves some eighteen elementary steps. The mechanism proposed by Richard Field, Endre Kőrös and Richard Noyes in 1972 (the FKN mechanism) reduces the system to three competing processes centred on bromide ions, which act as a control intermediate: when bromide concentration is high, bromate is reduced in a way that consumes bromide; once it falls below a threshold, autocatalytic production of the oxidised catalyst (Ce4+, or ferriin) takes over, and the subsequent reduction of the catalyst by malonic acid regenerates bromide, restarting the cycle. Field and Noyes further simplified this to the two-variable Oregonator model, named after the University of Oregon.

Patterns and significance

In an unstirred layer the reaction produces chemical waves — target patterns and rotating spiral waves — that are mathematically analogous to waves of excitation in heart tissue and to aggregation waves in slime moulds. The BZ reaction became a paradigm system for the study of self-organisation, dissipative structures and excitable media, and it is closely related to the Brusselator and to the work of Ilya Prigogine on non-equilibrium thermodynamics.
