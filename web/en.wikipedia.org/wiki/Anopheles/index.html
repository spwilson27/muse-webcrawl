Anopheles is a genus of mosquitoes first described in 1818 by Johann Wilhelm Meigen. Of roughly 500 recognised species, about 60–100 can transmit the Plasmodium parasites that cause malaria in humans, making Anopheles the only mosquito genus that spreads human malaria. The most important vectors include Anopheles gambiae and Anopheles funestus in Africa and Anopheles stephensi in Asia.

Life cycle

Like all mosquitoes, anophelines pass through egg, larva, pupa and adult stages. Eggs are laid singly on water and bear distinctive floats; the larvae lie parallel to the surface — unlike Culex larvae, which hang at an angle — and the adults rest with their abdomen angled away from the surface. Only females take blood meals, which they need for egg development, and it is the female that transmits malaria when she bites.

Malaria transmission

Transmission was demonstrated in 1897–98 by Ronald Ross and Giovanni Battista Grassi, who showed that Plasmodium completes part of its life cycle in the mosquito gut and salivary glands. Control measures target the vector: insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, larval source management, and more recently the release of genetically modified or Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes.
