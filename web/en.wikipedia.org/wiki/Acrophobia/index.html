Acrophobia

Acrophobia
SpecialtyPsychiatry, psychology
SymptomsIntense fear of heights, vertigo, panic, nausea
CausesTraumatic falls, learned behaviour, vestibular dysfunction
TreatmentExposure therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy

Acrophobia is an extreme or irrational fear of heights, especially when one is not particularly high up. It belongs to a category of specific phobias, called space and motion discomfort, that share both similar aetiology and options for treatment. Most people experience a degree of natural fear when exposed to heights, known as the fear of falling; acrophobia, by contrast, involves an intense, impairing dread that can provoke panic attacks and avoidance behaviour out of proportion to the actual danger.

Signs and symptoms

People with acrophobia can experience a panic attack in high places and become too agitated to get themselves down safely. Symptoms include rapid heartbeat, sweating, trembling, nausea, dizziness and a feeling of vertigo. Sufferers may clutch at supports, crouch, or crawl on all fours to feel stable. Roughly 2 to 5 per cent of the general population is thought to have acrophobia, with twice as many women affected as men.

Causes

Historically, acrophobia was attributed to conditioning after a traumatic fall, but research suggests that a fear of heights is largely innate and that acrophobia may arise from an exaggerated form of this normal fear, possibly combined with dysfunction in the vestibular system, which governs balance. Some psychologists argue it is an evolved adaptation that helps prevent dangerous falls.

Treatment

The main treatment is exposure therapy, in which the patient is gradually and repeatedly exposed to heights in a controlled way until the fear response diminishes; this may be done in real settings or using virtual reality. Cognitive behavioural therapy and, occasionally, short-term medication for anxiety may also be used.
