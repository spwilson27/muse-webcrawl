A lava lake is a large volume of molten lava, usually basaltic, contained in a volcanic vent, crater or broad depression. The term applies both to lakes of molten lava that are actively circulating, fed from below, and to bodies of lava that pool during an eruption and later solidify. Persistent lava lakes are rare: only a handful exist worldwide at any time, and they require a delicate balance between heat supplied by rising magma and gas, and heat lost at the surface.

Formation and behaviour

A stable lava lake forms where a volcano's conduit remains open and magma convects continuously: hot, gas-rich lava rises, cools and degasses at the surface, then sinks, driving a slow circulation. The surface develops a thin, dark crust of solidified lava that continually cracks apart along spreading zones, revealing incandescent molten rock beneath, in a process often compared to plate tectonics in miniature. Gas release, fountaining and changes in lake level reflect the state of the magma system below and are closely monitored as eruption indicators.

Notable lava lakes

Persistent or long-lived examples include the summit lake of Kīlauea in Hawaii (present within Halemaʻumaʻu crater during much of 2008–2018 and again from 2020), the phonolitic lake of Mount Erebus in Antarctica (present since the 1970s), the fast-moving, exceptionally fluid lake of Nyiragongo in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and the lakes of Erta Ale in Ethiopia's Danakil Depression, Marum on Ambrym (Vanuatu), Villarrica in Chile and Masaya in Nicaragua. Nyiragongo's lake is considered especially hazardous: in 1977 and 2002 its crater walls fractured and the lake drained catastrophically, sending lava through the city of Goma.
