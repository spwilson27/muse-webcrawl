Ship money was a tax of medieval origin levied intermittently in England to provide ships for the defence of the realm in time of war or emergency. Its revival and extension by Charles I during his Personal Rule of the 1630s — without the consent of Parliament, and in peacetime — made it one of the most controversial grievances leading to the English Civil War.

Background

Historically, coastal towns and counties could be required to furnish ships, or money in lieu, for naval defence. Elizabeth I and James I had levied such contributions in wartime. In October 1634 Charles I issued writs requiring the maritime counties and ports to provide ships of war; in August 1635 a second writ extended the levy to the whole kingdom, including inland counties, and it was renewed annually thereafter, raising roughly £200,000 a year — a sum that financed the great ship Sovereign of the Seas and made the tax, in effect, a permanent royal revenue.

Opposition and the Hampden case

Because Charles ruled without Parliament from 1629 to 1640, ship money was attacked as taxation without consent, contrary to the Petition of Right. In 1637 the Buckinghamshire landowner John Hampden refused to pay his assessment of twenty shillings and was prosecuted in the Court of Exchequer Chamber. The case of R v Hampden was argued before all twelve common-law judges, who found for the Crown by the narrow margin of seven to five — a legal victory that was widely read as a moral defeat for the king, and that turned Hampden into a popular hero.

Abolition

Collection collapsed amid the crisis of 1639–40. The Long Parliament declared ship money illegal, and the Ship Money Act 1640 formally abolished it and voided the judgment against Hampden. The principle established — that the Crown could not raise general taxation by prerogative alone — remained a foundation of English constitutional law, restated in the Bill of Rights 1689.
