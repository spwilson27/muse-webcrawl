Slow match

Slow match, also called match cord, is the slow-burning cord or twine used by early gunpowder weapons and for igniting artillery, explosives and fireworks fuses. It is typically a length of hemp, flax or cotton cord that has been treated with a solution of saltpetre (potassium nitrate) and dried, causing it to burn slowly and steadily without flame — roughly 30 cm (about one foot) per hour, depending on composition and weather.

Use in firearms

The slow match was the ignition source of the matchlock, the first mechanism for firing a gun: a smouldering length of match was clamped in the jaws of the serpentine, and pulling the trigger lowered it into the priming pan. Matchlock soldiers carried lengths of match burning at both ends so that a spare lit end was always available. Handling loose gunpowder near a constantly glowing match was hazardous, and the match gave off smoke and a glow that could betray a position at night, drawbacks that helped drive the adoption of the wheellock and flintlock.

Other uses

Artillerymen used slow match held in a linstock to fire cannon from a safe distance. Miners and sappers used match cord to light safety fuses and charges, and slow match remained in use for fireworks and ceremonial purposes long after it disappeared from small arms.
