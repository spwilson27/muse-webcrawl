Amyloid beta (Aβ or Abeta) denotes a family of peptides of 36–43 amino acids that are the main component of the amyloid plaques found in the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease. The peptides derive from the amyloid precursor protein (APP), which is cleaved by beta secretase (BACE1) and gamma secretase. The amyloid beta molecule was first sequenced in 1984 by George Glenner and Caine Wong from the meningeal amyloid of Alzheimer's patients.

Formation and forms

APP can be processed by two competing pathways. In the amyloidogenic pathway, β-secretase cuts APP first, then γ-secretase cuts within the membrane, releasing Aβ of varying length. Aβ40 is the most abundant species; the longer Aβ42 is more hydrophobic, aggregates more readily into oligomers, fibrils and plaques, and is considered the more pathogenic. Mutations in APP and in the presenilin components of γ-secretase that increase the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio cause rare early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease.

Role in disease

Under the influential amyloid cascade hypothesis, accumulation and aggregation of Aβ in the brain initiates a cascade — tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, neuroinflammation and neuronal death — leading to dementia. The hypothesis was long contested after many anti-amyloid drugs failed in trials, but monoclonal antibodies that clear amyloid (aducanumab, 2021; lecanemab, 2023; donanemab, 2024) modestly slowed decline in early Alzheimer's and received regulatory approvals, lending the pathway partial validation. Soluble Aβ oligomers, rather than mature plaques, are now often considered the most neurotoxic species.

Normal function

Aβ is produced throughout life in healthy brains and is cleared during sleep; proposed physiological roles include antimicrobial defence, regulation of synaptic activity and sealing leaks in the blood–brain barrier, though its normal function remains unsettled.
