Bow echo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bow echo is a term in meteorology describing the characteristic bow-shaped radar return of a mesoscale convective system that is often associated with long swaths of damaging straight-line winds, and occasionally tornadoes. The term was coined by meteorologist Ted Fujita in the 1970s.

Formation

A bow echo begins as a line of thunderstorms (a squall line) whose central portion surges forward, often where a strong rear-inflow jet descends behind the line and pushes it outward, giving the echo its archer's-bow shape. Counter-rotating vortices — "bookend vortices" — form at the northern and southern ends of the bow. Wind damage is most severe near the apex of the bow. Bow echoes typically range from 20 to 200 km in length and last three to six hours.

Hazards

Because the storms move fast and produce intense downbursts, bow echoes are prolific producers of severe wind damage, sometimes exceeding that of weak tornadoes. When a bow echo or squall line covers a very long distance and produces a widespread swath of damaging winds over many hours, the event is classified as a derecho. Brief tornadoes can also spin up along the line, particularly in and north of the bow's apex.
