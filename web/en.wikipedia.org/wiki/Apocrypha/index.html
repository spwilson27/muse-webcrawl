Apocrypha

"Apocrypha" (Greek: "hidden things") denotes writings whose canonicity is disputed between Christian traditions.

Apocrypha (from Ancient Greek ἀπόκρυφος, apókruphos, "hidden") are biblical or related writings not forming part of the accepted canon of Scripture, although some might be described as officially accepted, additional or recognised scripture by individual churches. The word's origin lies in the claim of some groups that the true meaning of their writings was hidden from all but the initiates.

Biblical apocrypha

In the usage established by Jerome and continued in Protestant tradition, the biblical apocrypha are the books included in the Greek Septuagint and Latin Vulgate but absent from the Hebrew Bible. These include Tobit, Judith, Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, together with additions to Esther and Daniel. The Roman Catholic Church, at the Council of Trent (1546), affirmed most of these as canonical, terming them deuterocanonical; Eastern Orthodox churches accept a slightly wider set. Protestant churches generally regard them as edifying but not authoritative for doctrine — Martin Luther placed them in an appendix "useful and good to read" — while the Jewish canon (Tanakh) excludes them.

New Testament apocrypha

The term also covers early Christian writings not accepted into the New Testament canon, such as the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Peter, the Infancy Gospel of James (Protoevangelium), and various apocryphal acts and apocalypses. Many survive only in fragments or in Coptic translations among the Nag Hammadi library discovered in 1945. These texts are studied for what they reveal about the diversity of early Christianity, though churches have historically treated many as heretical or spurious.

Other uses

By extension, "apocryphal" describes stories of doubtful authenticity attached to famous figures — sayings of Churchill or Einstein, for example — and the word is used for the hidden or rejected writings of other religions and for secular texts of disputed authorship.
