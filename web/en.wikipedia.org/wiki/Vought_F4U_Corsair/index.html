Vought F4U Corsair

The Vought F4U Corsair is an American fighter aircraft that saw service primarily in World War II and the Korean War. Designed around the powerful Pratt & Whitney R-2800 Double Wasp engine, its distinctive inverted gull wing allowed a large-diameter propeller while keeping the landing gear short.

Service history

The prototype first flew in May 1940. Early carrier trials exposed landing difficulties, so Corsairs first equipped land-based US Marine Corps squadrons in the Solomon Islands, where they earned a formidable reputation against Japanese fighters, with an overall claimed kill ratio of about 11:1. Cleared for carrier use in 1944, it also served with the Royal Navy's Fleet Air Arm. More than 12,500 were built by Vought and under license by Goodyear (FG) and Brewster (F3A).

Later use

The Corsair flew ground-attack missions in Korea and in French service over Indochina, Algeria, and Suez; the last production aircraft was delivered in 1953, one of the longest production runs of any piston-engined fighter. Honduras and El Salvador flew Corsairs in the 1969 Football War, the last combat between piston-engined fighters.
