Julius Robert Oppenheimer (22 April 1904 – 18 February 1967) was an American theoretical physicist who directed the Los Alamos Laboratory during World War II and is often called the 'father of the atomic bomb' for his role in the Manhattan Project. A professor at Berkeley and Caltech, he built the leading American school of theoretical physics in the 1930s — with landmark work on quantum tunnelling, the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, and (in 1939, with Hartland Snyder) the first model of gravitational collapse into what are now called black holes.

At Los Alamos (1943–45) Oppenheimer welded thousands of scientists into the team that tested the first atomic bomb on 16 July 1945 (Trinity), recalling the Bhagavad Gita: 'Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds.' After the war he chaired the Atomic Energy Commission's advisory committee, opposed the hydrogen bomb crash programme, and in 1954 — in the McCarthy-era security hearing orchestrated by Lewis Strauss — lost his clearance, a humiliation the US government formally vacated in 2022. He directed the Institute for Advanced Study from 1947 to 1966; the 2023 film Oppenheimer returned his story to global culture.

Physics before the bomb

Oppenheimer's pre-war papers include the theory of electron–positron showers, the Oppenheimer–Phillips process, and stellar collapse — the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit (1939) on neutron star masses parallels Chandrasekhar's white-dwarf result. His students (Serber, Bethe's collaborators, Weisskopf among them) seeded American physics; the war redirected that school into weapons work, with consequences he spent the rest of his life examining.
