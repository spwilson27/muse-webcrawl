Prompt neutron

In nuclear engineering, a prompt neutron is a neutron immediately emitted by a nuclear fission event, as opposed to a delayed neutron, which is emitted later by one of the fission products after beta decay. Prompt neutrons are emitted essentially instantaneously — within about 10−14 seconds of the fission event — and make up more than 99% of all neutrons produced by fission.

Characteristics

Fission of uranium-235 yields on average about 2.4 neutrons per fission, with an average energy of roughly 2 MeV, following a characteristic fission neutron spectrum. Because prompt neutrons appear so quickly, a chain reaction sustained on prompt neutrons alone would grow on the timescale of the prompt neutron lifetime (about 10−4 seconds in a thermal reactor), far too fast for mechanical control systems.

Prompt criticality

A system in which the chain reaction is self-sustaining on prompt neutrons alone is called prompt critical. The amount of reactivity needed to reach this state above ordinary criticality is measured in "dollars", one dollar being the effective delayed neutron fraction, β. Although delayed neutrons are only a small fraction of the total (β ≈ 0.0065 for uranium-235), their delayed emission stretches the reactor's response time and makes practical control of nuclear reactors possible. Exceeding prompt criticality played a central role in accidents such as Chernobyl and in the operation of nuclear weapons.
