Thomas Robert Malthus FRS (13 February 1766 – 29 December 1834) was an English economist, cleric and scholar influential in the fields of political economy and demography. In his 1798 book An Essay on the Principle of Population, Malthus observed that an increase in a nation's food production improved the well-being of the population, but the improvement was temporary because it led to population growth, which in turn restored the original per capita production level. In other words, he argued, mankind has a propensity to utilise abundance for population growth rather than for maintaining a high standard of living — a view that has become known as the "Malthusian trap".

The Essay on Population

Malthus argued that population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio (1, 2, 4, 8, 16…), while subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio (1, 2, 3, 4, 5…). Consequently, population must eventually outstrip the means of subsistence, and is kept in check by "positive checks" that raise the death rate — famine, disease and war — and by "preventive checks" that lower the birth rate, including moral restraint and delayed marriage. The Essay was first published anonymously in 1798 as a response to the optimistic writings of William Godwin and the Marquis de Condorcet; the greatly expanded second edition of 1803 incorporated evidence from Malthus's travels and observations of societies around the world.

Career

Malthus was educated at Jesus College, Cambridge, where he excelled in mathematics and became a fellow in 1793. He was ordained an Anglican curate, serving at Okewood in Surrey. In 1805 he became professor of history and political economy at the East India Company's college at Haileybury in Hertfordshire — the first academic post in political economy in Britain — a position he held for the rest of his life. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1818 and was a founding member of the Statistical Society of London in 1834.

Influence

Malthus's ideas influenced generations of economists, among them David Ricardo, with whom he corresponded and debated, and John Stuart Mill. His population principle also had a celebrated effect on biology: both Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace credited reading Malthus with helping them formulate the theory of evolution by natural selection. The terms "Malthusian" and "neo-Malthusian" remain common in debates about population, resources and the environment, though critics have long pointed to technological change, falling fertility and rising productivity as answers to his predictions.
