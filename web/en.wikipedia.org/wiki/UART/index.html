UART

A universal asynchronous receiver-transmitter (UART) is a computer hardware device for asynchronous serial communication in which the data format and transmission speeds are configurable. It sends data bits one by one, from the least significant to the most significant, framed by start and stop bits so that precise timing is handled by the communication channel. The UART converts parallel data from a processor into a serial bit stream, and converts received serial data back into parallel form.

Because the link is asynchronous, transmitter and receiver do not share a clock signal; instead each side is configured in advance with the same bit rate (baud rate), and the receiver synchronizes to the transitions of each incoming frame.

Framing

A typical UART frame consists of an idle (logic high) line, a start bit (low), five to nine data bits, an optional parity bit, and one or more stop bits (high). Common configurations include "8N1" — eight data bits, no parity, one stop bit — used at rates such as 9600 and 115200 baud. A break condition, in which the line is held low for longer than a full frame, can be used for signalling.

History

UART functionality was historically provided by discrete integrated circuits such as the Western Digital WD1402A of the early 1970s and the widely used Intel 8251 and National Semiconductor 8250. The 8250 and its successors, notably the 16550 with its 16-byte FIFO buffers, defined the serial ports of the IBM PC and its compatibles. The related USART also supports synchronous communication.

Applications

Although largely replaced by USB for connecting peripherals, UARTs remain ubiquitous in embedded systems: they provide debug consoles on routers and single-board computers, connect GPS receivers, Bluetooth modules and modems to microcontrollers, and appear in industrial buses related to RS-232, RS-422 and RS-485 standards. Most modern microcontrollers integrate one or more UART peripherals on-chip.
