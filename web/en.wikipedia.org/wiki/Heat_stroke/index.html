Heat stroke

Heat stroke
SpecialtyEmergency medicine, critical care
SymptomsCore temperature above 40.5 °C, confusion, seizures, loss of consciousness; hot skin
TypesClassic (non-exertional); exertional
TreatmentRapid cooling — cold-water immersion is most effective; supportive care
PrognosisMortality up to 10–65% depending on type, age and speed of cooling

Heat stroke is the most severe form of heat illness, a life-threatening condition in which the body's temperature regulation fails and core temperature rises above about 40.5 °C (104.9 °F), accompanied by dysfunction of the central nervous system such as confusion, seizures or coma. It requires emergency treatment: rapid cooling within the first half hour greatly improves survival.

Types and causes

Classic (non-exertional) heat stroke develops over days during heat waves, mainly in the elderly, infants and people with chronic illness or impaired sweating. Exertional heat stroke strikes healthy, active people — athletes, soldiers and labourers — when metabolic heat production overwhelms heat loss during exercise in hot conditions. Risk factors include dehydration, heavy clothing or protective equipment, alcohol, and certain medications.

Signs and complications

Hallmarks are very high core temperature and altered mental state. Skin is typically hot; sweating may be absent in classic heat stroke but present in exertional cases. Without treatment, heat stroke damages the brain, muscles (rhabdomyolysis), kidneys, liver and blood-clotting system, and can be fatal.

Treatment and prevention

The priority is immediate whole-body cooling — immersion in cold or ice water is fastest and preferred for exertional cases — before or during transport to hospital, followed by organ support. Prevention relies on acclimatisation, hydration, rest breaks, light clothing, work scheduling, and public-health measures during heat waves, which are becoming more frequent and intense with climate change.
