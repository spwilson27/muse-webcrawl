A fumarole (from Italian fumarola, ultimately from Latin fumus, "smoke") is a vent in the Earth's crust, usually in volcanic areas, that emits steam and volcanic gases such as carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen chloride and hydrogen sulfide. The steam forms when superheated water vapour expands and partly condenses as pressure drops on reaching the surface.

Characteristics

Fumaroles may occur along cracks and fissures, within craters, in lava fields, or in calderas and geothermal areas. Their temperatures range from just above the boiling point of water to several hundred degrees Celsius. Fumaroles rich in sulfur compounds are called solfataras (after the Solfatara crater near Naples), and carbon-dioxide-rich vents are called mofettes. Changes in fumarole temperature and gas chemistry are monitored as indicators of volcanic activity; new or intensifying fumarolic activity can precede eruptions. Famous fumarole fields include those of Yellowstone National Park, the Valley of Ten Thousand Smokes in Alaska, and Vulcano in the Aeolian Islands.

Fumarolic deposits can form encrustations of sulfur and other minerals, and the extreme environments around fumaroles support specialised heat-loving microorganisms studied by microbiologists and astrobiologists.
