Male reproductive system

The male reproductive system consists of the organs involved in sexual reproduction in males. Its primary functions are the production of spermatozoa (sperm), the synthesis of male sex hormones — chiefly testosterone — and the delivery of sperm into the female reproductive tract during intercourse.

External organs

The external organs are the penis, which contains the urethra and erectile tissue (the corpora cavernosa and corpus spongiosum) and serves both urination and copulation, and the scrotum, the skin sac that holds the testes outside the body cavity at a temperature about 2–3 °C below core body temperature, as required for normal sperm production.

Internal organs

The testes (testicles) are paired gonads in which sperm are produced in the seminiferous tubules through spermatogenesis, and testosterone is secreted by Leydig cells. Immature sperm mature and are stored in the epididymis, then travel through the vas deferens to the ejaculatory ducts. Accessory glands contribute the fluid of semen: the seminal vesicles add a fructose-rich secretion, the prostate gland adds a milky alkaline fluid, and the bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands secrete a lubricating mucus. Sperm constitute only a small fraction of the ejaculate volume.

Development and control

The system develops under the influence of the SRY gene on the Y chromosome, which directs testis formation in the embryo. At puberty, gonadotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus stimulates pituitary release of LH and FSH, driving spermatogenesis and testosterone production, and producing secondary sexual characteristics such as deepening of the voice and growth of facial hair.

Clinical significance

Common disorders include benign prostatic hyperplasia, prostate cancer, erectile dysfunction, infertility, and testicular cancer, as well as infections of the tract. See also: Testicle, Prostate, Spermatogenesis, and the female reproductive system.
