Phags-pa script

The Phags-pa script is an abugida script created in the thirteenth century by the Tibetan lama Drogön Chögyal Phagpa for Kublai Khan, ruler of the Mongol Empire and founder of the Yuan dynasty of China. It was decreed in 1269 as the official "new Mongolian script", intended to write all the languages of the empire — Mongolian, Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit and others — in a single system.

Design

Based on the Tibetan script, Phags-pa was written vertically from top to bottom, in the Mongolian manner, with characters joined in syllabic blocks. Its square forms were meant to echo the angular appearance of Chinese characters in vertical columns. It was used on imperial edicts, seals, coins, paper money and monumental inscriptions, of which the most famous is the bilingual inscription at Juyong Pass (1345).

Decline and legacy

The script never displaced the scripts already in use among the empire's peoples and was used chiefly in official contexts. After the fall of the Yuan in 1368 it fell out of general use in China, though it survived for centuries in Tibetan religious contexts and as a decorative script. Phags-pa is historically significant in linguistics because its transcriptions preserve features of thirteenth-century Chinese phonology, and it is thought to have influenced the structure of the Korean Hangul alphabet. The script is encoded in the Unicode block Phags-pa (U+A840–U+A877).
