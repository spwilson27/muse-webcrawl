A Grignard reagent or Grignard compound is a chemical compound with the general formula R–Mg–X, where X is a halogen and R is an organic group, normally an alkyl or aryl group. Two typical examples are methylmagnesium chloride (Cl–Mg–CH3) and phenylmagnesium bromide (Ph–Mg–Br). They are a subclass of the organomagnesium compounds.

Grignard compounds are popular reagents in organic synthesis for creating new carbon–carbon bonds. For example, when reacted with another halogenated compound R′–X′ in the presence of a suitable catalyst, they typically yield R–R′ and the magnesium halide MgXX′ as a byproduct; and they react with carbonyl compounds and various other electrophiles. The French chemist Victor Grignard discovered them in 1900 and shared the 1912 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the work.

Synthesis

Grignard reagents are prepared by treating an organic halide with magnesium metal, usually in an ether solvent such as diethyl ether or tetrahydrofuran (THF). The reaction proceeds at the metal surface and is believed to involve single-electron transfer steps. Formation requires rigorously dry, oxygen-free conditions, because the reagents are strong bases and nucleophiles that react readily with water, alcohols, and even carbon dioxide.

Reactions

As strong nucleophiles, Grignard reagents attack electrophilic carbon atoms. Their most important reactions are additions to carbonyl groups: with aldehydes they give secondary alcohols, with ketones tertiary alcohols, with esters tertiary alcohols (after two equivalents add), and with carbon dioxide carboxylic acids. They also react with epoxides, nitriles, and acid chlorides, and undergo metal-catalysed cross-coupling reactions such as the Kumada coupling.

Structure

In solution, Grignard reagents exist in a complex equilibrium known as the Schlenk equilibrium, interconverting R–Mg–X, R2Mg, and MgX2 species, further complicated by ether coordination and aggregation. The exact structure depends on the organic group, halide, solvent, and concentration.

Industrial applications

Grignard chemistry is used commercially in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals, silicones, and boron compounds, among many other products. The reagents are also employed to prepare many other organometallic compounds by transmetallation.
