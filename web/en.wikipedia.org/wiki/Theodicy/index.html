Theodicy

Theodicy
Term coined byGottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1710)
FieldPhilosophy of religion, theology
AddressesThe problem of evil

A theodicy is an attempt to reconcile the existence of evil and suffering in the world with the belief in an omnipotent, omnibenevolent, and omniscient God. The word derives from the Greek theos (god) and dikē (justice)—thus, a "justification of God"—and was coined by the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in his 1710 work Essais de Théodicée, which argued that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds God could have created.

Major approaches

Classic theodicies include the Augustinian tradition, which traces evil to the free choices of fallen creatures and treats evil as a privation of good, and the Irenaean or "soul-making" theodicy—revived by John Hick—which views suffering as necessary for moral and spiritual growth. The free-will defence, developed in modern form by Alvin Plantinga, argues that even an omnipotent God cannot create a world containing significantly free creatures who never do wrong. Other responses include process theology, which denies classical omnipotence, and sceptical theism, which holds that humans are in no position to judge God's reasons for permitting evil.

Criticism

Philosophers from Hume onward have questioned whether any theodicy succeeds; the evidential problem of evil argues that the scale and distribution of suffering make theism improbable even if not impossible. Events such as the 1755 Lisbon earthquake—which Voltaire attacked Leibnizian optimism over in Candide—and the Holocaust have been taken by many writers as decisive challenges. Some theologians, and "anti-theodicy" writers, argue that the attempt to justify suffering is itself morally objectionable.
