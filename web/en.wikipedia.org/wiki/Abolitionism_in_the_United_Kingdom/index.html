Abolitionism in the United Kingdom

Key milestonesSomerset v Stewart (1772); Slave Trade Act 1807; Slavery Abolition Act 1833
Key figuresGranville Sharp, Thomas Clarkson, William Wilberforce, Olaudah Equiano, Hannah More, Elizabeth Heyrick
OppositionWest India Interest (planter and merchant lobby)
OutcomeSlave trade abolished 1807; slavery abolished across most of the British Empire 1834–1838

Abolitionism in the United Kingdom was the movement, spanning the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, to end first the British transatlantic slave trade and then slavery itself throughout the British Empire. Britain had become the world's leading slave-trading nation in the eighteenth century, carrying an estimated three million Africans across the Atlantic, which made the movement's eventual success a defining moment in British and world history.

Origins

Legal opinion began shifting with Somerset v Stewart (1772), in which Lord Mansfield ruled that slavery had no basis in English common law, freeing James Somerset. Quakers, who had petitioned Parliament against the trade since 1783, joined with Anglicans and others in 1787 to form the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade, led in practice by Granville Sharp, Thomas Clarkson and the young MP William Wilberforce. Clarkson toured the slave ports gathering evidence; the committee's campaigns pioneered modern humanitarian lobbying — mass petitions, consumer boycotts of slave-grown sugar, and the famous Wedgwood medallion depicting a kneeling enslaved man asking "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?" Formerly enslaved writers such as Olaudah Equiano, whose Interesting Narrative appeared in 1789, gave the movement authentic African voices.

Abolition of the trade

Wilberforce's abolition bills repeatedly failed through the 1790s, as war with revolutionary France made reform suspect. A Foreign Slave Trade Act passed in 1806, and the Slave Trade Act 1807 finally abolished the trade throughout the empire; the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron thereafter policed the Atlantic against slavers of all nations.

Emancipation

The Anti-Slavery Society (founded 1823) initially pursued gradual abolition, but campaigners such as Elizabeth Heyrick demanded immediatism, and enslaved people's own resistance — above all the Baptist War in Jamaica (1831–32), led by Samuel Sharpe — destroyed the credibility of gradualism. The reformed Parliament passed the Slavery Abolition Act 1833, which freed enslaved children under six, converted other enslaved people into "apprentices" (a system ended early in 1838), and paid £20 million in compensation — to slave owners, not the enslaved — a loan whose repayment by British taxpayers concluded only in 2015. Abolitionists went on to campaign against slavery worldwide through the British and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society (1839), ancestor of today's Anti-Slavery International.

Historiography

Eric Williams's Capitalism and Slavery (1944) argued abolition reflected the declining economic value of slavery to British capitalism; later historians, notably Seymour Drescher, countered that the trade and slavery remained profitable and that mass public mobilisation — "econocide" against economic interest — drove abolition. The debate continues, alongside growing attention to reparations and to the legacies of slavery in modern Britain.
