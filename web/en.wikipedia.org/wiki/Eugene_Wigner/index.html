Wigner in later life.

Eugene Paul Wigner (born Jenő Pál Wigner; 17 November 1902 – 1 January 1995) was a Hungarian-American theoretical physicist who received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1963 'for his contributions to the theory of the atomic nucleus and the elementary particles, particularly through the discovery and application of fundamental symmetry principles'.

Wigner essentially created the group-theoretic foundations of quantum mechanics — showing how symmetries determine spectra, selection rules and the classification of particles (Wigner's theorem on symmetries as unitary/antiunitary operators; the Wigner–Eckart theorem; the Inönü–Wigner contraction). One of the Budapest 'Martians' (with von Neumann, Szilárd and Teller), he co-designed the plutonium production reactors at Hanford for the Manhattan Project, discovered the reactor-poisoning 'Wigner effect' on graphite (still a concern in decommissioning), and late in life wrote the famous philosophical essay 'The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences' (1960) and the Wigner's-friend thought experiment, still central to debates on measurement.

Symmetry as method

Before Wigner, physicists solved each system by brute force; he taught the field to ask first what symmetries a system has and let representation theory do the work — the template for everything from crystal physics to the Standard Model's particle classification (Wigner's own 1939 classification of Poincaré-group representations defines what a particle is in relativistic quantum theory).
