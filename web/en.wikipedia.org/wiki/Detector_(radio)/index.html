Detector (radio)

A detector is a device or circuit in a radio receiver that recovers the information-bearing signal (audio, video, or data) from a modulated radio-frequency carrier wave — a process called demodulation. The earliest detectors were crude devices for wireless telegraphy, such as the coherer; the breakthrough for voice reception was the crystal detector, followed by vacuum-tube and semiconductor diode detectors, and later sophisticated circuits such as the product detector and phase-locked loop.

Types

  • Coherer — a tube of metal filings whose resistance drops when RF is applied; used in the earliest (1890s–1900s) receivers for Morse code.
  • Electrolytic and magnetic detectors — intermediate technologies developed by Fessenden and Marconi.
  • Crystal detector — a point contact ("cat's whisker") on a galena or silicon crystal, rectifying the signal; the heart of the crystal radio.
  • Diode envelope detector — the standard AM demodulator: a diode plus RC filter that follows the envelope of the amplitude-modulated carrier.
  • Product detector — mixes the signal with a local oscillator to demodulate SSB and CW.
  • Discriminator / ratio detector / PLL — circuits that convert frequency variations into amplitude, used for FM demodulation; the Foster–Seeley discriminator is the classic example.

Envelope detection

For amplitude modulation, the simplest detector works because the information is encoded in the carrier's envelope. A diode rectifies the signal and a capacitor holds the peak value, reproducing the envelope (the audio waveform) while filtering out the carrier. Distortion occurs if the modulation index exceeds 1 (overmodulation) or if the RC time constant is poorly chosen.
