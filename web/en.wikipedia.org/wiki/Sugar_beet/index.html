The sugar beet is a cultivated variety of Beta vulgaris grown commercially for sugar production. Its large, white, conical taproot contains a high concentration of sucrose — typically 16 to 20 percent by weight — and it accounts for roughly 20 percent of the world's sugar production, the remainder coming mainly from sugarcane.

History

The sugar beet was developed through selective breeding in the eighteenth century from fodder beets, following the German chemist Andreas Marggraf's 1747 demonstration that beet roots contain sugar identical to cane sugar. His student Franz Karl Achard built the first beet sugar factory in Silesia in 1801. Production expanded rapidly during the Napoleonic Wars, when the British blockade cut off cane sugar supplies to continental Europe, and Napoleon promoted beet cultivation by decree.

Cultivation and processing

Sugar beet is a temperate-climate crop grown in rotation, sown in spring and harvested in autumn. At the factory, roots are sliced into cossettes, and sugar is extracted by diffusion in hot water, then purified, concentrated, and crystallised. By-products include molasses and beet pulp, used as animal feed, and the crop is also used for biofuel and biochemical production. Modern cultivars are often monogerm (single-seeded) and may include herbicide-tolerant genetically modified varieties, widely grown in the United States and Canada.
