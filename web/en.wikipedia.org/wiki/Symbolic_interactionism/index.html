Symbolic interactionism
FieldSociology, social psychology
Key figuresGeorge Herbert Mead, Herbert Blumer, Charles Cooley, Erving Goffman
Term coinedHerbert Blumer, 1937
Core textMind, Self, and Society (1934)

Symbolic interactionism is a sociological theory and research tradition that examines how people create and maintain society through face-to-face, repeated interactions, interpreting one another's actions through shared symbols — above all, language. It emerged from the work of the Chicago School, drawing on American pragmatism and the social psychology of George Herbert Mead.

Blumer's premises

Herbert Blumer, who coined the term in 1937, summarised the perspective in three premises: (1) human beings act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them; (2) such meanings arise out of social interaction with others; and (3) meanings are handled and modified through an interpretive process used by each person in dealing with the things encountered. Society is thus not a fixed structure but an ongoing process of negotiation and interpretation.

Key concepts

Mead distinguished the "I" (the spontaneous self) from the "me" (the socialised self formed by taking the attitudes of others). Charles Cooley's "looking-glass self" described self-concept as arising from imagining how we appear to others. Erving Goffman's dramaturgical analysis treated everyday life as theatrical performance, with front-stage and back-stage behaviour and impression management. Labelling theory in the study of deviance, and the Thomas theorem — "if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences" — are closely allied ideas.

Methods and criticism

Symbolic interactionists favour qualitative methods such as participant observation and ethnography, arguing that social reality can only be understood from within interaction. Critics charge that the tradition neglects macro-level structures, power, and institutions, and that its concepts can be vague and difficult to test; defenders reply that structure itself is produced and reproduced in interaction.
