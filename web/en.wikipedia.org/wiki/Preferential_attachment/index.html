A preferential attachment process is any of a class of processes in which some quantity, typically some form of wealth or credit, is distributed among a number of individuals or objects according to how much they already have, so that those who are already wealthy receive more than those who are not. In network science, preferential attachment means that new nodes are more likely to connect to existing nodes that already have many connections. The idea is captured by the phrase "the rich get richer", and it is closely related to the Matthew effect.

Preferential attachment was proposed as the growth mechanism behind scale-free networks by Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert in 1999. In the Barabási–Albert model, a network grows by the addition of new nodes, each of which attaches to existing nodes with probability proportional to their degree:

Π(ki) = ki / Σj kj

The resulting degree distribution follows a power law, P(k) ~ k−3 in the basic model, matching the heavy-tailed distributions observed in the World Wide Web, citation networks, and many social and biological networks.

History

Mathematically equivalent processes had been studied earlier: George Udny Yule's 1925 model of speciation, Herbert Simon's 1955 model of word frequencies and city sizes, and Derek de Solla Price's 1976 model of citation networks ("cumulative advantage") all contain the same reinforcing mechanism. The Barabási–Albert formulation brought the idea to prominence in the context of evolving networks.

Measuring preferential attachment

In empirical studies of growing networks, the attachment kernel Π(k) is estimated by tracking how the rate at which nodes acquire new links depends on their current degree k. Sublinear kernels (Π(k) ~ kα with α < 1) produce stretched-exponential degree distributions, linear attachment produces power laws, and superlinear attachment leads to condensation, in which a single node can capture a finite fraction of all links.

Applications

  • Growth of the World Wide Web and hyperlink networks
  • Bibliometric and citation networks
  • Wealth distribution models (related to the Pareto distribution)
  • Protein interaction and other biological networks
