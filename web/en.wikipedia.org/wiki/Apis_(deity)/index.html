In ancient Egyptian religion, Apis was a sacred bull deity worshipped at Memphis, regarded as the living manifestation and herald of the creator god Ptah and later associated with Osiris. Unlike most Egyptian gods depicted in animal form, Apis was embodied in a single living bull, chosen by specific markings, which was housed in a temple, consulted as an oracle, and on its death mourned, mummified and entombed with enormous ceremony.

Selection and life

Priests identified a new Apis calf by signs described by classical authors such as Herodotus and Pliny: a black hide with a white triangular mark on the forehead, an eagle-like mark on the back, doubled tail hairs, and a scarab-shaped mark under the tongue. The bull lived in luxury at the temple of Ptah in Memphis, appeared in processions, and its movements and behaviour were read as prophecies.

Death and burial

On the bull's death — Greek sources claimed a maximum lifespan of 25 years, after which it was ritually drowned — Egypt entered mourning while priests searched for the successor. The deceased bull was mummified over weeks and buried in the Serapeum of Saqqara, a vast underground gallery of huge granite sarcophagi excavated by Auguste Mariette in 1850. In death the Apis became Osiris-Apis, from which the Hellenistic god Serapis derived.

History

The Apis cult is among Egypt's oldest, attested from the Early Dynastic Period, and flourished for some three thousand years into the Roman era. Herodotus relates that the Persian king Cambyses II mocked and stabbed the Apis, an act Egyptians saw as sacrilege. Alexander the Great, by contrast, sacrificed to the Apis at Memphis.
