Knödel number

In number theory, a Knödel number for a given positive integer b is a composite number m with the property that every number i < m coprime to m satisfies im−b ≡ 1 (mod m). The concept is named after the Austrian mathematician Walter Knödel (1926–2018). The set of all Knödel numbers for a fixed b is denoted Kb.

Definition and examples

Knödel numbers generalise the Carmichael numbers: a composite number m is a Carmichael number if and only if it is a Knödel number for b = 1, since the defining condition then reads im−1 ≡ 1 (mod m) for all i coprime to m — Korselt-style, the strongest form of a Fermat pseudoprime. For b = 2 the Knödel numbers begin 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 20, 22, 24, 26, … (all even composite numbers, in fact, qualify for suitable bases), while for other bases the sequences are sparser and include both even and odd composites.

Every prime number is trivially a Knödel number for every b by Fermat's little theorem, so the definition restricts attention to composite m. It is known that for each b there are infinitely many composite Knödel numbers in Kb.

Properties

If m ∈ Kb and d divides m with certain compatibility conditions, related parameters yield further Knödel numbers, and the structure of Kb has been studied in computational number theory, where Knödel numbers provide test cases for primality proving and for the failure of simple Fermat-type primality tests with fixed exponents.
