Cardiac muscle (also called heart muscle or myocardium) is one of three types of vertebrate muscle tissues, the others being skeletal muscle and smooth muscle. It is an involuntary, striated muscle that constitutes the main tissue of the wall of the heart. The cardiac muscle forms a thick middle layer between the outer layer of the heart wall (the pericardium) and the inner layer (the endocardium), with blood supplied via the coronary circulation.

Cardiac muscle cells, called cardiomyocytes, contract in a coordinated fashion that pumps blood throughout the body. Their specialised structure — branching cells joined end-to-end by intercalated discs — makes the heart behave as a functional syncytium, in which an electrical impulse spreads rapidly from cell to cell so that whole chambers contract as a unit. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle contracts rhythmically on its own (myogenic activity), requires a long refractory period that prevents tetanic contraction, and has an exceptionally rich supply of mitochondria and myoglobin to sustain aerobic work without fatigue for a lifetime.

Structure

Cardiomyocytes are short (around 100 μm), branching cells, typically with a single central nucleus (some have two). Their cytoplasm contains myofibrils of actin and myosin arranged in sarcomeres, giving the striated appearance they share with skeletal muscle. Cells are joined at their ends by intercalated discs, which contain three kinds of junction: desmosomes and fascia adherens, which anchor the cells mechanically against the stresses of contraction, and gap junctions, which allow ions — and thus the action potential — to pass directly between cells.

Cardiac muscle has an extensive transverse (T) tubule system that brings the membrane close to the sarcoplasmic reticulum at regular intervals (at the Z-lines), coordinating calcium release and contraction. Mitochondria occupy roughly a third of the cell volume, reflecting the heart's continuous aerobic metabolism. A small population of specialised pacemaker and conducting cells — in the sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node, bundle of His and Purkinje fibres — initiates and distributes the heartbeat instead of contracting forcefully.

Physiology

Each heartbeat begins with spontaneous depolarisation in the sinoatrial node. The impulse spreads across the atria, is delayed briefly at the atrioventricular node, then travels rapidly through the ventricular conducting system so the ventricles contract from the apex upward. The cardiac action potential has a prolonged plateau phase (phase 2), maintained by calcium influx through L-type channels, lasting 200–300 ms; the resulting long absolute refractory period prevents summation and tetanus, which would be fatal in a pump that must relax to refill.

Excitation–contraction coupling depends on "calcium-induced calcium release": entering Ca²⁺ triggers a much larger Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through ryanodine receptors, and Ca²⁺ binding to troponin C initiates cross-bridge cycling. Relaxation follows as Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum and out of the cell. Contractility is increased by sympathetic stimulation (via β1-adrenergic receptors), and the force of contraction rises with stretch — the Frank–Starling law of the heart.

Differences from skeletal muscle

  • Cells are branched, shorter, and joined by intercalated discs; skeletal fibres are long, unbranched and multinucleated.
  • Contraction is involuntary and intrinsically rhythmic; the autonomic nervous system modulates but does not initiate it.
  • Source of activating Ca²⁺ is both extracellular and from the sarcoplasmic reticulum; skeletal muscle relies on internal stores alone.
  • Long refractory period prevents tetanus.
  • Very high mitochondrial density and obligatory aerobic metabolism; negligible capacity for regeneration — dead cardiomyocytes are replaced by scar tissue, as after a myocardial infarction.

Clinical significance

Diseases of cardiac muscle include the cardiomyopathies (hypertrophic, dilated, arrhythmogenic and restrictive), myocarditis (inflammation, often viral), and ischaemic damage in myocardial infarction ("heart attack"), where interruption of coronary blood flow kills muscle that the heart largely cannot replace. Abnormal impulse formation or conduction in the muscle produces arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation. Heart failure is, in essence, the failure of cardiac muscle to pump adequately, whether from systolic weakness or diastolic stiffness.
