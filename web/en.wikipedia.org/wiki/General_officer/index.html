A general officer is an officer of high rank in the armies, and in some nations' air and space forces, or marines. In some usages the term also applies to a ranking above colonel. The collective term for general officers is the general staff ranks; officers of these grades typically command formations of brigade size and above and hold the most senior staff and command appointments in a nation's armed forces.

Etymology and usage

The term general derives from the French capitaine général and ultimately from Latin generalis, "of the whole" — the officer in charge of the army as a whole, as opposed to officers commanding particular units. It entered English usage in the 16th century. In many countries general officers are divided into grades informally counted in "stars": a one-star brigadier general (or brigadier), two-star major general, three-star lieutenant general, and four-star general. A five-star rank, such as field marshal or general of the army, is usually reserved for wartime or ceremonial use.

Corresponding ranks

In navies, the equivalent senior officers are flag officers — commodores, rear admirals, vice admirals and admirals — so named because they are entitled to fly a personal flag. Air forces typically follow the army system or use marshal titles (as in the Royal Air Force: air commodore, air vice-marshal, air marshal, air chief marshal). The United States Space Force and United States Marine Corps also employ general officer grades.

History

The modern hierarchy of general ranks developed in the 17th and 18th centuries as standing armies grew. Serjeant-major general (later major general) was originally the chief of staff rank, which explains why a major general ranks below a lieutenant general despite "major" sounding senior to "lieutenant"; the lieutenant general was the deputy of the (captain) general.
