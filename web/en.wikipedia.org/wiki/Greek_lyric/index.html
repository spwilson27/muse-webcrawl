Greek lyric is the body of lyric poetry written in Ancient Greek dialects, mostly between the 7th and 5th centuries BC. "Lyric" originally meant verse composed for singing to the accompaniment of the lyre, and Greek scholars later distinguished it from epic and drama. Alexandrian scholars selected the Nine Lyric Poets as the canonical masters of the genre: Alcman, Sappho, Alcaeus, Anacreon, Stesichorus, Ibycus, Simonides, Pindar and Bacchylides.

Forms

Greek lyric is conventionally divided by performance mode and metre. Monody — solo song to the lyre — is represented by Sappho and Alcaeus of Lesbos, singing of love, wine, politics and war for aristocratic drinking groups (symposia). Choral lyric, sung and danced by a chorus, includes Alcman's maiden-songs, Stesichorus's mythic narratives, and the victory odes (epinicia) of Pindar and Bacchylides, commissioned to celebrate athletic triumphs. Related metres sung to the flute rather than the lyre include elegy (Tyrtaeus, Solon, Theognis, Mimnermus) and iambus (Archilochus, Hipponax) — poetry of war, politics, philosophy, love and invective.

Survival and influence

Most Greek lyric survives only in fragments quoted by later authors or recovered from papyri in Egypt; Pindar's victory odes are the great exception, preserved complete in manuscripts. The genre — brief, musical, personal — founded the Western lyric tradition and shaped Roman poets such as Catullus and Horace, and through them the lyric poetry of modern Europe.
