Community policing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Community policing (or community-oriented policing) is a philosophy and organisational strategy that promotes partnerships between police agencies and the communities they serve, emphasising proactive problem-solving of the conditions that contribute to crime and disorder rather than reactive response to incidents alone. Officers are typically assigned to permanent neighbourhood beats, where they build relationships with residents, businesses, and local organisations.

Principles

The United States Department of Justice's Office of Community Oriented Policing Services (COPS), created by the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, describes three core components: community partnerships, organisational transformation, and problem solving — the latter often using the SARA model (Scanning, Analysis, Response, Assessment). Roots of the philosophy are traced to Robert Peel's principles of policing by consent and to critiques of the mid-20th-century "professional" model, in which officers in patrol cars became isolated from the public.

Practice and evidence

Practices include foot patrol, neighbourhood substations, citizen academies, and regular beat meetings (such as Chicago's CAPS programme, launched in 1993). Research evaluations generally find that community policing improves citizens' perceptions of police legitimacy and satisfaction, with more modest and variable effects on measured crime; problem-oriented approaches tend to show the strongest crime-reduction results. Critics argue the term is often applied loosely to any public-relations activity and that it can blur into intelligence gathering or broken-windows enforcement.
