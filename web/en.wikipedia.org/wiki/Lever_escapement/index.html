Lever escapement

The lever escapement, invented by the English clockmaker Thomas Mudge in around 1754, is a type of escapement used in most mechanical watches and many clocks. It is the most successful escapement ever devised for portable timepieces and, in its refined "Swiss lever" form, remains the standard in mechanical watchmaking more than two and a half centuries after its invention.

How it works

An escapement is the mechanism that converts the steady unwinding of a mainspring into counted impulses, releasing the gear train one tooth at a time and giving the timing element — a balance wheel — small pushes to keep it oscillating. In the lever escapement, a T-shaped lever (the pallet fork) sits between the escape wheel and the balance. Its two jewelled pallets alternately lock and unlock the escape wheel's specially shaped club teeth. As the balance swings, a roller jewel on its staff enters the fork's horns, knocking the lever from one banking to the other; each swing releases exactly one tooth and receives an impulse in return.

Advantages

Unlike earlier escapements, the lever is detached: during most of the balance's swing it is entirely free of the mechanism, so friction and disturbances are minimized and the watch keeps good time in any position. It is also self-starting, robust against shock compared with the delicate chronometer detent escapement, and well suited to mass production — qualities that let it dominate first pocket watches and then wristwatches.

Variants

The English lever used pointed teeth and a ratchet wheel; the Swiss form adopted club teeth and pallets with carefully angled impulse faces, improving efficiency. The pin-pallet (Roskopf) escapement is a cheaper relative used in inexpensive movements. In 1999 Omega introduced George Daniels's coaxial escapement, a refinement that reduces sliding friction by splitting impulse between two wheels.
