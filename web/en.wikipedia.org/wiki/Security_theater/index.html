Security theater

Security theater is the practice of implementing security measures that are considered to provide the feeling of improved security while doing little or nothing to achieve it. The term was coined by security specialist Bruce Schneier for his book Beyond Fear (2003), and has gained currency in security circles, particularly for describing airport security measures after the September 11 attacks.

Concept

Schneier argues that security measures should be evaluated by whether they actually reduce risk, not by whether they reassure the public or demonstrate official action. Security theater measures tend to be highly visible, focused on specific past attack scenarios ("fighting the last war"), and resistant to cost-benefit analysis. Examples frequently cited include shoe removal at airport checkpoints, restrictions on liquids in cabin baggage, and photo identification checks that are not compared against any watch-list database.

Arguments for and against

Critics contend that theatrical measures waste resources that could be spent on effective security, create false confidence, and impose costs in money, time, and civil liberties. Defenders respond that visible measures may deter attackers, that deterrence is difficult to measure, and that public confidence in systems such as air travel has genuine economic and social value. Schneier distinguishes "security theater" from measures that provide both real and perceived security, and advocates for security that is effective even when its existence is known to adversaries.

Related concepts

Related ideas include "hygiene theater," applied to visible but ineffective disinfection practices during the COVID-19 pandemic, and cargo cult programming in software engineering, where rituals are copied without understanding their purpose.
