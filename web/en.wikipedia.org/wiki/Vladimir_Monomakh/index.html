Vladimir Monomakh

Reign: 1113–1125 (Grand Prince of Kiev)
Born: 26 May 1053
Died: 19 May 1125
Dynasty: Rurik
Father: Vsevolod I of Kiev
Mother: Maria, relative of Constantine IX Monomachos

Vladimir II Monomakh (1053–1125) was Grand Prince of Kiev from 1113 to 1125. One of the most celebrated rulers of Kievan Rus', he is remembered as a successful commander against the Cumans, a unifier of the Rus' principalities, and the author of the Instruction (Pouchenie), a landmark of early East Slavic literature. His byname derives from his mother, a relative of the Byzantine emperor Constantine IX Monomachos.

Early life and career

Vladimir was the son of Vsevolod I of Kiev and received the principality of Smolensk, then Chernigov, and from 1078 ruled Pereyaslavl, the exposed frontier principality facing the steppe. With his cousin Oleg and later in coalition with other princes — notably after the congress of Lyubech in 1097 and the Dolobsk congress of 1103 — he organised a series of deep offensive campaigns against the Cumans (Polovtsy) in 1103, 1107 and 1111 that broke their power for a generation and became the stuff of epic tradition.

Grand Prince of Kiev

After the death of Sviatopolk II in 1113, a popular uprising in Kiev against moneylenders and officials led the city's boyars to invite Vladimir to take the throne, passing over the customary order of succession. He accepted, restoring order while issuing a statute (the Ustav of Vladimir Monomakh) that limited interest rates and eased the position of debtors, later incorporated into the Russkaya Pravda law code. Through dynastic marriages and the placement of his sons in Novgorod, Suzdal and elsewhere, he restored much of the unity of Rus'.

Writings and legacy

Monomakh's Instruction, addressed to his children, combines autobiography, moral exhortation and an account of his campaigns and hunts; it survives in the Laurentian Chronicle. His reign is often seen as the last flourishing of a united Kievan Rus' before the fragmentation of the mid-12th century. The famous gold-embroidered "Cap of Monomakh" used in later Muscovite coronations was named for him, though it is of later, Central Asian workmanship. His son Mstislav the Great succeeded him.
