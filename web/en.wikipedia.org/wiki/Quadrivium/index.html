The quadrivium (plural: quadrivia) is the four subjects, or arts, taught in medieval universities after the trivium. The word is Latin for "four ways", and its subjects are arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy. Together with the trivium (grammar, logic, and rhetoric), the quadrivium comprised the seven liberal arts that formed the basis of education in classical antiquity and the Middle Ages.

Origins

The four mathematical arts were associated in the Platonic tradition with the study of number in its forms: arithmetic studies number in itself, music studies number in time (ratios and harmony), geometry studies magnitude at rest, and astronomy studies magnitude in motion. The scheme was codified in the early sixth century by Boethius, whose textbooks De arithmetica and De institutione musica remained standard for centuries, and it was transmitted through the encyclopaedic works of Martianus Capella, Cassiodorus, and Isidore of Seville.

In medieval education

In the medieval university, the quadrivium was taught within the faculty of arts, which every student completed before proceeding to the higher faculties of theology, law, or medicine. Arithmetic was studied through Boethius and later through Arabic-derived texts; geometry through Euclid's Elements; astronomy through Ptolemy and Sacrobosco's De sphaera; and music as the mathematical theory of harmony rather than performance. The quadrivium embodied the Pythagorean conviction that the cosmos is ordered according to number, and it provided the mathematical foundation for natural philosophy.

Modern usage

The paired trivium and quadrivium survive in modern classical-education movements, and the underlying scheme continues to shape the structure of liberal arts curricula. Some universities still award the Master of Arts on completion of a course descended from the seven liberal arts.
