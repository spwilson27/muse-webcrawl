Verdict

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, a verdict is the formal decision or finding made by a jury (or, in bench trials, by the judge as fact-finder) on the matters of fact submitted to it. In criminal cases the verdict is typically "guilty" or "not guilty" (Scots law famously retains a third verdict, "not proven" — an acquittal with a moral sting, abolition of which has been debated and legislated for in recent Scottish reforms); in civil cases the verdict finds for the plaintiff or defendant and may fix damages.

Rules and forms

Criminal verdicts require unanimity in most systems (England and Wales permit majority verdicts of 10–2 or 11–1 after deliberation; US federal and, since Ramos v. Louisiana (2020), state criminal juries must be unanimous). Special verdicts answer specific factual questions; general verdicts decide liability overall. A jury that cannot agree is "hung", leading to mistrial and possible retrial. Devices for managing verdicts include directed verdicts/judgments notwithstanding the verdict (where no reasonable jury could so find), jury nullification (acquittals against the evidence, extra-legal but historically significant), and post-verdict appeals on sufficiency or misconduct. Sentencing, in most systems, is a separate judicial act after a guilty verdict.
