Late string quartets (Beethoven)

The late string quartets are the final five string quartets — and the Große Fuge — composed by Ludwig van Beethoven in the last years of his life, between 1825 and 1826. They are widely regarded as among the supreme achievements of Western music, notable for their intellectual depth, emotional range, formal innovation, and radical departures from the conventions of the Classical string quartet. The works mystified many of Beethoven's contemporaries, but have since been recognised as visionary compositions that pointed far beyond their time.

Beethoven's late quartets are staples of the chamber music repertoire.

The quartets

  • String Quartet No. 12 in E-flat major, Op. 127 (1825)
  • String Quartet No. 13 in B-flat major, Op. 130 (1825) — including the famous Cavatina movement; the Große Fuge was originally its finale
  • String Quartet No. 14 in C-sharp minor, Op. 131 (1826) — seven movements played without pause; reportedly Beethoven's own favourite of the set
  • String Quartet No. 15 in A minor, Op. 132 (1825) — containing the "Heiliger Dankgesang" ("Holy Song of Thanksgiving"), written after Beethoven recovered from serious illness
  • String Quartet No. 16 in F major, Op. 135 (1826) — the last complete work Beethoven finished; its finale is headed "Muss es sein? Es muss sein!" ("Must it be? It must be!")
  • Große Fuge in B-flat major, Op. 133 — the monumental original finale of Op. 130, later published separately

Composition

The quartets were written in response to a commission from the Russian nobleman Prince Nikolai Galitzin, who had also commissioned the earlier Op. 127, Op. 132 and Op. 130 works. By this time Beethoven was almost completely deaf and in declining health; he died in March 1827, a few months after completing Op. 135. The premieres were given chiefly by the Schuppanzigh Quartet, led by Ignaz Schuppanzigh, a long-time associate of the composer.

Reception and legacy

Contemporary audiences and critics were often baffled — the Große Fuge in particular was described as incomprehensible, and Beethoven was persuaded (for a fee) to write a substitute finale for Op. 130. Over the following century the late quartets came to be seen as the summit of Beethoven's art: Igor Stravinsky called the Große Fuge "an absolutely contemporary piece of music that will be contemporary forever." T. S. Eliot's poem Four Quartets was partly inspired by repeated listening to Op. 132. The works remain central to the repertoire of every major string quartet and continue to shape how composers and listeners understand the genre.
