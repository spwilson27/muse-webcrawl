Etruscan art

Etruscan art was produced by the Etruscan civilization of ancient Italy between roughly the 9th and 1st centuries BCE. Drawing on Greek and Near Eastern influences, Etruscan artists developed distinctive traditions in sculpture, wall painting, metalwork, and terracotta that later fed into Roman art.

Sculpture

Etruscan sculptors excelled in terracotta and bronze. Celebrated works include the Apollo of Veii, a life-size painted terracotta statue that stood on a temple roof, the bronze Chimera of Arezzo, and the Sarcophagus of the Spouses, whose reclining couple conveys the lively naturalism typical of Etruscan funerary art. Large statues in stone were comparatively rare.

Painting

Most surviving Etruscan painting comes from the painted tombs of Tarquinia, where frescoes depict banquets, dancers, musicians, athletes, and scenes of the afterlife. Successive styles show strong Greek influence alongside local subjects, and provide key evidence for Etruscan daily life and dress.

Metalwork and jewellery

Etruscan goldsmiths were famed for granulation, a technique of decorating surfaces with tiny gold grains, while bronze workers produced mirrors engraved with mythological scenes, candelabra, and vessels exported across the Mediterranean.
