Unemployment

[Image: a queue of jobseekers outside an employment office]

Unemployment is the condition of people who are able and available to work and are actively seeking it but cannot find a job. The unemployment rate — the unemployed as a share of the labour force (employed plus unemployed), measured by labour-force surveys and, in the US, published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics — is a headline indicator of economic health and slack.

Types and theory

Economists distinguish frictional unemployment (normal job search and turnover), structural unemployment (mismatch of skills or location, technological displacement), cyclical unemployment (from recessions, linked to output by Okun's law) and seasonal unemployment. Even healthy economies retain a 'natural rate' (NAIRU) below which Inflation tends to accelerate. Long spells of joblessness scar workers — mass unemployment in the Great Depression exceeded 20% in the US — and policy responses span fiscal and monetary stabilisation, training and active labour-market programmes, unemployment insurance, and contested instruments such as the Minimum wage. Youth and long-term unemployment rates, and broader underemployment measures, qualify the headline figure.
