Innovation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Innovation
Definition (OECD Oslo Manual)A new or improved product or process that differs significantly from previous products or processes and is made available to users or brought into use
Key theoristsJoseph Schumpeter, Everett Rogers, Clayton Christensen

Innovation is the practical implementation of ideas that result in new or improved goods, services, processes or methods of organization. It is distinguished from invention — the creation of an idea or prototype — by its introduction into use or the market. Economist Joseph Schumpeter placed innovation at the centre of economic development, describing capitalism's "creative destruction," in which new combinations displace old industries.

Types and theory

Innovations are classed as incremental or radical, and as product, process, marketing or organizational. Clayton Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation describes how simpler, cheaper entrants upend established firms. Everett Rogers's Diffusion of Innovations (1962) modelled how new ideas spread through populations, from innovators and early adopters to the late majority. The OECD's Oslo Manual standardizes how innovation is measured in business surveys. Organizations foster innovation through research and development, design thinking, open innovation (drawing on external ideas), and protection of intellectual property, for example through patents such as the design patent.

Policy

Governments promote innovation through research funding, tax credits, universities, and competition policy, viewing it as the main long-run driver of productivity and living standards. Critics note that not all innovation is welfare-enhancing and that the term is sometimes used as a managerial buzzword.
