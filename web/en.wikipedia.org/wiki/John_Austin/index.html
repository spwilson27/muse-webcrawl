John Austin (3 March 1790 – 1 December 1859) was an English legal theorist who influenced Anglo-American jurisprudence through his analytical approach to law and his articulation of legal positivism. His best-known work, The Province of Jurisprudence Determined (1832), set out the "command theory" of law: law is the command of a sovereign, backed by the threat of a sanction, addressed to subjects who are in a habit of obedience.

Life

Austin was born in Suffolk, the son of a miller. He served briefly in the army during the Napoleonic Wars before studying law and being called to the bar in 1818. Discouraged by modest success in practice, he turned to academic study, travelling to Germany to study Roman law and the work of German jurists. In 1826 he became the first professor of jurisprudence at the newly founded University of London (later University College London), where his lectures attracted students including John Stuart Mill. Poor attendance later led him to resign the chair in 1834. He subsequently served on government commissions, including the Criminal Law Commission and a commission of inquiry into Malta.

The command theory

Austin argued that the proper subject of jurisprudence is positive law — law as it is, set by political superiors — and that it should be studied separately from morality, or law as it ought to be. Every positive law, he held, is set by a sovereign person or body to a member or members of the independent political society wherein that sovereign is supreme. Commands are expressions of desire backed by the power and purpose to inflict an evil or pain in case the desire is disregarded; the liability to that evil is the sanction, and the party commanded is under a duty to obey.

Influence and criticism

Austin's work laid the foundations of analytical jurisprudence in the English-speaking world and influenced later positivists such as H. L. A. Hart, who nevertheless criticised the command theory for failing to account for power-conferring rules, the internal point of view, and the continuity of legal systems after a sovereign's death. His Lectures on Jurisprudence were published posthumously in 1863, edited by his wife Sarah Austin.
