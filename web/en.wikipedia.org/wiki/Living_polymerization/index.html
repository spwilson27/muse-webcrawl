Living polymerization

In polymer chemistry, living polymerization is a form of chain-growth polymerization in which the ability of the growing polymer chain to terminate has been removed. Termination and chain-transfer reactions are absent (or negligible), so once initiated, chains grow at a roughly uniform rate until the monomer is consumed, and the chain ends remain active ("living") so that further monomer can be added to extend the chains or to form block copolymers.

Characteristics

Living polymerizations are recognised by several hallmarks: the number-average molecular weight increases linearly with monomer conversion; the molar-mass distribution is narrow (low dispersity); polymerisation continues when more monomer is supplied; and chain ends can be deliberately functionalised or used to initiate a second monomer, giving block copolymers of controlled architecture.

History

The concept was demonstrated in 1956 by the chemist Michael Szwarc, who showed that the anionic polymerisation of styrene initiated by sodium naphthalenide in tetrahydrofuran proceeded without termination: the polystyryl anions persisted and resumed growth when fresh monomer was added. Szwarc coined the term "living polymers" for these persistent chain ends.

Types

Classical living polymerisations are mostly ionic, including living anionic and living cationic polymerisation. Later developments extended living character to radical systems under the name reversible-deactivation radical polymerisation (controlled/"living" radical polymerisation), including atom transfer radical polymerisation (ATRP), reversible addition–fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT), and nitroxide-mediated polymerisation. Ring-opening metathesis polymerisation and group-transfer polymerisation can also display living behaviour.

Applications

Because they offer precise control over molecular weight, composition, and architecture, living polymerisations are used to make block copolymers such as styrene-butadiene-styrene thermoplastic elastomers, model polymers for research, and advanced materials for electronics, coatings, and drug delivery.
