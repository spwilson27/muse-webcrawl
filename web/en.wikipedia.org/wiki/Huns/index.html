Huns

The Huns were a nomadic people who lived in Central Asia, the Caucasus, and Eastern Europe between the 4th and 6th centuries AD. They first appeared west of the Volga River around 370 AD and, through conquest, built an empire stretching from the steppes of Central Asia into modern Germany. Under their most famous ruler, Attila, the Hunnic Empire became a major military threat to both the Eastern and Western Roman Empires, extracting large tributes of gold and invading the Balkans and Gaul. After Attila's death in 453, the empire rapidly disintegrated.

Origins

The origins of the Huns are debated. Since the 18th century, scholars have proposed a connection with the Xiongnu, a nomadic empire of the Mongolian steppe known from Chinese sources, largely on the basis of the similarity of names recorded in Chinese, Sogdian, and Greek texts. The hypothesis remains contested, and modern scholarship emphasises that the Huns were a shifting confederation of groups rather than a single ethnic people. Their language, Hunnic, is attested only in a handful of words and personal names and cannot be securely classified, though many of their leaders bore names of Gothic or other origins, reflecting the multi-ethnic character of their empire.

History

Around 370 AD the Huns crossed the Volga and subjugated the Alans, then attacked the Greuthungi Goths of Ermanaric, driving large numbers of Goths to seek refuge across the Danube in the Roman Empire — a displacement that helped trigger the Gothic War of 376–382 and the Roman disaster at Adrianople in 378. By the early 5th century the Hunnic centre of power had moved to the Hungarian Plain. Rulers such as Uldin and Rua expanded Hunnic power, and the Eastern Roman Empire paid an annual tribute in gold.

Attila

In 434 Attila and his brother Bleda succeeded their uncle Rua. After Bleda's death in 445 (later tradition accused Attila of murder), Attila ruled alone. He invaded the Balkans repeatedly, imposing the Treaty of Margus and later settlements that raised the annual tribute, and in 451 invaded Gaul, where a coalition under the Roman general Flavius Aetius and the Visigothic king Theodoric I fought him at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains, traditionally dated 20 June 451. In 452 he invaded Italy, sacking Aquileia and other cities, before withdrawing, possibly influenced by famine, disease, and negotiations with Pope Leo I. Attila died in 453, reportedly from a nosebleed on his wedding night. His sons fought over the succession, and at the Battle of Nedao in 454 subject peoples under the Gepid Ardaric defeated the Huns; the empire collapsed soon after.

Society and warfare

The Huns were famed as mounted archers, using powerful composite bows from horseback, feigned retreats, and rapid manoeuvre. Priscus, a Roman diplomat who visited Attila's court in 449, left the most detailed surviving description of Hunnic society: a wooden-palisaded royal settlement, a hierarchy of nobles (logades), and subject peoples including Goths, Alans, and others who provided infantry and administration. Some Huns practised cranial deformation, binding infants' heads to produce an elongated skull shape.

Legacy

The Huns left a lasting mark on European memory: Attila appears as Etzel in the German Nibelungenlied and as Atli in Norse legend. In Hungary, names such as Attila remain common and the Huns are woven into national origin traditions. The word "Hun" acquired a hostile connotation in the First World War, when it was applied to German soldiers, a usage popularised after Kaiser Wilhelm II's 1900 "Hun speech" (Hunnenrede) urging troops departing for China to fight without mercy.
