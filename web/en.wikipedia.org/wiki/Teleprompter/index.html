Teleprompter

A teleprompter, also known as an autocue in Britain, is a display device that prompts a speaker with an electronic visual text of a speech or script. It allows a presenter to read text while appearing to speak spontaneously and maintaining eye contact with the camera or audience.

Design

A studio teleprompter consists of a monitor lying flat, facing upward, in front of the camera lens, and a pane of beam-splitter glass mounted at an angle above it. The glass reflects the scrolling text toward the speaker while remaining transparent to the camera, which shoots through it. An operator (or the presenter, using a foot pedal or hand control) adjusts the scroll speed to the speaker's pace. Presidential or podium prompters instead use two slim glass panes on stands flanking a lectern, angled so the audience sees through them while the speaker reads the reflection.

History

The device was developed in the United States around 1950 by Fred Barton Jr., Hubert Schlafly, and Irving Kahn; Schlafly demonstrated an early mechanical version using a scrolling paper roll. The TelePrompTer Corporation marketed the system to television studios and, from 1952 onward, to political conventions. Digital prompters driven by computer software replaced paper-scroll and dedicated hardware systems from the 1980s, and tablet- and phone-based prompters are now common for video production.

Use

Teleprompters are standard in television news, political speeches, corporate addresses, and awards ceremonies. Skilled users rehearse with the text so that eye movement across lines stays subtle; wide screens and short line lengths help conceal reading. Critics occasionally attack politicians for reliance on prompters, though prepared-text delivery has been normal in public speaking since long before the device existed.
