Perceval, the Story of the Grail (French: Perceval ou le Conte du Graal) is the unfinished fifth verse romance by Chrétien de Troyes, written by him in Old French in the late 12th century. Later authors added 54,000 more lines to the original 9,000 in what are known collectively as the Four Continuations. The story concerns the adventures of the young Welsh knight Perceval, whose naïve upbringing by his widowed mother in isolation from the world of chivalry leads to both comic and tragic misunderstandings, and the more courtly exploits of King Arthur's knight Gawain.

Background

Perceval was dedicated to Chrétien's patron Philip, Count of Flanders, who may have suggested the subject matter. It was left unfinished, probably because of the deaths of either the patron (1191) or the author. The poem was written in Old French, and survives in fifteen manuscripts and three fragments. It is the earliest surviving account of a quest for the Holy Grail, though the object in Chrétien's poem — a wide, deep dish called a graal — is not yet identified with the cup of the Last Supper, and the mysterious procession Perceval witnesses at the castle of the Fisher King also includes a bleeding lance.

Plot summary

The poem opens in the Welsh forest where Perceval, raised in ignorance of knighthood by his mother, encounters five knights and resolves to travel to King Arthur's court. Mistaking instruction literally, he commits blunders, including causing his mother's death from grief and mishandling an encounter with a maiden in a tent. After training under the wise Gornemant of Gohort, he rescues and falls in love with Blancheflor. Visiting the Grail Castle, he sees the procession of the bleeding lance and the grail but, following advice not to talk too much, fails to ask whom the grail serves — a question that would have healed the wounded Fisher King and restored his land. Learning of his failure and of his mother's death, Perceval wanders for years seeking the castle again; the narrative then turns to Gawain's adventures before breaking off unfinished.

Legacy

The unfinished poem generated an enormous sequel literature: the Four Continuations, the anonymous Perlesvaus, Robert de Boron's prose reworkings, Wolfram von Eschenbach's Parzival, and the Welsh Peredur. Through these, Perceval's story shaped the entire later Grail tradition, influencing works as distant as Wagner's opera Parsifal and T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land.
