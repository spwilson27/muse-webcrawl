The epipelagic zone is the uppermost layer of the open ocean, extending from the surface to a depth of about 200 metres (656 feet). Also called the sunlight zone, it receives enough light for photosynthesis, and consequently supports the phytoplankton that underpin almost all oceanic food webs — as well as the vast majority of marine life by volume of habitat used.

Conditions and life

Temperature in the epipelagic decreases with depth and varies with latitude and season; the well-mixed, nutrient-poor waters of the open ocean contrast with productive coastal upwelling regions. Inhabitants include zooplankton, jellyfish, sardines, tuna, many sharks, dolphins, and sea turtles, many of which undertake nightly vertical migrations to feed near the surface. Only the uppermost portion — the euphotic zone — has light sufficient for photosynthesis to exceed respiration, so the effective base of primary production often ends well above 200 m in turbid water and below it in the clearest tropical seas.

Significance

Though it is a thin skin over the deep ocean, the epipelagic zone produces roughly half of Earth's oxygen via marine photosynthesis, supports nearly all commercial fisheries, and exchanges heat and carbon dioxide with the atmosphere, making it central to climate regulation.
