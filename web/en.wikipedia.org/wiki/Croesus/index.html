Croesus (Ancient Greek: Κροῖσος, Kroisos; c. 595 – c. 546 BC) was the king of Lydia who reigned from about 560 BC until his defeat by the Persian king Cyrus the Great in 546 BC. Proverbially wealthy, he gave his name to the expression "rich as Croesus", and his kingdom is credited with issuing some of the earliest true coins.

Wealth and coinage

Lydia's wealth derived from the gold-bearing sands of the river Pactolus and from Croesus's conquests of the Greek cities of Ionia. Herodotus describes his lavish gifts to the oracle of Delphi, including gold statues and vast offerings. Under Croesus, Lydia produced bimetallic coinage of pure gold and pure silver — among the earliest coins struck from refined metal — replacing the earlier electrum issues.

Solon and the question of happiness

In Herodotus's famous account, the Athenian sage Solon visited Croesus, who proudly displayed his treasures and asked who was the happiest of men. Solon named obscure Athenians who had died well, warning: "Count no man happy until he is dead." Only later, facing ruin, did Croesus grasp the meaning of Solon's words.

War with Persia and fall

Encouraged by the Delphic oracle's ambiguous prophecy that if he crossed the river Halys he would destroy a great empire, Croesus invaded Cappadocia. After the indecisive Battle of Pteria (547 BC), he withdrew to Sardis, which Cyrus besieged and captured after the Battle of Thymbra (546 BC). Croesus was taken prisoner; Herodotus relates that Cyrus placed him on a pyre but, moved by his invocation of Solon's wisdom, spared his life, after which Croesus became an adviser to the Persian king.

Legacy

Croesus's fall became a classic moral tale of fortune's reversal in Greek literature, retold by Herodotus, Xenophon, and Bacchylides, and the story of his wealth and wisdom made him a stock figure for proverbial riches into modern times.
