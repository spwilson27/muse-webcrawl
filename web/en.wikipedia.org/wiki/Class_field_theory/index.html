Class field theory

Class field theory is the branch of algebraic number theory concerned with describing the Galois extensions of local and global fields using objects associated to the ground field, such as its ideal class group or its group of units. The central result is the reciprocity law and the associated existence theorem, which together establish a bijection between finite abelian extensions of a number field and certain subgroups of its idele class group.

The theory had its origins in the work of Gauss on quadratic reciprocity, and was developed through the 19th and early 20th centuries by mathematicians including Dirichlet, Weber, Hilbert, Takagi, Artin, and Chevalley. Teiji Takagi proved the existence theorem around 1920, and Emil Artin proved the reciprocity law in 1927, completing the main edifice of what is now called global class field theory.

Main statements

For a number field K, the Artin reciprocity map gives an isomorphism between the Galois group of the maximal abelian extension of K and the profinite completion of the idele class group of K. In the classical formulation using ideals, abelian extensions of K correspond to subgroups of the ideal class group of generalized ray class groups. The Hilbert class field — the maximal unramified abelian extension of K — has Galois group isomorphic to the ideal class group of K, a cornerstone result of the theory.

Local class field theory

The local theory describes abelian extensions of a local field, such as a finite extension of the p-adic numbers. In this case, the reciprocity map relates the multiplicative group of the field to the Galois group of its maximal abelian extension, and the valuation corresponds to the action of Frobenius elements, linking ramification to the unit groups of the field.

Applications and generalizations

Class field theory provides explicit descriptions of abelian extensions: for the rational numbers, the Kronecker–Weber theorem states that every abelian extension is contained in a cyclotomic field. For imaginary quadratic fields, the theory of complex multiplication, rooted in Kronecker's Jugendtraum, gives a similar explicit construction using elliptic curves. The search for a non-abelian generalization of class field theory is one of the motivations of the Langlands program.
