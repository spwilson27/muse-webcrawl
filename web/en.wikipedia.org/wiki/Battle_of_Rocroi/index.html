The Battle of Rocroi was a major battle of the Thirty Years' War, fought on 19 May 1643 between a French army commanded by the 21-year-old Louis II de Bourbon, Duke of Enghien (later the Great Condé), and a Spanish army under Francisco de Melo besieging the fortress of Rocroi in the Ardennes. The French victory destroyed the Spanish field army of the Army of Flanders and is traditionally regarded as the end of the era of Spanish military dominance and of the feared tercio infantry formation.

Battle

The Spanish army of about 27,000 men had invested Rocroi when Enghien advanced to relieve it with roughly 23,000. On the morning of 19 May the French cavalry on both wings, after hard fighting, drove off their Spanish counterparts. While the Spanish centre's veteran tercios stood firm and repelled repeated attacks, Enghien's victorious cavalry wheeled behind them, destroyed the supporting units, and then, with infantry and artillery, surrounded and crushed the isolated Spanish infantry. Spanish losses were enormous — often estimated around 7,000–8,000 killed and 7,000 captured, including much of the irreplaceable veteran infantry.

Significance

Rocroi occurred days after the death of Louis XIII and the accession of the child Louis XIV, and it secured the new reign against invasion. Historians have often treated it as the symbolic transfer of military supremacy in Europe from Habsburg Spain to Bourbon France; modern scholarship qualifies this, noting that Spanish arms remained formidable, but the destruction of the Army of Flanders' elite infantry was a genuine turning point in the Franco-Spanish struggle, which continued until the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659.
