Homophony

In music, homophony is a texture in which two or more voices move together in the same rhythm, forming chords, with one voice — usually the highest — carrying a distinct melody and the others providing accompaniment. It is contrasted with monophony (a single melodic line), polyphony (several independent melodic lines), and heterophony (simultaneous variants of one line). The term derives from the Greek homophōnos, "sounding together" or "in unison".

Characteristics and history

In homophonic writing, harmony rather than counterpoint is the primary structural force: the accompanying parts are subordinate to the melody and articulate the chord progression. Hymns and chorales in four parts are classic examples, as are the chordal passages that punctuate Baroque recitative and the block-chord textures of Classical-era themes. Homophony became the dominant texture of Western art music from about 1600, when the early Baroque reaction against Renaissance polyphony produced the monodic style — a solo melody over basso continuo — and opera; thereafter most Classical and Romantic music alternates homophonic themes with polyphonic development. Melody-and-accompaniment patterns such as the Alberti bass are homophony with the chordal parts rhythmically broken.

Other uses

In linguistics, "homophony" refers to the relation between homophones — words pronounced alike but differing in meaning or spelling, such as "to", "too" and "two" — though in music theory the textural meaning above is standard. Rhythmic unison in chant, sometimes called homophony in older writings, is more precisely classified as monophony when there is only a single line.
