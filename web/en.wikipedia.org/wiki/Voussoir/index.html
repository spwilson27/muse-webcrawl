Voussoir

A voussoir (UK: /ˈvuːswɑːr/, US: /vuːˈswɑːr/) is a wedge-shaped element, typically a stone, that is used in building an arch or vault. Each voussoir is cut so that its faces converge toward the center of the arch's curve; assembled in a ring, the voussoirs press against one another, converting the weight of the structure above into compressive forces that are carried down to the supports.

Description

Although each voussoir in a true arch is a truncated wedge, most are roughly rectangular in visible form, with the wedge taper on their inner faces. The central, highest voussoir is the keystone, the last placed during construction; the voussoirs that rest directly on the supporting piers or walls are called springers. The visible face of a voussoir is its archivolt surface, and the underside of the arch ring is the intrados (soffit), the outer curve the extrados.

Construction

Arches are traditionally built over temporary wooden centering. Masons lay the voussoirs in mortar from both springings toward the crown; when the keystone is set and the mortar hardens, the centering is struck and the arch becomes self-supporting. Voussoir arches were perfected in Roman engineering and remained the standard way of spanning openings in masonry — in bridges, aqueducts, cathedrals, and vaults — until the advent of steel and reinforced concrete. In some traditions, alternating colored voussoirs (ablaq masonry) decorate arches in Islamic and Romanesque architecture.
