Limited war

A limited war is one in which the belligerents do not expend all of the resources at their disposal, whether human, industrial, agricultural, military, natural, technological, or otherwise; or in which the fighting is restricted in geography, targets, or objectives. Limited war contrasts with total war, in which a state mobilizes fully and seeks the complete destruction of the enemy's capacity and will to fight.

Characteristics

Limits may be imposed deliberately by one or both sides: restricting combat to a particular theatre, avoiding strikes on the enemy's homeland or capital, declining to use certain weapons (notably nuclear weapons), or limiting aims to a negotiated settlement rather than unconditional surrender. Limitations are often maintained tacitly, through signals and mutual restraint — a process theorized during the Cold War by strategists such as Thomas Schelling.

Examples

The classic modern example is the Korean War (1950–1953), in which the United States and its allies fought to restore the status quo ante without invading China or using nuclear weapons, while China fought with "volunteers" and avoided attacking American bases outside Korea. Other frequently cited cases include the Vietnam War, the Falklands War of 1982, the Sino-Indian War of 1962, and many eighteenth-century "cabinet wars" fought for limited dynastic aims. The advent of nuclear weapons made limited war a central concern of post-1945 strategy, since escalation to total war risked annihilation.
