Dorset is a ceremonial county in South West England. It is bordered by Devon to the west, Somerset to the north-west, Wiltshire to the north-east, Hampshire to the east, and the English Channel to the south. The largest settlement is Bournemouth, the county town is Dorchester, and other towns include Poole and Weymouth. Dorset is largely rural with low population density, no cities (until Bournemouth, Christchurch and Poole were re-organised), and no motorway.

Geography

The county's landscape is dominated by chalk downland — the Dorset Downs and Cranborne Chase — and by the Jurassic Coast, a 95-mile stretch of coastline designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site for its geological importance, featuring landforms such as Durdle Door, Lulworth Cove, Chesil Beach, and the Isle of Portland. Dorset's highest point is Lewesdon Hill at 279 m.

History

Dorset takes its name from Dorchester (Durnovaria), founded by the Romans near the territory of the Celtic Durotriges, whose hillforts include Maiden Castle, one of the largest in Britain. In the early medieval period the area was absorbed into Wessex. Notable episodes include the Tolpuddle Martyrs of 1834, whose trade-union protest became a landmark of labour history. The novelist and poet Thomas Hardy was born in the county and set his fictional "Wessex" novels in its towns and villages.

Economy

Tourism — drawn by the coast and countryside — and agriculture are mainstays, alongside Poole Harbour's port, marine industries, and digital and creative businesses around Bournemouth. Dorset has no university of its own in the county town, though Bournemouth University and Arts University Bournemouth serve the conurbation.
