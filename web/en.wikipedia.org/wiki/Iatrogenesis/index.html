Iatrogenesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Iatrogenesis refers to harm caused by medical examination or treatment.

Iatrogenesis is the causation of a disease, a harmful complication, or other ill effect by any medical activity, including diagnosis, intervention, error, or negligence. The word derives from the Greek iatros (physician) and genesis (origin), and literally means "brought forth by a healer". Iatrogenic effects range from minor, transient side effects of medication to serious injury and death, and the concept is central to patient safety research and to the maxim primum non nocere — "first, do no harm".

Forms

Iatrogenic harm takes many forms. Adverse drug reactions are among the most common, arising from known side effects, drug interactions, dosing errors, or allergic responses. Hospital-acquired infections (nosocomial infections) occur when patients acquire infections during the course of treatment for other conditions. Surgical complications include wrong-site surgery, retained instruments, anaesthetic accidents, and postoperative complications. Diagnostic iatrogenesis includes harm from false positive results, overdiagnosis, radiation exposure from imaging, and complications of invasive procedures such as biopsy. Psychological and social iatrogenesis — harm from labelling, unnecessary medicalisation of normal life, and the nocebo effect — is also recognised.

Epidemiology

Estimates of the burden of iatrogenic harm vary with method and definition. The Harvard Medical Practice Study (1991) found adverse events in about 3.7% of hospitalisations in New York State, a substantial fraction of which were attributable to negligence. The Institute of Medicine's 1999 report To Err Is Human estimated between 44,000 and 98,000 deaths per year in United States hospitals from medical error, figures that galvanised the modern patient safety movement. Later studies using broader definitions of harm, including diagnostic error and failures of preventive care, have produced higher estimates, though these remain methodologically contested. Iatrogenesis is consistently ranked among the leading categories of preventable harm in health care.

Causes

Causes operate at individual and system levels. Individual factors include lapses in attention, fatigue, inadequate training, and cognitive biases such as anchoring and premature closure in diagnosis. System factors — the focus of modern safety science — include poor communication during handovers, look-alike medication packaging, understaffing, fragmented records, and production pressure. James Reason's "Swiss cheese model" describes how latent organisational weaknesses align with active errors to produce harm.

Prevention

Preventive strategies include standardised checklists (such as the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist), computerised physician order entry with decision support, medication reconciliation, hand hygiene programmes, incident reporting and root cause analysis, simulation training, and a "just culture" that encourages reporting of errors without reflexive blame. Informed consent and shared decision-making reduce harm from unwanted interventions, while deprescribing initiatives address polypharmacy in older patients.

History

The Hippocratic writings already warned that physicians could harm as well as heal. In the nineteenth century, Ignaz Semmelweis showed that puerperal fever was iatrogenically transmitted on doctors' hands, and Florence Nightingale documented hospital mortality attributable to sanitary conditions. Ivan Illich's Medical Nemesis (1975) popularised a broader critique, distinguishing clinical, social, and cultural iatrogenesis.
