Solfège

In music, solfège (also called solfeggio or solfa) is a method of naming pitches by assigning a syllable to each note of a scale. The best-known syllables in English-speaking countries are do, re, mi, fa, sol (so), la, and ti (si), which correspond to the degrees of the major scale. Solfège is fundamental to sight-singing and ear-training instruction.

The ascending major scale with movable-do solfège syllables.

Origins

The syllables derive from the Latin hymn Ut queant laxis, whose successive phrases begin on ascending scale degrees: Ut queant laxis, Resonare fibris, Mira gestorum, Famuli tuorum, Solve polluti, Labii reatum. The system is associated with the 11th-century Benedictine monk Guido d'Arezzo, who also devised the Guidonian hand as a teaching aid. Ut was later largely replaced by the more singable do (often credited to Giovanni Battista Doni in the 17th century), and a seventh syllable, si (from Sancte Iohannes), was added; in English-language teaching si is frequently changed to ti so each syllable begins with a distinct consonant.

Fixed do and movable do

Two main systems are in use:

  • Fixed do — each syllable is permanently attached to a specific pitch class: do is always C, re always D, and so on. This system prevails in much of continental Europe, China, and Japan, where the syllables effectively serve as the note names themselves.
  • Movable do — do always denotes the tonic of the key in use, so the syllables represent scale degrees rather than absolute pitches. It is common in English-speaking countries and in the Kodály method.

Chromatic syllables

In movable-do systems, altered notes take modified syllables. Raised degrees change the vowel to i (e.g. di, ri, fi, si, li), while lowered degrees (other than re) take the vowel e or a (e.g. me, le, se, te, ra).

Related systems

Comparable syllabic systems exist worldwide. Indian classical music uses sargam (sa, re, ga, ma, pa, dha, ni), Byzantine chant has its own syllables, and the English "shape note" tradition used a four-syllable system (fa, sol, la, mi). The solfège syllables became widely known in popular culture through the song "Do-Re-Mi" from The Sound of Music (1959).
