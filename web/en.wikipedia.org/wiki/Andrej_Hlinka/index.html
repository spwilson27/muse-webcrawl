Andrej Hlinka (27 September 1864 – 16 August 1938) was a Slovak Catholic priest and nationalist politician, the longtime leader of the Slovak People's Party and the most prominent figure of the Slovak national movement in interwar Czechoslovakia.

Career

Ordained in 1889, Hlinka became parish priest of Ružomberok and emerged as a champion of Slovak language and political rights against Magyarisation in the Kingdom of Hungary; in 1907 his parish of Černová was the scene of the Černová massacre, in which Hungarian gendarmes shot fifteen Slovak villagers, an outrage that drew European attention to the Slovak cause. In 1918 he supported the creation of Czechoslovakia and signed the Pittsburgh Agreement, which promised Slovak autonomy — autonomy the Prague government never granted. Hlinka spent the following two decades, in parliament and through his party's press, campaigning for Slovak autonomy within (and sometimes ambiguously beyond) the Czechoslovak state; his slogan was "For God's sake, life for the nation."

Legacy

Hlinka died weeks before Slovakia gained autonomy in October 1938, after which his party, renamed Hlinka's Slovak People's Party under Jozef Tiso, led the wartime Slovak state aligned with Nazi Germany; the paramilitary Hlinka Guard took his name. His legacy in Slovakia remains contested: revered by many as a national awakener and defender of Slovak identity, criticised by others for authoritarian inclinations and for the movement he founded.
