Observability

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Observability: inferring internal states from external outputs.

Observability is a measure of how well the internal states of a system can be inferred from knowledge of its external outputs. The term is used in two related senses: in control theory, where it is a formal property of a dynamical system, and in software engineering, where it describes the ability to understand the behaviour of a complex system from its telemetry.

Control theory

In control theory, a system is observable if, for any possible sequence of state and control vectors, the current state can be determined in finite time using only the outputs. The concept, introduced by Rudolf E. Kálmán, is the dual of controllability. For a linear time-invariant system, observability can be tested by checking whether the observability matrix — built from the output matrix and powers of the state matrix — has full rank. Observability is a prerequisite for building state observers, such as the Luenberger observer and the Kalman filter, which estimate unmeasured states from measurements.

Software systems

In software and site reliability engineering, observability refers to instrumenting systems so their internal behaviour can be understood from the outside, typically through three kinds of telemetry — often called the "three pillars": metrics (numeric measurements over time), logs (timestamped event records) and traces (records of requests propagating through distributed services). A highly observable system lets engineers ask new questions about failures and performance without shipping new instrumentation in advance. Open-source standards and tools such as OpenTelemetry, Prometheus and distributed tracing systems grew out of the operational demands of microservice architectures in the 2010s.
