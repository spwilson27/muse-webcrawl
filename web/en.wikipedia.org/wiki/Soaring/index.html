Soaring

Soaring flight exploits rising air to gain or maintain altitude without engine power.

Soaring is a mode of flight in which a bird or an aircraft gains or maintains altitude by exploiting rising air currents, without using thrust from an engine. In aviation, soaring is practised with gliders (sailplanes) and is both a recreational sport and a competitive discipline, administered internationally by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale. Pilots may remain airborne for many hours and cover distances of over 1,000 kilometres using only natural sources of lift.

Sources of lift

Thermals are columns of warm rising air created by solar heating of the ground; pilots circle within them to climb, often beneath cumulus clouds that mark their tops. Ridge lift forms where wind is deflected upward by a slope or mountain range, allowing flight along the ridge. Wave lift occurs downwind of mountains in stable air, where smooth, lens-shaped lenticular clouds mark the crests of standing waves that can carry gliders to altitudes above 15,000 m; the Perlan Project has used Patagonian wave to set world altitude records for gliders. Convergence and sea-breeze fronts provide additional lines of lift.

Birds

Many large birds — eagles, vultures, storks, albatrosses — soar to travel and hunt with minimal energy expenditure. Land birds use thermals and ridge lift, climbing in circles and gliding between sources of lift; oceanic birds such as albatrosses use dynamic soaring, extracting energy from the wind-speed gradient above the waves.

Competition and records

Competitive soaring involves racing around pre-set tasks, scored by speed and distance. The sport dates to the 1920s in Germany and spread worldwide; Otto Lilienthal's earlier gliding experiments laid its foundations. Modern racing sailplanes achieve glide ratios exceeding 50:1.
