Spanish Revival architecture

Spanish Revival (also known as Spanish Eclectic) is an architectural style movement that arose in the United States in the early 20th century, drawing on Spanish colonial and Mediterranean precedents. It flourished especially in California, Florida, Arizona, and Texas between about 1915 and 1931.

Origins

The style gained momentum after the 1915 Panama–California Exposition in San Diego, where architect Bertram Goodhue designed elaborate Spanish Baroque-inspired buildings for Balboa Park. It built on the earlier Mission Revival style but used a freer mix of Spanish Gothic, Moorish, Baroque, and colonial elements.

Characteristics

Typical features include low-pitched red clay tile roofs, smooth stucco walls (often white or cream), asymmetrical facades, arched windows and doorways, decorative ironwork, colorful ceramic tiles, courtyards and patios, and sometimes towers or balconies. The style was applied to houses, hotels, railway stations, movie palaces, and entire planned communities such as Santa Barbara, California, which rebuilt in the style after its 1925 earthquake.

Legacy

Spanish Revival houses remain strongly associated with the American Southwest and Florida, and the style continues to influence residential design in those regions.
