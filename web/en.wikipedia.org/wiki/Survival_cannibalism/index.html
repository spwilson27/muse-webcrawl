Survival cannibalism

Survival cannibalism is the consumption of human flesh by starving people as a last resort to stay alive, as distinct from ritual, funerary, or pathological cannibalism. Documented instances typically occur in isolated groups cut off from food supplies — shipwrecked sailors, besieged cities, famine-stricken regions, and stranded expeditions. In most recorded cases the dead were eaten first; in the most notorious cases lots were drawn and a living person was killed.

The wreck of the Méduse (1816) is one of the best-known survival cannibalism cases.

Notable cases

The Donner Party, a group of American pioneers snowbound in the Sierra Nevada in the winter of 1846–47, resorted to eating the dead; about half of the 87 members survived. The wreck of the French frigate Méduse in 1816 left 147 people on a raft, of whom only 15 survived after thirteen days. In 1972, Uruguayan Air Force Flight 571 crashed in the Andes; the 16 survivors sustained themselves for 72 days by eating the bodies of deceased passengers preserved in the snow, a decision later widely discussed in ethical and religious terms. During the Siege of Leningrad (1941–44) and the Holodomor, thousands of cases were recorded by authorities.

Legal and ethical aspects

The English case of R v Dudley and Stephens (1884) arose from the sinking of the yacht Mignonette: two sailors who killed and ate the cabin boy were convicted of murder, establishing that necessity is not a defence to a charge of murder in English law. In other cases — notably the Andes survivors — authorities and clergy accepted survival cannibalism of the already-dead as morally permissible, and no charges were brought.
