Britannia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Britannia is the national personification of Britain, pictured as a goddess-like female figure wearing a Corinthian helmet and white robes, carrying a trident and a shield emblazoned with the Union Flag, and often accompanied by a lion. The image derives from the Roman personification of the province of Britannia and was revived in the early modern period as a symbol of British power, unity and maritime supremacy.

Roman origins

The Romans depicted Britannia on coins issued under Hadrian (r. 117–138) and Antoninus Pius; Hadrian's issues of around AD 119 show a female figure seated on rocks, leaning on a shield and holding a spear, in a pose sometimes described as dejected or watchful. The images personified the province of Britain at the far north-western edge of the empire. After the Roman period the figure largely disappeared from British art for more than a millennium.

Revival and coinage

Britannia reappeared in the reign of Elizabeth I, who was sometimes celebrated in the guise of the goddess, and the figure became prominent on the coinage of Charles II: from 1672 copper farthings and halfpennies carried Britannia modelled, according to Samuel Pepys, on Frances Teresa Stuart, later Duchess of Richmond. The design, engraved by John Roettier, showed her seated with spear and shield; the composition echoed the Roman prototypes. With the Acts of Union of 1707 joining England and Scotland, Britannia's shield took on the combined crosses of St George and St Andrew, making her a natural emblem of the new Great Britain. She acquired her trident in the later 18th century, emphasising British naval power, and a lion companion in the 19th. Britannia appeared continuously on British coins into the decimal era; she long adorned the penny ("copper") and appeared on the 50 pence piece from 1969 until 2008. In 2015 she returned as the principal design of the £2 coin, and the Bank of England's £50 note issued in 1981 carried her image.

"Rule, Britannia!"

The patriotic song "Rule, Britannia!" originated in the masque Alfred (1740), with music by Thomas Arne and words by James Thomson, premiered at Cliveden for Frederick, Prince of Wales. Its refrain — "Rule, Britannia! Britannia, rule the waves; Britons never, never, never shall be slaves" — cemented the association of the personification with naval dominance, and the song remains a fixture of the Last Night of the Proms.

Modern uses

Britannia's name has been given to ships, including the royal yacht HMY Britannia (1953–1997), to airlines, insurance companies (the Britannia Building Society, later part of the Co-operative Bank), to the Britannia Bridge across the Menai Strait, and to statues in Plymouth, Wigan and elsewhere. Modern depictions are sometimes satirical, contrasting the confident classical figure with contemporary British circumstances.
