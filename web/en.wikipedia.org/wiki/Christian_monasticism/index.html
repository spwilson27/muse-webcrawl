Christian monasticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Christian monasticism is the devotional practice of Christians who live ascetic and typically cloistered lives dedicated to Christian worship. It began to develop early in the history of the Christian Church, modelled upon scriptural examples and ideals, including those in the Old and New Testaments. Monastics take vows of poverty, chastity and obedience and follow a rule of life combining prayer, work and study.

Origins

Monasticism arose in the deserts of Egypt and Syria in the 3rd–4th centuries. Anthony the Great (c. 251–356) became the model hermit; Athanasius's Life of Anthony spread the ideal. Pachomius organised the first cenobitic (communal) monasteries in Egypt, while Basil of Caesarea gave the Eastern church its enduring monastic rule.

Western orders

In the West, Benedict of Nursia's Rule (c. 530) became the standard, balancing prayer (the opus Dei), manual labour and reading. Benedictine monasteries preserved learning through the early Middle Ages. Later orders included the Cistercians (1098), the mendicant Franciscans and Dominicans (13th century), the Carmelites, and, after the Reformation, the Jesuits. Monasticism was suppressed in Protestant countries and during the Dissolution of the Monasteries in England, but revived in Anglicanism in the 19th century.

Eastern monasticism

Eastern Orthodox monasticism centres on Mount Athos in Greece and on great lavras such as Saint Catherine's in Sinai. Orthodox monks pursue hesychasm, prayer centred on the Jesus Prayer.
