Holographic principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Illustration of a hologram: a three-dimensional volume encoded on a two-dimensional boundary surface]
The holographic principle: the bulk's contents encoded on its boundary.

The holographic principle is a property of String theory and a supposed property of Quantum gravity that states that the description of a volume of space can be thought of as encoded on a lower-dimensional boundary to the region — preferably a light-like boundary like a gravitational horizon. First proposed by Gerard 't Hooft (1993) and given a precise string-theoretic form by Leonard Susskind (1995), it grew from the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy law: a black hole's entropy — hence its total information content — is proportional to its horizon area, not its volume, as if the interior's information were written on the surface.

The principle's concrete realisation is the AdS/CFT correspondence (Maldacena, 1997): a gravitational theory in anti-de Sitter space is exactly equivalent to a conformal field theory on its boundary, one dimension lower. Holography has reshaped thinking about spacetime (which appears emergent, built from entanglement) and produced practical tools for strongly coupled systems from quark–gluon plasma to strange metals. Whether our universe — with its positive Cosmological constant — is holographic in the same sense is unknown and actively researched.

Entropy bounds

Bekenstein's bound limits the information in any region by its boundary area in Planck units — about 10⁶⁹ bits per square metre of area — a density no physical system approaches. Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking's area law, plus 't Hooft's insistence that quantum gravity must respect it, made holography a principle rather than a curiosity: any correct theory of quantum gravity must be area-limited in its information capacity.
