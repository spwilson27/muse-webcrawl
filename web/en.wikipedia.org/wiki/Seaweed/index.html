Seaweed

A kelp forest of giant brown seaweed

Seaweed, or macroalgae, refers to thousands of species of macroscopic, multicellular marine algae. Seaweeds belong to three distinct groups: green algae (Chlorophyta), red algae (Rhodophyta), and brown algae (Phaeophyceae) — the last including the kelps, some of which grow tens of metres long and form underwater forests. Unlike terrestrial plants, seaweeds lack true roots, stems, and leaves; the body, or thallus, attaches to the seabed with a holdfast and absorbs nutrients over its whole surface.

Ecology

Seaweeds are primary producers that anchor many coastal food webs and provide habitat for fish, invertebrates, and species such as sea otters and abalone. Most grow in shallow sunlit water; red algae penetrate deepest thanks to pigments that harvest blue light. Free-floating Sargassum forms entire ecosystems in the open Sargasso Sea, while since 2011 vast blooms of it in the tropical Atlantic have caused major beach strandings.

Human uses

Seaweed has been eaten for millennia, especially in East Asia — nori (Porphyra/Pyropia), kombu and wakame (kelps), and hijiki are staples of Japanese, Korean, and Chinese cuisines — and in parts of the British Isles (laver, dulse, carrageen moss). Hydrocolloids extracted from seaweeds are globally important: agar and carrageenan from red algae and alginates from brown algae are used as thickeners and gelling agents in foods, cosmetics, and biotechnology. Seaweed farming (mariculture), chiefly in China, Indonesia, South Korea, and the Philippines, produces tens of millions of tonnes annually, making it one of the largest aquaculture sectors by weight. Interest is also growing in seaweed as fertiliser, animal feed (including methane-reducing Asparagopsis), biofuel feedstock, and a tool of carbon sequestration.
