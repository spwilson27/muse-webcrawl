Umlaut

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, umlaut (from German Umlaut, meaning "changed sound") is a sound change in which a vowel is pronounced more like a following vowel or semivowel. The term was coined by Jacob Grimm in reference to Germanic languages, in which the process occurred prominently. Umlaut is also the name of the diacritic mark (¨) used to indicate the resulting vowel sounds in the orthographies of several languages.

The most common form is i-umlaut, also known as i-mutation, in which a back vowel is fronted when a following syllable contains /i/, /iː/, or /j/. For example, in the prehistory of English, the plural of *fōt "foot" became *fōti, which developed into "feet" as the final vowel was lost and the stem vowel was fronted.

Umlaut in Germanic languages

In Old English, Old Norse, and Old High German, i-umlaut systematically altered vowel qualities. In German, umlauted vowels are written ä, ö, and ü, and the process remains morphologically productive: Mann (man) becomes Männer (men), and many adjective comparatives and diminutives are formed with umlaut. In English, only irregular relics survive, such as foot/feet, goose/geese, man/men, tooth/teeth, and mouse/mice.

The diacritic

The umlaut sign consists of two dots placed over a vowel. In German typography it developed from a superscript e written above the vowel, which in cursive hands shrank into two short strokes and eventually two dots. The same two-dot mark serves a different purpose as the diaeresis, which indicates that two adjacent vowels are pronounced separately, as in French naïf or English coöperate (in older spelling). Although graphically identical, umlaut and diaeresis are historically and functionally distinct.

Other languages

Umlaut-like processes appear in many language families. In Turkish, vowel harmony governs suffix vowels in a way historically related to umlaut. Hungarian and Finnish orthographies use ä, ö, and ü for front vowels. In Chinese pinyin, ü represents a front rounded vowel. The heavy metal music scene adopted the decorative "metal umlaut" — dots added gratuitously over letters in band names such as Motörhead and Mötley Crüe — which has no phonetic function.
