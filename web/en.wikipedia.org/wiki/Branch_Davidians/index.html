Branch Davidians

The Branch Davidians (or the General Association of Branch Davidian Seventh-day Adventists) are a religious movement founded in 1955 by Benjamin Roden. The movement is an offshoot of the Davidian movement, which itself broke away from the Seventh-day Adventist Church in the 1930s under the leadership of Victor Houteff. The Branch Davidians are best known for the 1993 Waco siege, a 51-day standoff between the group, led by David Koresh, and federal law enforcement at the Mount Carmel Center ranch near Waco, Texas.

History

The Davidian movement began when Victor Houteff, a Bulgarian immigrant and Seventh-day Adventist, published The Shepherd's Rod in 1929, arguing that the Adventist Church needed reform. After his excommunication, Houteff established the Mount Carmel Center near Waco in 1935. Following Houteff's death in 1955, his widow Florence moved to dissolve the association and prophesied an apocalyptic event for 22 April 1959. When the prophecy failed, the movement fractured. Benjamin Roden, who had contested Florence Houteff's leadership, founded the Branch Davidians, taking the name from the biblical branch of Jesse.

Roden led the group until his death in 1978, after which his wife Lois Roden assumed leadership and promoted the doctrine of the feminine nature of the Holy Spirit. During the 1980s, Vernon Wayne Howell, who later took the name David Koresh, joined the group and eventually prevailed in a power struggle with Lois Roden's son, George Roden. Koresh taught that he was the final prophet of the movement and interpreted the Seven Seals of the Book of Revelation.

Waco siege

On 28 February 1993, agents of the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, and Firearms (ATF) attempted to execute a search warrant at Mount Carmel, suspecting the group of stockpiling illegal weapons. A gunfight erupted in which four ATF agents and six Branch Davidians were killed. The FBI then conducted a 51-day siege. On 19 April 1993, federal agents launched a tear gas assault to force the occupants out; a fire broke out and destroyed the compound. Seventy-six Branch Davidians, including David Koresh and more than twenty children, died. The events at Waco remain controversial and are cited in debates over religious freedom, federal law-enforcement tactics, and the militia movement; the siege and the earlier Ruby Ridge incident were cited by Timothy McVeigh as motivations for the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing.

Survivors and legacy

A small number of Branch Davidians survived the fire and some were tried and imprisoned on weapons and manslaughter charges, most being released by the mid-2000s. A remnant group continues to maintain a presence at Mount Carmel, where a new chapel and visitor displays have been built. The theology of the surviving Branch Davidians continues to emphasize the Rodens' and Koresh's interpretations of prophecy.
