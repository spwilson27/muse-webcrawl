The Mogao Caves (Chinese: 莫高窟), also known as the Thousand Buddha Grottoes, form a system of 735 caves in the cliff face of Mount Mingsha, some 25 km southeast of Dunhuang, an oasis town on the Silk Road in Gansu province, China. The caves preserve roughly 45,000 square metres of murals and more than 2,000 painted sculptures created over a millennium, from the 4th to the 14th century, and constitute one of the largest and richest repositories of Buddhist art in the world. The site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.

History

According to tradition, in 366 CE the monk Le Zun (Lèzūn) saw a vision of a thousand Buddhas bathed in golden light on the cliff and commissioned the first cave. Dunhuang's position at the western end of the Hexi Corridor, where the Silk Road split around the Taklamakan Desert, made the caves a flourishing centre of worship, art, and trade for a thousand years. Patronage came from local rulers, merchants, soldiers, and the imperial court; the great Cave 96 houses a 35.5-metre Buddha statue built under Tang-dynasty patronage in 695. After the Yuan dynasty, as sea trade displaced the Silk Road, the caves were gradually abandoned.

The Library Cave

In 1900 the Daoist caretaker Wang Yuanlu discovered the sealed Library Cave (Cave 17), which contained some 50,000 manuscripts, paintings, and printed documents dating from the 5th to the early 11th century — including the world's earliest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE. The explorers Aurel Stein (1907) and Paul Pelliot (1908), among others, acquired tens of thousands of these documents for museums in London, Paris, and elsewhere, a removal that remains controversial in China. The remaining manuscripts were transferred to Beijing in 1910.

Art

The murals depict sutra stories, jataka tales, paradise scenes, donors, and everyday life, and their style charts the evolution of Chinese Buddhist art and its fusion of Indian, Central Asian, and Chinese influences. Sculpture ranges from monumental Tang Buddhas to delicate stucco bodhisattvas.

Conservation

The Dunhuang Academy, founded in 1944, administers the site. Threats include sand abrasion, humidity, salt migration, and mass tourism; a digital archive (Digital Dunhuang) and strict visitor quotas now help protect the fragile paintings.
