Yagi–Uda antenna

A Yagi–Uda antenna, commonly called a Yagi antenna, is a directional antenna consisting of multiple parallel elements in a line, usually half-wave dipoles made of metal rods. It consists of a single driven element connected to the transmitter or receiver, and additional parasitic elements — typically a reflector behind the driven element and one or more directors in front.

Operation

The parasitic elements are not electrically connected to the feed line; currents induced in them by the driven element re-radiate, and the geometry is arranged so that radiation adds constructively in the forward direction and cancels to the rear. Adding more directors increases gain and narrowness of the beam, at the cost of bandwidth. Typical consumer television Yagis achieve gains of roughly 7–15 dBi.

History

The antenna was invented in 1926 by Shintaro Uda of Tohoku Imperial University in Japan and described in English by his colleague Hidetsugu Yagi, whose name became attached to it in the West. It was widely used during the Second World War for radar, and afterwards became the standard form of rooftop television antenna for VHF and UHF reception. It remains common for amateur radio, point-to-point links and other directional applications.
