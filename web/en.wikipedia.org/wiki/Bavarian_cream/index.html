Bavarian cream

Bavarian cream (French: crème bavaroise, or simply bavarois) is a classic French dessert of custard (crème anglaise) thickened with gelatin and lightened with whipped cream, set in a mould and turned out for serving. It is usually flavoured with vanilla, chocolate, coffee or fruit purées, and served chilled, sometimes with a fruit sauce.

History

The dessert's origins are uncertain. One tradition credits the great chef Marie-Antoine Carême in the early nineteenth century; the name is often explained by the dish's popularity with, or supposed origin among, Bavarian princes and visitors to Paris — Bavarian Wittelsbach princes were frequent guests at the French court in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Isidore of Seville is sometimes cited in fanciful accounts, but the modern gelatin-set form belongs to the era of grande cuisine.

Preparation

A pouring custard of egg yolks, sugar and milk or cream is cooked until it coats the spoon, then softened gelatin is stirred in. When the mixture has cooled to the point of beginning to thicken, softly whipped cream is folded through; the mousse-like cream is poured into moulds and refrigerated until firmly set. If allowed to gel too far before folding, the texture becomes rubbery or layered, the classic faults of the dish. Unmoulded bavarois hold their shape softly and melt in the mouth.

Variations

Bavarian cream is the filling of the Charlotte (surrounded by ladyfinger biscuits) and a component of many entremets. In the United States, "Bavarian cream" is also the name of a custard-like filling used in doughnuts, similar to Boston cream. Modern versions may use vegetarian gelling agents such as agar in place of gelatin.
