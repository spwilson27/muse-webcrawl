William Kingdon Clifford

Born4 May 1845, Exeter, England
Died3 March 1879 (aged 33), Madeira, Portugal
OccupationMathematician, philosopher
Known forClifford algebra; "The Ethics of Belief"

William Kingdon Clifford (4 May 1845 – 3 March 1879) was an English mathematician and philosopher. Building on the work of Hermann Grassmann, he introduced what is now termed geometric algebra — Clifford algebra — now widely used in geometry and theoretical physics. He was also an early exponent of the idea that gravity might be a manifestation of curved geometry, anticipating themes of general relativity.

Mathematics

Clifford studied at King's College London and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he was second wrangler. In 1871 he became professor of applied mathematics at University College London. His 1878 paper "Applications of Grassmann's Extensive Algebra" unified quaternions and Grassmann's exterior algebra into Clifford algebra. He also worked on Riemann surfaces, biquaternions, and the classification of geometries, and coined terms including "scalar product" in common usage. In 1870 he told the Cambridge Philosophical Society that matter might be nothing more than curvature in space — a striking anticipation of Einstein.

Philosophy

Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) argues that "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence" — a position known as evidentialism, famously answered by William James in "The Will to Believe". His popular science writings, posthumously collected in Lectures and Essays (1879), made him one of Victorian Britain's most admired scientific stylists.

Death

Clifford's health collapsed from overwork; he died of tuberculosis in Madeira aged 33. The structures named for him — Clifford algebras, Clifford tori, and Clifford parallels — remain standard in mathematics and physics.
