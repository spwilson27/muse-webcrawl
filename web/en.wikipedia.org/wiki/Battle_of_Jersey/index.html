The Battle of Jersey was fought on 6 January 1781 when a French expedition attempted a surprise seizure of the island of Jersey during the American Revolutionary War. Although the French landed undetected and briefly occupied the capital, St Helier, British regulars and Jersey militia counter-attacked and destroyed the invading force in street fighting in the Royal Square.

Background

Jersey, close to the French coast and a base for privateers preying on French shipping, was an inviting target. The expedition was commanded by the Baron de Rullecourt, an adventurer in French service who had earlier reconnoitred the island. His force, variously estimated between about 700 and 1,400 men, sailed from Granville and landed at La Rocque in the early hours of 6 January, marching on St Helier before the garrison was aware of the attack.

The battle

The French surprised the island's lieutenant governor, Major Moses Corbet, in his bed and compelled him to order a surrender, but British officers elsewhere on the island refused to obey an order given under duress. The 24-year-old Major Francis Peirson of the 95th Foot rallied the regulars and militia and attacked the French in the Royal Square. In the sharp fight that followed, both Peirson and Rullecourt were killed; Peirson's servant Pompey is traditionally credited with shooting Rullecourt. Leaderless, the French surrendered.

Legacy

Peirson's death became a celebrated patriotic subject, immortalised in John Singleton Copley's painting The Death of Major Peirson. The battle is often described as the last land battle fought by a foreign invader on the British home islands' soil (in the Channel Islands), and it prompted the construction of the round coastal towers that still ring Jersey's shores.
