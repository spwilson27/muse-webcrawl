Standard score

In statistics, the standard score (also called a z-score or normal score) is the number of standard deviations by which the value of an observation is above or below the mean of what is being observed. It is calculated as z = (x − μ) / σ, where x is the raw score, μ the population mean, and σ the population standard deviation. Scores above the mean have positive standard scores; those below, negative.

Applications

Standard scores allow values measured on different scales to be compared directly, because the z-score expresses position in units of spread. They are used in standardised testing (many IQ and achievement scores are linear rescalings of z-scores), in process control, in computing probabilities from the standard normal distribution, and in identifying outliers — absolute z-scores above about 3 are often flagged as unusual. The z-score of a sample mean forms the basis of the z-test.

Standardising samples

When the population parameters are unknown, the sample mean and sample standard deviation are substituted, producing a studentised value. The process of converting raw scores to z-scores is called standardising or normalising, though it does not by itself make a distribution normal. Related transformations include the T-score (mean 50, standard deviation 10) and stanines.
