The Iliad is one of two major ancient Greek epic poems attributed to Homer. Set towards the end of the Trojan War, it is a central pillar of the Greek literary canon and the oldest surviving work of European literature. It tells of the wrath of Achilles, the greatest of the Greek warriors: insulted by his commander Agamemnon, Achilles withdraws from battle, and only the death of his beloved companion Patroclus — killed by the Trojan prince Hector — brings him back to the fighting, to kill Hector and then, in a famous closing scene, to return Hector's body to his grieving father, King Priam.

Content

Despite its epic scale, the poem covers only a few weeks of the war's tenth year, alluding to the whole conflict — the abduction of Helen, the gathering of the Greek fleet, and Troy's eventual fall — through speeches and prophecies. The gods of Olympus intervene constantly, taking sides and shaping events. The poem's themes are glory (kleos), honour, fate, rage and mortality: Achilles must choose between a long, obscure life and a short, glorious one, and chooses glory — while the poem itself shows war's cost to victors and victims alike. Its companion poem, the Odyssey, follows Odysseus's ten-year voyage home.

Composition and influence

The Iliad emerged from a long oral tradition and was probably written down in the 8th century BC in an artificial poetic dialect mixing Greek dialects. Scholars debate whether "Homer" was one poet or a tradition's name. Copied and taught throughout antiquity, the poem shaped Greek education and, through Virgil's Aeneid and later literature, the whole Western tradition; together with works like the Divine Comedy and Don Quixote, it stands among the most influential texts ever composed. Famous translations include those of Alexander Pope (rhyming couplets) and, in recent decades, Emily Wilson's iambic pentameter version.
