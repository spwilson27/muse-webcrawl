Light-sport aircraft

A light-sport aircraft (LSA), or light sport aircraft, is a fairly new category of small, lightweight aircraft that are simple to fly. LSAs tend to be heavier and more sophisticated than ultralight aircraft, but LSA restrictions on weight and performance separates the category from larger general aviation aircraft. There is no standard worldwide description of an LSA.

United States definition

The United States Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) created the light-sport category in 2004. Under the FAA definition, a light-sport aircraft has a maximum gross takeoff weight of not more than 1,320 lb (600 kg) for aircraft not intended for operation on water, or 1,430 lb (650 kg) for seaplanes; a maximum airspeed in level flight of 120 knots (138 mph) calibrated airspeed; a maximum stalling speed of 45 knots; a maximum of two seats; a single, non-turbine engine; and fixed landing gear and a fixed-pitch or ground-adjustable propeller for airplanes.

Certification and operation

Rather than being certificated under the traditional FAA type-certification process, LSAs are built to ASTM consensus standards and receive a special light-sport airworthiness certificate. In the United States, sport pilots may fly LSAs with a driver's licence in lieu of a medical certificate, though the MOSAIC rule adopted in 2025 expands the aircraft that sport pilots may fly and revised elements of the category. Factory-built aircraft are termed S-LSA, while kit aircraft are certificated as experimental light-sport aircraft (E-LSA).

Other countries

Similar categories exist elsewhere: in Europe the CS-LSA and later light categories under EASA, and in Australia aircraft administered by Recreational Aviation Australia. Many popular designs, such as the Flight Design CT series, the Czech Sport Aircraft Piper Sport, and the Icon A5 amphibian, are marketed in the category.
