Cover version

Cover versions are new recordings of previously released songs.

In popular music, a cover version, cover song, revival, or simply cover is a new performance or recording by a musician other than the original performer or composer of the song. Originally, competing record labels would release rival versions of a hit song — one label "covering" another's release — and the term later broadened to any later rendition, whether imitative or transformative.

History

Covering was central to the early recording industry, when songs were the property of publishers and many artists recorded the same popular standards. In the 1950s, white performers such as Pat Boone covered rhythm-and-blues songs by Black artists such as Fats Domino and Little Richard for mainstream pop markets — a practice now widely criticised. From the 1960s onward, singer-songwriter culture made original material the norm, but covers remained an important way for artists to pay tribute, reinterpret repertoire, or reach new audiences.

Notable covers

  • "Hallelujah" — Leonard Cohen's song, popularised by Jeff Buckley's 1994 recording.
  • "All Along the Watchtower" — Bob Dylan's song, famously reinterpreted by the Jimi Hendrix Experience (1968).
  • "Hurt" — Nine Inch Nails' song, covered by Johnny Cash in 2002 to wide acclaim.
  • "Respect" — written and first recorded by Otis Redding, transformed by Aretha Franklin in 1967.
  • "I Will Always Love You" — Dolly Parton's song, a global hit for Whitney Houston in 1992.

Legal aspects

In the United States, once a song has been released to the public, anyone may record a cover by obtaining a compulsory mechanical licence and paying the statutory royalty rate to the songwriter or publisher; the new performer acquires no rights in the underlying composition. Covers that substantially alter the melody or lyrics may instead be treated as derivative works requiring permission.
