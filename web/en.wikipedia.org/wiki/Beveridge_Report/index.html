The Beveridge Report, officially titled Social Insurance and Allied Services, was a government report published in November 1942 that proposed a comprehensive system of social security in the United Kingdom. Written by the economist and Liberal politician William Beveridge, it became the blueprint for the post-war welfare state and the foundation of the National Health Service.

Background

In June 1941, during the Second World War, the government appointed Beveridge to chair a committee investigating existing social insurance schemes. The report was published on 1 December 1942 and became an unexpected bestseller, selling over 600,000 copies.

The five giants

Beveridge identified "five giant evils" to be defeated on the road to reconstruction: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, and Idleness. He proposed that Want be abolished through a unified system of social insurance covering unemployment, sickness, old age, and widowhood, funded by flat-rate contributions in return for flat-rate benefits.

Legacy

Following the Labour Party's victory in the 1945 general election, Clement Attlee's government implemented most of Beveridge's recommendations, passing the National Insurance Act 1946 and creating the NHS in 1948. The report remains one of the most influential documents in British social policy.
