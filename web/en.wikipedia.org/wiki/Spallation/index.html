Spallation

Spallation is a process in which fragments of material (spall) are ejected from a body due to impact or stress. In nuclear physics, spallation is the process in which a heavy nucleus emits a large number of nucleons as a result of being hit by a high-energy particle, thus greatly reducing its atomic weight. It is one of the most efficient ways to produce neutrons and exotic isotopes.

Nuclear spallation

When a high-energy proton (typically hundreds of MeV to several GeV) strikes a heavy target nucleus such as lead, tungsten, tantalum, mercury, or uranium, an intranuclear cascade knocks out individual nucleons, after which the excited residual nucleus "evaporates" further neutrons and light fragments, sometimes undergoing fission. A single incident proton can produce 20–30 neutrons in a suitable target.

Applications

Spallation neutron sources — such as the Spallation Neutron Source at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, ISIS in the United Kingdom, the European Spallation Source in Sweden, and J-PARC in Japan — use accelerator-driven spallation to produce intense pulsed neutron beams for materials science and condensed-matter research. Spallation is also studied for accelerator-driven transmutation of nuclear waste, production of medical and industrial radioisotopes, and understanding cosmic-ray interactions with planetary surfaces and spacecraft shielding, where spallation alters both radiation fields and material properties.

Other meanings

In materials science and geology, spallation refers to mechanical flaking or fragmentation of a surface under impact, thermal stress, or weathering; in planetary science it contributes to the erosion of rock surfaces and the production of regolith.
