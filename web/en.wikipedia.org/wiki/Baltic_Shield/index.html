Baltic Shield

TypeGeological shield (craton)
LocationFennoscandia, northwestern Russia, Baltic Sea floor

Also known asFennoscandian Shield

The Baltic Shield (or Fennoscandian Shield) is a segment of the Earth's crust belonging to the East European Craton, representing a large area of exposed Precambrian crystalline rocks in northern Europe. It occupies Finland, Sweden, Norway's eastern highlands, the Kola Peninsula and Karelia in Russia, and the floor of the Baltic Sea. The shield's rocks, principally gneisses and granites, are between about 1 and 3.7 billion years old and form some of the oldest crust in Europe.

Geology

The shield is divided into provinces of differing age: the Archaean core of Karelia and the Kola Peninsula in the northeast (up to ~3.7 billion years), the Palaeoproterozoic Svecofennian orogen formed around 1.9 billion years ago across Finland and Sweden, and the younger Transscandinavian Igneous Belt and Sveconorwegian orogen in the southwest. Because erosion has stripped away most younger cover rocks, these ancient basement rocks lie at or near the surface over vast areas, producing the lake-dotted, thin-soiled landscapes of Finland and northern Sweden.

Glaciation and rebound

During the Quaternary ice ages the shield was repeatedly covered by the Fennoscandian ice sheet, which scoured its surface and depressed the crust. Since the ice melted, the region has been undergoing post-glacial rebound — around the Gulf of Bothnia the land is still rising by up to about 9 mm per year, among the fastest rates on Earth.

Economic significance

The shield hosts major deposits of iron ore (Kiruna and Malmberget in Sweden), copper, gold, and nickel, and its stable, low-seismicity basement has made it a candidate location for deep geological repositories for spent nuclear fuel in Sweden and Finland (such as the Onkalo repository).
