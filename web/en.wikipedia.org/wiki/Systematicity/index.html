Systematicity

Systematicity is a property of language and of cognition: the ability to understand certain sentences is intrinsically connected to the ability to understand certain other, related sentences. A speaker who can understand "John loves Mary" can invariably also understand "Mary loves John", because the two sentences are built from the same constituents according to the same grammatical rules. The notion is closely related to compositionality and productivity, and together they figure prominently in arguments about the nature of human thought.

The systematicity argument

In their influential 1988 paper "Connectionism and Cognitive Architecture", Jerry Fodor and Zenon Pylyshyn argued that systematicity (and compositionality) is a law-like feature of natural language and cognition that is best explained by a classical, language-of-thought architecture, in which mental representations have combinatorial syntactic structure and mental processes are sensitive to that structure. Because connectionist networks of the time lacked such built-in compositional structure, Fodor and Pylyshyn claimed they could provide at most implementations of a classical architecture, not alternatives to it. The argument became a central point of debate between classical computationalism and connectionism, and echoes of it persist in discussions of whether modern neural networks genuinely generalize systematically.

Related notions

Systematicity differs from productivity — the capacity to produce and understand an unlimited number of novel sentences — though both follow from a compositional grammar. It also underpins inferences in linguistics: children's rapid generalization across constructions is evidence that they acquire rules and constituent structures rather than lists of memorized sentences.
