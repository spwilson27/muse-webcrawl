River Fleet

CountryEngland, United Kingdom
SourceHampstead Heath, London
MouthRiver Thames at Blackfriars, London
StatusSubterranean (culverted as a sewer)

The River Fleet is the largest of London's subterranean rivers. It rises from springs on Hampstead Heath — its two branches feeding the Hampstead and Highgate ponds — and flows about 6 kilometres south through Kentish Town, King's Cross, Clerkenwell and Farringdon before entering the River Thames beneath Blackfriars Bridge. Once a significant tidal river marking the western boundary of the City of London, it was progressively covered over between the 18th and 19th centuries and now runs as a major storm sewer.

History

In Roman and medieval London the Fleet (from Old English flēot, a tidal inlet) was a substantial waterway, wide enough at its mouth for shipping, with wharves, mills and bridges — Holborn Bridge and Fleet Bridge among them. The valley carried the notorious Fleet Prison on its east bank and the Fleet Market, and couples could be married clandestinely in the "Fleet marriages" around the prison. Shakespeare and Ben Jonson mention the increasingly foul river; Jonson's mock-heroic poem On the Famous Voyage describes a boat trip up the Fleet ditch. Jonathan Swift described storm debris in it, and Alexander Pope made it a set piece in The Dunciad.

After the Great Fire of 1666, Christopher Wren proposed remaking part of the lower Fleet as a quay-lined canal, which was built but quickly became an open sewer. The upper reaches were arched over as the city expanded; the stretch beneath Farringdon Street was covered in the 1730s–1760s, and the final sections disappeared by the 1870s, when Joseph Bazalgette incorporated the Fleet into his intercepting sewer system — its line can still be traced by streets such as Farringdon Road and New Bridge Street, and by the grating near Blackfriars where its waters pour into the Thames. In wet weather the Fleet sewer still discharges into the river, a problem targeted by the Thames Tideway Tunnel project.

Legacy

The Fleet gave its name to Fleet Street, for centuries the home of the British newspaper industry, and to the phrase "Fleet Street" as a metonym for the press. Campaigns occasionally propose daylighting parts of London's buried rivers, but the Fleet's depth and role in drainage make its restoration impractical; it remains the most famous of London's lost rivers.
