Conservation law

A conservation law is a physical law stating that a particular measurable property of an isolated physical system does not change as the system evolves over time. Examples include the conservation of energy, momentum, angular momentum, electric charge, colour charge and baryon number. In addition to these exact laws, there are approximate conservation laws that apply in restricted circumstances, such as the conservation of mass at non-relativistic energies.

Noether's theorem

Conservation laws are fundamentally consequences of symmetries of physical space and time. Noether's theorem, proved by Emmy Noether in 1915 and published in 1918, states that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law. Time-translation symmetry gives conservation of energy; spatial-translation symmetry gives conservation of momentum; rotational symmetry gives conservation of angular momentum.

Exact and approximate laws

Some conservation laws are exact for all known processes: energy, linear momentum, angular momentum and electric charge have never been observed to be violated. Others, such as parity, charge conjugation and CP symmetry, were long believed exact but are now known to be violated in weak interactions. Conservation of mass is an approximation valid in everyday chemistry and mechanics, subsumed by mass–energy equivalence in relativity.

Continuity equations

Conservation laws are often expressed as continuity equations, which state that the change of the conserved quantity within a volume equals the quantity flowing in or out through its boundary. Examples include the continuity equation for electric charge and the equations of fluid dynamics derived from conservation of mass, momentum and energy.

History

The idea of conservation dates to ancient philosophy. The conservation of momentum was developed in the 17th century by Descartes, Huygens and Newton; the conservation of energy was established in the mid-19th century through the work of Mayer, Joule, Helmholtz and others, and became the first law of thermodynamics.
