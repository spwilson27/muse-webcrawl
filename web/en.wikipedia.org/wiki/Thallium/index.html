Thallium is a chemical element with the symbol Tl and atomic number 81. It is a silvery-white post-transition metal that is not found free in nature. When isolated, thallium resembles tin, but it discolours when exposed to air. It was discovered in 1861 by William Crookes, who detected its bright green spectral emission line; its name derives from the Greek thallos, meaning a green shoot or twig.

Properties

Thallium is soft enough to be cut with a knife and has a low melting point of 304 °C. It occurs in two oxidation states, +1 and +3, with the +1 state resembling the alkali metals and potassium in particular — a similarity that underlies its extreme toxicity, since the thallium ion is taken up by potassium-handling pathways in the body.

Applications

Thallium compounds are used in specialised electronics, infrared optics, and scintillation detectors (sodium iodide doped with thallium), and thallium-201 was used in nuclear medicine. Thallium sulfate was formerly used as a rodenticide and insecticide but has been banned in most countries because of its toxicity.

Toxicity

Thallium salts are highly toxic, odourless, and tasteless, giving the element a notorious history in poisonings. Symptoms include hair loss, nerve damage, and organ failure. Prussian blue is an effective antidote, sequestering thallium in the gut.
