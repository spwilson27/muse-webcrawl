Protein family

A protein family is a group of evolutionarily related proteins. In many cases, a protein family has a corresponding gene family, in which the genes encode the proteins of the family, sharing a common ancestor. Protein families are typically grouped together because their member proteins share similar amino acid sequences, three-dimensional structures, and biological functions.

Structural alignment of members of a protein family

The term protein family has been used in various ways in the literature. Members of a family typically descend from a common ancestor and are usually detectable by sequence similarity searches, such as BLAST, or by matching against profile models such as those in the Pfam database. Families may be subdivided into subfamilies, or grouped into superfamilies whose members are more distantly related and whose similarity may only be evident at the level of structure.

Terminology and hierarchy

As with much of biological terminology, the usage of "protein family" is somewhat context dependent. A commonly used hierarchy is:

  • Superfamily – a large group of proteins with low sequence similarity but related structures and functions, indicating a common evolutionary origin.
  • Family – a group of proteins with significant sequence similarity, usually more than 30% identity over their lengths, and usually similar functions.
  • Subfamily – a subdivision of a family whose members are even more closely related and often have subtly different functions.

Protein families may be organized into clans in databases such as Pfam, where a clan is a collection of families believed to share a common origin. The InterPro consortium integrates family classifications from several member databases, including Pfam, PROSITE, PRINTS, and SMART.

Identification

Families are identified computationally by comparing a query sequence against libraries of family models. Profile hidden Markov models (HMMs), built from multiple sequence alignments of known family members, are particularly sensitive: tools such as HMMER can detect remote homologues that pairwise searches would miss. Conserved domains within proteins are often classified individually, since a single protein may contain domains belonging to several different families.

Evolution

Protein families expand and contract over evolutionary time through gene duplication, divergence, gene loss, and occasionally horizontal gene transfer. Following duplication, copies may accumulate mutations and acquire new or subdivided functions (neofunctionalization and subfunctionalization), giving rise to subfamilies with distinct biochemical roles. Well-studied examples include the globins, the protein kinases, and the immunoglobulin superfamily.
