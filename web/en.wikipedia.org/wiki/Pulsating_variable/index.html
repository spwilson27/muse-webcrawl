Pulsating variable star


Light curve of a classical Cepheid, a type of pulsating variable.

A pulsating variable star, or pulsating variable, is a variable star whose brightness changes because the star itself expands and contracts in size. The pulsations are driven by periodic expansion and contraction of the surface layers of the star, which alters its temperature and luminosity. Pulsation periods range from minutes (in some white dwarfs) to years (in Mira variables).

Mechanism

Most pulsating variables are powered by the kappa mechanism: a layer of partially ionized gas inside the star (often helium, or hydrogen in cooler stars) increases in opacity when compressed, trapping heat and pushing the layer outward; when the layer expands and cools, the gas recombines, opacity falls, and gravity pulls the star back in. This acts like a heat engine and sustains regular oscillations. Non-radial pulsations, in which different parts of the surface move in and out, also occur and are studied by asteroseismology.

Classes

Important classes include:

  • Classical Cepheids – luminous supergiants with periods of days to weeks, obeying the period–luminosity relation used to measure cosmic distances.
  • RR Lyrae variables – older, lower-mass stars with periods under a day, standard candles for globular clusters.
  • Mira variables – red giants with periods of months and large amplitudes.
  • Delta Scuti and Gamma Doradus variables – main-sequence pulsators with short periods.
  • Slowly pulsating B stars and Beta Cephei variables – hot massive stars with periods of hours.
  • ZZ Ceti variables – pulsating white dwarfs.

Importance

Pulsating variables are fundamental to astronomy: the period–luminosity relation discovered by Henrietta Swan Leavitt for Cepheids made them the first reliable standard candles for extragalactic distances, and asteroseismology of pulsating stars reveals stellar interiors in a manner analogous to seismology on Earth.
