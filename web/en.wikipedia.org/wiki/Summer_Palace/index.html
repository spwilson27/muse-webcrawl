Summer Palace

Kunming Lake and the Tower of Buddhist Incense on Longevity Hill

The Summer Palace (Chinese: 颐和园; pinyin: Yíhéyuán) is a vast ensemble of lakes, gardens and palaces in Beijing, China. It was an imperial garden during the Qing dynasty and is dominated by Longevity Hill (60 metres high) and Kunming Lake, which covers about three-quarters of the site's roughly 3 square kilometres. In 1998 the Summer Palace was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as "a masterpiece of Chinese landscape garden design".

History

The origins of the garden reach back to the Jin dynasty, but its classic form was created by the Qianlong Emperor, who in 1750 commissioned the Garden of Clear Ripples (Qingyiyuan) to celebrate his mother Empress Dowager Chongqing's 60th birthday. Kunming Lake was enlarged by hand, modelled on the West Lake in Hangzhou, and the excavated earth formed Longevity Hill.

In 1860, during the Second Opium War, Anglo-French forces looted and burned the garden. The Empress Dowager Cixi rebuilt it beginning in 1886 — notoriously diverting funds earmarked for the imperial navy — and renamed it Yiheyuan, the "Garden of Preserved Harmony". It was damaged again during the Boxer Rebellion of 1900 and restored in 1902. After the fall of the Qing, it opened to the public in 1924.

Layout

The grounds contain over 3,000 structures: pavilions, halls, temples, bridges and corridors. The most famous is the Long Corridor, a covered walkway 728 metres long decorated with more than 14,000 paintings. At the northern shore of the lake sits the white Marble Boat, a lakeside pavilion in the shape of a paddle steamer. The Seventeen-Arch Bridge connects the eastern shore to Nanhu Island, and the Tower of Buddhist Incense crowns Longevity Hill.
