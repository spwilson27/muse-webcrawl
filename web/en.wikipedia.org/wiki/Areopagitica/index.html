Areopagitica; A Speech of Mr. John Milton for the Liberty of Unlicenc'd Printing, to the Parlament of England is a 1644 prose polemic by the English poet, scholar, and author John Milton opposing licensing and censorship. Areopagitica is among the most influential and impassioned philosophical defences of the principle of a right to freedom of speech and expression ever written, and many of its expressed principles have formed the basis for modern justifications of that right.

Background

Areopagitica was published on 23 November 1644 at the height of the English Civil War. It takes its title in part from Areopagitikos, a speech written by the Athenian orator Isocrates in the 4th century BC. (The Areopagus is a hill in Athens, the site of a real, as well as mythical and legendary, tribunal.) Some commentators argue that the title also refers to the defences that St Paul and Jesus of Nazareth made at the Areopagus in Athens against charges of blasphemy.

The tract addressed the English Parliament, which had reintroduced the Licensing Order of 1643 requiring that books be licensed by the government before publication. Milton himself had recently published unlicensed tracts on divorce, in defiance of the order, which provoked the Stationers' Company to petition Parliament to enforce the licensing law.

Argument

According to Milton, prior restraint is ineffective at achieving suppression of harmful books, and it debases authors, teachers, and the press. In particular, Milton argues that prohibiting the reading of books prior to publication is unnecessary, because such suppression cannot prevent evil results. His basic premise was that God's truth will triumph over falsehood in a free and open encounter. Milton, echoing the words of John Selden, Classically presages a core argument of the American First Amendment by claiming that freedom from prior restraint of speech is an inherent good.

Milton opposed the concept that truth is static and shines by its own light in any mind, requiring only an authorised teacher to point it out. Rather, he believed truth emerges through a collision of ideas, and wrote one of the most famous passages:

"And though all the winds of doctrine were let loose to play upon the earth, so Truth be in the field, we do injuriously, by licensing and prohibiting, to misdoubt her strength. Let her and Falsehood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the worse, in a free and open encounter?"

Legacy

Although Milton's immediate objective — the repeal of the Licensing Order — failed, the pamphlet became a foundational text for the development of freedom of the press. Several passages were later invoked by jurists in cases concerning press freedom, including in the jurisprudence of the United States Supreme Court. Areopagitica is frequently cited as one of the earliest and most eloquent arguments against pre-publication censorship.
