International Criminal Court

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The International Criminal Court (ICC) is an international court seated in The Hague, Netherlands, that investigates and tries individuals charged with the gravest crimes of international concern: genocide, war crimes, crimes against humanity and the crime of aggression. Established by the Rome Statute of 1998 and operating since 2002, it is the first permanent international criminal tribunal, with around 125 states parties in the mid-2020s — though major powers including the United States, Russia, China and India are not members.

Operation

The Court acts on the principle of complementarity: it may intervene only where national courts are unwilling or unable genuinely to prosecute. Cases reach it by state referral, UN Security Council referral, or the prosecutor's own initiative with judicial authorisation. The ICC has no police force and depends on states for arrests — a chronic weakness illustrated by long-unexecuted warrants. Its situations have concentrated on Africa in its first decades (drawing criticism of selectivity), later expanding to Afghanistan, Palestine, the Philippines, Venezuela and Ukraine; arrest warrants issued in 2023 against Russia's president over deportation of Ukrainian children, and in 2024 concerning the Israel–Hamas war, tested the Court's reach against powerful non-members and allies of members alike.

Background

The ICC descends from the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals after the Second World War and the ad-hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda in the 1990s, which demonstrated both the possibility and the cost of case-by-case justice.
