False vacuum decay

In quantum field theory, false vacuum decay is the process by which a metastable state of a field — a false vacuum, which sits in a local but not global minimum of its potential energy — decays to a lower-energy true vacuum. If the vacuum of our universe were a false vacuum, such a decay could in principle occur through quantum tunnelling, nucleating a bubble of true vacuum that expands at nearly the speed of light and destroys the existing universe in its path.

Theory

The theory of vacuum decay was developed in the 1970s, notably by Sidney Coleman and collaborators. Coleman showed that the decay proceeds by the nucleation of bubbles of the lower-energy phase, in a process analogous to boiling. The lifetime of a false vacuum depends exponentially on the height and width of the energy barrier separating the two vacua, and for realistic parameters can vastly exceed the current age of the universe.

The Higgs vacuum

Measurements of the masses of the Higgs boson (about 125 GeV) and the top quark suggest that the Higgs potential of the Standard Model may be metastable: at very high energies the potential appears to turn down, implying our vacuum is not the lowest-energy state. Calculations suggest the expected lifetime of such a metastable vacuum is enormously longer than the age of the universe, so the scenario poses no practical danger, though it has attracted popular attention as a hypothetical "doomsday" mechanism.

In cosmology

False vacuum decay plays a role in inflationary cosmology: in "old inflation" models the universe was trapped in a false vacuum whose energy drove exponential expansion, ending when bubbles of true vacuum nucleated. Because bubble nucleation did not complete gracefully in those models, they were superseded by "new inflation" and slow-roll scenarios. In eternal inflation, false vacuum decay continues indefinitely in some regions of a multiverse.
