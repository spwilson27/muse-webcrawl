Antonin Artaud

Born4 September 1896, Marseille, France
Died4 March 1948, Ivry-sur-Seine, France
OccupationPlaywright, poet, actor, theatre director
Known forThe Theatre of Cruelty

Antonin Artaud (1896–1948) was a French artist, writer and theatre theorist whose concept of the Theatre of Cruelty, set out in The Theatre and Its Double (1938), profoundly influenced 20th-century avant-garde performance. He argued that theatre should assault the senses and confront audiences with gesture, sound and spectacle rather than psychological dialogue.

Life and work

Artaud acted in films including Abel Gance's Napoléon (1927) and Dreyer's The Passion of Joan of Arc (1928), co-founded the Théâtre Alfred Jarry, and was briefly associated with the Surrealists before breaking with André Breton. He travelled to Mexico in 1936 to study Tarahumara ritual. Plagued by mental illness, he spent much of 1937–1946 confined in asylums, including at Rodez, where he underwent electroshock treatment. His banned 1947 radio work To Have Done with the Judgment of God is a landmark of sound poetry.

Influence

Artaud's ideas influenced Peter Brook, Jerzy Grotowski, the Living Theatre and much experimental performance; his drawings and writings remain central to studies of madness and creativity.
