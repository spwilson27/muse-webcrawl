"In Flanders Fields"
AuthorJohn McCrae
Written3 May 1915
First published8 December 1915, in Punch
FormRondeau, 15 lines

"In Flanders Fields" is a war poem in the form of a rondeau, written during the First World War by Canadian physician Lieutenant-Colonel John McCrae. He was inspired to write it on 3 May 1915, after presiding over the funeral of friend and fellow soldier Lieutenant Alexis Helmer, who had died in the Second Battle of Ypres. It was first published in the London magazine Punch on 8 December 1915.

Text

In Flanders fields the poppies blow
Between the crosses, row on row,
That mark our place; and in the sky
The larks, still bravely singing, fly
Scarce heard amid the guns below.

We are the Dead. Short days ago
We lived, felt dawn, saw sunset glow,
Loved and were loved, and now we lie,
In Flanders fields.

Take up our quarrel with the foe:
To you from failing hands we throw
The torch; be yours to hold it high.
If ye break faith with us who die
We shall not sleep, though poppies grow
In Flanders fields.

Background

McCrae wrote the poem while serving as a medical officer at a dressing station near Essex Farm, north of Ypres, during the Second Battle of Ypres, in which the German army used poison gas for the first time on the Western Front. Wild poppies were already blooming among the graves of soldiers buried in the battle zone. McCrae reportedly discarded an early draft, which was retrieved by fellow officers and later published.

Popularity and legacy

The poem became one of the most popular of the war, quoted in recruiting and fundraising campaigns across the British Empire and the United States. Its reference to poppies gave rise to the remembrance poppy, adopted by the Royal British Legion and veterans' organisations in Canada and other Commonwealth countries as a symbol of remembrance for fallen soldiers. An American response poem, "We Shall Keep the Faith" (1918) by Moina Michael, helped spread the poppy symbol. The poem is read at Remembrance Day ceremonies each 11 November across Canada, where McCrae's birthplace in Guelph, Ontario, is a national historic site and museum. The final stanza's "torch" imagery is also associated with the Montreal Canadiens hockey club, whose dressing room bears the poem's words.
