Cooperative housing (often called a co-op) is a form of home ownership in which residents own shares in a corporation or association that owns the building, rather than holding title to their individual units. Each shareholding entitles the member to occupy a specific dwelling under a proprietary lease, and members collectively pay for the building's mortgage, taxes, maintenance and utilities through monthly charges.

Legal structure

In the classic model, a housing cooperative is a corporation whose shareholders are its residents. Prospective buyers purchase shares allocated to a unit and must usually be approved by the cooperative's board, which can make co-op purchases slower and more selective than condominium purchases. The board governs the building, sets house rules, and may restrict subletting and resale.

Variations by country

Cooperative housing is a major tenure in New York City, where tens of thousands of apartments are co-ops, many built by unions in the early 20th century. In Sweden, the bostadsrätt system is a close equivalent and accounts for a large share of housing. Norway's housing cooperatives (borettslag), Finland's housing companies, German Wohnungsgenossenschaften and British housing co-operatives all follow similar principles. Limited-equity cooperatives cap resale prices to preserve affordability.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include democratic control, pooled maintenance costs, and in limited-equity models, long-term affordability. Disadvantages include board approval processes, restrictions on financing and subletting, collective liability for the building's finances, and the fact that shares, rather than real property, may be treated differently for tax and inheritance purposes depending on the jurisdiction.
