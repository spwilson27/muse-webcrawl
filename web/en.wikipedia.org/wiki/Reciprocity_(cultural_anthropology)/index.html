Reciprocity (cultural anthropology)

In cultural anthropology, reciprocity is a non-market exchange of goods, labor, or gifts in which giving creates an obligation to return the favor. Reciprocity is a defining feature of exchange in many small-scale and kinship-based societies, and it remains influential in modern social life. In Karl Polanyi's influential scheme, reciprocity is one of three principal modes of economic integration, alongside redistribution and market exchange.

Mauss and the gift

The foundational study is Marcel Mauss's essay The Gift (1925), which examined systems such as the potlatch of the Pacific Northwest and the kula ring described by Bronisław Malinowski. Mauss argued that gifts in such societies are never free: they carry a threefold obligation — to give, to receive, and to reciprocate — and the gift retains something of the giver (the Maori concept of hau), binding persons and groups together. Exchange is thus a "total social phenomenon" simultaneously economic, legal, moral, and religious.

Sahlins' typology

Marshall Sahlins, in Stone Age Economics (1972), arranged reciprocity along a spectrum correlated with social (kinship) distance:

  • Generalized reciprocity — giving without expectation of a specified or immediate return; typical among close kin, as in parental care or food sharing.
  • Balanced reciprocity — direct exchange of goods of roughly equivalent value within a stated time frame; typical between more distant relatives, trading partners, and in ceremonial exchanges such as the kula.
  • Negative reciprocity — attempting to get something for as little as possible, shading into haggling, chicanery, or theft; typical in dealings with strangers or enemies.
