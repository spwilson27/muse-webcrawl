Duchy of Saxony

The Duchy of Saxony in the twelfth century

The Duchy of Saxony (Low German: Hartogdom Sassen; German: Herzogtum Sachsen) was originally the territory of the Saxons in the late Early Middle Ages and later became one of the five stem duchies of the East Frankish and German kingdom, existing in its classic form from 804 until 1296. At its greatest extent it covered most of what is now northwestern Germany, including the modern states of Lower Saxony, Westphalia, northern Saxony-Anhalt and Schleswig-Holstein.

Origins

Charlemagne's Saxon Wars (772–804) ended with the incorporation of Saxony into the Frankish Empire and the forcible conversion of the Saxons. In the ninth and tenth centuries the Liudolfing (Ottonian) family rose as dukes of Saxony; with Henry the Fowler's election as king in 919 and the reign of Otto the Great, the Saxon ducal house became the imperial dynasty. The duchy was then granted to the Billung family (936–1106), under whom Saxon influence expanded eastward into Slavic territory through the Saxon Eastern March.

Henry the Lion

After the Billungs, the duchy passed via Lothair of Supplinburg to the Welf family, and reached its apogee under Henry the Lion (duke 1142–1180), who also held Bavaria, founded Munich and Lübeck, and pushed colonisation east of the Elbe. His refusal to support Emperor Frederick Barbarossa's Italian campaign led to his imperial ban in 1180 and the dismemberment of the duchy: Westphalia went to the archbishop of Cologne, and the reduced ducal title passed to the Ascanian counts of Anhalt.

Partition and legacy

The shrunken Ascanian duchy, centred on Wittenberg and Lauenburg, was divided in 1296 into Saxe-Wittenberg and Saxe-Lauenburg. Saxe-Wittenberg received the Saxon electoral dignity under the Golden Bull of 1356, and its name — rather than the old duchy's geography — is the ancestor of the modern German state of Saxony, far to the southeast of the original Saxon homeland.
