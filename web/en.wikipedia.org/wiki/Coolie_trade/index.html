Coolie trade

The coolie trade was the system of transporting and employing indentured labourers — called coolies — from Asia, principally China and India, to colonies and countries in the Americas, Oceania, Africa, and Southeast Asia during the 19th and early 20th centuries. It expanded dramatically after the abolition of slavery in the British and French empires created demand for cheap plantation labour.

History

Beginning in the 1830s and 1840s, Indian workers were shipped to Mauritius, British Guiana, Trinidad, and Natal under indenture contracts, while Chinese labourers were recruited — often through Macao and Hong Kong — for the guano islands of Peru, the sugar plantations and railroads of Cuba, and later for mines, railways, and agriculture in North America and Australasia. Conditions on many voyages were brutal, with high mortality, and recruitment frequently involved deception or coercion; contemporaries sometimes called the system "a new system of slavery."

Regulation and end

Abuses led to regulation and eventual suppression: the United States prohibited American participation in the Chinese coolie trade in 1862, Britain tightened emigration rules, and the Portuguese authorities closed the Macao trade in 1874. Indian indentured emigration was suspended during the First World War and abolished in 1917–1920 following nationalist campaigns in India led by figures such as Mahatma Gandhi and C. F. Andrews.

Legacy

The trade created enduring Indo-Caribbean, Indo-Fijian, and Chinese diaspora communities across the world and remains a subject of historical research on labour migration, race, and empire. The word "coolie" itself is now regarded as a derogatory ethnic slur in many contexts.
