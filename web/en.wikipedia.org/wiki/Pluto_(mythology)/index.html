Pluto (mythology)

Pluto
God of the dead and wealth
Greek equivalent: Hades / Plouton
Consort: Proserpina
Siblings: Jupiter, Neptune, Juno, Ceres, Vesta

Pluto (Latin: Plūtō) is the Roman god of the underworld and of the dead, corresponding broadly to the Greek Hades, though the Roman Pluto absorbed characteristics of the Greek Plouton, a god of the earth's hidden wealth, including precious metals and fertile soil. His consort was Proserpina (Greek Persephone), whom he abducted from her mother Ceres to be his queen.

Mythology

After the defeat of the Titans, the three sons of Saturn — Jupiter, Neptune, and Pluto — divided the world by lot: Jupiter took the sky, Neptune the sea, and Pluto the underworld. In the most famous myth, Pluto emerged from the earth in his chariot and carried off Proserpina while she gathered flowers in Sicily; her mother's grief brought winter upon the earth until a compromise allowed Proserpina to spend part of the year above ground, explaining the seasons.

Cult and symbols

Pluto's symbols included the cornucopia, the sceptre, the two-pronged fork, and the three-headed dog Cerberus, guardian of the underworld's gates. Unlike the Olympian gods, Pluto had few temples and was propitiated chiefly to avert harm. The dwarf planet Pluto was named for him in 1930, continuing the tradition of naming planets after Roman gods.
