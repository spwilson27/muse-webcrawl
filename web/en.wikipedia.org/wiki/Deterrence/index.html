Deterrence

Nuclear-armed submarines underpin second-strike deterrence

Deterrence is the use of threats — by one party to another — to convince the other party to refrain from initiating some course of action. A threat serves as a deterrent to the extent that it convinces its target that the costs or risks of the action outweigh its benefits. The term is used in two main fields: criminology and penal theory, where deterrence is a justification for punishment; and international relations, where deterrence theory concerns the prevention of war, especially nuclear war.

Deterrence in criminal justice

In criminology, deterrence is the idea that punishment discourages crime, both by the individual punished (specific deterrence) and by others who observe the consequences (general deterrence). Classical theorists such as Cesare Beccaria and Jeremy Bentham held that punishment deters best when it is certain, swift and proportionate. Modern research generally finds that the certainty of apprehension deters more reliably than the severity of punishment.

Deterrence in international relations

Military deterrence rests on the threat of retaliation. It is conventionally divided into deterrence by denial — convincing an adversary that its objectives cannot be achieved — and deterrence by punishment — threatening costs so severe that the action is not worth taking. During the Cold War, nuclear deterrence theory was elaborated around second-strike capability and mutual assured destruction: if both sides retain the ability to retaliate after absorbing a first strike, neither can rationally begin a nuclear war. Strategists such as Bernard Brodie and Thomas Schelling developed the theory's concepts of credibility, signalling and escalation.

Criticism

Deterrence assumes rational decision-makers who accurately perceive threats and capabilities; critics note that misperception, accident, domestic politics and non-state actors can defeat those assumptions. In penology, critics question the evidence that harsh penalties reduce crime and raise moral objections to punishing one person to influence others.
