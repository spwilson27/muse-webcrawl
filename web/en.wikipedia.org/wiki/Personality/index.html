Personality
FieldPersonality psychology
Major modelsBig Five, Myers–Briggs, trait theory

Personality is any person's collection of interrelated behavioral, cognitive and emotional patterns that comprise a person's unique adjustment to life. These interrelated patterns are relatively stable, but can change over long time periods. Personality is influenced by a combination of genetic and environmental factors, and plays a key role in how individuals perceive and interact with the world.

Study

Personality psychology is the branch of psychology that studies personality and its variation among individuals. Approaches include trait theory, which describes personality in terms of measurable dimensions; psychoanalytic theory, originating with Sigmund Freud; humanistic theories associated with Abraham Maslow and Carl Rogers; and social-cognitive theories emphasising learning and situation.

Traits and models

The most widely researched model in contemporary personality psychology is the Big Five, which describes personality along five broad dimensions: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Other influential frameworks include Hans Eysenck's three-factor model, Raymond Cattell's 16 personality factors, and type-based instruments such as the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator, which remains popular despite criticism of its psychometric properties.

Development and change

Personality develops through childhood and adolescence and tends to become more stable in adulthood, though research shows meaningful change continues across the lifespan: people on average become more agreeable and conscientious and less neurotic with age, a pattern called the maturity principle. Temperament in infancy is considered an early precursor of adult personality traits.

Assessment

Personality is assessed through self-report questionnaires, informant reports, behavioural observation and projective tests. Personality measures are used in clinical diagnosis — including the classification of personality disorders — and in occupational and educational settings.
