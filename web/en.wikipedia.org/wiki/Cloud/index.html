Cloud

In meteorology, a cloud is an aerosol consisting of a visible mass of miniature liquid droplets, ice crystals, or other particles suspended in the atmosphere of a planetary body or similar space. Water or various other chemicals may compose the droplets and crystals. On Earth, clouds are formed as a result of saturation of the air when it is cooled to its dew point, or when it gains sufficient moisture from an adjacent source to raise the dew point to the ambient temperature.

Formation

Clouds form when moist air rises, expands, and cools adiabatically until it becomes saturated and water vapor condenses onto microscopic particles called cloud condensation nuclei. Rising motion can result from convection, frontal lifting, orographic lift over mountains, or convergence. If the air is cooled below freezing, ice crystals may form, and the mixture of supercooled droplets and ice drives precipitation processes.

Classification

The World Meteorological Organization classifies clouds into ten genera based on shape and altitude:

LevelGenera
High (5–13 km)Cirrus, cirrocumulus, cirrostratus
Middle (2–7 km)Altocumulus, altostratus, nimbostratus
Low (0–2 km)Stratus, stratocumulus, cumulus, cumulonimbus

Cumulonimbus, the towering thunderstorm cloud, can span all levels. Luke Howard first proposed a systematic nomenclature in 1802, using Latin names that survive in modified form today.

Role in climate

Clouds reflect sunlight back to space (a cooling effect) while also trapping outgoing infrared radiation (a warming effect). The net effect at present is a modest cooling, but how cloud cover will change with global warming is one of the largest sources of uncertainty in climate sensitivity estimates. Clouds are also essential to the water cycle, transporting and delivering precipitation.

Clouds on other planets

Venus is covered by sulfuric acid clouds; Mars has thin water-ice and carbon-dioxide clouds; the gas giants display banded ammonia clouds, and Titan has clouds of methane.
