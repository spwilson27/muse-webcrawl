Cypress

Cypress is a common name for various coniferous trees or shrubs from the Cupressus genus of the family Cupressaceae, typically found in warm-temperate and subtropical regions of Asia, Europe, and North America. The name also extends by usage to many other trees of the cypress family, including false cypresses (Chamaecyparis), bald cypress (Taxodium), and the swamp cypresses.

Description

True cypresses (Cupressus) are evergreen trees or large shrubs, reaching 5–40 metres tall. Their foliage grows in dense, scale-like sprays, and they bear small, round woody cones 8–40 mm long, with a few scales each holding numerous small seeds. Many species are adapted to fire, holding their seeds in closed cones for years until the parent tree is killed and the cones open — a trait known as serotiny.

Species and distribution

Depending on taxonomic treatment, the genus contains 17–30 species divided between Old World species of southern Europe and Asia — including the Mediterranean cypress (Cupressus sempervirens), famous for its narrow columnar form — and New World species of western North America, such as the Monterey cypress (Cupressus macrocarpa) of coastal California.

Uses and symbolism

Cypress wood is durable and aromatic, valued for construction, fence posts, and furniture; the trees themselves are widely planted as ornamentals, hedges, and windbreaks. In classical antiquity the cypress became a symbol of mourning, associated with the underworld and planted in cemeteries around the Mediterranean — an association that persists in graveyards today. In Persian gardens, the cypress (sarv) symbolises beauty and uprightness.
