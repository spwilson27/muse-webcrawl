Polyurethane

Polyurethane (often abbreviated PUR and PU) refers to a class of polymers composed of organic units joined by carbamate (urethane) links. In contrast to other common polymers such as polyethylene and polystyrene, polyurethanes are produced from a wide range of starting materials, giving materials that range from flexible foams to rigid plastics and elastomers.

Chemistry

Polyurethanes are formed by reacting a diisocyanate or polymeric isocyanate with a polyol in the presence of suitable catalysts and additives. The chemistry was pioneered by Otto Bayer and co-workers at IG Farben in Germany in 1937.

Applications

The main uses of polyurethane are flexible and rigid foams for furniture, bedding, automotive seating, and insulation panels; elastomers for wheels and seals; coatings, adhesives, and sealants; and synthetic fibres such as spandex.

Health and environment

Fully reacted polyurethane polymer is chemically inert, but isocyanate precursors are potent respiratory sensitisers, and foams can burn to produce toxic gases including hydrogen cyanide. Recycling and recovery of polyurethane waste remains an active area of research.
