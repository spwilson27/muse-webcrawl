Saltern

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[hero image: Saltern]
Salt evaporation ponds

A saltern is an area or installation for making salt by the evaporation of seawater or brine, typically a series of shallow ponds through which water is channelled and progressively concentrated until salt crystallises. Salterns were historically of great economic importance, and the debris of ancient salt-making — heaps of fired clay and briquetage known in England as "red hills" — is a notable feature of Iron Age and Roman archaeology on coasts such as those of Essex and Lincolnshire.

History

Salt production by evaporation is among the oldest chemical industries. In Britain, salterns flourished from the Iron Age through the medieval period; the Domesday Book records hundreds of salt pans, particularly around the Wash and the Droitwich brine springs inland. The term survives in place names such as Salterns in Hampshire and in the Portsmouth harbour area. In medieval England coastal salterns used sand- and peat-firing to concentrate brine before boiling ("sleeching"), while from the early modern period solar evaporation works dominated in sunnier climates.

Modern salterns

Major surviving solar salterns include those of Guérande and the Île de Ré in France, Trapani in Sicily, the Sečovlje Salina in Slovenia, and the salinas of San Francisco Bay and Baja California. Such sites are also ecologically significant: the colourful series of ponds, coloured by algae and brine shrimp, supports flamingos and other wading birds, and several former salterns are now protected wetlands managed for wildlife as much as for the prized hand-harvested fleur de sel and sea salt they still produce.
