A jackscrew, or screw jack, is a mechanical device used as a lifting device to lift heavy loads or to apply great forces. A mechanical jack employs a screw thread for lifting heavy equipment; power is applied by turning a leadscrew. In aviation, the term refers specifically to the screw mechanism that drives the trimmable horizontal stabilizer of an aircraft.

Mechanical screw jacks

A screw jack converts rotational motion into linear motion and provides a large mechanical advantage, allowing a modest input torque to lift many tonnes. Because the screw thread is typically a high-friction Acme or square thread, the jack is self-locking: the load does not cause the screw to rotate backwards, so the load stays in place when the input torque is removed. Screw jacks are used in house lifting, bridge work, theatre stage lifts, and industrial positioning.

Aircraft jackscrew

Many airliners move their entire horizontal stabilizer for pitch trim using a jackscrew driven by electric motors and commanded by the flight crew or autopilot. The design received intense scrutiny after accidents in which jackscrew threads failed due to inadequate lubrication — most prominently Alaska Airlines Flight 261 in 2000, where wear and stripping of the Acme nut threads caused the stabilizer to jam and then fail, leading to loss of control. The accident prompted extensive changes in maintenance and inspection requirements for jackscrew assemblies.

Comparison with other jacks

Unlike hydraulic jacks, screw jacks need no seals or fluids and hold position without power, but they lift more slowly and are subject to thread wear. Ball screw variants reduce friction and are used where backdriving or high duty cycles are acceptable with a brake.
