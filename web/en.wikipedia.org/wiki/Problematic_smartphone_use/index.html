Problematic smartphone use

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

StatusNot a standalone diagnosis in DSM-5 or ICD-11

Problematic smartphone use is the excessive or compulsive use of a smartphone that interferes with a person's daily life, work, relationships, sleep or health. It is sometimes called "smartphone addiction", although it is not recognised as a formal diagnosis in either the DSM-5 or the ICD-11, and researchers debate whether the problem lies in the device itself or in the applications — social media, gaming and video — accessed through it.

Signs and correlates

Reported features include an inability to cut down despite trying, preoccupation with the phone, withdrawal-like anxiety when separated from it ("nomophobia"), tolerance (needing ever more use), use in hazardous situations such as driving, and continued use despite harm. Studies associate heavy problematic use with poor sleep quality, depression, anxiety, stress, and lower academic performance, though the direction of causation is disputed: distressed people may use phones more, rather than phones causing the distress.

Prevalence

Estimates vary widely depending on the scale used; a widely cited 2021 meta-analysis of studies between 2014 and 2020 estimated that roughly one in four children and young people showed problematic smartphone use, with rates in individual studies ranging from under 10% to over 50%.

Management

Proposed responses include cognitive behavioural therapy adapted from treatment for behavioural addictions, screen-time and notification management built into operating systems, school phone restrictions, and treatment of underlying conditions such as anxiety and depression. Critics caution against pathologising ordinary behaviour and note that "screen time" is a crude measure that ignores what the phone is being used for.
