Intelligence testing

Intelligence testing refers to the administration of standardized psychometric tests designed to measure a person's intellectual capabilities, typically summarized as an intelligence quotient (IQ). Such tests are used in education, clinical psychology, employment screening, and research.

Matrix reasoning items, like those in Raven's Progressive Matrices, are common in intelligence tests.

History

In 1905 Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon developed the Binet–Simon scale to identify Parisian schoolchildren needing special help. Lewis Terman of Stanford University revised the scale in 1916 as the Stanford–Binet test and popularized the intelligence quotient, computed as mental age divided by chronological age, multiplied by 100. During World War I, Robert Yerkes led the development of the Army Alpha and Beta tests, administered to nearly two million recruits.

David Wechsler introduced deviation IQ scoring and his Wechsler–Bellevue scale in 1939, the ancestor of the WAIS and WISC. Deviation IQ compares an individual's score to the distribution of scores in their age group, with a mean of 100 and standard deviation of 15.

Major tests

  • Wechsler scales — WAIS (adults), WISC (children), WPPSI (preschool); yield Verbal Comprehension, Perceptual Reasoning, Working Memory, and Processing Speed indices plus a Full Scale IQ.
  • Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales — currently in its fifth edition, measuring five factors including fluid reasoning and knowledge.
  • Raven's Progressive Matrices — nonverbal test of abstract reasoning, designed to minimize cultural and linguistic bias.
  • Cattell Culture Fair — nonverbal tests intended to reduce cultural loading.
  • Woodcock–Johnson Tests of Cognitive Abilities — based on the Cattell–Horn–Carroll model.

Interpretation

IQ scores are normally distributed; roughly 68% of people score between 85 and 115, and about 2.5% score above 130 or below 70. Scores are periodically re-normed; the Flynn effect documented gains of roughly 3 points per decade across the 20th century in many countries, with evidence of stagnation or reversal in some recent studies.

Uses and criticism

Intelligence tests are used to diagnose intellectual disability, identify gifted students, assess cognitive impairment after brain injury, and in some selection contexts. Critics point to cultural bias, the narrowness of what tests measure, coaching and practice effects, and the historical misuse of testing in eugenics programs. Test publishers employ bias review and differential item functioning analyses to mitigate these concerns.
