Zerodur is a lithium-aluminosilicate glass-ceramic produced by the German glass company Schott AG. Renowned for an extremely low coefficient of thermal expansion — in the highest grades, essentially zero over relevant temperature ranges — it is the material of choice for large telescope mirror blanks and other applications where dimensional stability under temperature change is critical. The name is a contraction of "zero durability expansion".

Composition and properties

Zerodur is a two-phase material: nanometre-scale crystalline particles with a negative thermal expansion are embedded in a residual glassy matrix with a positive expansion, so that the composite's net expansion can be tuned to near zero. It is homogeneous, non-porous, polishable to optical quality, and can be coated with reflective aluminium like ordinary mirror glass. Schott classifies it by expansion tolerance: Class 0 guarantees a mean coefficient within 0 ± 0.02 × 10−6 per kelvin between 0 °C and 50 °C.

Applications

The first great Zerodur casting was the 4.2 m mirror of the Max Planck Institute's telescope at Calar Alto Observatory in the early 1970s. Subsequent blanks include the mirrors of the Keck telescopes' successors, the four 8.2 m unit-telescope mirrors of the Very Large Telescope, the 10.4 m primary of the Gran Telescopio Canarias — assembled from hexagonal Zerodur segments — and segments cast for the Extremely Large Telescope's 39-metre primary. Outside astronomy, Zerodur serves in ring laser gyroscopes, semiconductor lithography stages, precision measurement frames and etalons.

Manufacture

Blanks are cast from a melt, then subjected to a lengthy controlled heat treatment ("ceramisation") that grows the crystalline phase uniformly through the blank; large pieces may take many months to produce and anneal. The material competes with other ultra-low-expansion materials such as Corning's ULE titanium silicate glass and the glass-ceramic Clearceram.
