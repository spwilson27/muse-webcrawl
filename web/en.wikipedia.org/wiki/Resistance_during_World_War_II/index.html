Resistance during World War II

Resistance movements during World War II occurred in every occupied country by a variety of means, ranging from non-cooperation and disinformation, to espionage, to direct warfare and the recapture of towns. Resistance cells included the armed underground (often called partisans), secret press, intelligence networks, escape lines for downed Allied airmen and persecuted people, and acts of sabotage against the Axis occupation forces. Many movements were supported by the Allied governments-in-exile, the British Special Operations Executive (SOE) and the American Office of Strategic Services (OSS).

Forms of resistance

Resistance took many forms: publication of underground newspapers and forbidden literature; intelligence gathering for the Allies; sabotage of railways, factories and communications; guerrilla warfare in mountains and forests; strikes and civil disobedience; hiding Jews, escaped prisoners of war and Allied airmen; and participation in uprisings as Allied forces approached. Rescuers and couriers, many of them women, sustained these networks under threat of arrest, deportation and execution.

By country

In Poland, the Home Army (Armia Krajowa), loyal to the government-in-exile, became one of the largest underground movements in Europe, culminating in the Warsaw Uprising of 1944. In France, resistance groups gradually united under Jean Moulin and the National Council of the Resistance, contributing intelligence for D-Day and rising in 1944. In Yugoslavia, Tito's Partisans waged a large-scale guerrilla war and liberated much of the country largely by their own efforts. In the Soviet Union, partisan units behind German lines disrupted supply routes on a vast scale.

Elsewhere, the Greek, Italian, Dutch, Belgian, Norwegian, Danish, Czech, Filipino, Korean, Chinese and many other movements resisted Axis occupation in widely varying conditions. Jewish resistance included ghetto uprisings, most famously the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of 1943, partisan units, and escape and rescue networks. Within Germany itself, groups such as the White Rose and the military conspirators of the 20 July 1944 plot opposed the Nazi regime.

Effectiveness and cost

Historians debate the military weight of resistance, but agree that movements gathered crucial intelligence, tied down Axis garrisons, sustained national morale and governments' legitimacy, and helped restore liberated states. Occupiers responded with brutal reprisals — hostage shootings, village massacres such as Lidice and Oradour-sur-Glane, and mass deportations — killing hundreds of thousands of resisters and civilians.
