Ground-penetrating radar

Ground-penetrating radar (GPR) is a geophysical method that uses radar pulses to image the subsurface. It is a non-intrusive method of surveying below the surface of the ground, used to investigate underground utilities, archaeological features, voids and changes in material properties.

Principles

A GPR transmitter emits short pulses of high-frequency radio waves (typically 10 MHz to several GHz) into the ground. When the pulse encounters a boundary between materials with different dielectric properties, part of the energy is reflected back to a receiver antenna. Recording the travel time and amplitude of reflections as the antennas move along a survey line produces a profile (radargram) of subsurface structures. Lower frequencies penetrate deeper but give coarser resolution; higher frequencies give finer detail over shallower depths, generally from a few tens of centimetres to roughly 15 metres in favourable soils. Conductive soils such as wet clay and salt water strongly attenuate the signal.

Applications

GPR is used in archaeology to locate buried structures and graves without excavation, in construction and engineering to detect buried utilities, rebar and voids, in geology and glaciology to profile strata and ice thickness, in environmental work to find buried tanks and contamination, and by police and forensic teams to search for clandestine burials. Variants of the technology have been used to sound the interiors of planetary bodies.
