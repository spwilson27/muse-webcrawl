Cutting fluid

Cutting fluid is a type of coolant and lubricant designed specifically for metalworking processes, such as machining and stamping. There are various kinds of cutting fluids, which include oils, oil-water emulsions, pastes, gels, aerosols (mists), and air or other gases. Cutting fluids are made from petroleum distillates, animal fats, plant oils, water and air, or other raw ingredients. Depending on context and on which type of cutting fluid is being considered, it may be referred to as cutting fluid, cutting oil, cutting compound, coolant, or lubricant.

Functions

Most metal cutting processes generate significant heat at the interface between the cutting tool and the workpiece, and friction quickly wears tool edges. Cutting fluid serves several functions at once:

  • Cooling the workpiece and tool, carrying away heat to preserve tool hardness and dimensional accuracy.
  • Lubrication of the chip-tool interface, reducing friction, cutting forces, and built-up edge.
  • Flushing chips and swarf away from the cutting zone.
  • Corrosion protection of the finished surface and machine.

Types

Straight oils are undiluted petroleum or vegetable oils, used where lubrication matters more than cooling, such as in heavy broaching or thread cutting. Soluble oils form milky emulsions when mixed with water and are the most widely used general-purpose coolants. Semi-synthetic and fully synthetic fluids contain little or no oil and offer strong cooling and cleanliness for high-speed machining and grinding. Delivery ranges from simple flood application to high-pressure through-tool jets and minimum-quantity lubrication (MQL) mists.

Health and disposal

Skin contact and inhalation of mists can cause dermatitis and respiratory irritation, and spent fluids require regulated disposal because of their oil content and microbial growth in sumps. Modern formulations increasingly use biodegradable vegetable-oil bases.
