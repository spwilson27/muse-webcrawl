Darjeeling tea

Darjeeling tea is a tea made from the leaves of Camellia sinensis var. sinensis grown and processed in the Darjeeling and Kalimpong districts of West Bengal, India. Renowned for its muscatel flavour — a distinctive musky spiciness — it is often called "the Champagne of teas". Since 2004, "Darjeeling tea" has held geographical indication protection in India, the first Indian product so registered; it is also protected in the European Union.

Production

Tea was first planted in the Darjeeling hills in the 1840s under British colonial administration, using seed and plants brought from China. The district's ~87 gardens lie at elevations of roughly 600 to 2,000 metres, where cool temperatures, mist, and well-drained slopes shape the leaf's character. Leaves are harvested in seasonal "flushes": the delicate first flush (spring), the more full-bodied second flush (early summer, the source of the prized muscatel note), the monsoon flush, and the autumn flush. Most Darjeeling tea is processed as black tea, though oolong and white styles are also produced.

Adulteration and protection

Far more tea is sold worldwide as "Darjeeling" than the district produces — annual output is around 8–10 million kilograms, while sales under the name have been estimated at several times that. The Tea Board of India's GI certification and logo system, and licensing of the word mark, aim to combat counterfeiting.
