Textual criticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Textual criticism is a branch of textual scholarship, philology, and literary criticism that is concerned with the identification of textual variants, or different versions, of either manuscripts or printed books. Such texts may range in dates from the earliest writing in cuneiform, hieroglyphics, or alphabetic scripts on clay, stone, parchment, papyrus, or paper, to the present time.

Textual critics aim to reconstruct, as accurately as possible, texts as their authors originally wrote them, eliminating errors introduced by copyists over centuries of manual transmission and assessing the relationships among the surviving witnesses to a text. The discipline is essential to the editing of classical literature, the Bible, Shakespeare, and other works whose originals do not survive.

Basic principles

The process of textual criticism typically involves:

  • Recensio — collecting and examining all known witnesses (manuscripts, early editions, quotations) to the text.
  • Examinatio — evaluating the variants to determine which readings are original and which are corruptions.
  • Emendatio — correcting errors in the archetype, sometimes by conjecture (emendatio ope ingenii) where all witnesses are corrupt.

A key tool is the stemmatics method associated with Karl Lachmann: witnesses that share distinctive errors are grouped into families, and a genealogical tree (stemma codicum) is constructed leading back to a lost archetype. Shared errors thus allow the critic to eliminate manuscripts copied from surviving exemplars and to weigh, rather than merely count, the evidence for each reading. Guiding principles such as lectio difficilior potior ("the more difficult reading is stronger") and lectio brevior potior ("the shorter reading is stronger") help decide between competing variants, since copyists tended to simplify and harmonise rather than to complicate.

History

Textual criticism was practised in the ancient world: scholars at the Library of Alexandria, such as Zenodotus and Aristarchus, edited Homer using critical signs to mark suspected lines. Renaissance humanists — most famously Lorenzo Valla, who exposed the Donation of Constantine as a forgery, and Desiderius Erasmus, who produced an edition of the Greek New Testament — revived systematic criticism of texts. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, classical scholars such as Richard Bentley and Karl Lachmann formalised the discipline, while New Testament critics including Johann Jakob Griesbach and later Brooke Foss Westcott and Fenton John Anthony Hort developed genealogical methods for the thousands of surviving Greek manuscripts.

Approaches and debates

Twentieth-century scholarship challenged mechanical stemmatics: Joseph Bédier argued that the method produced implausibly tidy trees, and advocateded editing a single "best text" instead. The Anglo-American tradition of copy-text editing, associated with W. W. Greg and Fredson Bowers, distinguishes between "substantives" (the author's words) and "accidentals" (spelling and punctuation), using a chosen copy-text for the latter. More recently, the "New Philology" has questioned the goal of reconstructing a single original text at all, treating variance (French mouvance) as an inherent feature of medieval textual culture. Digital methods — computerised collation, phylogenetic analysis borrowed from evolutionary biology — are now widely used alongside traditional judgement.

Applications

Textual criticism underpins critical editions of the Hebrew Bible and New Testament (such as the Nestle–Aland Greek text), of classical authors from Homer to Virgil, of medieval literature, and of early modern authors such as Shakespeare, whose plays survive in differing quarto and folio versions. It also informs forensic and legal analysis of documents.
