Son cubano

Son cubano is a genre of music and dance that originated in the highlands of eastern Cuba during the late 19th century. It is a syncretic genre that blends elements of Spanish and African origin. Among its fundamental Hispanic components are the vocal style, lyrical metre and the primacy of the tres, a three-course chordophone derived from the Spanish guitar; its characteristic African elements are the clave rhythm, call-and-response structure and percussion instruments.

A classic septeto performing son cubano

History

Son emerged in Oriente province (around Santiago de Cuba, Guantánamo and Baracoa) from rural traditions such as changüí and nengón. Brought to Havana by soldiers and musicians around 1909–1916, it was initially marginalised as lower-class music before radio and recordings spread it nationally in the 1920s. Ensembles evolved from tríos to sextetos (adding bongó, maracas, claves and botija/marímbula bass) and septetos (adding trumpet, famously Ignacio Piñeiro's Septeto Nacional, founded 1927).

Structure and rhythm

Son is built on the five-stroke son clave pattern in 2-3 or 3-2 orientation. Songs typically alternate a composed verse section (largo) with a faster montuno section of improvised call-and-response singing over repeating piano- or tres-driven vamps — the template later inherited by salsa.

Influence

In the 1930s–40s, Arsenio Rodríguez expanded the conjunto format with conga drums, piano and multiple trumpets, laying foundations for mambo and New York salsa. The 1990s Buena Vista Social Club project revived international interest in traditional son, spotlighting veterans such as Compay Segundo and Ibrahim Ferrer.
