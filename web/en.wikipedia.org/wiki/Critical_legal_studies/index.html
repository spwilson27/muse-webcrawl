Critical legal studies

Critical legal studies (CLS) is a school of legal theory that emerged in the United States in the 1970s, arguing that law is neither neutral nor autonomous but is shaped by politics, social power, and ideology, and that legal doctrine serves to legitimise existing hierarchies. Its adherents, sometimes called "crits," developed the argument that legal rules are indeterminate — capable of justifying opposing outcomes — and that supposedly free legal reasoning masks choices favouring the status quo.

CLS originated in elite American law schools in the 1970s.

History

The movement coalesced with the founding of the Conference on Critical Legal Studies in 1977, drawing scholars such as Roberto Unger, Duncan Kennedy, and Morton Horwitz, mainly at Harvard and Yale law schools. It inherited from the American legal realists of the 1920s–30s — who had stressed that judges make law — and from Marxist and Frankfurt School thought. Kennedy's pamphlets and disputes over faculty appointments at Harvard in the 1980s made CLS one of the most controversial movements in legal academia.

Core claims

  • Indeterminacy — legal materials underdetermine results; doctrines contain contradictory principles that can be deployed on either side.
  • Law as politics — adjudication is a form of political decision-making, not neutral deduction.
  • Legitimation — the appearance of legal neutrality makes unequal social arrangements seem natural and just.
  • "Trashing" — a critical method of exposing contradictions within legal doctrines and texts.

Influence and offshoots

Although CLS as an organised movement faded by the early 1990s, its methods fed into successor movements including critical race theory, feminist legal theory, and critical approaches to international law. Critics charged the movement with nihilism, excessive abstraction, and offering no constructive programme — charges its proponents answered in varying ways, with Unger proposing an elaborate programme of institutional reconstruction.
