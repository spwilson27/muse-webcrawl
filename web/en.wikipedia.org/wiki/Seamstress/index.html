Seamstress

A seamstress is a woman who sews, especially one who makes, alters or repairs garments as an occupation; the corresponding term for a man is seamster, while sewer is gender-neutral. The word derives from Old English seam plus the feminine agent suffix -stress, and is recorded from the 17th century.

History

Before industrialisation, sewing clothing was largely domestic work and a central part of women's labour. In the 19th century, seamstresses became a large urban workforce, often working long hours for low piece-rate pay in workshops and tenements — conditions exposed by Thomas Hood's 1843 poem "The Song of the Shirt" and by parliamentary inquiries in Britain. The spread of the sewing machine, patented in practical form in the 1840s–50s, transformed the trade, enabling factory garment production while also increasing output expectations.

Modern trade

Today seamstresses work in alterations and repair shops, bespoke tailoring, costume departments for theatre and film, bridal and formalwear, and the sample rooms of fashion houses, where skilled machinists construct prototype garments from designers' patterns.
