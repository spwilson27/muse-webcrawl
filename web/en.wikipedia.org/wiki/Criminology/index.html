Criminology is the interdisciplinary study of crime and deviant behavior — its causes, the making and breaking of laws, victims, and societal reactions including policing, courts, punishment, and prevention. Criminologists draw on sociology, psychology, law, economics, and statistics, and their findings inform criminal-justice policy debates worldwide.

History and schools of thought

The classical school of the Enlightenment — Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764) and Jeremy Bentham — treated offenders as rational actors and argued for proportionate, certain punishment and legal reform. The 19th-century positivist school, led by Italian physician Cesare Lombroso, sought scientific causes of criminality; Lombroso's theory of the "born criminal" identified by physical atavism is now discredited, but his empirical turn founded the discipline. In the 20th century, the Chicago School mapped crime onto urban social disorganization; Robert Merton's strain theory blamed blocked legitimate opportunity; social-control and learning theories (Hirschi; Sutherland's differential association) emphasized bonds and peer influence; and labeling theory showed how official reaction can deepen deviance. Later movements include rational-choice and routine-activity theories, developmental and life-course criminology, feminist and critical criminology, and green criminology.

Research methods and data

Criminologists use official statistics (e.g., the FBI's Uniform Crime Reports and the National Crime Victimization Survey in the U.S.), self-report surveys, cohort studies, field observation, and experiments. Persistent issues include the "dark figure" of unreported crime, measurement differences between police data and victimization surveys, and ecological fallacies. The late-20th-century crime drop in many Western countries — unexplained by any single factor — remains a major research puzzle, with candidate causes ranging from policing and incarceration to lead exposure and demographics.

Subfields and applications

Victimology studies victims' experiences and rights; penology addresses punishment and corrections; comparative criminology examines cross-national variation; and biosocial and psychological criminology study individual risk factors. Applied criminology evaluates interventions — from hot-spots policing to restorative justice — and has become central to debates about mass incarceration, deterrence, and rehabilitation.
