Screening (medicine)

In medicine, screening is a strategy used to look for as-yet-unrecognised conditions or risk markers in a population. It is a form of secondary prevention applied to apparently healthy individuals, with the aim of identifying disease at an early, more treatable stage.

Principles

The classic criteria for a worthwhile screening programme, set out by Wilson and Jungner for the World Health Organization in 1968, include that the condition should be an important health problem, that there should be an accepted treatment for patients with recognised disease, that facilities for diagnosis and treatment should be available, and that there should be a recognisable latent or early symptomatic stage. The test should be acceptable to the population and the cost of case-finding balanced against overall medical expenditure.

Examples

Common screening programmes include mammography for breast cancer, Pap tests and HPV testing for cervical cancer, faecal occult blood testing and colonoscopy for colorectal cancer, newborn screening for metabolic disorders such as phenylketonuria, and screening for hypertension and abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Benefits and harms

Screening can reduce morbidity and mortality but carries risks: false positives cause anxiety and further invasive testing, false negatives give false reassurance, and overdiagnosis detects conditions that would never have caused harm. Evaluation of programmes therefore weighs lead-time bias, length bias and overall population benefit.
