Oiran

Oiran (花魁) were courtesans in Japan during the Edo period (1600–1868). The oiran were considered a type of yūjo (遊女, "woman of pleasure") and were distinguished from ordinary yūjo in that they were high-ranking courtesans, renowned for their skills in the traditional arts, dance, music, and conversation, as well as their elaborate dress and hairstyles.

History

Oiran worked within the licensed pleasure quarters, most famously the Yoshiwara district of Edo (modern Tokyo). Unlike geisha, who primarily entertained through art and conversation, oiran were high-class prostitutes; however, their rank meant they could refuse clients and were sought after by wealthy patrons. Prospective clients underwent elaborate rituals and negotiations, and an oiran's services were extremely expensive.

Appearance and procession

Oiran wore multiple layered kimono, tall black lacquered geta (koma geta), and elaborate hairstyles decorated with many kanzashi hair ornaments. The oiran dōchū, the slow procession of an oiran through the quarter accompanied by attendants, became a celebrated spectacle and is still re-enacted at festivals today.

Decline

The world of the oiran declined in the late Edo and Meiji periods as geisha grew in popularity, and the institution effectively ended with the Prostitution Prevention Law of 1956.
