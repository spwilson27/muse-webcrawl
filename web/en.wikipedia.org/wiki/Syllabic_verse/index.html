Syllabic verse

Syllabic verse
BasisFixed syllable count per line
Common inFrench, Japanese, Welsh poetry
English practitionersMarianne Moore, Dylan Thomas, Thom Gunn

Syllabic verse is a poetic form in which the metre is determined by the number of syllables in each line rather than by the number or pattern of stresses. It is the dominant principle of versification in languages such as French and Japanese, whose prosody does not rest on strong stress contrasts, and it has been adopted experimentally by a number of poets writing in English.

In different traditions

French classical verse is built on fixed syllable counts, most famously the twelve-syllable alexandrine. Japanese forms such as the haiku (5–7–5) and tanka (5–7–5–7–7) count sound units (on, roughly syllables). Welsh strict-metre poetry (cynghanedd) also works by syllable counting, typically seven syllables per line combined with intricate sound correspondence.

In English

Because English verse is normally accentual or accentual-syllabic, syllabic verse in English can sound indistinguishable from free verse to the ear, since the listener cannot readily perceive the count. Marianne Moore built elaborate stanza forms in which each stanza repeats an identical, arbitrary syllable pattern. Dylan Thomas used counted syllables in poems such as "Poem in October" and in his villanelle-like structures; Thom Gunn and Elizabeth Daryush also wrote syllabics. Robert Bridges experimented with syllabic metres based on his theories of English pronunciation.
