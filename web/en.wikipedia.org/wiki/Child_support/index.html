Child support

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Child support (or child maintenance) is an ongoing, periodic payment made by a parent for the financial benefit of a child (or parent/guardian) following the end of a marriage or similar relationship — in most systems owed by the non-resident parent to the parent with care, calculated under statutory guidelines that take income, number of children and time-sharing as inputs (formula-based administrative assessment in many countries; court-set guideline orders in the United States, where federal law since the 1980s requires state guidelines and supports a vast enforcement apparatus).

Child support is legally the child's right, enforced by wage withholding, tax-refund intercepts, licence suspensions and, controversially, imprisonment for contempt — a practice critics say jails indigent fathers for poverty (the US Supreme Court in Turner v. Rogers, 2011, imposed procedural safeguards without recognising a right to counsel). Non-payment is widespread: only a fraction of ordered support is collected in full in many jurisdictions (in the US, custodial parents receive on average roughly half to two-thirds of what is owed in Census Bureau survey data). International recovery runs through the 2007 Hague Child Support Convention and reciprocal arrangements. The policy debate balances children's poverty (maintenance measurably reduces it) against low-income non-resident parents' capacity and contact incentives.
