Watergate scandal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C., at dusk
The Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C., at dusk

The Watergate scandal was a political scandal in the United States involving the administration of President Richard Nixon from 1972 to 1974. It began with the arrest of five men breaking into the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C., on 17 June 1972, and ended with Nixon becoming the only US president to resign from office.

Break-in and cover-up

The burglars were linked to Nixon's re-election organisation, CREEP, and to White House figures including E. Howard Hunt and G. Gordon Liddy. The administration denied involvement and paid hush money; reporting by Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein of The Washington Post, guided by the source 'Deep Throat' (revealed in 2005 as FBI associate director Mark Felt), kept the story alive through Nixon's landslide re-election.

Investigations and the tapes

A Senate select committee under Sam Ervin opened televised hearings in 1973; aide Alexander Butterfield revealed Nixon's secret taping system. Special prosecutor Archibald Cox subpoenaed the tapes; Nixon's firing of Cox in the 'Saturday Night Massacre' (October 1973) triggered a public backlash. After the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in United States v. Nixon (July 1974), the 'smoking gun' tape proved Nixon had approved the cover-up days after the break-in.

Resignation and aftermath

Facing certain impeachment, Nixon resigned on 9 August 1974; his successor Gerald Ford pardoned him a month later. Forty-eight people were convicted, including former attorney general John N. Mitchell. The scandal produced reforms — the Ethics in Government Act, amended campaign-finance law, and the suffix '-gate' for every scandal since — and permanently damaged public trust in government.
