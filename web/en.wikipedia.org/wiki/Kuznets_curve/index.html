Kuznets curve

The Kuznets curve expresses a hypothesis advanced by economist Simon Kuznets in the 1950s and 1960s. According to this hypothesis, as an economy develops, market forces first increase and then decrease overall economic inequality. The relationship is conventionally drawn as an inverted U: inequality rises in the early stages of industrialization and urbanization, then falls as a country becomes wealthier and develops a welfare state, mass education and redistribution through taxation.

Origins

Kuznets proposed the idea in his 1954 presidential address to the American Economic Association, published in 1955 as "Economic Growth and Income Inequality" in the American Economic Review. Drawing on limited historical data for the United States, the United Kingdom and Germany, he suggested that the shift of labour from low-productivity, relatively equal agriculture into higher-productivity, initially unequal industry raises inequality at first. In later stages, legislated redistribution, declining returns to capital concentration, and the political weight of urban populations push inequality down. Kuznets himself cautioned that the work was largely speculative — "perhaps 5 per cent empirical information and 95 per cent speculation".

Criticism and later work

Empirical tests have produced mixed results. Cross-country studies in the 1970s and 1980s often appeared to support the curve, but later panel-data work found the inverted-U relationship fragile and sensitive to the countries included. Critics such as Thomas Piketty have argued that mid-20th-century declines in inequality in rich countries owed more to wars, taxation policy and shocks than to any automatic stage of development, and that inequality rose again in many advanced economies after 1980. Anand and Kanbur and others showed that results depend heavily on the functional form and data set used.

Related concepts

The environmental Kuznets curve adapts the idea to pollution: environmental degradation is hypothesized to rise and then fall with income per capita. A "Great Gatsby curve" describes a cross-country association between inequality and intergenerational mobility, and is unrelated in mechanism though similar in name.
