Microbrewery

A microbrewery or craft brewery is a brewery that produces small amounts of beer, typically much smaller than large-scale corporate breweries, and is independently owned. Such breweries are generally characterized by their emphasis on quality, flavour, and traditional brewing methods.

Definitions

In the United States, the Brewers Association defines a "craft brewer" as small (annual production of 6 million barrels of beer or less), independent (less than 25 percent owned or controlled by a beverage alcohol industry member that is not itself a craft brewer), and traditional. The term "microbrewery" is more specifically applied to breweries producing fewer than 15,000 US beer barrels (approximately 1.8 million litres) per year.

History

The modern microbrewery movement began in the United Kingdom in the 1970s, where the term described a new generation of small breweries focused on traditional cask ale, encouraged by the Campaign for Real Ale (CAMRA). The movement spread to the United States, where homebrewing legalization in 1978 and pioneers such as Anchor Brewing, Sierra Nevada Brewing Company, and Boston Beer Company sparked rapid growth. From a handful of breweries in the early 1980s, the number of craft breweries in the US grew to many thousands by the 2020s.

Characteristics and styles

Microbreweries frequently experiment with styles and ingredients, reviving historical styles (such as India pale ale, porter, and sour beers) and creating new ones. Many sell much of their output locally through taprooms and brewpubs, fostering direct relationships with customers.

Related terms

A brewpub is a pub or restaurant that brews beer on the premises. A nanobrewery is an even smaller operation, often producing batches of a few barrels at a time. Farmhouse breweries, contract brewing, and regional craft breweries occupy other niches of the sector.
