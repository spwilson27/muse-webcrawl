Obedience training

Obedience training (placeholder image)

Obedience training usually refers to the training of a dog and the term is most commonly used in that context. It ranges from basic training, such as teaching the dog to respond to basic commands such as "sit", "down", "come", and "stay", to high-level competition within clubs such as the American Kennel Club, the United Kennel Club and the Canadian Kennel Club, where additional commands, accuracy and performance are scored and judged.

Whether a dog is trained for basic pet obedience or for competitive sport, the underlying principles derive from learning theory: behaviours that are reinforced tend to be repeated, and behaviours that are not reinforced tend to fade. Modern trainers commonly use reward-based methods such as food, play, and marker (clicker) training, while older traditions relied more heavily on corrections.

History

Formal dog training grew out of military and police work with dogs in the early twentieth century. Konrad Most's Training Dogs (1910) is an early influential text. Obedience competition was formalised in the 1930s, when the American Kennel Club developed obedience trials as a sport open to pet owners.

Competitive obedience

In obedience trials, dog and handler teams perform a prescribed set of exercises — heeling, recalls, stays, retrieving, and scent discrimination among them — and are scored against an ideal performance. Titles are awarded at novice, open, and utility levels of increasing difficulty.
