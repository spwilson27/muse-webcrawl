Garamantes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Garamantes were an ancient Saharan people of Berber origin who established a prosperous kingdom in the Fezzan region of present-day Libya. Emerging around 1000 BC, their civilization flourished from about 500 BC to AD 700, built on an extensive underground irrigation system — the foggaras — that tapped fossil water to farm the desert.

Society and economy

Centered on the capital Garama (modern Germa), the Garamantes built towns, temples and more than 600 miles of foggara tunnels, cultivating wheat, barley, grapes, figs and dates and raising livestock. They controlled trans-Saharan trade routes carrying gold, ivory, wild animals and enslaved people north to the Mediterranean, and imported Roman wine, oil and pottery. Greek and Roman writers, from Herodotus onward, portrayed them as formidable charioteers and raiders; Rome campaigned against them repeatedly and concluded treaties under Cornelius Balbus's expedition of 19 BC.

Decline

Garamantian power waned as groundwater levels fell, making the foggaras ever costlier to extend, and as camel-borne trade shifted routes. Their kingdom faded in the early medieval period, absorbed into the successor states of the Sahara. Archaeological work, notably by David Mattingly's Fazzan Project, has revised the classical image of a marginal desert tribe, revealing an urbanized, literate trading state with its own script derived from Tifinagh.
