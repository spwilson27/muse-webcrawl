Sally Clark

Sally Clark (August 1964 – 15 March 2007) was an English solicitor who became the victim of a notorious miscarriage of justice. After the sudden deaths of her two infant sons, Christopher in 1996 and Harry in 1998, she was prosecuted for their murder, convicted in 1999, and imprisoned. Her conviction was overturned on appeal in 2003 after it emerged that exculpatory pathology evidence had been withheld and that statistical evidence presented at trial was seriously misleading. The case fundamentally changed the handling of sudden infant death prosecutions in the United Kingdom.

The trial

At trial, paediatrician Professor Sir Roy Meadow testified that the chance of two natural cot deaths in an affluent family was "one in 73 million", squaring the estimated frequency of a single sudden infant death as if the events were independent. The Royal Statistical Society issued a public statement criticising the figure and identifying it as an example of the prosecutor's fallacy. It later emerged that pathologist Dr Alan Williams had failed to disclose microbiology results showing a staphylococcal infection that could have caused Harry's death.

Appeal and aftermath

Clark's first appeal failed in 2000, but a second appeal in January 2003 succeeded, and she was released. The Attorney General subsequently reviewed hundreds of similar cases, and convictions in the related cases of Angela Cannings and Trupti Patel were also overturned or abandoned. Meadow was struck off by the General Medical Council, though partly reinstated on appeal. Clark never recovered from her ordeal; she developed severe alcohol problems and died of acute alcohol intoxication at home in Essex in March 2007, aged 42. Her case remains a standard example in discussions of expert evidence and statistical reasoning in court.
