Cognitive rehabilitation therapy

Cognitive rehabilitation therapy (CRT) is a programme of therapeutic activities designed to retrain cognitive abilities — such as memory, attention, problem solving, and executive function — that have been impaired by brain injury, stroke, or other neurological conditions. It is typically delivered by neuropsychologists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists as part of a broader rehabilitation plan.

Approaches

Two broad strategies are distinguished. Restorative approaches aim to rebuild the impaired function itself through repeated, graded exercises — for example, attention process training or computerised memory drills — on the theory that practice drives neural plasticity. Compensatory approaches teach patients to work around deficits using external aids (diaries, alarms, smartphones), environmental modifications, and new strategies, such as errorless learning for people with severe memory impairment.

Well-studied techniques include goal management training for executive dysfunction, spaced retrieval for learning new information, scanning training for visual neglect, and mnemonic strategies such as the method of loci or PQRST for reading comprehension after injury.

Evidence and practice

Systematic reviews, notably the evidence-based reviews led by Keith Cicerone and colleagues, support cognitive rehabilitation for attention and executive deficits after traumatic brain injury, for language and visuospatial deficits after stroke, and for selected memory interventions. Practice standards emphasise that therapy should be embedded in real-world, personally meaningful activities rather than relying on decontextualised drills alone, and that family education and metacognitive strategy training improve generalisation.

Settings

CRT is provided in inpatient rehabilitation hospitals, outpatient clinics, community programmes, and increasingly through telerehabilitation and commercial brain-training software — the latter attracting regulatory scrutiny when marketed with unsupported claims. It has also been adapted for psychiatric populations, particularly schizophrenia, where cognitive remediation targets social cognition and functional outcomes.
