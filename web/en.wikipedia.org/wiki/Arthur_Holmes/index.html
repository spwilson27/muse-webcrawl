Arthur Holmes (14 January 1890 – 20 September 1965) was an English geologist who made two major contributions to the understanding of the Earth. He pioneered the use of radiometric dating of minerals to establish absolute ages for rocks and for the Earth itself, and he proposed that convection currents within the Earth's mantle could provide the driving force for continental drift, an idea that later became central to the theory of plate tectonics.

Early life and education

Holmes was born in Hebburn, County Durham, the son of a cabinet maker. He won a scholarship to the Royal College of Science (now part of Imperial College London), where he studied physics before switching to geology. While still an undergraduate he performed one of the first uranium–lead datings of a rock, obtaining an age of about 370 million years for a Devonian rock from Norway — far older than most geologists of the day believed possible.

Radiometric dating and the age of the Earth

In his influential book The Age of the Earth (1913, revised 1927 and 1937), Holmes argued from radioactive decay measurements that the Earth was at least 1.6 billion years old, and probably much older, demolishing the short timescales derived from Lord Kelvin's cooling calculations. Later editions pushed the figure to around 3 billion years; the modern accepted value is about 4.54 billion years. His persistent advocacy gradually won over the geological community and gave geology a reliable time scale.

Mantle convection and continental drift

In 1929 Holmes published a mechanism for Alfred Wegener's continental drift hypothesis: heat from radioactive decay in the Earth's interior would drive slow convection currents in the mantle, carrying the continents apart like parcels on a conveyor belt. Although drift remained controversial for decades, Holmes's mechanism anticipated the sea-floor spreading and subduction processes recognised in the 1960s.

Career and honours

Holmes worked in Burma for an oil company, then held chairs at Durham (1924–1942) and Edinburgh (1943–1956). His textbook Principles of Physical Geology (1944) became a standard work. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1932 and received both the Wollaston Medal of the Geological Society and the Penrose Medal of the Geological Society of America, as well as the Vetlesen Prize, sometimes called the Nobel Prize of geology.
