Sprain

Sprain
SpecialtySports medicine, orthopaedics, emergency medicine
SymptomsPain, swelling, bruising, limited movement, joint instability
CausesForced stretching or tearing of a ligament beyond its normal range
TreatmentProtection, rest, ice, compression, elevation; physiotherapy; rarely surgery

A sprain is an injury to a ligament — the tough bands of fibrous tissue connecting bones at a joint — caused by the joint being forced beyond its normal range of motion, stretching or partially or completely tearing the ligament. Sprains are graded by severity: grade I (stretching and microscopic tearing), grade II (partial tear), and grade III (complete rupture). They differ from strains, which involve muscles or tendons.

Common sites

The ankle is the most frequently sprained joint, usually when the foot rolls inwards (inversion), injuring the lateral ligaments such as the anterior talofibular ligament. Other common sites include the wrist (from falling on an outstretched hand), the knee (including the anterior cruciate ligament), the thumb ("skier's thumb"), and the fingers.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Signs include immediate pain, rapid swelling, bruising, warmth, and difficulty bearing weight or using the joint; a "pop" at the moment of injury may signal a complete tear. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by stress testing of the joint; X-rays are taken under decision rules such as the Ottawa ankle rules to exclude fractures, and MRI may be used for severe or persistent injuries.

Treatment

Initial management follows the PRICE protocol — Protection, Rest, Ice, Compression, and Elevation — with analgesics for pain. Early gentle mobilisation generally speeds recovery better than prolonged immobilisation. Physiotherapy emphasises restoring range of motion, strength, and proprioception (balance training), which reduces the risk of recurrent sprains and chronic instability. Complete tears with gross instability, especially in the knee, may require surgical repair or reconstruction.

Prognosis

Mild sprains heal in days to a few weeks; severe ones may take months. Inadequately rehabilitated ankle sprains can lead to repeated injury and chronic ankle instability in a substantial minority of patients.
