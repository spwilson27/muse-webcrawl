Bulldozer

A bulldozer is a large, motorized machine equipped with a substantial metal blade at the front, used to push soil, sand, rubble and other material during construction and earthworks. Most bulldozers are tracked vehicles (crawler tractors) giving excellent traction and weight distribution on soft ground, though wheeled models exist. A ripper — a claw-like shank or shanks — is often fitted at the rear to break up hard ground and rock.

Description

The blade is controlled hydraulically and comes in several forms: the straight blade (S blade), the universal blade (U blade) with curved wings for carrying larger loads, and the combination S-U blade. Work includes clearing and grubbing land, grading, pushing scrapers, spreading fill, backfilling trenches and demolishing small structures. The largest production bulldozers, such as the Komatsu D575A and Caterpillar D11, weigh well over 100 tonnes and are used mainly in surface mining.

History

The bulldozer developed from farm crawler tractors in the 1920s. In 1923 farmer James Cummings and draftsman J. Earl McLeod produced an early design for a blade mounted on a tractor; another early pioneer was the Best and Holt manufacturing lineage that became Caterpillar. Hydraulic controls replaced cable-operated blades after the Second World War, when military engineering units — notably the US Navy Seabees — demonstrated the machine's value in airfield and road construction. The word "bulldozer" itself predates the machine, originally meaning a severe dose or coercive "bull dose" of punishment, and was applied to the tractor by about 1930.

Armoured and military use

Armies use armoured bulldozers for combat engineering — breaching obstacles, digging in armour and clearing routes under fire; examples include the IDF Caterpillar D9, nicknamed "Teddy Bear", and the armoured dozers improvised by factory workers such as Marvin Heemeyer's notorious "Killdozer" of 2004.
