Alexander Suvorov

Count Alexander Vasilyevich Suvorov-Rymniksky, Prince of Italy (24 November 1730 – 18 May 1800) was a Russian generalissimo, reckoned among the few great commanders never to have lost a battle. Across more than fifty engagements in the wars against the Ottoman Empire, Poland and revolutionary France, his aggressive doctrine — speed, surprise, the bayonet, and intimate care for the common soldier — made him a legend in his own lifetime and a permanent fixture of Russian military identity.

Career

A sickly boy who built his body by regimen and study, Suvorov rose through the Seven Years' War and Catherine the Great's Turkish wars, winning at Kozludzha, Focșani and — with Prince Josias of Coburg — the crushing victory of Rymnik (1789). His storming of the fortress of Izmail in December 1790, after a rejected summons, was one of the century's most audacious assaults, conducted at heavy cost. In Poland (1794) his troops stormed Praga, Warsaw's suburb, amid a massacre that still darkens his name there.

The Italian and Swiss campaigns

Recalled by Paul I in 1799 to command the Austro-Russian army against revolutionary France, the 69-year-old Suvorov swept the French from northern Italy in a summer — Adda, Trebbia, Novi — before allied strategy diverted him into Switzerland to join a Russian corps that had already been destroyed. Trapped, he fought his way over the Panix Pass in early snow, saving his army in a retreat Russians compare to Hannibal's crossing of the Alps. He was made generalissimo, then disgraced by Paul over court etiquette, and died months later.

Doctrine and legacy

Suvorov's maxims — "the bullet is a fool, the bayonet is a fine fellow"; "train hard, fight easy" — distilled a system of speed, shock and morale. He shared his soldiers' hardships and was idolised by them. The USSR created the Order of Suvorov in 1942, and his Alpine crossing is commemorated at the Schöllenen Gorge in Switzerland.
