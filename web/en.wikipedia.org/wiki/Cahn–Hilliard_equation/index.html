Cahn–Hilliard Equation

The Cahn–Hilliard equation is a fourth-order partial differential equation describing phase separation (spinodal decomposition) in binary alloys and fluid mixtures. It was introduced by John W. Cahn and John E. Hilliard in 1958.

Formulation

The equation governs a conserved order parameter c (the concentration difference between phases): ∂c/∂t = ∇·(M ∇μ), where the chemical potential μ is derived from a free-energy functional combining a double-well bulk potential (often (c²−1)²/4) with a gradient-energy term penalizing sharp interfaces. Substituting μ yields a nonlinear fourth-order diffusion equation. Unlike ordinary diffusion, the effective diffusivity is negative in the unstable region, so small concentration fluctuations grow — "uphill diffusion" — producing the intertwined phase patterns of spinodal decomposition that coarsen over time.

Applications and analysis

The Cahn–Hilliard equation is a central model in materials science, image processing (for inpainting), and multiphase fluid dynamics, where it is coupled to Navier–Stokes equations. It conserves the total mass of the order parameter and dissipates free energy, properties that guide the design of numerical schemes such as convex splitting and spectral methods.
