Victor of Aveyron (French: Victor de l'Aveyron; c. 1788–1828) was a French feral child who was found at about the age of twelve in the woods near Saint-Sernin-sur-Rance in southern France. His education by the physician Jean Marc Gaspard Itard became one of the foundational case studies in the development of special education and developmental psychology.

Discovery

Victor was sighted and captured several times from 1797, finally emerging from the forest in January 1800. He could not speak, walked sometimes on all fours, preferred raw food and showed scars including one on his throat. Brought to Paris as a supposed "wild boy of nature", he was examined by Philippe Pinel, who judged him an incurable "idiot", but Itard took charge of him, named him Victor, and set out to civilise and educate him.

Education

Itard's two reports (1801 and 1806) describe five aims: socialising Victor, awakening his senses, extending his ideas, teaching speech, and developing reasoning. Victor learned to read and write a few words (notably lait, milk), to use signs, and to form attachments — above all to his housekeeper Madame Guérin — but he never acquired fluent language. Itard pioneered techniques of sensory training and behaviour shaping that influenced Édouard Séguin and, through him, Maria Montessori.

Interpretations

Modern writers debate whether Victor was truly raised wild or was an abandoned child, possibly with autism or intellectual disability. His story inspired François Truffaut's film L'Enfant sauvage (1970), in which Truffaut played Itard.
