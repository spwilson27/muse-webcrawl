Reliquary

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A reliquary (also referred to as a shrine, or by the French term châsse) is a container for relics. These may be the purported physical remains of saints, such as bones, pieces of clothing, or objects associated with saints or other religious figures. The Christian tradition developed elaborate reliquaries during the Middle Ages, often crafted from precious metals, ivory, and enamel, and many are among the masterpieces of medieval metalwork.

Forms

Reliquaries take many forms. Body-part reliquaries are shaped like the body part they contain — a head, arm, hand, or foot — a type especially common in the later Middle Ages. Container reliquaries include caskets and boxes, often shaped like a church or house with a pitched roof (châsses), portable shrines, and monstrance-like vessels (ostensoria) with a glass window through which the relic can be viewed. Elaborate architectural shrines, such as the Shrine of the Three Kings in Cologne Cathedral, house major relics within churches.

Small personal reliquaries include pendant crosses, lockets, and the phylacteries worn by pilgrims. In the Philippines, religious statues called urnas serve a related function. Buddhist traditions use reliquaries such as stupas and small caskets to house the cremated remains (śarīra) of the Buddha and eminent monks.

History

The veneration of relics became a central feature of Christian practice in late antiquity, and the translation of relics to new shrines drew pilgrimages and donations. Under Charlemagne and his successors, churches were expected to possess relics, and altar stones were required to contain them. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea decreed that no church should be consecrated without relics. During the Crusades, large numbers of relics reached Western Europe from the Holy Land and, after the sack of Constantinople in 1204, from Byzantium; the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris was built by Louis IX to house the Crown of Thorns.

The Protestant Reformation rejected the veneration of relics, and many reliquaries in northern Europe were destroyed or melted down. The Catholic Counter-Reformation reaffirmed the practice at the Council of Trent. Today reliquaries are prized as works of art and are held by major museums as well as by churches.

Notable reliquaries

  • The Shrine of the Three Kings (Cologne Cathedral), the largest reliquary in the Western tradition
  • The Reliquary Shrine of Saint Eleutherius (Tournai Cathedral)
  • The Holy Thorn Reliquary (British Museum), made for Jean, duc de Berry
  • The reliquary of St. Taurin (Évreux), a masterpiece of thirteenth-century Limoges enamel
  • The Samtavisi reliquaries and other treasures of medieval Georgia
