Duct tape

A standard roll of duct tape

Duct tape (historically also called duck tape) is a cloth- or scrim-backed, pressure-sensitive adhesive tape, usually coated with polyethylene and characterised by a strong, flexible, water-resistant fabric backing and aggressive natural or synthetic rubber adhesive. Its combination of strength, hand-tearability and adhesion to many surfaces has made it one of the most widely used general-purpose tapes in the world.

History

Duct tape was developed during the Second World War for the United States military. Vesta Stoudt, an ordnance-factory worker in Illinois, suggested a cloth-based waterproof tape to seal ammunition cases; the idea reached President Franklin D. Roosevelt, and Johnson & Johnson developed the product in 1942 — an olive-drab, cotton-duck-backed tape that soldiers nicknamed "duck tape". After the war the tape was sold for civilian use, including for sealing heating and air-conditioning ductwork, and its colour changed to the now-familiar aluminium silver, giving rise to the name "duct tape".

Properties and uses

Standard duct tape consists of three layers: a polyethylene skin, a fabric scrim (which gives tensile strength and allows straight hand-tearing) and a rubber-based adhesive. Despite the name, building researchers have found ordinary duct tape performs poorly as a long-term sealant on actual ducts, where foil tapes are preferred. Its real fame is as an improvisational repair material — famously used by NASA crews, most notably in the Apollo 13 mission in 1970, and standard equipment on spacecraft and in workshops, stages (where matte "gaffer tape" is its close relative) and homes everywhere.
