The Japanese tea ceremony, known as chanoyu (茶の湯, "hot water for tea"), sadō or chadō (茶道, "the way of tea"), is a traditional Japanese art form centred on the preparation, serving and drinking of matcha, powdered green tea. More than a method of making tea, it is a choreographed ritual in which host and guests share an aesthetic and contemplative experience shaped by Zen Buddhism, the wabi-sabi aesthetic of rustic simplicity, and exacting etiquette governing every movement, utensil and word.

History

Tea was introduced to Japan from China by Buddhist monks in the early medieval period; the monk Eisai, who promoted tea's health benefits in his Kissa Yōjōki (1211), is often called Japan's "tea saint". In the Muromachi period, tea drinking was codified under shogunal patronage, first as a luxurious pastime of displaying Chinese treasures, then transformed by the Zen monk Murata Jukō and the merchant Takeno Jōō toward a humbler, more inward practice. Their heir, the tea master Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591), adviser to the warlords Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi, perfected the wabi-cha style in small rustic tea houses with rough earthen walls — before Hideyoshi ordered him to commit seppuku in 1591, for reasons still debated.

The ceremony

A full formal gathering (chaji) may last four hours and include a kaiseki meal, a charcoal-laying ceremony, and two servings of tea: thick tea (koicha) shared from one bowl, and thin tea (usucha) served individually. Guests enter through a garden path (roji), purify themselves at a stone water basin, and crawl through a small entrance (nijiriguchi) into the tea room, whose alcove (tokonoma) displays a hanging scroll and a seasonal flower arrangement. Utensils — the tea bowl (chawan), caddy (natsume), scoop (chashaku), whisk (chasen), kettle and brazier — are chosen for the season and occasion and handled with prescribed, graceful movements, and guests admire each in turn. Rikyū's principles are summarised as harmony, respect, purity and tranquillity, and the encounter is framed by the ideal of ichi-go ichi-e: "one time, one meeting", never to be repeated.

Schools and present practice

Rikyū's descendants founded the three Sen schools — Urasenke (the largest today), Omotesenke and Mushakōjisenke — which preserve distinct but related forms (temae). Once a pursuit of warriors and merchants, chanoyu is now practised by millions in Japan and abroad, taught in schools and clubs, and studied by many practitioners for years to obtain teaching licences. It has deeply influenced Japanese architecture, garden design, ceramics (such as Raku ware), cuisine and calligraphy.
