Chagas cardiomyopathy

Chagas cardiomyopathy is a chronic inflammatory disease of the heart muscle that develops in roughly 20–30% of people infected with the protozoan parasite Trypanosoma cruzi, the cause of Chagas disease. It is the most serious manifestation of chronic Chagas disease and a leading cause of non-ischaemic heart failure in endemic regions of Latin America.

Pathophysiology

After the acute phase of infection subsides, most patients enter an asymptomatic "indeterminate" phase that may last decades. In those who progress, persistent low-grade parasite presence in cardiac tissue, combined with an intense and dysregulated immune response, produces chronic myocarditis. Progressive destruction of myocytes and of the cardiac conduction system, autonomic denervation, microvascular damage, and replacement fibrosis lead to ventricular dilation, wall-motion abnormalities — classically of the inferolateral wall — and thinning of the ventricular apex, which may form an apical aneurysm, a near-pathognomonic finding.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic features include:

  • Conduction disturbances — right bundle branch block, often combined with left anterior fascicular block, and atrioventricular block.
  • Ventricular arrhythmias — frequent premature ventricular contractions, non-sustained and sustained ventricular tachycardia; sudden cardiac death is a leading cause of mortality.
  • Heart failure — typically biventricular, with a worse prognosis than cardiomyopathies of other causes at comparable functional class.
  • Thromboembolism — mural thrombus in the apical aneurysm or dilated ventricle can cause stroke and systemic embolism.
  • Palpitations, presyncope, and syncope.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on positive serology for T. cruzi together with compatible cardiac findings. The electrocardiogram is an inexpensive and sensitive screening tool; echocardiography assesses chamber size, ejection fraction, and apical aneurysm. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging can demonstrate the characteristic pattern of late gadolinium enhancement (fibrosis), often in the inferolateral wall. Risk stratification scores, such as the Rassi score, estimate mortality risk from factors including functional class, cardiomegaly, and non-sustained ventricular tachycardia.

Treatment

Management follows standard heart-failure therapy (beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors or ARBs, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists), with amiodarone frequently used for ventricular arrhythmias and anticoagulation for patients with apical aneurysm, mural thrombus, or prior embolism. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators are used for survivors of sustained ventricular arrhythmia, though evidence in this population is limited; advanced cases may require heart transplantation, with good long-term survival despite the risk of parasite reactivation under immunosuppression.

Antitrypanosomal treatment with benznidazole clears or reduces parasite burden in chronic infection but, in the large BENEFIT trial (2015), did not significantly reduce clinical cardiac progression in patients who already had established cardiomyopathy, underscoring the importance of treating infection before cardiac damage occurs.

Epidemiology

Chagas disease affects an estimated 6–7 million people, mainly in Latin America; migration has made chronic Chagas cardiomyopathy an increasing concern in the United States, Spain, and other non-endemic countries.
