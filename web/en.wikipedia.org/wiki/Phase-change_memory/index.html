Phase-change memory

TypeNon-volatile random-access memory
MaterialChalcogenide glass (e.g. Ge2Sb2Te5)
PrincipleAmorphous–crystalline phase change
Commercialized2015 (Intel/Micron 3D XPoint, as Optane)

Phase-change memory (also known as PCM, PCME, PRAM, PCRAM, and chalcogenide RAM) is a type of non-volatile random-access memory. PRAMs exploit the unique behaviour of chalcogenide glass: with heat applied by an electrical current, the material can be switched between two states — crystalline and amorphous — which differ greatly in electrical resistance. Recent versions can achieve two additional distinct intermediate states, effectively doubling memory capacity.

Background

The phase-change properties of chalcogenide materials were explored by Stanford R. Ovshinsky in the 1960s. The same materials underlie rewritable optical discs (CD-RW and DVD-RW), where laser heating changes reflectivity. In the 2000s, companies including Samsung, Intel, STMicroelectronics, and Micron pursued PCM as a successor to flash memory.

Operation

A PCM cell consists of a small volume of chalcogenide alloy (commonly Ge2Sb2Te5, "GST") between two electrodes, with a resistive heating element. A long, lower-current pulse anneals the material into its crystalline (low-resistance, "set") state, while a short, high-current pulse melts and quenches it into the amorphous (high-resistance, "reset") state. Resistance differences of orders of magnitude allow reliable sensing.

Comparison and commercialization

PCM offers faster write speeds, better endurance, and bit alterability compared with NAND flash, but faces challenges in thermal cross-talk, resistance drift, and cost. In 2015 Intel and Micron announced 3D XPoint technology, marketed by Intel as Optane, which was widely reported to be based on phase-change storage; Optane products were discontinued in 2022. PCM is also used in some embedded microcontrollers and in research neuromorphic computing devices.
