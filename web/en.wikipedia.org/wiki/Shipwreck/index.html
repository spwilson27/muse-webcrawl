Shipwreck

A shipwreck is the wreckage of a ship that is located either beached on land or sunken to the seabed of a body of water. Shipwrecks occur through storms, collisions, groundings, fire, warfare, and structural failure; bad weather remains one of the most common causes. The term also refers to the event that caused the loss of the vessel.

Causes and survival

Historically, navigation errors, uncharted reefs, and foundering in heavy seas claimed thousands of vessels. Famous wrecks include the Mary Rose (1545), the Spanish treasure fleets lost to hurricanes, HMS Association (1707), the Titanic (1912), and the Edmund Fitzgerald (1975). Survival depended on lifeboats, rafts, proximity to shore, and rescue — factors greatly improved by modern safety regulation, radio, and satellite distress systems.

Salvage and law

Wrecks are governed by maritime salvage law, which rewards the recovery of vessels and cargo. The law of finds and admiralty courts determine ownership, and historically coastal "wrecking" — luring ships ashore for plunder — was a notorious practice. The UNESCO Convention on the Protection of the Underwater Cultural Heritage (2001) protects wrecks over 100 years old as cultural property.

Archaeology and ecology

Shipwrecks are rich archaeological sites preserving cargoes, ship construction, and daily life of past eras. Sunken hulls also act as artificial reefs, colonised by marine organisms, and as hazards or pollution sources when they carry fuel oil or ordnance. An estimated three million wrecks lie on the world's ocean floors.
