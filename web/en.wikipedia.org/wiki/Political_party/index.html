Political party

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A political party is an organisation that coordinates candidates to compete in a country's elections, seeking to win government power or a share of it. Members of a party share, broadly, an ideology or policy programme; parties nominate candidates, mobilise voters, organise legislatures and form governments. Modern parties grew out of parliamentary factions — the English Whigs and Tories of the late 17th century are early examples — and became mass organisations in the 19th century as the franchise expanded, evolving from elite "cadre" parties to mass-membership parties (socialist and confessional parties) and later to professionalised "catch-all" and cartel parties competing through media and state funding.

Party systems and functions

Political scientists classify party systems by the number and interaction of parties: dominant/one-party systems (China's Communist Party; historically Mexico's PRI), two-party systems (the United States' Democrats and Republicans under first-past-the-post), and multi-party systems typical of proportional representation (Germany, the Netherlands, India at national level — see India). Parties also structure the work of a legislature, discipline voting, recruit political elites, and give voters a simplified choice between governing teams. Their decline — falling membership, volatile electorates, personalisation around leaders — is a standing theme of modern democratic analysis, alongside their resilience: no large democracy has yet found a way to run elections without them. Party organisation, finance and internal democracy are regulated very differently across countries, and banned or underground parties mark the boundary of authoritarian systems.
