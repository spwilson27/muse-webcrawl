Plateau's laws

In a soap foam, three films meet at 120° along each edge (Plateau border), and four edges meet at a vertex.

Plateau's laws describe the structure of soap films and foams. They were formulated by the Belgian physicist Joseph Plateau, who published them in his 1873 work Statique expérimentale et théorique des liquides soumis aux seules forces moléculaires, based on experimental observations of soap films spanning wire frames.

The laws

  1. Soap films are made of smooth surfaces.
  2. The mean curvature of a portion of a film is constant on that portion: films are surfaces of constant mean curvature, and where a film separates regions of equal pressure its mean curvature is zero (a minimal surface).
  3. Films meet three at a time along a curve called a Plateau border, and they do so at equal angles of 120°.
  4. Plateau borders meet four at a time at a vertex, at the tetrahedral angle of arccos(−1/3) ≈ 109.47°.

Significance

These rules follow from surface tension minimising the total area, subject to constraints, and are examples of the geometric problem of minimal surfaces. Jean Taylor proved in 1976 that the configurations allowed by Plateau's laws are the only stable singularities for area-minimising soap films. Plateau's laws govern the structure of foams — the Plateau border channels drain liquid and give foams their mechanical properties — and appear in contexts from materials science (grain boundaries and cellular structures) to architecture and the mathematics of minimal surfaces and bubble clusters, including the double bubble problem.
