Great Molasses Flood

Date15 January 1919
LocationNorth End, Boston, Massachusetts, United States
CauseStructural failure of a molasses storage tank (Purity Distilling Company / U.S. Industrial Alcohol)
Deaths21
Injuriesabout 150
Volumeabout 2.3 million US gallons (8,700 m³) of molasses

The Great Molasses Flood, also known as the Boston Molasses Disaster, was a catastrophic industrial accident that occurred on 15 January 1919 in the North End neighborhood of Boston, Massachusetts. A huge storage tank filled with molasses burst, releasing a wave of molasses reported to be up to 25 feet (7.6 m) high that raced through the streets at about 35 mph (56 km/h). The flood killed 21 people and injured around 150, crushed buildings and a firehouse, buckled an elevated railway structure and derailed a train.

The tank and the disaster

The tank, on Commercial Street, was built in 1915 by the Purity Distilling Company, a subsidiary of United States Industrial Alcohol, to store molasses imported from the Caribbean for industrial alcohol production — including, during the First World War, munitions alcohol. The 50-foot-tall, 90-foot-diameter steel tank leaked from the start; local children collected the dripping molasses, and the company painted the tank brown to disguise the leaks rather than repair it. It had recently been filled nearly to capacity with a fresh, warm shipment — an estimated 2.3 million gallons. Shortly after noon on 15 January, an unseasonably warm day following bitter cold, rivets began to fail with sounds witnesses compared to machine-gun fire, and the tank burst apart.

Damage and rescue

The collapsing wall of molasses swept away people, horses, wagons and vehicles, destroyed the nearby Boston Elevated Railway structure on Atlantic Avenue and lifted a train from the tracks, and demolished several buildings including a city firehouse — several firefighters were among the dead. Victims were drowned, crushed or suffocated in the sticky mass, which hardened as it cooled and made rescue excruciating: rescuers from the Navy training ship Nantucket, the Red Cross, police and passers-by waded knee-deep for days. Bodies were still being recovered days later, and harbour waters stayed brown for months; local legend holds that the North End smelled of molasses on hot days for decades. Cleanup employed hundreds of workers for weeks, using seawater hoses and sand.

Aftermath and legacy

The ensuing litigation — 119 lawsuits consolidated into one of the longest cases in Massachusetts history — ended in 1925 with the court-appointed auditor finding U.S. Industrial Alcohol liable; the company paid out roughly $628,000 (in some accounts up to $1 million) to victims and families, having blamed anarchist sabotage and other causes. Investigations attributed the failure to grossly inadequate design, steel too thin and brittle for the load, untested construction, and negligent inspection — the tank had never been filled with water to test it. The scandal spurred reforms: Massachusetts thereafter required engineers to stamp and file tank plans and calculations, and similar requirements for licensed professional engineering oversight spread across the United States. Today a small plaque in Puopolo Park marks the site, and the disaster is a staple of Boston history and of engineering-ethics teaching. Modern scientific interest continued: a 2016 Harvard study modelled how the cold January air thickened the molasses and trapped victims.
