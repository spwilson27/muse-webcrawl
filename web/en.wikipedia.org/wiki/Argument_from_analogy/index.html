Argument from analogy

[Image: Argument from analogy — Diagram illustrating inference from a known case to a similar unknown case]

The argument from analogy is a special type of inductive argument, whereby perceived similarities are used as a basis to infer some further similarity that has yet to be observed. Analogical reasoning is one of the most common methods by which human beings attempt to understand the world and make decisions. When a person has a bad experience with a product and decides not to buy anything further from the producer, this is often a case of analogical reasoning. It is also implicit in much of science; for instance, experiments on rats typically proceed with the assumption that some physiological similarities between rats and humans entail some further similarity (e.g., possible reactions to a drug).

Structure

The process of analogical inference involves noting the common properties of two or more things, and from this basis inferring that they also share some further property. The structure or form may be schematized as follows:

  • P and Q are similar in respects a, b, and c.
  • P has been observed to have further property x.
  • Therefore, Q probably has property x also.

The strength of such an argument depends on the relevance of the shared properties, the number of similarities and instances, and the absence of relevant dissimilarities — points systematised by John Stuart Mill in A System of Logic (1843).

Uses

Analogical arguments play a central role in philosophy of religion (the teleological argument, as in William Paley's watchmaker analogy), philosophy of mind (arguments about other minds), ethics, law (reasoning from precedents), and artificial intelligence (case-based reasoning). David Hume's Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion offers classic criticisms of the argument from analogy applied to the design of the universe.

False analogy

A false analogy (or weak analogy) is a fallacy in which the items compared are not relevantly similar, so the conclusion does not follow. Critics evaluate analogies by hunting for disanalogies — relevant differences that undermine the inference.
