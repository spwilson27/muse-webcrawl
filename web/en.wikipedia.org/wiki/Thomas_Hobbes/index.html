Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) was an English philosopher whose Leviathan (1651), written amid civil war, founded modern political philosophy's Social contract tradition. Hobbes argued that humans in the state of nature — equal, fearful and competing — live lives 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short', and rationally surrender nearly all rights to an absolute sovereign for security; his materialism made even thought a kind of motion.

Life and thought

Educated at Oxford, tutor to the Cavendish family, Hobbes travelled with Francis Bacon, translated Thucydides, and quarrelled with Descartes and the Royal Society alike. Leviathan's psychology of fear and glory, its covenant theory and its Erastian subordination of church to state scandalised contemporaries ('the Monster of Malmesbury') and still structure debates on authority, anarchy and international relations realism.
