Larne gun-running

The Larne gun-running was a major operation of smuggling arms by Ulster unionists in April 1914, during the crisis over Irish Home Rule. In the night of 24–25 April 1914, the Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) landed some 25,000 rifles and between three and five million rounds of ammunition at the ports of Larne, Donaghadee and Bangor in the north of Ireland, without interference from the authorities.

Background and operation

The UVF had been formed in 1913 to resist the Third Home Rule Bill by force if necessary. The weapons — mostly German, Austrian and Italian rifles bought through arms dealers in Hamburg — were shipped aboard the steamer Fanny and transferred at sea to the collier Clyde Valley, renamed Mountjoy II in an allusion to the Siege of Derry. On the night of the landing, the UVF sealed off Larne, cut telegraph lines, and used a fleet of several hundred motor vehicles, coordinated by Frederick Crawford and overseen on the ground by General Sir William Adair, to distribute the weapons to prearranged dumps across Ulster before dawn.

Consequences

No arrests were made, and the operation was a propaganda and practical triumph for the unionist leadership under Edward Carson: the UVF was now genuinely armed. Three months later, Irish nationalists imitated the feat in the Howth gun-running of 26 July 1914, which ended in the Bachelor's Walk shootings. The arming of private militias on both sides is widely seen as a decisive step on Ireland's path toward the Easter Rising, the War of Independence and partition.
