Title sequence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A title sequence is the method by which films and television programmes present their title, key cast and crew, often accompanied by music and a sequence of images. In television the term is commonly shortened to "intro" or "opening credits", while the closing sequence is the credits scroll or end title card.

History

Early films presented credits as static cards. In the 1950s, graphic designer Saul Bass revolutionised the form with animated sequences for The Man with the Golden Arm (1955), Vertigo (1958) and North by Northwest (1959), treating the opening as a distinct artistic statement that set the mood of the film. Bass's work made title design a recognised craft. Later masters included Maurice Binder, whose gun-barrel sequences for the James Bond films became iconic, and Pablo Ferro.

Television and modern practice

Television title sequences combine a theme song or music cue with a montage that establishes characters, setting and tone — from the train-whistle themes of westerns to the CGI maps of Game of Thrones. In streaming-era television, many viewers use "skip intro" buttons, which have pushed some shows toward shorter sequences or cold opens. Title sequences are also a recognised award category, with the Emmy for Outstanding Main Title Design.
