Mount Athos

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Byzantine monastery clinging to the forested slopes of Mount Athos above the Aegean]
A Byzantine monastery clinging to the forested slopes of Mount Athos above the Aegean

Mount Athos is a mountain and peninsula in northeastern Greece, home to an autonomous monastic community of 20 Eastern Orthodox monasteries under the spiritual jurisdiction of the Ecumenical Patriarchate. Known as the 'Holy Mountain' (Ἅγιον Ὄρος), it has been a centre of Orthodox monasticism since the ninth century; its charter tradition famously excludes women — the avaton rule applies even to female animals — and entry for male visitors is by permit (the diamonitirion).

History and community

Organised monasticism began with the Great Lavra's foundation by Athanasios the Athonite in 963 under imperial patronage; the peninsula's typikon (1060) fixed its self-governing structure under a council at Karyes. Athos preserved Byzantine chant, iconography and manuscripts through Ottoman centuries — the sultans confirmed its privileges — and its thousand-strong brotherhood today includes monasteries of Greek, Russian (St Panteleimon), Serbian (Hilandar), Bulgarian (Zograf) and Romanian (Prodromos) traditions.

Art and controversy

Its treasuries hold Byzantine icons, illuminated manuscripts and relics (the Belt of the Theotokos among them) unmatched outside museums, and its chant and icon-painting schools shape Orthodox practice globally. The avaton and Greek state funding, and the peninsula's status within the EU (recognised by treaty declarations), provoke periodic debate about gender equality and sovereignty, so far without effect on the rule.
