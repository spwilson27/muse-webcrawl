Laurence–Moon syndrome (LMS) is a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder associated with retinitis pigmentosa, spastic paraplegia, hypogonadism, and intellectual disabilities. It was described in 1866 by the English ophthalmologists John Zachariah Laurence and Robert Charles Moon in a family of four siblings with retinal degeneration, developmental difficulties, and neurological abnormalities.

Relationship to Bardet–Biedl syndrome

For much of the twentieth century, Laurence–Moon syndrome was grouped with Bardet–Biedl syndrome (BBS) under the combined name "Laurence–Moon–Bardet–Biedl syndrome". Modern practice distinguishes them: patients with Laurence–Moon syndrome characteristically show prominent spastic paraplegia and ataxia and typically lack the postaxial polydactyly and early-onset obesity typical of BBS, which is now understood as a ciliopathy with its own set of causative genes. Mutations in the PNPLA6 gene have been identified in families with the Laurence–Moon phenotype, linking it to a spectrum that includes other hereditary spastic paraplegia and retinal disorders.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features are progressive visual loss from pigmentary retinal degeneration beginning in childhood, spasticity affecting mainly the lower limbs, cerebellar ataxia, hypogenitalism, short stature, and intellectual disability of variable degree. Renal abnormalities, a hallmark of Bardet–Biedl syndrome, are not a characteristic feature.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical and by exclusion of Bardet–Biedl syndrome, increasingly supported by genetic testing. There is no cure; management is supportive and includes low-vision services, physiotherapy for spasticity, hormone replacement where indicated, and genetic counselling for affected families.
