Mediation (statistics)

In statistics, a mediation model seeks to identify and explain the mechanism that underlies an observed relationship between an independent variable (X) and a dependent variable (Y) via the inclusion of a third, intervening variable known as a mediator (M). Rather than X causing Y directly, the model proposes that X causes the mediator M, which in turn causes Y—helping explain how or why an effect occurs.

The basic model

A simple mediation model involves three path coefficients:

  • a: the effect of X on the mediator M
  • b: the effect of M on Y, controlling for X
  • c′: the "direct effect" of X on Y, controlling for M

The indirect effect is the product a × b, and the total effect c (X on Y without the mediator) equals c′ + ab. If the direct effect c′ is reduced to zero when the mediator is included, mediation is called complete; if c′ is reduced but remains nonzero, it is partial mediation.

The Baron and Kenny approach

The widely cited causal-steps approach of Reuben Baron and David Kenny (1986) required: (1) X significantly predicts Y; (2) X significantly predicts M; (3) M significantly predicts Y controlling for X; and (4) the effect of X on Y shrinks when M is included. The significance of the indirect effect was traditionally tested with the Sobel test. Modern practice—following work by Andrew Hayes, Kristopher Preacher, and others—favors directly testing the indirect effect with bootstrapped confidence intervals (e.g., via the PROCESS macro), and has dropped the requirement of a significant total effect, since indirect and direct effects of opposite sign can cancel ("inconsistent mediation").

Causal assumptions

Causal interpretation of mediation requires strong assumptions: no unmeasured confounding of the X–M, M–Y, and X–Y relationships, and correct temporal ordering (X precedes M precedes Y). These assumptions are rarely fully satisfied in cross-sectional observational data, a central critique of the mediation literature in psychology and epidemiology. Counterfactual (potential-outcomes) frameworks formalize natural direct and indirect effects and their identification conditions.

Mediation vs. moderation

Mediation explains a mechanism (an intervening process answering "how"), whereas moderation describes when or for whom an effect is stronger or weaker, tested as an interaction. The two can be combined in moderated mediation and mediated moderation models.
