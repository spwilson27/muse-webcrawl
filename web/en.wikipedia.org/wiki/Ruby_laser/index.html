Ruby laser

A ruby laser is a solid-state laser that uses a synthetic ruby crystal as its gain medium. It was the first laser ever operated successfully, demonstrated by Theodore H. Maiman at Hughes Research Laboratories on 16 May 1960. The ruby laser produces pulses of coherent red light at a wavelength of 694.3 nanometres.

Construction and operation

The gain medium is a rod of synthetic ruby—aluminium oxide (corundum) doped with a small percentage of chromium ions, which give the crystal its pink-red colour and provide the energy levels used for lasing. The rod's ends are polished flat and silvered to form an optical cavity, one end partially transparent to let the beam out. A helical xenon flash lamp surrounding the rod optically "pumps" the chromium ions into an excited metastable state, creating the population inversion needed for stimulated emission.

Ruby is a three-level laser system, which makes it inefficient: more than half the chromium ions must be excited before lasing begins, so ruby lasers require intense pumping and operate almost exclusively in short, powerful pulses rather than continuously. Their output appears as a deep red beam of spiky pulses, each lasting only microseconds or less; Q-switching can produce even shorter, more energetic bursts.

Applications and legacy

Early applications included rangefinding, holography—ruby's pulsed output was ideal for holographic portraits—and materials research. Medical uses followed, notably in dermatology for tattoo and pigmented-lesion removal, where the 694 nm wavelength is strongly absorbed by melanin and many tattoo pigments. Although largely superseded by more efficient laser media such as Nd:YAG, the ruby laser remains historically significant as the device that launched the laser age, and Maiman's original apparatus is preserved at the Smithsonian Institution.
