Ghetto

The Venetian Ghetto, the first district to bear the name

A ghetto is a part of a city in which members of a minority group live, especially as a result of political, social, legal, religious or economic pressure or compulsion. The term was originally used for the Venetian Ghetto, established in 1516, in which the city's Jewish population was compelled to live; it derives from the Italian getto, "foundry", the site being near a former copper foundry.

History

Segregated Jewish quarters existed across medieval Europe — called Judengassen in Germany and juderías in Spain — but compulsory ghettos spread after the 16th century, notably in the Papal States, where Jews were confined until the 19th century. Emancipation in the 1800s abolished most European ghettos.

During the Second World War, Nazi Germany established ghettos across occupied Eastern Europe — the largest being the Warsaw Ghetto — as a stage in the persecution and deportation of Jews to extermination camps in the Holocaust.

Modern usage

In the 20th-century United States the term came to describe inner-city districts where Black Americans were confined by segregation, redlining and restrictive covenants, such as Harlem and Chicago's Bronzeville. Sociologists use "ghetto" more broadly for ethnically or socially homogeneous, disadvantaged enclaves, and the word's connotations remain contested.
