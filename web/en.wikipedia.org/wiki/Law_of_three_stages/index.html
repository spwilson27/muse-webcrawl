Law of three stages

Auguste Comte's three stages of human thought

The law of three stages is a theory of intellectual and social development formulated by French philosopher Auguste Comte (1798–1857), generally regarded as the founder of sociology and of the doctrine of positivism. According to the law, human thought — in each branch of knowledge, and in society as a whole — passes successively through three theoretical states: the theological (or fictitious), the metaphysical (or abstract), and the scientific (or positive).

The three stages

In the theological stage, phenomena are explained as the products of supernatural agents — first fetishes, then many gods, and finally a single God. In the metaphysical stage, supernatural agents are replaced by abstract forces and essences inherent in things. In the positive stage, the mind gives up the search for absolute causes and instead seeks the invariable laws governing phenomena through observation and reasoning.

Development in Comte's thought

Comte sketched the law in his early writings of the 1820s and developed it fully in the Course of Positive Philosophy (1830–1842). He held that the sciences themselves mature into positivism in a definite order — mathematics, astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, and finally sociology — corresponding to the decreasing generality and increasing complexity of their subject matter. The law supplied the organising principle of his hierarchy of the sciences and of his later plans for a positively organised society.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the law's universality and its suggestion of inevitable linear progress, noting that the three modes of thought coexist within societies and individuals. Nevertheless, the law remains one of the earliest systematic theories of social and intellectual change and a landmark in the history of sociology.
