Mental space

Mental spaces are partial conceptual structures built up as we think and talk.

Mental spaces are small conceptual packets constructed as we think and talk, for purposes of local understanding and action. The theory of mental spaces was developed by the French linguist Gilles Fauconnier, notably in Mental Spaces (1985; English edition 1994), and forms a foundation of cognitive linguistics and of the theory of conceptual blending.

The theory

Unlike formal semantic models, mental spaces are partial structures: they contain elements (entities, properties, relations) and are structured by frames and cognitive models. Expressions called space builders — such as "in 1929", "in that film", "if", "Mary believes", or a proper name with a modal — set up new spaces related to a base space. Elements in different spaces can be linked by connectors based on identity, analogy, or representation: the painting in an art gallery and the woman it depicts, for instance, occupy different spaces yet are connected.

Discourse continually opens, shifts between, and elaborates spaces, and much of the apparent complexity of reference, counterfactuals, and presupposition can be explained by this dynamics. A classic example: "In the painting, the girl with blue eyes has green eyes" — true in the representation space, false in the reality space, without contradiction.

Conceptual blending

Fauconnier and Mark Turner extended the theory into conceptual blending (The Way We Think, 2002): two or more input spaces project selected structure into a blended space with emergent meaning of its own — as in the "grim reaper", counterfactual debates with oneself, or complex metaphors. Blending is held to be a basic cognitive operation underlying grammar, metaphor, analogy and creativity.
