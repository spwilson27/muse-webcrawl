Cullin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Placeholder image

Cullins are a family of hydrophobic scaffold proteins that provide a structural framework for a class of E3 ubiquitin ligases known as cullin-RING ligases (CRLs) — the largest family of ubiquitin ligases in eukaryotes. By assembling substrate-recognition modules with the catalytic RING protein, CRLs target specific cellular proteins for ubiquitination and usually proteasomal degradation, thereby regulating the cell cycle, signal transduction, transcription and DNA repair.

Family members

Mammals have several cullins, including CUL1, CUL2, CUL3, CUL4A, CUL4B, CUL5 and CUL7. Each nucleates a distinct class of complex: CUL1 forms the SCF (Skp1–Cullin–F-box) complexes; CUL2 works with the von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) substrate receptor; CUL3 binds BTB-domain proteins such as Keap1; CUL4 uses DWD/DDB2 receptors in DNA-damage responses.

Regulation

CRL activity is switched on by neddylation — attachment of the ubiquitin-like protein NEDD8 to the cullin — and switched off by the COP9 signalosome, which removes NEDD8. The exchange factor CAND1 drives remodelling of substrate receptors. The neddylation inhibitor pevonedistat (MLN4924) exploits this pathway as an anti-cancer strategy, and mutations in cullin genes (notably CUL7, causing 3-M syndrome, and CUL4B, linked to X-linked intellectual disability) cause human disease.
