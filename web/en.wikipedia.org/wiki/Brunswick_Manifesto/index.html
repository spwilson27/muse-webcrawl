The Brunswick Manifesto was a proclamation issued by Charles William Ferdinand, Duke of Brunswick, commander of the Austro-Prussian army invading France, on 25 July 1792 during the War of the First Coalition. Drafted largely by émigré advisers, it threatened the people of Paris with dire reprisals if the French royal family were harmed.

Contents

The manifesto demanded that the French submit to King Louis XVI, restore his full authority, and warned that if the Tuileries Palace were stormed or the royal family insulted, the allied armies would subject Paris to "military execution and total destruction" and execute rebellious National Guards. It was intended to intimidate the revolutionaries and protect the king.

Consequences

The manifesto had the opposite effect. News of it reached Paris on 1 August and inflamed public opinion: it was taken as proof that Louis XVI was conspiring with foreign invaders against the Revolution. The fury it provoked helped precipitate the insurrection of 10 August 1792, in which a mob stormed the Tuileries, massacred the Swiss Guards and forced the Legislative Assembly to suspend the king — a decisive step toward the abolition of the monarchy and the proclamation of the French First Republic the following month. Brunswick's army was checked at the Battle of Valmy on 20 September 1792.
