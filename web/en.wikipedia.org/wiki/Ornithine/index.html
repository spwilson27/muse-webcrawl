Ornithine is a non-proteinogenic α-amino acid that plays a role in the urea cycle. Ornithine is abnormally accumulated in the body in ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency. The radical is ornithyl.

Role in the urea cycle

L-Ornithine is one of the products of the action of the enzyme arginase on L-arginine, creating urea. Therefore, ornithine is a central part of the urea cycle, which allows for the disposal of excess nitrogen. Ornithine is recycled and, in a manner, is a catalyst. First, ammonia + CO2 creates carbamoyl phosphate. Ornithine is then converted into a urea derivative by ornithine transcarbamylase, which adds the carbamoyl group to form citrulline, which is released into the cytosol. A second nitrogen (from aspartate) is incorporated on the way to arginine, and arginase then splits arginine into urea and ornithine, closing the cycle.

Other reactions

Ornithine, via the action of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC), can be converted to putrescine, the precursor of the polyamines spermidine and spermine, which are important for cell growth and division. Ornithine is also a starting material for the synthesis of other amino acids and compounds, and it is an intermediate in the biosynthesis of arginine from glutamate in many organisms. In some bacteria and plants, ornithine is used in the synthesis of siderophores and certain antibiotics.

Uses

L-Ornithine is sold as a dietary supplement, often combined with arginine, and has been studied for effects on fatigue and exercise performance. In clinical medicine, L-ornithine L-aspartate (LOLA) is used to treat hepatic encephalopathy because it promotes ammonia detoxification. Elevated blood ornithine occurs in gyrate atrophy of the choroid and retina, caused by ornithine aminotransferase deficiency.
