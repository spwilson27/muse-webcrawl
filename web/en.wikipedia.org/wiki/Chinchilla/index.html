Chinchilla

The long-tailed chinchilla (Chinchilla lanigera)

Chinchillas are either of two species (Chinchilla chinchilla and Chinchilla lanigera) of crepuscular rodents of the parvorder Caviomorpha, native to the Andes Mountains of South America. They live in colonies called "herds" at high elevations up to 4,270 m (14,000 ft). Historically, chinchillas lived in an area that included parts of Bolivia, Peru, Argentina, and Chile, but today, colonies in the wild are known only in Chile.

Named after the Chincha people of the Andes, who wore their soft, dense fur, chinchillas have the densest fur of all land mammals, with up to 20,000 hairs per square centimetre and 50–75 hairs growing from a single follicle. The fur trade drove wild populations to near extinction; both species are now listed as endangered, though chinchillas are widely bred in captivity for the pet trade and for fur farming.

Description

Chinchillas have large ears, big dark eyes, long whiskers, and bushy tails. They weigh between 400 and 800 grams. They are agile jumpers and can leap up to 1.8 metres. To keep their fur clean they take regular dust baths in fine volcanic ash or pumice dust.

Diet and behaviour

Wild chinchillas eat grasses, herbs, and other sparse mountain vegetation. They are social animals living in burrows or rock crevices in herds ranging from a few individuals to over a hundred. Females are the dominant sex and are generally larger than males.

Domestic chinchillas

The domestic chinchilla descends primarily from C. lanigera. Domestication began in the 1920s when engineer Mathias F. Chapman brought eleven chinchillas from Chile to California. Domestic chinchillas are popular exotic pets, prized for their soft fur and lively temperament, and can live 10–20 years in captivity.
