The dirham (Arabic: درهم) is a unit of currency and a historic silver coin used throughout the Islamic world. The name derives from the Greek drachma, transmitted through the Sasanian Persian drahm. Under the caliph Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan, around 696–697 CE, the Umayyad Caliphate introduced a reformed, purely epigraphic silver dirham of standard weight, replacing earlier Arab-Sasanian types that bore rulers' portraits.

History

Dirhams were struck across an enormous area, from al-Andalus in the west to Central Asia in the east, and became the principal trade coin of the early medieval world. Vast hoards of Islamic dirhams have been found in northern Europe, particularly around the Baltic and in Scandinavia, carried there by Viking-age trade routes along the Russian rivers in exchange for furs, slaves and other goods.

In Islamic law and classical jurisprudence the dirham is a unit of weight as well as money, used in calculations of zakat and blood money. The classical dirham weighed about 2.97 grams, with ten dirhams conventionally equalling the weight of seven gold mithqals.

Modern currencies

The dirham survives as the name of the official currencies of Morocco (since 1960) and the United Arab Emirates (since 1973). It is also a subunit of the Libyan dinar (1,000 dirhams), the Qatari riyal (100 dirhams), the Jordanian dinar (1,000 dirhams), and nominally of the Tajikistani somoni (100 dirams).
