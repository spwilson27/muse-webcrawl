The McDonnell Douglas DC-10 is an American trijet wide-body aircraft manufactured by McDonnell Douglas. It has two turbofan engines mounted under the wings and a third mounted at the base of the vertical stabilizer. Developed after American Airlines requested a wide-body smaller than the Boeing 747 but able to operate from shorter runways, the DC-10 first flew in 1970 and entered service in 1971.

Development and variants

The initial DC-10-10 served US domestic routes; the longer-range -30 and -40 series, with an additional centre landing gear, served intercontinental routes and sold in larger numbers. The aircraft competed with the Lockheed L-1011 TriStar. Production ended in 1989 after 386 commercial aircraft; sixty KC-10 Extender aerial tankers were also built for the US Air Force. The stretched, updated derivative was marketed as the MD-11.

Safety record

The DC-10's early reputation was damaged by high-profile accidents linked to design flaws: the outward-opening cargo door caused the crash of Turkish Airlines Flight 981 in 1974 (346 deaths, then the deadliest air disaster in history), and an engine separating from the wing caused American Airlines Flight 191 to crash at Chicago in 1979 (273 deaths), after which the type was briefly grounded worldwide. Later accidents, such as United Airlines Flight 232 at Sioux City in 1989, involved other causes. With the door and maintenance issues corrected, the DC-10's safety record in later service became comparable to other jets of its generation.

Later service

Passenger operations ended in 2014 (Biman Bangladesh Airlines), but converted freighters — marketed as MD-10s with updated cockpits — continued with cargo carriers, and the type has served as an aerial firefighting tanker and as the KC-10 tanker until its US retirement in 2024.
