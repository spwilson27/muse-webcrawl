Roman triumph

The Roman triumph (triumphus) was a civil ceremony and religious rite of ancient Rome, held to publicly celebrate and sanctify the success of a military commander who had led Roman forces to victory. The Senate granted the honour only when strict conditions were met, traditionally including a decisive victory over a foreign enemy with at least 5,000 enemy killed. On the day of the triumph the general (triumphator) became for a day a near-divine, kingly figure at the centre of the city.

The procession

The parade (pompa) assembled outside the city at the Porta Triumphalis and wound through the Circus Maximus and along the Via Sacra to the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill. It displayed spoils of war, paintings and placards depicting the campaign, sacrificial animals, chained captives — sometimes including enemy kings who might be executed afterwards, as were Vercingetorix and Simon bar Giora — and the victorious troops. The triumphator rode in a four-horse chariot wearing the purple and gold toga picta, his face painted red like the statue of Jupiter, a laurel wreath held over his head by a public slave who, according to tradition, whispered memento mori — "remember you are mortal."

History

Triumphs were celebrated from Rome's earliest days; the triumphal Fasti list over 300 from Romulus to Vespasian and Titus. Under the Empire, full triumphs were restricted to the imperial family, and generals received instead the lesser ornamenta triumphalia. The last triumph in Rome was celebrated by Belisarius (or, by some accounts, the ceremony faded with late antiquity). The institution inspired later triumphal arches, Renaissance pageantry, and the imagery of military parades to this day.
