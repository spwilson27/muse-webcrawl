Alpine farming is the traditional system of mountain agriculture practised across the Alps, centred on seasonal transhumance: in summer, cattle, sheep, and goats are driven from valley farms up to high pastures — called alp or alm — where the animals graze species-rich grassland too steep or too high for cultivation, while the valley meadows below are cut for winter hay. In autumn the herds return in the ceremonial descent known in German as the Alpabzug or Viehscheid.

The system

The classic arrangement has three levels: the valley farm (permanent home), an intermediate spring/autumn station (maiensäss or Vorsäss), and the high alp with its huts, stables, and dairy. On the alp, milk — too bulky to transport daily down the mountain — is processed on site into long-keeping products, above all hard cheeses such as Gruyère, Emmental, Bergkäse, and Beaufort, as well as butter. Many alps are operated cooperatively by village associations, with herders and cheesemakers hired for the season.

History

High-pasture grazing in the Alps is documented from prehistory and was systematised in the Middle Ages, when monasteries and peasant communes codified grazing rights, herd sizes, and cheese-sharing in written alp statutes. The system shaped the cultural landscape: centuries of grazing and mowing created the flower-rich alpine meadows that are now recognised as biodiversity hotspots and protected habitats.

Modern situation

Alpine farming has contracted since the mid-20th century as labour costs rise and valley agriculture intensifies; abandoned alps revert to scrub and forest, reducing the open meadow habitat. Switzerland, Austria, and EU mountain regions therefore pay direct subsidies for summer grazing and landscape maintenance, recognising the alps' roles in tourism, avalanche and erosion control, and heritage. Swiss alpine farming culture, including the seasonal descent festivals, has been inscribed in national inventories of intangible cultural heritage.
