Hypovolemia

Intravenous fluids are the mainstay of treatment for hypovolemia.

Hypovolemia, also known as volume depletion or volume contraction, is a state of abnormally low extracellular fluid volume in the body. This may be due to either loss of both salt and water or a decrease in blood volume. Hypovolemia refers to the loss of extracellular fluid and should not be confused with dehydration, which is the loss of body water alone.

Causes

Common causes of hypovolemia include haemorrhage, vomiting, diarrhoea, excessive sweating, burns, excessive urination (as in uncontrolled diabetes or diuretic use), and third-space losses in conditions such as pancreatitis or bowel obstruction. Blood loss is the most immediately dangerous cause because of the loss of oxygen-carrying red blood cells as well as volume.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs include thirst, dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, and postural hypotension. As volume loss increases, patients develop weak pulse, cool extremities, reduced urine output, confusion, and eventually hypovolemic shock, a life-threatening state of inadequate tissue perfusion. In haemorrhage, classes of severity are conventionally described by the fraction of blood volume lost, from class I (under 15%) to class IV (over 40%).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supported by measurements of heart rate, blood pressure, urine output, mental status, and laboratory tests such as urea and creatinine (often with a raised urea-to-creatinine ratio), sodium, and lactate.

Treatment

Treatment consists of stopping ongoing losses and replacing volume, usually with intravenous isotonic crystalloids such as normal saline or lactated Ringer's solution. In haemorrhagic hypovolemia, transfusion of red blood cells, plasma, and platelets may be required, and definitive control of bleeding — by surgery or interventional radiology — is essential.
