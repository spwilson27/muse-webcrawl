Faroese language

Faroese (Faroese: føroyskt mál) is a North Germanic language spoken as a first language by about 72,000 people, around 54,000 of whom reside on the Faroe Islands and 15,000 in Denmark. It is one of five languages descended from Old West Norse spoken in the North Atlantic, the others being Norwegian, Icelandic, and the extinct Norn and Greenlandic Norse.

History

The settlers of the Faroe Islands were predominantly Norse, and their speech developed into Old Faroese, closely related to Old Icelandic and Old Norwegian. Under Danish rule, Danish became the language of church, law, and education, and Faroese survived mainly as a spoken language and in oral ballad traditions. In the nineteenth century, V. U. Hammershaimb devised an etymological orthography for Faroese modelled on Icelandic, first published in 1846, which remains in use.

Status

Faroese became the official language of the Faroe Islands' church in 1938 and of schools in 1938–39; with the Home Rule Act of 1948 it was recognized as the principal language of the Faroe Islands, with Danish retained as a well-taught foreign language. Today Faroese is used in government, media, education, and literature on the islands.

Features

Faroese retains a conservative inflectional system with three genders and four cases, but its pronunciation has diverged considerably from its spelling, with distinctive vowel shifts and diphthongizations. Its closest relative is Icelandic, with which it is partly mutually intelligible in written form.
