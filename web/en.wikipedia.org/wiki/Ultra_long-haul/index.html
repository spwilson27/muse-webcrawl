Ultra long-haul (also known as ULH) refers to the duration of a very long airline flight. The International Air Transport Association (IATA), the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), and many airlines define such flights differently, but most major carriers and aviation publications treat a flight longer than about 12 hours as long-haul, and flights of roughly 16 hours or more as ultra long-haul. Ultra long-haul routes connect distant city pairs nonstop that previously required one or more intermediate stops.

The longest scheduled passenger flights in the world, such as Singapore Airlines' services between Singapore and New York, cover distances of more than 15,000 kilometres (9,300 mi) and take around 18 to 19 hours. The development of fuel-efficient twin-engine wide-body aircraft with very long range, notably the Airbus A350 and the Boeing 777-200LR, made such routes commercially viable.

Definition

There is no single universal definition of flight length categories. IATA has used a definition in which a flight longer than 12 hours is considered ultra long-haul for the purposes of planning crew rest and in-flight service. Other reference points include:

  • Short-haul: typically under 3 hours
  • Medium-haul: roughly 3 to 6 hours
  • Long-haul: roughly 6 to 12 hours
  • Ultra long-haul: over 12 hours, with the longest routes exceeding 18 hours

History

Nonstop flights of extreme length became technically possible with early long-range aircraft, but for decades most intercontinental journeys involved multiple stops. Pan American World Airways operated multi-stop round-the-world services, and aircraft such as the Boeing 747SP of the 1970s enabled unusually long nonstop sectors for their era, including New York to Tokyo.

The modern era of ultra long-haul flying began in the early 2000s. In 2004 Singapore Airlines launched nonstop services from Los Angeles to Singapore and later from Newark to Singapore using the Airbus A340-500; the Newark route, at about 18 hours 45 minutes, was for years the world's longest scheduled flight. Thai Airways operated Bangkok–New York services with the same aircraft type. High fuel prices later made four-engine ultra long-haul operations uneconomical, and several routes were suspended.

A new generation of twin-engine aircraft revived the market. The Boeing 777-200LR, delivered from 2006, offered a range of about 17,400 km, and the Airbus A350-900ULR (Ultra Long Range), introduced in 2018, allowed Singapore Airlines to relaunch the Singapore–New York route, which remains among the longest in the world. Qantas has announced Project Sunrise, using specially configured A350-1000 aircraft to fly nonstop from Sydney to London and New York.

Aircraft

Ultra long-haul routes are flown by aircraft with exceptional range and fuel efficiency:

  • Airbus A350-900ULR – range around 17,960 km; used by Singapore Airlines on its longest routes.
  • Airbus A350-1000 – planned for Qantas Project Sunrise flights with an additional fuel tank.
  • Boeing 777-200LR – former world record holder for the longest range of any commercial airliner.
  • Boeing 787 Dreamliner – used on routes such as Perth–London and Auckland–Chicago.
  • Airbus A380 – used on routes such as Dallas/Fort Worth–Sydney and Dubai–Auckland, though less efficient per seat than newer twins.

Longest routes

Among the longest scheduled nonstop passenger routes by great-circle distance are:

  • New York (JFK) – Singapore, operated by Singapore Airlines, about 15,332 km
  • Newark – Singapore, about 15,344 km
  • Auckland – Doha, operated by Qatar Airways
  • Perth – London Heathrow, operated by Qantas
  • Melbourne – Dallas/Fort Worth, operated by Qantas
  • Auckland – New York (JFK), operated by Qantas and Air New Zealand
  • Singapore – Los Angeles, operated by Singapore Airlines

Passenger health and operations

Flights of 16 hours or more raise particular issues for passenger and crew wellbeing. Concerns include deep vein thrombosis, dehydration, disrupted circadian rhythms and jet lag. Airlines use specialised lighting, meal timing, and cabin humidity and pressure improvements on newer aircraft to mitigate fatigue. Flight crews on ultra long-haul sectors operate with augmented crews—typically four pilots—and dedicated rest facilities so that each pilot can take controlled rest during the flight. Regulatory authorities impose specific flight-time limitations on such operations.

Economically, ultra long-haul flights carry extra fuel to transport the fuel needed later in the flight, which reduces payload; airlines therefore often configure aircraft with fewer seats and a larger proportion of premium cabins to make the routes profitable.
