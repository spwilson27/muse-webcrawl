American Craftsman

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

American Craftsman is an American domestic architectural style, inspired by the Arts and Crafts movement, which flourished between the 1890s and 1930s, spanning the time between Art Nouveau and Art Deco. The style developed in response to the ornate, mass-produced aesthetic of the Victorian era, emphasizing handcrafted quality, natural materials, and harmony with the surrounding landscape.

Origins

The style drew heavily on the work of Gustav Stickley, whose magazine The Craftsman (first published in 1901) popularized plans for modest, well-built homes, and on the California architecture of brothers Charles Sumner Greene and Henry Mather Greene, whose "ultimate bungalows" such as the Gamble House in Pasadena (1908) represented the style's high end. English Arts and Crafts leaders such as William Morris and the earlier work of the British movement strongly influenced American designers, as did Japanese timber architecture.

Characteristics

Typical Craftsman houses feature low-pitched gabled or hipped roofs with wide eaves and exposed rafter tails; deep front porches supported by tapered square columns, often on stone or brick piers; open floor plans with built-in cabinetry, inglenooks, and window seats; and the honest use of natural materials — wood shingles, stone, stucco, and hand-finished woodwork. The one- to one-and-a-half-story bungalow is the most common Craftsman house form, and kit houses sold by companies such as Sears spread the style nationwide.

Legacy

The Craftsman style fell from favor in the late 1920s but has seen renewed popularity since the late 20th century, with many historic bungalows preserved in districts across the United States and new construction borrowing its vocabulary of porches, exposed structure, and natural materials.
