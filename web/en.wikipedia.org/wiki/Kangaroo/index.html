Kangaroo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Kangaroos are marsupials of the family Macropodidae, native to Australia and New Guinea, adapted for hopping locomotion with enormous hind feet, powerful hind legs, a muscular balancing tail and small forelimbs. The red kangaroo is the largest living marsupial, standing up to about 2 metres, and kangaroos can hop at 20–25 km/h (faster in bursts) — an gait that becomes more efficient, not less, with speed, thanks to elastic tendons.

As marsupials, females give birth to a tiny, embryonic joey (about the size of a jellybean) that crawls to the pouch and nurses for months; females can nurse two joeys of different ages on different milks simultaneously and pause a third embryo in reserve (diapause).

Ecology and humans

Four main species number in the tens of millions in good years, culled commercially and by farmers as rangeland competitors; they are Australia's national symbol (on the coat of arms and the airline's tail). Wallabies are the family's smaller members; tree kangaroos returned to the canopy in New Guinea.

Evolution

Kangaroos are the convergent answer to grazing antelope on another continent — a comparison that hardened into the textbook case for convergent evolution. Giant extinct kangaroos, some walking rather than hopping, vanished with Australia's megafauna in the late Pleistocene.
