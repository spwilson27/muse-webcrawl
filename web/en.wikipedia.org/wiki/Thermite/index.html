Thermite

[Hero image placeholder: a thermite reaction producing molten iron and brilliant sparks.]

Thermite is a pyrotechnic composition of metal powder and metal oxide. When ignited by heat or chemical reaction, thermite undergoes an exothermic reduction-oxidation (redox) reaction. Most varieties are not explosive, but can create brief bursts of heat and high temperature in a small area. Its form of action is similar to other fuel-oxidizer combinations.

Iron thermite

The most common composition is iron(III) oxide with aluminium powder: Fe2O3 + 2 Al → 2 Fe + Al2O3. The reaction can reach temperatures around 2,500 °C (4,500 °F) and produces molten iron. Developed by Hans Goldschmidt in 1893 and patented in 1895, the process ("Goldschmidt process" or aluminothermy) is widely used for welding railway rails and repairing heavy machinery in situ.

Other types and uses

Other oxides, such as copper(II) oxide, chromium(III) oxide and manganese dioxide, form thermites with different characteristics; copper thermite burns extremely fast and is used in some pyrotechnic and welding applications. Thermite has also been used in incendiary weapons, in metal refining, and for disabling machinery. Because it needs no external oxygen and burns extremely hot, it is very difficult to extinguish.
