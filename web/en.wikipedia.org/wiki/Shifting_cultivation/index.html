Shifting cultivation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A plot cleared by slash-and-burn, the best-known form of shifting cultivation.

Shifting cultivation is an agricultural system in which plots of land are cultivated temporarily, then abandoned while post-disturbance fallow vegetation is allowed to grow freely. Cultivators typically clear a field by slash-and-burn methods, farm it for a few years until soil fertility declines or weeds and pests become unmanageable, and then move on to a new plot, leaving the old one to regenerate during a long fallow period. The practice is also known by many local names, including swidden agriculture, jhum in South Asia, milpa in parts of Mesoamerica, and chitemene in parts of Africa.

Description

In a typical system, a household clears and burns a patch of forest or bush. The ash provides a nutrient pulse that supports one to three (sometimes more) years of crops such as upland rice, maize, cassava, yams, or plantains, often grown in mixed intercropped stands. When yields fall, the plot is abandoned to fallow for ten to twenty-five years in traditional systems, during which secondary vegetation restores organic matter and nutrient stocks. Because only a fraction of a community's land is under crops at any time, shifting cultivation historically supported low population densities over large areas, particularly in the humid tropics of Africa, Southeast Asia, and Latin America.

Environmental debate

Shifting cultivation has long been blamed for deforestation and soil erosion, and many governments have sought to suppress or sedentarize it. Researchers note, however, that at low population densities with long fallows the system can be sustainable and can maintain mosaics of habitat that support biodiversity. Problems arise mainly when population pressure, land expropriation, or commercial encroachment shortens fallows faster than the soil can recover, leading to degraded grassland such as Imperata savannas. Total carbon emissions from traditional swidden cycles are partly offset by regrowth during fallow, though the practice remains controversial in climate policy debates.

Alternatives and development

Proposed alternatives and improvements include improved fallows with planted nitrogen-fixing trees and shrubs, agroforestry systems, alley cropping, and integration with tree cash crops. Anthropologists have also emphasized that shifting cultivators often possess detailed ecological knowledge and that swidden plots can harbor high agrobiodiversity, including many landraces of staple crops.
