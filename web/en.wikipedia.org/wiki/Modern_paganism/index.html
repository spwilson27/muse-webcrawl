Modern paganism

Modern paganism, also known as contemporary paganism or neopaganism, is a collective term for new religious movements influenced by or claiming to revive the pre-Christian beliefs of Europe, North Africa and the Near East. Practitioners are often polytheistic or nature-venerating, and most celebrate seasonal festivals based on the Wheel of the Year. Major traditions include Wicca, Heathenry, Druidry and Hellenism.

History

Romanticism and folklore scholarship revived interest in pre-Christian religion in the 19th century. In the 1950s Gerald Gardner publicised Wicca in England after the repeal of the Witchcraft Act, claiming initiation into a surviving coven; parallel revivals produced Ásatrú/Heathenry in Iceland and Scandinavia, Druid orders, and reconstructionist movements in Greece, Rome, Egypt and Slavic and Baltic lands from the 1970s onward.

Beliefs and practices

Common threads include reverence for nature, divinity as immanent and often plural or dual (Goddess and God in Wicca), magic in some traditions, and ethics such as the Wiccan Rede. Rituals mark eight sabbats and esbats (full moons); many practitioners are solitary "eclectic" pagans assembling personal practice from books and the internet. Scholars debate continuity claims, preferring "inspired by" to literal survival.

Demographics

Estimates are difficult; surveys suggest hundreds of thousands to over a million adherents in the US and UK combined, with growth among younger demographics. Modern paganism is legally recognised in several countries, with pagan chaplains in prisons, hospitals and armed forces.
