Bowling Green is a small public park in the Financial District of Lower Manhattan, New York City, at the southern end of Broadway. The park, an ellipse enclosed by an 18th-century cast-iron fence, is the oldest public park in New York City. Originally part of a parade ground and market site laid out in the Dutch colonial era, the land was leased in 1733 to local landlords for use as a bowling green — hence its name. At its northern apex stands the Charging Bull, the bronze statue by Arturo Di Modica that has become an icon of the nearby financial district.

History

The site served in the 17th century as a cattle market and parade ground outside Fort Amsterdam, at the foot of the road that became Broadway. In 1733 the city leased the green to three prominent citizens for a peppercorn rent of one peppercorn per year, with the understanding that it be maintained as a bowling green "for the beauty and ornament of the said street" and the recreation of the inhabitants. It remained in private hands as a leased amenity until 1770, when the colony erected an equestrian statue of King George III there.

On July 9, 1776, after the Declaration of Independence was read to Continental troops in New York, a crowd pulled down the gilded lead statue of George III at Bowling Green and later melted most of it into musket balls — tradition holds some 42,088 of them. Fragments of the statue survive in museum collections, and the statue's tail was famously recovered and preserved.

The fence and the statue of George III

The cast-iron fence surrounding the park was erected in 1771 to protect the king's statue. During the Revolution, the small crowns that topped its posts were sawn off by patriots — the cut marks are still described in park lore — yet the fence itself survives, making it one of the oldest fences in the city. The fence and park are designated New York City landmarks, and the park is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Where the statue stood, a fountain was later installed; the present fountain dates from a 20th-century restoration of the green.

Charging Bull

On December 15, 1989, sculptor Arturo Di Modica deposited his 3.5-ton bronze Charging Bull beneath the Christmas tree in front of the New York Stock Exchange as a piece of guerrilla art symbolising American resilience after the 1987 stock market crash. Impounded by police, the bull was returned amid public demand and installed at Bowling Green, where it became one of the most photographed sculptures in the city. In 2017 the Fearless Girl statue was placed facing the bull (later moved to face the Stock Exchange), sparking debate about the alteration of Di Modica's work's meaning.

Surroundings

Bowling Green marks the traditional starting point of ticker-tape parades up Broadway's "Canyon of Heroes." Around it stand the Alexander Hamilton U.S. Custom House (now home to the National Museum of the American Indian), the Bowling Green subway station of 1905, and some of the city's oldest surviving commercial buildings along lower Broadway and State Street. Evacuation Day (November 25, 1783), marking the departure of British troops, was celebrated nearby at Fraunces Tavern and Bowling Green, where the last British flag was famously replaced by the American flag on a greased pole.
