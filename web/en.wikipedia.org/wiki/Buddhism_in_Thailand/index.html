Buddhism in Thailand

Buddhism in Thailand is predominantly of the Theravada tradition, followed by about 93–95% of the population, making Thailand one of the most Buddhist countries in the world by proportion. Thai Buddhism is deeply integrated into Thai culture and daily life, and the country's temples (wat) serve as centres of community life, education and welfare. Thailand has the second-largest Buddhist population in the world after China.

History

Buddhism is believed to have been introduced to the region as early as the 3rd century BCE by missionaries sent by the Indian emperor Ashoka. Over the centuries, Theravada Buddhism from Sri Lanka became dominant during the Sukhothai Kingdom (13th century), when King Ramkhamhaeng promoted the Sri Lankan (Lankavamsa) tradition. Successive kingdoms — Ayutthaya and then the Chakri dynasty's Rattanakosin kingdom — maintained close ties between the monarchy and the sangha (monastic community).

Organisation

Thai Buddhism is administered by the Sangha Supreme Council, headed by the Sangharaja (Supreme Patriarch), under the framework of the Sangha Acts. There are two principal monastic orders (nikaya): the majority Maha Nikaya and the smaller, stricter Dhammayuttika Nikaya, founded by Prince Mongkut (later King Rama IV) in the 19th century. Most Thai men traditionally ordain as monks for a temporary period at least once in their lives, a practice believed to generate merit for themselves and their families.

Practices and influence

Thai Buddhist practice blends orthodox Theravada doctrine with merit-making (tham bun), veneration of amulets, and elements of Brahmanism and folk religion, including spirit houses and astrology. Major festivals include Magha Puja, Visakha Puja, Asalha Puja and the end of the rains retreat (Wan Ok Phansa), as well as the Kathina robe-offering season. The Thai Forest Tradition, associated with Ajahn Sao, Ajahn Mun and Ajahn Chah, has gained an international following for its emphasis on meditation and ascetic practice.
