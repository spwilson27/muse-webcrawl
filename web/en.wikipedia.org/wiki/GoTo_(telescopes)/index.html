GoTo (telescopes)

In amateur astronomy, GoTo refers to a type of computerised telescope mount and related software that automatically points a telescope at astronomical objects selected by the user. After an initial alignment procedure, the observer chooses an object from a hand-controller database — often containing tens of thousands of stars, galaxies, nebulae and planets — and motors slew the telescope to it and then track it across the sky.

How it works

A GoTo system combines motor drives on both axes of the mount (altitude and azimuth for alt-az mounts, or right ascension and declination for equatorial mounts), shaft encoders or stepper/servo motors, and a hand controller containing a catalogue database and a model of the sky. During setup the user points the telescope at two or three known bright stars; the controller uses these reference points to build a pointing model that accounts for the mount's orientation, location, date and time. Modern systems may use GPS receivers and electronic compasses to automate much of this, and some use plate solving — matching a camera image of the star field — to align themselves fully automatically.

History

Computerised pointing became commercially significant in the 1990s with telescopes such as Meade's LX200 series and Celestron's NexStar line, which brought GoTo to mainstream amateur astronomy. Related "push-to" systems use encoders and a display to tell the user which direction to move the telescope manually, without motors.

Reception

Supporters note that GoTo mounts let observers in light-polluted areas find faint objects quickly and spend more time observing than searching. Critics argue that automatic pointing discourages beginners from learning the night sky and star-hopping techniques, and adds cost and power requirements. GoTo functionality is now standard on many mid-range telescopes and is also used, in more sophisticated forms, by professional observatories.
