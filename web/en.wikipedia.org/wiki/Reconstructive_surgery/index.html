Reconstructive surgery

Reconstructive surgery is the branch of surgery concerned with restoring the form and function of body parts damaged by trauma, disease, infection, tumours or congenital abnormalities. It forms one of the two main divisions of plastic surgery, the other being cosmetic (aesthetic) surgery; unlike cosmetic procedures, reconstructive operations are generally regarded as medically necessary.

Techniques

Reconstruction follows the "reconstructive ladder", choosing the simplest effective option: healing by secondary intention, direct closure, skin grafting, local tissue rearrangement and flaps, and finally free tissue transfer. Modern practice relies heavily on microsurgery, in which free flaps of skin, muscle or bone are transferred with their blood vessels and reconnected under the microscope, and on perforator-flap techniques that spare underlying muscle. Skin expanders, bone distraction and, more recently, vascularised composite allotransplantation — face and hand transplants — extend what can be rebuilt.

Areas of practice

Major areas include breast reconstruction after mastectomy; head and neck reconstruction after cancer surgery; treatment of burns; hand and limb salvage after injury; correction of cleft lip and palate and other craniofacial anomalies; and repair of pressure sores and complex wounds.

History

Reconstructive techniques are described in ancient Indian texts, including the forehead-flap rhinoplasty of the Sushruta Samhita. The field expanded dramatically during the two World Wars, when surgeons such as Harold Gillies and Archibald McIndoe developed systematic methods for rebuilding the faces of wounded servicemen.
