Liquid–liquid extraction

Liquid–liquid extraction

Also known as: Solvent extraction, partitioning

Field: Separation processes, chemical engineering

Key parameter: Distribution coefficient (KD)

Liquid–liquid extraction, also known as solvent extraction and partitioning, is a method to separate compounds or metal complexes, based on their relative solubilities in two different immiscible liquids, usually water (polar) and an organic solvent (non-polar). There is a net transfer of one or more species from one liquid phase to another, generally from aqueous to organic. The transfer is driven by chemical potential; once the transfer is complete, the chemical potential of the transferred species is equal in both phases.

Principles

The distribution of a solute between two phases is described by the Nernst distribution law. The distribution coefficient KD is the ratio of the solute's concentration in the organic phase to its concentration in the aqueous phase at equilibrium. The efficiency of an extraction depends on this coefficient, the relative volumes of the phases, and the number of extraction stages. Countercurrent extraction cascades, such as mixer-settler batteries and extraction columns, are used industrially to achieve high recoveries.

Applications

Liquid–liquid extraction is used in many industries: in hydrometallurgy for recovering copper, uranium, and rare earth elements; in nuclear reprocessing (for example the PUREX process); in the petrochemical industry for separating aromatics; in pharmaceutical production; and in analytical chemistry and laboratory work using a separatory funnel.
