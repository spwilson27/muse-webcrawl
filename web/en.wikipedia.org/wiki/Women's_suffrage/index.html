Women's suffrage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Women's suffrage is the right of women to vote in elections. Beginning in the late 19th century, it was won country by country through campaigns of petitioning, protest, civil disobedience and, in some places, militancy — and through the political calculations those campaigns forced. New Zealand (1893) was the first self-governing country to grant it; Finland (1906) was first in Europe and first to let women also stand for parliament. Many states followed around the First World War (Canada, Germany, Poland, the UK in 1918 (restricted) and 1928 (equal), the US by the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920), France and Italy after the Second World War (1944/45), Switzerland at federal level only in 1971, and Saudi Arabia for municipal elections in 2015 — leaving near-universal formal suffrage today.

Movements

The organised movements — suffragists and, in Britain, the militant suffragettes of Emmeline Pankhurst's WSPU (founded 1903), with hunger strikes and forcible feeding — reframed citizenship itself. Counter-arguments invoked separate spheres, family representation and alleged incapacity; enfranchisement's effects have been studied ever since (women's turnout and policy preferences, welfare-state expansion, and the persistence of representation gaps that later motivated quotas). Historians also note suffrage's entanglement with race and empire: in settler democracies, white women's enfranchisement sometimes preceded that of indigenous men and women.
