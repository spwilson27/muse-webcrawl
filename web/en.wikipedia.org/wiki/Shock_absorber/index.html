Shock absorber

A shock absorber or damper is a mechanical or hydraulic device designed to absorb and damp shock impulses — smoothing out sudden jolts and dissipating kinetic energy, usually as heat. In vehicles, shock absorbers keep the tyres in contact with the road, improve ride comfort and handling, and form a key part of the suspension system alongside springs, which carry the load and store the energy that the damper dissipates.

How it works

Most automotive shock absorbers are hydraulic: a piston attached to a rod moves inside an oil-filled cylinder, forcing oil through small orifices and spring-loaded valves in the piston. The resistance to this flow converts the suspension's motion into heat. Damping forces are typically greater in rebound (extension) than in compression. Common designs are the twin-tube damper and the gas-pressurised monotube damper, in which a charge of nitrogen reduces foaming (aeration) of the oil. In a MacPherson strut suspension the damper is a structural part of the strut itself.

Other applications

Dampers are used in many other settings: oleo struts in aircraft landing gear, seismic dampers that protect buildings and bridges in earthquakes, door closers, recoil mechanisms in artillery, and bump stops and buffers in railway rolling stock. Related devices that resist velocity include viscous dampers and friction dampers; a tuned mass damper, such as those in skyscrapers, counteracts oscillation of the whole structure.
