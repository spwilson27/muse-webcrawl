Protectorate

A protectorate, in its modern political meaning, is a territory that is protected diplomatically or militarily by a stronger state or entity. In exchange, the protectorate usually accepts specified obligations, often including the surrender of control over its foreign relations to the protecting power, while retaining a degree of internal self-government. The term is distinct from a colony, where sovereignty is formally assumed by the ruling power, and from a protected state, in which an established state keeps more of its own institutions.

History

Protectorates became a common instrument of European imperial expansion in the 19th century, allowing powers to control territories without the costs of direct annexation. British protectorates included Bechuanaland (now Botswana), Northern Rhodesia, Uganda and, for a time, Egypt (1914–1922). France administered protectorates in Tunisia and Morocco, while Germany and Italy used similar arrangements in Africa. The term was also used for the British Protectorate of 1653–1659 under Oliver Cromwell, an unrelated usage in which the head of state was styled Lord Protector.

International law

Historically, agreements establishing protectorates were often concluded under unequal conditions, and many were later judged inconsistent with the principle of self-determination. After 1945, surviving arrangements mostly evolved into associated statehood, free association, or integration into UN trust territories. A few modern dependencies and special jurisdictions have sometimes been described as de facto protectorates.

Examples

  • Bechuanaland Protectorate (1885–1966), now Botswana
  • British Protectorate of Egypt (1914–1922)
  • French Protectorate of Morocco (1912–1956)
  • Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia under Nazi Germany (1939–1945)
