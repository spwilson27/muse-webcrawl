Geiger–Marsden experiments

The Geiger–Marsden experiments were a series of experiments conducted between 1908 and 1913 by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden in Ernest Rutherford's laboratory at the University of Manchester, investigating the scattering of alpha particles by thin metal foils. They led to the discovery of the atomic nucleus and the Rutherford model of the atom.

Sequence of experiments

In 1908 Geiger showed that alpha particles passing through foils were scattered slightly. In 1909, at Rutherford's suggestion, Geiger and Marsden looked for large-angle reflection and found that a small fraction of particles bounced back from a platinum plate. Rutherford's 1911 theoretical paper explained the angular distribution by scattering from a point-like central charge. In 1913 Geiger and Marsden published detailed measurements verifying Rutherford's scattering formula quantitatively, varying foil material, particle energy and angle.

Significance

The experiments overthrew the plum pudding model, established that the atom's mass and positive charge are concentrated in a nucleus roughly ten thousand times smaller than the atom, and founded experimental nuclear physics. The 1913 verification made Rutherford's model the working picture of atomic structure, soon refined by Niels Bohr's quantum model.
