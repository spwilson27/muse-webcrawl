New Left

The New Left was a broad political movement that emerged in the 1960s, mainly in Western Europe and North America, consisting of activists and intellectuals who sought to broaden the focus of left-wing politics beyond the labour and economic concerns of the "Old Left." New Leftists emphasized civil rights, feminism, environmentalism, opposition to war, participatory democracy, and liberation movements in the developing world, while generally distancing themselves from Soviet-style communism and orthodox Marxist party structures.

Origins

The term gained currency after 1956, when the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian Revolution and Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" disillusioned many Western communists. In Britain, the New Left Review (founded 1960) gave the movement an intellectual home; thinkers such as E. P. Thompson and Raymond Williams shaped its cultural analysis. Sociologist C. Wright Mills popularized the term in his 1960 "Letter to the New Left."

United States

In the United States the New Left grew out of the civil rights movement and campus activism. Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), founded in 1960, articulated its ideals in the Port Huron Statement (1962), drafted principally by Tom Hayden, which called for "participatory democracy." As the Vietnam War escalated, the movement became a mass anti-war force, and it intertwined with the counterculture of the 1960s, the women's liberation movement, and Black Power. By the end of the decade the movement fragmented amid repression and factionalism; a small fringe, the Weather Underground, turned to violence.

Europe

European New Left movements crested in the events of May 1968 in France and student movements in West Germany (where Rudi Dutschke was a leading figure) and elsewhere. The "extra-parliamentary opposition" and autonomous movements of the 1970s carried forward parts of its legacy, as did later Green parties.

Legacy

The New Left permanently shifted progressive politics toward issues of identity, culture, environment, and direct democracy, and deeply influenced academia through cultural studies, critical theory, and feminist and postcolonial scholarship. Critics, including many on the Old Left, charged it with abandoning class politics and political economy.
