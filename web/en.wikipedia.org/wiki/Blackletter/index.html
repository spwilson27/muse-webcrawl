Blackletter, also known as Gothic script, is a script that was used throughout Western Europe from approximately 1150 until well into the 17th century, and in Germany — in forms such as Fraktur — until the mid-20th century. It is characterised by dense, dark, angular letterforms with heavy vertical strokes.

Forms and development

Blackletter developed from Carolingian minuscule as scribes compressed letterforms to save expensive parchment. Its principal grades include the highly formal Textura used for liturgical books, the rounded southern Rotunda, cursive hands used for documents, and the later German printed types Schwabacher and Fraktur. Gutenberg's Bible was printed in a Textura type imitating manuscript hands.

Decline and survival

Humanist scholars of the Renaissance derided the script as "Gothic" (barbarous) and promoted the roman typefaces that became standard across most of Europe. Germany retained Fraktur for German-language text until 1941, when the Nazi regime abruptly banned it as allegedly "Jewish". Today blackletter survives in decorative uses: newspaper mastheads such as The New York Times, diplomas, signage — including London's Blackfriars area pubs — tattoo lettering and heavy-metal artwork. In English law, "black letter law" is a related metaphor for settled basic principles.
