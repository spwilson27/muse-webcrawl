Inelastic collision

An inelastic collision, in contrast to an elastic collision, is a collision in which kinetic energy is not conserved because of the action of internal friction. In collisions of macroscopic bodies, some kinetic energy is converted into vibrational energy of the atoms, producing heat, and the bodies are deformed. Total momentum, however, is always conserved in the absence of external forces.

Perfectly inelastic collisions

In a perfectly inelastic collision, the colliding objects stick together after impact and move with a common final velocity, maximising the loss of kinetic energy consistent with conservation of momentum. A ballistic pendulum, used to measure the speed of a bullet, exploits this: the bullet embeds in a suspended block, and the height of the swing gives the combined velocity. Everyday examples include car crashes (where crumple zones deliberately make collisions inelastic to absorb energy) and raindrops coalescing as they fall.

Coefficient of restitution

The degree of inelasticity can be quantified by the coefficient of restitution, the ratio of the speed of separation to the speed of approach along the line of impact. It equals 1 for a perfectly elastic collision and 0 for a perfectly inelastic one.

Partially inelastic collisions

Most real collisions are partially inelastic: the objects separate after impact but with less kinetic energy than before, the difference appearing as heat, sound and permanent deformation.
