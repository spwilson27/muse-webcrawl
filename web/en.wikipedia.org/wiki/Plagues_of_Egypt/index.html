The Plagues of Egypt (Hebrew: מכות מצרים), in the account of the Book of Exodus, are ten disasters inflicted on Egypt by God (Yahweh) in order to convince the Pharaoh to emancipate the enslaved Israelites, each of them confronting Pharaoh and one of his Egyptian gods. The plagues serve in the biblical narrative as "signs and marvels" given by God to answer Pharaoh's taunt that he did not know Yahweh.

The ten plagues

The plagues, in order, are: (1) water turning to blood; (2) frogs; (3) lice (or gnats); (4) flies (or wild animals); (5) pestilence on livestock; (6) boils; (7) hail and fire; (8) locusts; (9) darkness for three days; and (10) the death of the firstborn. After the tenth plague, Pharaoh releases the Israelites, setting in motion the Exodus, commemorated in the festival of Passover, at whose Seder the plagues are recited.

Interpretation

Scholars have long debated the composition and historicity of the account. Some propose naturalistic explanations — for instance, a chain of ecological events following a red tide in the Nile, or effects of the Thera volcanic eruption — while many scholars treat the plagues as a theological and literary construction of the biblical authors. In the narrative, the earlier plagues are initially replicated by Pharaoh's magicians, and God is said to "harden Pharaoh's heart" so that the demonstration of divine power may continue.
