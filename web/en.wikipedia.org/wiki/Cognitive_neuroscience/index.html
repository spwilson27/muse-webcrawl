Cognitive neuroscience

Cognitive neuroscience is the scientific study of the neural mechanisms underlying cognition — the brain processes that support perception, attention, memory, language, learning, decision-making and consciousness. It bridges cognitive psychology and neuroscience, asking how mental functions are produced by neural circuits. The term was coined in the late 1970s by Michael Gazzaniga and George Armitage Miller.

History

The field's roots lie in nineteenth-century neurology and phrenology's localisation debates, the case studies of Paul Broca and Carl Wernicke on language, and twentieth-century neuropsychology of brain-injured patients, such as the famous amnesic patient H.M. Its modern form emerged when non-invasive brain imaging — PET in the 1980s and functional MRI in the 1990s — made it possible to observe activity in the healthy living brain during cognitive tasks.

Methods

Methods include electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) for millisecond timing, fMRI and PET for spatial localisation, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) to test causal roles of brain regions, single-unit recording and optogenetics in animals, lesion studies, and computational modelling. A central concern is linking levels of analysis from synapses and neurons to cognitive theory.
