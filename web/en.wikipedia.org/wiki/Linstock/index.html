Linstock

A linstock (also called a lintstock) is a staff with a fork at its end to hold a lighted slow match. The name was adapted from the Dutch lonstok, "match stick". Linstocks were used for discharging cannons in the early days of artillery; the cannoneer stood well to one side of the gun while firing, avoiding the recoil, and the long staff kept the gunner's hand away from the vent hole and its flash of priming powder.

Description

The linstock typically consisted of a wooden staff about three to four feet long with iron prongs at the end forming a fork or pair of jaws, sometimes fitted with a screw or spring clip to grip the slow match — a cord soaked in saltpetre that burned slowly and steadily. Some linstocks had a pointed iron butt so that the staff could be stuck upright in the ground or deck when not in use.

Use

To fire the cannon, the touch hole was primed with fine powder, and the gunner applied the glowing end of the match held in the linstock. The linstock allowed the gunner to stand clear of the barrel's recoil path. As artillery technology advanced — through wheel locks, flintlock firing mechanisms, friction tubes and percussion systems — the linstock became obsolete, though it survived longest in naval and garrison service.

Heraldry and legacy

The linstock appears as a charge in heraldry, particularly in arms associated with artillery and ordnance. The Master Gunner of the British Royal Artillery's emblem and the crossed linstocks in some ordnance insignia recall the tool's long service.
