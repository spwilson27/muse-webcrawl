Saccharin is an artificial sweetener with effectively no caloric food energy. It is about 300–400 times as sweet as sucrose but has a bitter or metallic aftertaste, especially at high concentrations. Saccharin is used to sweeten products such as drinks, candies, cookies, and toothpaste. It is one of the oldest artificial sweeteners, having been discovered in 1879, and it remains in wide use, commonly sold under brand names such as Sweet'N Low.

History

Saccharin was discovered in 1879 by chemists Constantin Fahlberg and Ira Remsen at Johns Hopkins University, while working on coal-tar derivatives. Fahlberg noticed a sweet taste on his hands after work and traced it to the compound benzoic sulfimide. The discovery led to a patent dispute, as Fahlberg filed patents in his own name. Saccharin quickly became commercially important, particularly during sugar shortages in the World Wars.

In the early 20th century, U.S. food-safety chief Harvey Wiley sought to restrict its use, but President Theodore Roosevelt, who used saccharin on medical advice, intervened. Saccharin was one of the first substances reviewed under the U.S. Food and Drug Administration regime.

Safety controversy

In the 1970s, studies in rats linked high doses of saccharin to bladder cancer, leading Canada to ban it and the United States to require warning labels under the Saccharin Study and Labeling Act of 1977. Subsequent research showed the rat mechanism— involving urinary proteins and high pH—does not apply to humans. In 2000, the U.S. National Toxicology Program delisted saccharin as a carcinogen, and Congress repealed the warning-label requirement. Saccharin is approved for use in more than 100 countries.

Chemistry

Saccharin is a benzoic sulfimide. It is poorly soluble in water, so it is usually used as its sodium salt, sodium saccharin, which dissolves readily. It is stable when heated, giving it a long shelf life, though it is often blended with other sweeteners such as aspartame or sucralose to mask its aftertaste and round out sweetness profiles. Unlike sugar substitutes such as stevia extracts, saccharin is entirely synthetic.

Uses

Saccharin is used in soft drinks, tabletop sweeteners, baked goods, jams, chewing gum, canned fruit, and personal-care products such as toothpaste and mouthwash. Because it passes through the human digestive system unchanged, it contributes no calories, making it popular in products aimed at people managing diabetes or body weight. People with phenylketonuria may use saccharin freely, unlike aspartame, which contains phenylalanine.
