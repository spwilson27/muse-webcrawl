Brassica rapa is a plant species growing in various widely cultivated forms including the turnip (a root vegetable); napa cabbage, bok choy, and komatsuna (leaf vegetables); and Brassica rapa subsp. oleifera, an oilseed commonly known in English as turnip rape, field mustard, or keblock. The plant is one of the most important vegetable and oilseed crops in the world, particularly in Asia and Europe.

Description

Brassica rapa is typically an annual or biennial herb with a stout taproot, hairy or glabrous basal leaves, and bright yellow four-petalled flowers characteristic of the cabbage family. Its seeds are small, rounded, and dark. The species has been selected over millennia into morphologically distinct crop types: swollen-root forms (turnips), leafy forms (Chinese cabbages and pak choi types), and oilseed forms grown for their seed oil.

Cultivars and subspecies

Different cultivar groups are recognised, including subsp. rapa (turnip), subsp. pekinensis (napa cabbage), subsp. chinensis (bok choy), subsp. perviridis (komatsuna/tendergreen), subsp. rapifera, and subsp. oleifera (oilseed rape types). It is closely related to Brassica napus (rapeseed/canola) and Brassica oleracea (cabbage and relatives); together these species form the classic "triangle of U" model of Brassica evolution described by Woo Jang-choon.

Cultivation and uses

Turnips are eaten as a root vegetable and their tops as greens; leafy forms are staples of East Asian cuisines, used in stir-fries, soups and pickles such as kimchi. Oilseed forms are crushed for a vegetable oil and the remaining meal used as animal feed. Brassica rapa is also a model organism in plant biology: the rapid-cycling "Fast Plants" lines complete their life cycle in about six weeks and are widely used in teaching and research.
