Granulation tissue

Granulation tissue is the new connective tissue and microscopic blood vessels that form on the surface of a wound during the proliferative phase of healing. Its name comes from its granular appearance, caused by loops of new capillaries showing through the tissue as small red points. Healthy granulation tissue is bright red or pink, moist, and slightly bumpy, and its presence is a sign that a wound is healing normally.

Composition

Granulation tissue consists of new capillaries formed by angiogenesis, embedded in a loose extracellular matrix of collagen (initially type III), fibronectin, and hyaluronic acid, together with fibroblasts, macrophages, and other inflammatory cells. Growth factors such as VEGF and PDGF, released by macrophages and platelets, drive vessel growth and fibroblast proliferation.

Role in healing

Beginning a few days after injury, granulation tissue fills the wound bed, provides a scaffold over which epithelial cells migrate, and contracts the wound through the action of myofibroblasts. Over time it matures into scar tissue as collagen is remodelled and the vascularity decreases.

Clinical problems

Granulation tissue may be deficient in chronic wounds such as venous and diabetic ulcers, appearing pale and fragile. Conversely, excessive "proud flesh" — exuberant granulation, particularly common in horse wounds — rises above the skin surface and prevents epithelialisation, requiring trimming or cauterisation. Infection produces pale, friable tissue that bleeds easily and delays healing.
