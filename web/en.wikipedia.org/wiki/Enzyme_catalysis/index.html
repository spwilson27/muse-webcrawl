Enzyme catalysis

Enzyme catalysis is the increase in the rate of a chemical process by an enzyme, a mostly protein-based biological catalyst. By binding a substrate in an active site and stabilising the reaction's transition state, enzymes lower activation energy and can accelerate reactions by factors of millions to trillions, all under the mild conditions of the cell.

Mechanisms

Enzymes use the same chemical strategies as other catalysts, combined with precise positioning: acid–base catalysis by amino acid side chains, covalent catalysis through transient intermediates (as in the serine proteases' acyl-enzyme), metal-ion catalysis, and catalysis by approximation and orientation, which pays the entropic cost of bringing reactants together. Binding energy from noncovalent interactions with the transition state is the principal source of rate enhancement, as articulated in the transition-state stabilisation model that succeeded the older lock-and-key picture; the induced-fit model of Daniel Koshland describes how substrate binding reorganises the active site.

Kinetics

Enzyme kinetics is commonly described by the Michaelis–Menten equation, relating reaction rate to substrate concentration through the parameters Vmax and Km. Inhibitors may be competitive, non-competitive or uncompetitive; allosteric enzymes deviate from Michaelis–Menten behaviour and are central to metabolic regulation.

Specificity and evolution

Enzymes typically discriminate strongly between substrates, though many show useful promiscuity exploited in biotechnology. Directed evolution of enzymes — recognised by the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Frances Arnold — now produces catalysts for industrial processes, and enzymes are used in detergents, food processing, pharmaceuticals and biofuel production.
