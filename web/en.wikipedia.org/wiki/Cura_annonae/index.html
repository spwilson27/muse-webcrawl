Cura annonae


Measuring out the grain dole in Rome

Cura annonae ("care of the grain supply") was the term for the administration of the supply and distribution of grain — and later bread, olive oil, wine, and pork — to the population of the city of Rome, and later to Constantinople, within the Roman Empire. The term also refers by extension to the grain dole itself (the annona), one of the defining institutions of Roman public life and a source of the phrase "bread and circuses".

History

Subsidised grain sales began in the late Republic: the tribune Gaius Gracchus established regular sales below market price in 123 BC, and in 58 BC Clodius made the distribution free. The number of recipients, the plebs frumentaria, was capped by Augustus at roughly 200,000 adult male citizens, identified by tokens (tesserae). Under the Empire the prefect of the annona (praefectus annonae), an equestrian official of high rank, supervised the whole supply chain: grain fleets from Egypt, Africa Proconsularis and Sicily, the harbour at Ostia and Portus, state granaries (horrea), and the milling and baking trade.

Under the later Empire

Aurelian converted the grain dole into a daily bread ration and added pork and oil distributions; Septimius Severus had earlier added olive oil. When Constantinople became the capital, it received its own annona supplied by Egyptian grain, and the institution — and the politically vital need to keep the capital fed — survived in the East after the Western system collapsed in the fifth century. The loss of North Africa to the Vandals and later the Arab conquest of Egypt progressively dismantled the system.
