Eugenics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A 1920s eugenics exhibition poster with the slogan about heredity, typical of the era's state-fair displays]
A 1920s eugenics exhibition poster with the slogan about heredity, typical of the era's state-fair displays

Eugenics is a set of beliefs and practices aimed at improving the genetic quality of a human population, typically by excluding people and groups judged inferior from reproduction and promoting those judged superior. Coined by Francis Galton in 1883, eugenics became an influential international movement in the early twentieth century — shaping immigration law, compulsory sterilisation programmes and marriage restrictions — before its association with Nazi Germany's racial policies and the Holocaust discredited it as pseudoscience and atrocity.

Rise

Galton, Darwin's cousin, drew on animal breeding and his studies of hereditary genius. 'Positive' eugenics encouraged the 'fit' to breed; 'negative' eugenics sought to prevent the 'unfit' — the disabled, the mentally ill, the poor, racial minorities — from reproducing. Indiana passed the first compulsory sterilisation law in 1907; the US Supreme Court upheld Virginia's in Buck v. Bell (1927, Justice Holmes: 'three generations of imbeciles are enough'); some 60,000–70,000 Americans were sterilised. Sweden sterilised about 63,000 people (1934–75), and Canada, Japan and other democracies had programmes.

Nazi Germany and discrediting

German 'racial hygiene' radicalised the programme: the 1933 sterilisation law (modelled partly on American statutes) sterilised some 400,000; the Aktion T4 'euthanasia' programme murdered 200,000–300,000 disabled people; and eugenic antisemitism supplied the 'scientific' rationale of the Holocaust. After 1945, the UNESCO statements on race and the new genetics reframed human variation, and eugenics became a byword for state abuse of science.

Legacy and modern debates

Mainstream genetics repudiated eugenics, though coercive programmes lingered (Peru in the 1990s; China's 1995 law) and victims have received apologies and compensation in several countries. Contemporary debates — prenatal screening, embryo selection, gene editing (CRISPR), 'liberal eugenics' — are conducted largely in explicit reaction against the term's history.
