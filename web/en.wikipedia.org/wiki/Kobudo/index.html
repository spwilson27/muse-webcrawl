Kobudo (古武道, "old martial way") is a collective term for Japanese traditional martial arts, but it is most commonly used to refer to Okinawan kobudo (沖縄古武道), the weapon systems of Okinawa. Okinawan kobudo was at its peak around the time of the Ryukyu Kingdom and is thought to have been influenced by both indigenous fighting methods and techniques imported from China and Southeast Asia.

History

The development of kobudo is often attributed to restrictions on weapons imposed on the Okinawan peasantry, first under King Shō Shin in the late 15th and early 16th centuries, who centralized weapons in the royal capital, and later under the Satsuma Domain after the Japanese invasion of 1609. According to tradition, commoners adapted everyday farming and fishing implements into weapons of self-defense. Modern historians note, however, that many kobudo practitioners historically came from the Okinawan aristocratic (pechin) class, and that the weapons tradition developed alongside empty-hand karate.

Weapons

The best-known weapons of Okinawan kobudo include the bō (six-foot staff), sai (truncheon), tonfa (handled club), nunchaku (two sticks connected by rope or chain), and kama (sickle). Other weapons include the eku (oar), tekko (knuckle-duster), tinbe-rochin (shield and short spear), surujin (weighted chain), nunti, and kuwa (hoe). Each weapon has its own set of kata (forms) preserved within the various schools.

Styles and organizations

Prominent lineages include Matayoshi kobudo, founded by Matayoshi Shinkō and continued by his son Matayoshi Shinpō, and Ryūkyū kobudo, systematized by Taira Shinken, who traveled throughout Okinawa collecting and preserving kata that were in danger of being lost. Major international organizations promote the practice and preservation of these traditions today.
