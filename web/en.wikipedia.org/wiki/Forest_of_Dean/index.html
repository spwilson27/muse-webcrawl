Forest of Dean

Location: Gloucestershire, England
Between: River Wye (west) and River Severn (east)
Status: Former royal hunting forest; statutory forest
Main towns: Coleford, Cinderford, Lydney

The Forest of Dean is a geographical, historical and cultural region in the western part of Gloucestershire, England. It forms a roughly triangular area bounded by the River Wye to the west and northwest, the River Severn to the south and east, and Herefordshire to the north. Centred on one of England's surviving ancient royal forests, the area is noted for its woodland, industrial heritage and distinctive local identity.

History

Settled since Mesolithic times, the Forest was reserved as a royal hunting ground after the Norman Conquest and was governed by special forest law administered from St Briavels Castle. Its resources — timber (including oak for ships), iron ore and coal — made it an early industrial landscape: iron was worked from Roman times, and freeminers, exercising birthright rights to mine coal and iron, remain a distinctive institution. Verderers, who historically administered the forest and its deer, still meet at the Speech House, built as a hunting lodge for Charles II.

Geography and nature

The core forest, managed by Forestry England, comprises some 27,000 acres of mixed woodland. Notable sites include Puzzlewood, Symonds Yat, the Sculpture Trail at Beechenhurst, and Clearwell Caves. Wildlife includes fallow deer, wild boar (re-established from escapes in recent decades) and the small pearl-bordered fritillary butterfly. The Forest of Dean district is a local government district covering a wider area than the statutory forest itself.
