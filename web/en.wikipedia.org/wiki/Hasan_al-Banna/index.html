Hasan Ahmed Abdel Rahman Mohammed al-Banna (Arabic: حسن أحمد عبد الرحمن محمد البنا; 17 October 1906 – 12 February 1949), known as Hasan al-Banna, was an Egyptian schoolteacher and religious leader who founded the Muslim Brotherhood in 1928, one of the largest and most influential Islamist movements of the twentieth century.

Early life and education

Al-Banna was born in Mahmoudiyah, a small town in the Nile Delta province of Beheira. His father, Sheikh Ahmed Abdel Rahman al-Banna al-Sa'ati, was a watchmaker and a scholar of hadith who compiled and classified the hadith collection of Ahmad ibn Hanbal. As a youth, Hasan was deeply influenced by Sufism, joining the Hasafiyya Sufi order, and by the religious reform ideas circulating in Egypt after the First World War.

He trained as a teacher at the Dar al-Ulum college in Cairo, graduating in 1927. During his years in Cairo he witnessed the effects of British occupation and Western cultural influence, which he came to regard as a threat to Islamic identity. After graduating he was posted as a schoolteacher to Ismailia, a town on the Suez Canal where the foreign presence was especially visible.

Founding of the Muslim Brotherhood

In March 1928, in Ismailia, al-Banna and six workers from the Suez Canal Company founded the Society of the Muslim Brothers (Jama'at al-Ikhwan al-Muslimin). The organisation began as a religious and social welfare movement, establishing mosques, schools, clinics and small businesses, but it rapidly grew into a mass political movement advocating the application of Islamic law and the end of British occupation.

Under al-Banna's leadership the Brotherhood expanded across Egypt and into other Arab countries. He built a disciplined hierarchical organisation with himself as Supreme Guide, developed its paramilitary "Special Apparatus," and articulated the movement's slogan: "Allah is our objective, the Prophet is our leader, the Qur'an is our law, jihad is our way, dying in the way of Allah is our highest hope."

Political activity and assassination

During the 1930s and 1940s the Brotherhood became a major force in Egyptian politics, clashing with the monarchy, the Wafd party and the British. Members of the movement volunteered in the 1948 Arab–Israeli War in Palestine. In December 1948 the Egyptian government dissolved the Brotherhood after a series of assassinations attributed to its secret apparatus; weeks later Prime Minister Mahmoud Fahmi al-Nuqrashi was assassinated by a Brotherhood member.

On 12 February 1949 al-Banna himself was shot in Cairo, in an attack widely believed to have been carried out by government agents in retaliation. He died of his wounds the same day. The Muslim Brotherhood survived his death and went on to become a central actor in Middle Eastern politics.

Writings and thought

Al-Banna's ideas are collected in his epistles (Rasa'il), including the influential "Epistle of the Teachings" addressed to the movement's activist core. He preached a comprehensive understanding of Islam as "a complete system" regulating all aspects of life, gradual social reform from below, and the eventual restoration of Islamic governance. His thought combined traditional Salafi themes with modern organisational methods adapted from contemporary mass movements.
