Radiometric dating

Radiometric dating, or radioactive dating, is a technique used to date materials such as rocks or carbon, by measuring the proportions of naturally occurring radioactive isotopes and their decay products, using known decay rates. It is the principal source of information about the absolute age of rocks and other geological features, including the age of the Earth itself, and it is a key tool of geochronology and archaeology.

Principles

Radioactive parent isotopes decay to daughter isotopes at a rate characterised by the isotope's half-life, which is unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical environment. By measuring the ratio of parent to daughter atoms in a sample and knowing the half-life, the time since the material formed (or since it last cooled below the closure temperature) can be calculated. For the method to work, the system must have remained closed — neither parent nor daughter may have entered or left the sample since formation.

Isochron dating

Isochron methods plot ratios measured in several minerals from the same rock; the slope of the resulting line gives the age without needing to know the initial daughter concentration, and the method can reveal whether the system has been disturbed.

Applications and history

The method was pioneered in the early 20th century by Ernest Rutherford and Bertram Boltwood, and developed into a precise science with mass spectrometry. Uranium–lead dating of meteorites by Clair Patterson in 1956 established the age of the Earth at about 4.55 billion years. Radiocarbon dating, developed by Willard Libby in the late 1940s, revolutionised archaeology and earned Libby the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
