The Dieppe Raid, known officially as Operation Jubilee, was an Allied amphibious attack on the German-occupied port of Dieppe in northern France on 19 August 1942, during the Second World War. Over 6,000 infantry — predominantly Canadian, with British commandos, American Rangers, and Free French — landed at dawn with naval and air support to seize and briefly hold the port, test defences, gather intelligence, and boost morale. Within hours the assault had failed with heavy losses.

The raid

The plan (an evolution of the cancelled Operation Rutter) called for flank attacks by No. 3 and No. 4 Commandos on coastal batteries, with the main force of the 2nd Canadian Infantry Division landing frontally at Dieppe and at Puys and Pourville. Surprise was compromised en route by a chance encounter with a German coastal convoy; at Puys the Royal Regiment of Canada landed in daylight beneath cliffs swept by fire, and on the main beach the Essex Scottish and Royal Hamilton Light Infantry were pinned on the shingle behind the seawall. Of 29 Churchill tanks landed, none returned, though some crossed the seawall. Withdrawal began late in the morning under heavy fire.

Losses and consequences

Of the roughly 4,963 Canadians who embarked, 3,367 were killed, wounded, or captured; the Royal Navy lost a destroyer and 33 landing craft, and the RAF lost 106 aircraft — its heaviest single-day loss of the war — against Luftwaffe losses of about half that. German casualties were comparatively light. Although Combined Operations under Lord Louis Mountbatten defended the operation as an indispensable rehearsal, historians still debate whether its lessons — overwhelming fire support, specialised armour, avoiding fortified ports, and the Mulberry harbours and PLUTO that followed — justified the cost, or whether the raid mainly demonstrated what the Allies should already have known. In Canada, where the 2nd Division had waited two years in England for action, Dieppe became a defining and controversial national memory.
