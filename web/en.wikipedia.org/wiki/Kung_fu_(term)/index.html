Kung fu (term)

Kung fu (Chinese: 功夫; pinyin: gōngfu) is a Chinese term referring to any discipline or skill achieved through hard work and practice. In popular usage outside China, and especially in the West, it has come to denote the Chinese martial arts collectively, although the more precise Chinese term for those arts is wushu (武術, "martial art").

Etymology

The term is composed of 功 (gōng, "work", "merit", "achievement") and 夫 (fū, "man"). Colloquially, gōngfu can describe any skill cultivated over a long period of effort: a chef, painter or calligrapher may all be said to have good "kung fu" in their craft. The phrase xià gōngfu (下功夫) means to expend effort on something. Its association specifically with unarmed combat is comparatively recent and was popularised globally in the 20th century.

Usage in martial arts

In the late 20th century, the term was popularised in the West by Hong Kong martial arts cinema — notably the films of Bruce Lee — and by the 1970s American television series Kung Fu, starring David Carradine. In this usage, "kung fu" serves as an umbrella label for hundreds of Chinese fighting styles, from external styles such as Shaolinquan and Hung Gar to internal styles such as tai chi, baguazhang and xingyiquan. Within China, practitioners more commonly use wushu, and the People's Republic of China promotes a modern standardised sport under that name, while "kung fu" often connotes the traditional lineages.

Cultural influence

Beyond the martial arts, "kung fu" has entered English slang as a jocular suffix for any field of expertise (for example " spreadsheet kung fu"), and appears in product and media names worldwide. The word is also widely applied metaphorically to mastery in video games and hacking culture.
