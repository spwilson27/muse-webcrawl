Ancient Greek sculpture

The sculpture of ancient Greece is the main surviving type of fine ancient Greek art, as, with the exception of painted pottery, almost no Greek painting has survived. Sculpture in stone and bronze developed through distinct periods — Archaic, Classical and Hellenistic — and formed one of the most influential traditions in Western art.

Archaic period

Free-standing Archaic sculpture is dominated by the kouros (male) and kore (female) types: rigid, frontal figures influenced by Egyptian statuary, with a characteristic "Archaic smile". Over the 6th century BC figures became steadily more naturalistic.

Classical period

The 5th century BC saw the introduction of contrapposto and increasingly idealised anatomy, codified in Polykleitos' treatise the Canon. Phidias supervised the sculptures of the Parthenon in Athens. In the 4th century, Praxiteles popularised the S-curve and produced the first monumental female nude, the Aphrodite of Knidos; Lysippos became the court sculptor of Alexander the Great.

Hellenistic period

After Alexander, sculpture became more emotional, dynamic and virtuosic, as in the Laocoön group, the Winged Victory of Samothrace and the Pergamon Altar frieze. Most famous Greek bronzes survive only through Roman marble copies.
