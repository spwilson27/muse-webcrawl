The Treaty of Canandaigua is a treaty signed after the American Revolutionary War between the Grand Council of the Six Nations and President George Washington, representing the United States of America. It was signed at Canandaigua, New York on November 11, 1794, by fifty sachems and war chiefs of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) and by Timothy Pickering, official agent of George Washington.

Background

The treaty grew out of the desire of the United States to secure peace on its northern frontier following the devastating Sullivan Expedition of 1779 and continuing tensions over land in western New York. The Haudenosaunee had suffered greatly during the Revolution, and the 1784 Treaty of Fort Stanwix had imposed heavy land cessions under pressure.

Provisions

The treaty established peace and friendship between the United States and the Six Nations, acknowledged the lands reserved to the Oneida, Onondaga and Cayuga Nations, and confirmed the boundaries of Seneca land in western New York. The western boundary of Seneca territory generally followed a line near the Genesee River region, and the treaty returned a strip of land along Lake Erie and Lake Ontario that had been ceded at Fort Stanwix. The United States promised never to claim or disturb the free use and enjoyment of the reserved lands.

Legacy

The Treaty of Canandaigua remains legally significant. It is still honored annually by the United States government, which sends cloth goods to the Haudenosaunee as required by Article VI of the treaty, and it has been cited in modern land-claims litigation by the Oneida and other nations. The anniversary is commemorated each November 11 in Canandaigua.
