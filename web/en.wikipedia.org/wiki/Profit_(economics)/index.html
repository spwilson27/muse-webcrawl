Profit (economics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics, profit is the surplus of revenue over the costs of production. Economists distinguish it from the accounting concept by insisting that all costs — including implicit opportunity costs, such as the return the owner's capital and labour could have earned elsewhere — must be counted.

Accounting vs economic profit

Accounting profit = total revenue − explicit costs (wages, materials, rent, interest actually paid). Economic profit = total revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs. When economic profit is zero the firm still earns an accounting profit equal to normal profit — the minimum needed to keep resources in their current use — and economists say the firm is "breaking even" in the economic sense.

Profit in market models

In long-run perfect competition, entry and exit drive economic profit to zero. Positive economic profit in the long run requires barriers to entry, monopoly power, innovation, or superior efficiency; such above-normal returns are sometimes called supernormal profit or rents. Negative economic profit (economic loss) signals that resources would earn more elsewhere.

Theories of profit

Classical and later economists have explained profit variously as a reward for risk-bearing and uncertainty (Knight), for entrepreneurship and innovation (Schumpeter), as interest on capital, or — in Marxian economics — as surplus value extracted from labour. Profit also serves a signalling and allocation function, directing resources toward activities consumers value most.
