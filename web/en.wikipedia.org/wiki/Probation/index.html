Probation

Probation in criminal law is a period of supervision over an offender, ordered by a court instead of serving time in prison. The offender is released into the community subject to conditions set by the court and supervised by a probation officer. If the conditions are breached, the offender may be brought back before the court and the suspended sentence may be activated.

History

The origins of probation lie in nineteenth-century practices of judicial reprieve and recognizance. John Augustus, a Boston shoemaker, is regarded as the first probation officer: from 1841 he bailed out offenders, supervised them, and reported on their conduct to the court. Massachusetts enacted the first probation statute in 1878, and England followed with the Probation of Offenders Act 1907, which created a probation service staffed by court missionaries.

Conditions

Standard conditions typically require the probationer to keep the peace, report regularly, reside at an approved address, and notify the officer of changes in employment or address. Courts may add requirements such as unpaid work, curfews, exclusion zones, drug or alcohol treatment, and programmes addressing offending behaviour.

Probation and parole

Probation is an alternative to imprisonment imposed at sentencing, whereas parole is early release from a prison sentence under supervision. Both aim to rehabilitate offenders and reduce recidivism while protecting the public.
