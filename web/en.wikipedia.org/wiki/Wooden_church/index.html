Wooden church

A wooden church is a church building constructed substantially of timber. Wooden churches are found across northern and eastern Europe, Russia and parts of Asia, ranging from medieval stave churches to log-built village chapels.

Traditions

In Norway, stave churches built from the 12th century used load-bearing staves (posts) and elaborate carving; about 28 survive, including Borgund and the UNESCO-listed Urnes Stave Church. In Russia, multi-domed wooden churches such as those on Kizhi Island were built entirely of logs without nails. The wooden churches of Maramureș in Romania and the wooden tserkvas of the Carpathians are also UNESCO World Heritage Sites, as are the wooden churches of southern Lesser Poland and the Churches of Peace-adjacent timber traditions.

Construction

Common techniques include horizontal log construction with interlocking corner joints, post-and-beam stave work, and timber framing with wattle or plank infill. Steep shingled roofs shed snow, and interiors are often richly painted.

Preservation

Wooden churches are vulnerable to fire, rot and insect damage. Many have been moved to open-air museums such as Skansen in Stockholm and the Ukrainian Museum of Folk Architecture in Kyiv for protection and study.
