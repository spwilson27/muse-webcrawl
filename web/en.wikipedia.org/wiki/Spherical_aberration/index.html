Spherical aberration

Spherical aberration is an optical aberration in which light rays passing through a lens or reflecting from a mirror near its edge are focused at a different point than rays passing through the centre. It occurs because spherical surfaces do not bring parallel rays to a single perfect focus, producing a blurred image surrounded by a halo of light.

Overview

In an ideal optical system, all rays from a point on the optical axis would converge to a single image point. For a spherical refracting or reflecting surface, rays striking far from the axis (marginal rays) are bent more strongly than paraxial rays, so they cross the axis at a different distance. The longitudinal spherical aberration is the difference along the axis between the marginal and paraxial focal positions; the transverse aberration is the radius of the blur spot in the paraxial focal plane. The smallest blur occurs at the circle of least confusion, located between the two foci.

Causes

The effect is an intrinsic property of spherical surfaces described by the exact form of Snell's law: the sine of the angle of refraction is not proportional to the height of the ray except in the paraxial approximation. Spherical aberration increases rapidly with aperture — roughly with the fourth power of the aperture radius for the wavefront error — so fast optical systems are most affected.

Correction

Several strategies reduce spherical aberration:

  • Stopping down the aperture blocks the offending marginal rays.
  • Combining lens elements of opposite power distributes refraction so their aberrations cancel, as in the achromatic doublet and modern multi-element photographic lenses.
  • Aspheric surfaces depart deliberately from a sphere to bring all rays to a common focus.
  • Parabolic mirrors in reflecting telescopes eliminate spherical aberration for on-axis objects; the Schmidt corrector plate and Maksutov corrector achieve a similar result for spherical mirrors.
  • Gradient-index optics use a varying refractive index to correct the aberration.

Historical example

The Hubble Space Telescope was launched in 1990 with a primary mirror ground to a shape that differed from the intended figure by about 2 micrometres at the edge, introducing severe spherical aberration. The error was corrected in 1993 by the COSTAR corrective optics installed during the first servicing mission, and later instruments incorporated the correction internally.
