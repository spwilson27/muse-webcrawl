Cursed soldiers

[Image: A memorial plaque honouring the cursed soldiers in a Polish town square]

The cursed soldiers (Polish: Żołnierze wyklęci), also called the indomitable soldiers (Żołnierze niezłomni), were members of Polish anti-communist resistance movements that continued armed struggle against the Soviet-backed communist government of Poland after the Second World War. Many had previously fought in the Home Army (Armia Krajowa) against Nazi Germany.

History

After the Home Army was disbanded in January 1945, thousands of its soldiers refused to accept communist rule and joined organisations such as Freedom and Independence (WiN), the National Armed Forces (NSZ), and the National Military Union (NZW). At their height the movements numbered in the tens of thousands, including support networks. The communist security services (UB), backed by the Soviet NKVD, waged a brutal counter-insurgency; an amnesty in 1947 induced most fighters to reveal themselves, and many were then arrested. Organised resistance was largely crushed by the early 1950s, though the last known cursed soldier, Józef Franczak ("Lalek"), was killed in 1963.

Repression and rehabilitation

Thousands of cursed soldiers were executed or imprisoned after show trials, and communist propaganda portrayed them as bandits and fascist collaborators; their memory was suppressed until 1989. Since the fall of communism they have been officially rehabilitated and celebrated as heroes. In 2011 Poland established 1 March as the National Day of Remembrance of the Cursed Soldiers. Some individual figures remain controversial because of documented crimes against civilian minorities, a subject of ongoing historical debate.
