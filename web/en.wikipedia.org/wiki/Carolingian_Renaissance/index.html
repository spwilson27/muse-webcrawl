The Carolingian Renaissance was a period of cultural and intellectual revival in the Carolingian Empire during the late eighth and ninth centuries — the first of three medieval "renaissances", followed by the Ottonian and 12th-century renaissances. Under Charlemagne and his successors, the Frankish court sponsored schools, scriptoria, and scholarship that standardized Latin learning and preserved much of classical literature that would otherwise have been lost.

Origins and programme

Charlemagne's reforms were driven by the practical need for a literate clergy and administration. His Admonitio generalis (789) and the circular letter De litteris colendis ordered monasteries and cathedrals to establish schools and to correct their books. The emperor gathered scholars from across Europe at his palace school at Aachen: Alcuin of York from England, Paul the Deacon and Peter of Pisa from Italy, Theodulf from Visigothic Spain, and the Irishman Clement, among others. Einhard's Life of Charlemagne consciously modelled itself on Suetonius.

Caroline minuscule and book production

The era's most enduring innovation was Caroline minuscule, a clear, rounded book hand developed in scriptoria such as Corbie and Tours. Replacing the bewildering variety of regional scripts, it made texts legible across Europe, and its letterforms later became the model for Renaissance humanist script and thus for modern lowercase type. Carolingian scriptoria copied classical authors on a large scale: the oldest surviving manuscripts of most Latin classics — Virgil, Horace, Cicero, Livy, Tacitus — are Carolingian copies, through which the texts survived.

Education and learning

Alcuin reorganized the curriculum around the seven liberal arts (the trivium of grammar, rhetoric and dialectic, and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy). Monastic centres such as Tours, Fulda, Reichenau, St. Gall and Ferrières became engines of learning; Alcuin's pupil Hrabanus Maurus, abbot of Fulda, earned the title "Teacher of Germany". Alcuin also produced a corrected edition of the Vulgate Bible, and Theodulf another, as part of a drive for uniform liturgy and texts. In the later ninth century, the Irish philosopher John Scottus Eriugena at the court of Charles the Bald translated Greek works of Pseudo-Dionysius and wrote the ambitious Periphyseon.

Art and architecture

Carolingian art revived Roman and early Christian models in ivory carving, metalwork, and illuminated manuscripts such as the Utrecht Psalter and the gospel books of the Ada and Ebbo schools. Charlemagne's Palatine Chapel at Aachen, modelled partly on San Vitale in Ravenna, survives as the masterpiece of Carolingian architecture.

Legacy

Political fragmentation after the Treaty of Verdun (843) and Viking, Magyar and Muslim raids disrupted the movement by c. 900, but its achievements — the script, the schools, the saved classical corpus, and the idea of a Christian Roman renewal — formed the foundation for all later medieval learning.
