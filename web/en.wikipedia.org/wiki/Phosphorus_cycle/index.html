The phosphorus cycle is the biogeochemical cycle that describes the movement of phosphorus through the lithosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. Unlike many other biogeochemical cycles, the atmosphere does not play a significant role in the movement of phosphorus, because phosphorus and phosphorus-based compounds are usually solids at the typical ranges of temperature and pressure found on Earth.

Phosphorus is an essential element for all life: it forms part of DNA, RNA, ATP, and the phospholipids that make up cell membranes, and it is a key component of bones and teeth. In ecosystems, phosphorus is frequently the limiting nutrient for plant growth, particularly in freshwater systems.

Movement through the environment

The main reservoir of phosphorus is sedimentary rock, especially phosphate-rich deposits formed from ocean sediments. Weathering and erosion release phosphate into soils and waterways, where plants absorb it as orthophosphate ions. Animals obtain phosphorus by eating plants or other animals. When organisms die and decompose, phosphorus returns to the soil or is washed into rivers and oceans, where it may be deposited as sediment and eventually uplifted again as rock over geological time — a cycle that takes tens of thousands to millions of years.

Human impact

Humans have greatly accelerated the phosphorus cycle by mining phosphate rock for fertilizer and detergents. Excess phosphorus in agricultural runoff is a major cause of eutrophication: nutrient enrichment of lakes, estuaries, and coastal waters stimulates algal blooms whose decomposition depletes oxygen, creating hypoxic "dead zones". Because phosphate rock is a finite resource concentrated in a few countries (notably Morocco and China), concerns about "peak phosphorus" and long-term food security have grown.

Enzymes and ecology

Most phosphorus in soil is locked in organic or mineral forms unavailable to plants. Microorganisms and plant roots release phosphatase enzymes and organic acids to mobilize it, and mycorrhizal fungi help plants acquire phosphate in exchange for carbon — one of the most ancient symbioses in terrestrial ecosystems.
