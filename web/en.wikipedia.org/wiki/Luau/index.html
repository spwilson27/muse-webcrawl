A luau (Hawaiian: lūʻau) is a traditional Hawaiian party or feast, usually accompanied by entertainment such as Hawaiian music and hula. It may feature foods such as poi, kālua pig, salmon, beer and coconut pudding known as haupia. The term comes from the name of a food always served at such a feast: chicken lūʻau, a dish of chicken and taro leaves cooked in coconut milk.

A traditional luau feast

History

In ancient Hawaii, men and women ate separately, and commoners and women of all ranks were forbidden to eat certain delicacies. In 1819 King Kamehameha II abolished the religious taboos (the kapu system) by symbolically eating with women, an event known as the ʻAi Noa (free eating). The modern luau developed in the following decades; the great feast given by Kamehameha III in 1847 is often cited as an early example, reportedly serving thousands of guests.

Traditional foods

  • Kālua pig — a whole pig cooked in an underground oven (imu)
  • Poi — pounded taro root paste
  • Lomi-lomi salmon — diced salted salmon with tomatoes and onions
  • Laulau — pork or fish wrapped in taro leaves
  • Haupia — coconut-milk pudding
  • Poke — seasoned raw fish

Entertainment

Modern luaus, especially those held for visitors, typically feature hula performances telling stories through dance, Samoan fire-knife dancing, and live music with ukulele and slack-key guitar.
