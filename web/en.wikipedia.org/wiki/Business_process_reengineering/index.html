Business process reengineering

BPR advocates radical redesign of core business processes rather than incremental improvement.

Business process re-engineering (BPR) is a business management strategy, originally pioneered in the early 1990s, focusing on the analysis and design of workflows and business processes within an organization. BPR aims to help organizations fundamentally rethink how they do their work in order to improve customer service, cut operational costs, and become world-class competitors.

Origins

The concept was introduced in a 1990 article by Michael Hammer, "Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate", in the Harvard Business Review, and was popularised by Hammer and James Champy's best-selling 1993 book Reengineering the Corporation: A Manifesto for Business Revolution. They defined reengineering as "the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical, contemporary measures of performance, such as cost, quality, service, and speed."

Methodology

BPR projects typically identify an organization's key processes, analyse them "as-is", and redesign them "to-be" around outcomes rather than tasks, often leveraging new information technology. Characteristic themes include organizing around processes rather than functions, empowering front-line workers, capturing information once at its source, and treating geographically dispersed resources as though centralized.

Criticism

BPR became notorious in the mid-1990s as a management fad and as a euphemism for downsizing and mass layoffs. Reported failure rates for reengineering projects were high — Hammer himself acknowledged figures around 70 percent — with critics blaming neglect of people, culture and change management. Its core ideas were later absorbed into the more incremental discipline of business process management (BPM).
