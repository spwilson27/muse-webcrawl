Gaussian quadrature

In numerical analysis, an n-point Gaussian quadrature rule, named after Carl Friedrich Gauss, is a quadrature rule constructed to yield an exact result for polynomials of degree 2n − 1 or less by a suitable choice of the nodes xᵢ and weights wᵢ for i = 1, ..., n. The modern formulation using orthogonal polynomials was developed by Christoffel and others in the late 19th century.

Gauss–Legendre quadrature

The most common case is Gauss–Legendre quadrature on the interval [−1, 1] with weight function 1. The nodes are the roots of the Legendre polynomial Pₙ(x), and the weights are given by wᵢ = 2 / ((1 − xᵢ²)[P′ₙ(xᵢ)]²). Integrals over other intervals [a, b] are handled by a change of variables.

General formulation

For an integral ∫ w(x) f(x) dx over an interval with weight function w, the optimal nodes are the roots of the orthogonal polynomial of degree n associated with w. Important variants include Gauss–Hermite quadrature (weight e^(−x²) on the real line), Gauss–Laguerre quadrature (weight e^(−x) on [0, ∞)), and Gauss–Chebyshev quadrature.

Golub–Welsch algorithm

The standard method for computing nodes and weights, due to Golub and Welsch (1969), expresses them in terms of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a symmetric tridiagonal matrix built from the recurrence coefficients of the orthogonal polynomials.

Error estimates

The error of Gaussian quadrature depends on the 2n-th derivative of the integrand; for smooth integrands the method converges extremely rapidly as n increases, which is why it is a default choice for high-accuracy numerical integration of well-behaved functions.
