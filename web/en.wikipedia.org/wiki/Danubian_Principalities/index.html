Danubian Principalities

The Danubian Principalities (Romanian: Principatele Dunărene) was a conventional name for the two Romanian principalities of Moldavia and Wallachia, which emerged in the 14th century. The term was coined in the Austrian Empire and became common in diplomatic usage especially after the Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca (1774). It excluded Transylvania, which was a separate crown land.

Both principalities were, for most of their history, vassal states of the Ottoman Empire, retaining internal autonomy while paying tribute and accepting the sultan's approval of their rulers. Their union in 1859 under Alexandru Ioan Cuza created the nucleus of the modern Romanian state.

History

Wallachia (founded c. 1330) and Moldavia (founded c. 1359) resisted Ottoman expansion under rulers such as Mircea the Elder, Stephen the Great and Michael the Brave, but by the 16th century both had become tributary states. From 1711 (Moldavia) and 1716 (Wallachia) the Porte appointed Phanariot Greek princes as hospodars, a system marked by corruption and frequent changes of ruler, lasting until 1821.

Between empires

The principalities became a buffer and battleground between the Ottoman, Russian and Austrian empires. Russia occupied them repeatedly during the Russo-Turkish wars; the Treaty of Adrianople (1829) gave them greater autonomy and a Russian protectorate, under which the Organic Regulations (Regulamentul Organic, 1831–32) provided their first quasi-constitutional frameworks. Austria annexed Bukovina (1774) and Russia annexed Bessarabia (1812) at Moldavia's expense.

Toward union

After the Crimean War, the Treaty of Paris (1856) replaced the Russian protectorate with a collective European guarantee and returned southern Bessarabia to Moldavia. Divans in both principalities voted for union, and in January 1859 assemblies in Iași and Bucharest both elected Alexandru Ioan Cuza as prince — achieving a personal union that was consolidated as the United Principalities in 1862, formally named Romania in 1866.
