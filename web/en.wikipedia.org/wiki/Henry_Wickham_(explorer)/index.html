Sir Henry Alexander Wickham (29 May 1846 – 27 September 1928) was a British explorer, botanist, planter, and author, best known for his role in the "theft" of rubber seeds from Brazil in 1876. Acting on commission from the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, and the India Office, Wickham collected about 70,000 seeds of the rubber tree Hevea brasiliensis on the Tapajós River and shipped them to London. The resulting seedlings were sent to British colonies in Asia, founding the plantation rubber industry of Malaya and Ceylon that eventually destroyed Brazil's Amazon rubber monopoly.

Early life and travels

Wickham was born in Hampstead, London; his father, a solicitor, died when he was young. At about twenty he travelled to Central America, working in Nicaragua and British Honduras in mahogany cutting, bird collecting, and plantation ventures. He later explored Venezuela's Orinoco and travelled widely in the Amazon basin, where he made his home for periods at Santarém. In 1872 he published an account of his travels, Rough Notes of a Journey Through the Wilderness. His knowledge of the Amazon and its peoples led Kew's director Joseph Hooker to engage him for the rubber seed commission, at a fee of £10 per thousand seeds delivered in good condition.

The rubber seeds of 1876

In 1876 Wickham chartered a steamer, collected some 70,000 seeds from rubber trees near Boim on the Tapajós, and — famously — declared them at Belém customs as delicate botanical specimens for herbaria. The seeds, which lose viability quickly, reached Kew within weeks; about 2,700 germinated, and in August 1876 the seedlings were dispatched in Wardian cases to Ceylon and Singapore. From these, and the work of Henry Ridley at the Singapore Botanic Gardens in encouraging planting, grew the great rubber estates of British Malaya, which by the 1910s–1920s had overtaken Brazil to supply most of the world's rubber.

Whether Wickham's act counts as "smuggling" or "biopiracy" is debated: no Brazilian law at the time clearly forbade the export of the seeds, and Wickham shipped them openly — but the episode is still remembered in Brazil as an act of economic espionage, and Wickham himself embellished the tale in retelling it. He was knighted in 1920, largely in recognition of the seed transfer.

Later life

Wickham went on to plant quinine, tobacco, and other crops in Queensland, Australia, and spent time in British New Guinea; most of his ventures were commercial failures. He died in London in 1928. His reputation rests entirely on the 1876 commission, one of the most consequential acts of economic botany of the imperial era — comparable to the transfer of tea from China to India and cinchona from Peru to Asia.
