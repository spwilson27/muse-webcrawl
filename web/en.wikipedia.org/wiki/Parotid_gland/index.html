Parotid gland

The parotid gland is the largest of the major salivary glands in humans. A pair of parotid glands lie on either side of the face, below and in front of the ear. The gland produces serous saliva, delivered to the mouth through the parotid (Stensen's) duct, which opens opposite the second upper molar.

Anatomy

The gland is wrapped in the parotid fascia and is traversed by the facial nerve, which divides it functionally into superficial and deep lobes — a relationship of great surgical importance. The retromandibular vein and external carotid artery also pass through it. Parasympathetic secretomotor supply reaches the gland from the glossopharyngeal nerve via the otic ganglion.

Clinical significance

The parotid is the gland swollen in mumps. Tumours of the parotid are most often benign pleomorphic adenomas; surgical removal (parotidectomy) risks facial nerve injury. Stones and bacterial infection can cause painful parotitis.
