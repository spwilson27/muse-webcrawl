Indigenous rights

Indigenous rights are those rights that exist in recognition of the specific condition of the Indigenous peoples of a region. They include not only fundamental human rights concerning physical survival and integrity, but also rights over land, language, culture, religion and other elements of heritage that are bound up with Indigenous peoples' distinct identities and traditional territories.

International framework

The principal international instruments are ILO Convention No. 169 (1989), binding on ratifying states, and the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP), adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2007. UNDRIP affirms rights to self-determination, to traditional lands and resources, to free, prior and informed consent regarding projects affecting them, and to maintain and strengthen distinct institutions, cultures and languages. The UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues, established in 2000, advises the Economic and Social Council.

National implementation

Implementation varies widely: some constitutions, such as Bolivia's and Ecuador's, expressly recognise Indigenous nations and rights to territory and prior consultation, while in settler states including Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the United States, Indigenous rights are mediated through treaties, statutes and landmark court decisions — for example Mabo v Queensland (1992), which recognised native title in Australia. Ongoing disputes concern land and resource extraction, repatriation of remains and artefacts, and language revitalisation.
