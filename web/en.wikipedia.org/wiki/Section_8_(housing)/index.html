Section 8 is the informal name for the Housing Choice Voucher Program, the United States federal government's major program for assisting very low-income families, the elderly, and people with disabilities to afford decent, safe, and sanitary housing in the private market. The program is named after Section 8 of the Housing Act of 1937, as amended by the Housing and Community Development Act of 1974.

Participants are free to choose any housing that meets the requirements of the program, including single-family homes, townhouses, and apartments, and are not limited to units located in subsidized housing projects. A housing subsidy is paid to the landlord directly by the local public housing agency (PHA) on behalf of the participating family; the family then pays the difference between the actual rent charged by the landlord and the amount subsidized by the program.

History

The program was created as part of a broader shift in federal housing policy away from building large public housing projects and toward subsidizing rents in the private market. The Housing and Community Development Act of 1974 established the Section 8 program, which originally included certificates for existing housing, moderate rehabilitation, and new construction. The Quality Housing and Work Responsibility Act of 1998 merged the certificate and voucher programs into the modern Housing Choice Voucher Program.

Eligibility and administration

Eligibility is determined by PHAs based on the total annual gross income and family size of applicants. By law, a PHA must provide 75 percent of its vouchers to applicants whose incomes do not exceed 30 percent of the area median income. Because demand far exceeds supply, waiting lists in many cities are years long or closed entirely. Under the program, a family's rent contribution is generally capped at about 30 percent of its monthly adjusted gross income.

Landlord participation

Participation by private landlords is voluntary in most jurisdictions, though a growing number of states and cities have enacted source-of-income discrimination laws prohibiting landlords from refusing tenants solely because they pay with a voucher. Units must pass a housing quality standards inspection and rents must be reasonable compared with similar unassisted units in the market.
