Sui iuris

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The concept of sui iuris originates in Roman law.

Sui iuris (also spelled sui juris) is a Latin legal phrase meaning "of one's own right". In Roman law, a person who was sui iuris was legally independent — not subject to the authority (patria potestas) of a head of household — and could own property, make contracts and wills, and sue and be sued in their own name. Its opposite was alieni iuris, "of another's right", describing children in power, wives in certain forms of marriage, and enslaved persons.

In Roman law

Under classical Roman law the paterfamilias was the only fully independent person in a household. Fathers, and emancipated sons and widowers who had become heads of their own families, were sui iuris; most other free persons were alieni iuris under another's power. A person became sui iuris on the death of the paterfamilias or by emancipation.

In canon law

The phrase is used in the canon law of the Catholic Church to describe autonomous particular churches in full communion with the Holy See. The Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches (canon 27) defines a sui iuris church as a group of Christian faithful united by a hierarchy according to the norm of law which the supreme authority of the Church expressly or tacitly recognises as sui iuris. The Catholic Church comprises 24 such churches: the Latin Church and 23 Eastern Catholic Churches, each with its own liturgical rite, theology, spirituality and canon-law tradition.

Other uses

In broader legal usage the term can describe any body or person that is self-governing and not legally subordinate to another, in contrast to entities acting under another's authority.
