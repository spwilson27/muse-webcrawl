Rheumatic fever

Rheumatic fever is an inflammatory disease that can develop after infection with group A Streptococcus, the bacterium responsible for many cases of strep throat and scarlet fever. It may affect the heart, joints, skin and brain. Repeated episodes can cause rheumatic heart disease, especially damage to the mitral and aortic valves.

Cause
Autoimmune response following group A streptococcal infection
Typical age
Most often 5–15 years
Major manifestations
Carditis, migratory arthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum and subcutaneous nodules

Mechanism

The illness is not caused by bacteria directly invading all affected tissues. Instead, immune molecules produced against streptococcal antigens cross-react with human proteins in a process called molecular mimicry. Genetic susceptibility and repeated infections influence risk.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually begin one to five weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis. Fever and painful joints that move from one joint to another are common. Carditis can produce a new murmur, chest pain or heart failure. Sydenham chorea causes involuntary movements and emotional lability, sometimes appearing later. The rash erythema marginatum and painless nodules are less common but characteristic.

Diagnosis

The revised Jones criteria combine major and minor manifestations with evidence of a preceding group A streptococcal infection, such as a positive culture, rapid antigen test or rising antibody titres. Echocardiography is important because valve inflammation may be present without an audible murmur.

Treatment and prevention

Prompt antibiotic treatment of strep throat greatly reduces the risk of a first attack. After rheumatic fever, long-term preventive antibiotics are often prescribed to stop recurrences.

Penicillin or another appropriate antibiotic eradicates streptococci, while anti-inflammatory medicines treat arthritis and fever. Significant carditis may require heart-failure treatment and specialist care. Prevention also depends on access to diagnosis, crowded-housing reduction and reliable supplies of benzathine penicillin in high-burden communities.

Epidemiology

Rheumatic fever has become uncommon in many high-income settings but remains a major cause of acquired heart disease among children and young adults in parts of Africa, South Asia, the Pacific and Indigenous communities in Australia and New Zealand.
