Supermini

A supermini is a car classification used mainly in Europe to describe a small car larger than a city car but smaller than a small family car. In European Commission terms it corresponds to the B-segment, and in North America such cars are generally marketed as subcompact cars. Superminis are typically hatchbacks around 3.9 to 4.2 metres long, designed for low purchase and running costs, easy urban manoeuvrability, and economical fuel consumption.

History

The modern supermini emerged in the 1970s with front-wheel-drive hatchbacks such as the Fiat 127 (1971), Renault 5 (1972), and Ford Fiesta (1976), which combined small external dimensions with space-efficient packaging. The formula proved enormously popular in Europe, and models such as the Volkswagen Polo, Peugeot 205 and 206, Opel Corsa, and Renault Clio became perennial best-sellers. Over successive generations superminis grew in size, safety equipment, and refinement, absorbing features once reserved for larger cars.

Characteristics

Superminis are usually offered as three- or five-door hatchbacks, with small petrol or diesel engines (and increasingly hybrid or fully electric powertrains), front-wheel drive, and seating for four or five. Performance-oriented "hot hatch" derivatives, such as the Ford Fiesta ST and Volkswagen Polo GTI, form a recognised sub-category. Estate and saloon variants are produced for some markets, and several superminis have been adapted into small crossover SUVs.

Market

The segment has traditionally been one of the largest in Europe by sales volume, especially in Italy, France, Spain, and the United Kingdom. In the 2020s, the economics of emissions regulation and electrification led several manufacturers to discontinue small petrol models or shift the segment toward electric vehicles, such as the Renault 5 E-Tech.
