Bill (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A bill is a proposal for a new law, or a proposal to change an existing law, presented for debate before a legislature. Bills are usually introduced in the lower house of a bicameral legislature, and in many jurisdictions may originate in either house except for money bills, which are often reserved to the lower house. A bill does not become law until it has been passed by the legislature and, in most systems, formally assented to by the head of state or allowed to take effect by the executive.

Bills are classified as public or private (according to whether they affect the general public or particular persons or places), and as government bills or private members' bills according to their sponsor. Drafting is usually done by specialist legislative counsel to ensure the text achieves its purpose within the existing body of law.

Passage

The classic Westminster sequence involves a first reading (formal introduction), a second reading (debate on principle), a committee stage (detailed examination and amendment), a report stage, and a third reading (final approval), followed by the same process in the other house and royal assent. The United States process differs in detail: bills are referred to committees of Congress, reported to the floor, passed by both houses in identical form, and presented to the President, whose veto may be overridden by two-thirds majorities. Conference committees reconcile differing versions.

Money bills

Bills imposing taxation or authorising expenditure are treated specially in many constitutions: in the United Kingdom the Parliament Acts allow money bills to become law without the Lords' consent after a short delay; India's constitution similarly gives the Lok Sabha primacy over money bills, certified as such by the Speaker.
