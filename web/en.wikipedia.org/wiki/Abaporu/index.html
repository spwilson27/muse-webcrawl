Abaporu is a 1928 oil painting by the Brazilian artist Tarsila do Amaral. It is considered the most famous work of Brazilian modernism and the emblem of the Antropofagia (Cannibalist) movement. The title comes from the Tupi language and means "the man who eats people" (abá, man; pora, people; ú, eats).

Description

The painting depicts a solitary, monstrous figure seated beneath a blazing sun: a tiny head, an enormous foot planted on the ground, and elongated limbs rendered in smooth, simplified volumes. Beside the figure stands a green cactus topped with a flower, and above it a slice of sun resembling a lemon. The landscape is reduced to a few undulating bands of blue and green.

History and significance

Tarsila painted Abaporu in January 1928 as a birthday present for her husband, the writer Oswald de Andrade. Impressed by the image, Oswald and the poet Raul Bopp named it and Oswald soon wrote the Manifesto Antropófago (Cannibalist Manifesto, 1928), which proposed that Brazilian culture should "devour" European influences and transform them into something original.

After passing through private collections, the painting was sold at auction in New York in 1995 to the Argentine collector Eduardo Costantini, founder of MALBA in Buenos Aires, where it remains on display. It has become one of the most valuable Latin American artworks ever sold and an icon of Brazilian cultural identity.

Legacy

Abaporu helped define the "Pau-Brasil" and Antropofagia phases of Brazilian modernism, influencing literature, music, and the visual arts, including the later Tropicália movement of the 1960s.
