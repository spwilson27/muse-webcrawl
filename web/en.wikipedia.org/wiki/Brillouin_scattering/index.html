Brillouin scattering

Brillouin scattering, named after Léon Brillouin, is the inelastic scattering of light by acoustic phonons — thermally excited sound waves in a material. The scattered light is shifted in frequency by the Doppler-like effect of the moving sound waves, producing characteristic Stokes and anti-Stokes sidebands typically offset by a few gigahertz from the incident frequency.

Theory

Spontaneous Brillouin scattering results from light reflecting off moving density fluctuations. Conservation of energy and momentum links the frequency shift to the speed of sound and elastic properties of the medium, so measuring the shift and linewidth yields sound velocity, attenuation, and elastic moduli. At high optical intensities, stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) occurs: the light itself generates a coherent acoustic wave through electrostriction, acting back on the beam — an important nonlinear effect that limits power transmission in optical fibres and is exploited in Brillouin lasers and sensors.

Applications

Brillouin spectroscopy is used to probe the mechanical properties of materials, including biological tissue and cells (Brillouin microscopy), geological samples, and polymers. Distributed Brillouin sensing along optical fibres measures temperature and strain over tens of kilometres, applied to pipelines, dams, and power cables. In remote sensing, Brillouin lidar measures ocean temperature profiles.
