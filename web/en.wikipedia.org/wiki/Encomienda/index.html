Encomienda

Encomienda

Region: Spanish America and the Philippines

Period: 16th – 18th centuries

Grant holder: Encomendero

Obligation of holder: Protect natives, provide Christian instruction

In return: Tribute and labour from the granted community

The encomienda was a Spanish labour and tribute system that rewarded conquerors and settlers in the Americas and the Philippines with the labour and tribute of particular groups of Native peoples. In theory, the encomendero — the holder of the grant — was responsible for protecting the natives in his charge and instructing them in the Spanish language and the Catholic faith; in practice, the system frequently became a form of coerced, often brutal exploitation.

Origins

The institution drew on precedents from the Reconquista of Spain and was introduced to the Caribbean by Christopher Columbus and formalised under Governor Nicolás de Ovando in Hispaniola from 1503. It spread with the conquests of Mexico and Peru: Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro distributed encomiendas of Aztec and Inca communities to their followers, and the grants became the economic foundation of the early colonial order.

Operation and abuse

An encomienda was a grant of people, not of land (land grants were separate mercedes), and was legally not hereditary in perpetuity. The Crown repeatedly tried to limit the system in response to catastrophic native population decline and to denunciations by clergy such as Bartolomé de las Casas. The New Laws of 1542 prohibited native enslavement and ordered encomiendas to lapse on the holder's death; they provoked armed revolt in Peru and were partially rolled back, but the institution gradually declined over the 16th and 17th centuries as the Crown replaced it with the repartimiento labour draft, the hacienda, and salaried royal administration (corregimientos). Encomiendas were formally abolished in most of the empire in the early 18th century.

Legacy

Historians continue to debate the encomienda's role in the demographic collapse of indigenous America — in which epidemic disease was the largest killer — and its place in the "Black Legend" of Spanish colonisation. Its legacy of coerced labour and concentrated privilege shaped Latin American societies long after abolition.
