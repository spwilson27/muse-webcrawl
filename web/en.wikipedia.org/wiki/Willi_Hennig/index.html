Willi Hennig

Born: April 20, 1913, Dürrhennersdorf

Died: November 5, 1976, Ludwigsburg

Known for: Phylogenetic systematics (cladistics)

Emil Hans Willi Hennig (April 20, 1913 – November 5, 1976) was a German biologist who is considered the founder of phylogenetic systematics, also known as cladistics. His ideas revolutionised biological classification by insisting that taxa should reflect evolutionary relationships — specifically, that only monophyletic groups, consisting of an ancestor and all its descendants, are natural.

Hennig trained as an entomologist, specialising in Diptera (true flies), and worked at the German Entomological Institute in Berlin. Drafted during the Second World War, he drafted the core of his theory while a prisoner of war. His masterwork, Grundzüge einer Theorie der phylogenetischen Systematik, appeared in 1950 but reached a wide audience only with its revised English translation, Phylogenetic Systematics (1966).

Key concepts

Hennig introduced the distinction between shared derived characters (synapomorphies), which diagnose clades, and shared ancestral characters (symplesiomorphies), which do not; between sister groups; and the principle that classification should mirror the branching pattern of evolution, displayed in a cladogram. His auxiliary principle — that shared derived characters should be assumed homologous rather than convergent unless contradicted — remains foundational.

Legacy

Cladistic methods, later combined with molecular data and computer algorithms, became the standard framework of modern systematics. The Willi Hennig Society, founded in 1981, promotes phylogenetic systematics and publishes the journal Cladistics.
