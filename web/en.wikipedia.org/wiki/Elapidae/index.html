Elapidae

Elapidae
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Reptilia
Order: Squamata
Family: Elapidae (Boie, 1827)

Elapidae (/əˈlæpɪdiː/, commonly known as elapids) is a family of snakes characterised by their permanently erect fangs at the front of the mouth. Most elapids bear potent venom, mainly neurotoxic in action, and many are known to be dangerous to humans. The family includes about 400 species in some 55 genera, among them cobras, mambas, kraits, coral snakes, taipans, death adders, and sea snakes.

Description

Elapids range in size from small burrowing species under 20 cm long to the king cobra, which may exceed 5 m. They have fixed, hollow fangs at the front of the maxilla through which venom is injected — a condition called proteroglyphous dentition. Unlike vipers, elapids lack hinged, folding fangs and tend to have round pupils and smooth scales, resembling typical non-venomous colubrid snakes.

Distribution and habitat

Elapidae are found in tropical and subtropical regions around the world, terrestrially in Asia, Australia, Africa, the North and South America, and in the Pacific and Indian Oceans as sea snakes. Australia has more venomous elapid species than any other continent; North and South America are home to the coral snakes. Elapids are absent from Europe.

Venom

Elapid venoms typically contain postsynaptic neurotoxins that block neuromuscular transmission, potentially causing paralysis and respiratory failure. Many species also produce other toxins, including cardiotoxins and, in some Australian species, procoagulants. Sea snakes (subfamily Hydrophiinae) are fully marine in some genera and are among the most venomous snakes known, though they are generally not aggressive toward humans.

Genera

Well-known elapid genera include Naja (true cobras), Ophiophagus (king cobra), Dendroaspis (mambas), Bungarus (kraits), Micrurus (American coral snakes), Oxyuranus (taipans), Acanthophis (death adders), Notechis (tiger snakes), and Laticauda (sea kraits).
