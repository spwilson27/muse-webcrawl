Twenty-fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Twenty-fourth Amendment (Amendment XXIV) to the United States Constitution prohibits both Congress and the states from conditioning the right to vote in federal elections on payment of a poll tax or other types of tax. It was proposed by Congress on August 27, 1962, and ratified by the states on January 23, 1964.

Background

Poll taxes had been used in several Southern states since the late nineteenth century to disenfranchise African American and poor white voters. President Franklin D. Roosevelt and later John F. Kennedy supported abolition. The amendment applied only to federal elections; state poll taxes were struck down two years later by the Supreme Court in Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections (1966), which held them unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Text

"The right of citizens of the United States to vote in any primary or other election for President or Vice President, for electors for President or Vice President, or for Senator or Representative in Congress, shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or any State by reason of failure to pay any poll tax or other tax."
