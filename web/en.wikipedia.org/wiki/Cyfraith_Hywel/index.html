Cyfraith Hywel (Welsh for "the law of Hywel"), also known in Latin as Leges Hywel Dda and in English as the Laws of Hywel the Good, is the system of native Welsh law that operated in medieval Wales until the incorporation of Wales into the Kingdom of England in the sixteenth century. Tradition attributes its codification to Hywel ap Cadell, known as Hywel Dda (Hywel the Good), who ruled most of Wales in the tenth century and is said to have summoned an assembly at Whitland (Hendygwyn) around 945 to revise and record the laws.

Manuscripts and redactions

No text survives from Hywel's lifetime; the earliest manuscripts date from the mid-thirteenth century, and the lawbooks reflect centuries of development. Scholars distinguish three Welsh redactions named after the regions in which they circulated: the Llyfr Iorwerth (Book of Iorwerth) for Gwynedd, the Llyfr Blegywryd for Deheubarth, and the Llyfr Cyfnerth, together with several Latin versions. About forty manuscripts survive. The texts are organized around the laws of the court (concerning the king, his household and officers), the laws of the country (land law, suretyship, theft, homicide and other matters), and in some redactions collections of legal triads and value-lists.

Features

The law is notable for its elaborate system of payments: galanas, the homicide compensation paid by a killer and his kin to the victim's kin — a form of blood money — and sarhaad, the payment for insult or injury, assessed according to the victim's status. Land was generally held by kin groups and partible among sons, including acknowledged illegitimate sons, a practice at odds with English primogeniture. Welsh law gave women certain protections unusual for the period, including defined property rights on separation in some circumstances, though it still placed them under significant disabilities. The status of the professional judge (ynad) and the court of the lord were central institutions.

Abolition and legacy

After the Edwardian conquest of 1282–83, the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) introduced English criminal law while allowing Welsh civil customs to continue in part. The Laws in Wales Acts 1535–1542 completed the legal union, formally abolishing Cyfraith Hywel. It remains an important source for Welsh social history and a symbol of national identity; a modern Welsh act of 2008 made no change to legal practice, but the tradition is commemorated at Whitland and in the name of the Senedd building's debating chamber, the Hywel Dda chamber.
