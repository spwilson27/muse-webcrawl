Staphylococcus aureus

Domain: Bacteria
Phylum: Bacillota
Class: Bacilli
Order: Bacillales
Family: Staphylococcaceae
Genus: Staphylococcus
Species: S. aureus
Gram stain: Positive; cocci in grape-like clusters; non-motile, non-spore-forming

Staphylococcus aureus is a Gram-positive, round-shaped bacterium of the phylum Bacillota, a frequent member of the human microbiota, colonising the skin and the anterior nares (nostrils) of roughly 20–30% of people as a persistent carrier and a larger fraction intermittently. Although often a harmless commensal, it is also one of the most important human pathogens, capable of causing diseases ranging from superficial skin infections to life-threatening invasive illness.

Microbiology

S. aureus cells are spherical cocci about 0.8–1.0 micrometres in diameter that divide in three planes to form irregular, grape-like clusters (staphyle is Greek for a bunch of grapes). Colonies on blood agar are typically golden-yellow — hence aureus, "golden" — owing to the carotenoid pigment staphyloxanthin, which protects the bacterium against reactive oxygen species. The organism is facultatively anaerobic, catalase-positive and coagulase-positive, tests that distinguish it from other staphylococci.

Diseases

Infections attributable to S. aureus include boils, impetigo, cellulitis and wound infections; pneumonia, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis and bacteraemia; and toxin-mediated conditions such as staphylococcal food poisoning (caused by preformed enterotoxins), toxic shock syndrome (mediated by TSST-1) and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome. Its virulence factors include protein A, which binds the Fc region of antibodies; alpha-toxin and other haemolysins; superantigen toxins; and the ability to form biofilms on tissues and implanted devices.

Antibiotic resistance

S. aureus is notorious for acquiring resistance. Penicillin-resistant strains producing beta-lactamase emerged within years of penicillin's introduction. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), carrying the mecA gene on the SCCmec chromosome cassette, was first reported in 1961 and became a major cause of hospital-acquired infection; community-associated MRSA strains emerged in the 1990s. Vancomycin has since been a mainstay treatment for serious MRSA infections, though strains with intermediate and, rarely, full vancomycin resistance have been described.

Prevention and treatment

Control measures emphasise hand hygiene, screening and decolonisation of carriers in high-risk settings, and antimicrobial stewardship. Treatment depends on severity and susceptibility: beta-lactam antibiotics for methicillin-susceptible strains, and agents such as vancomycin, daptomycin or linezolid for MRSA. Despite decades of effort, no licensed vaccine exists.
