Nuclear spallation

Nuclear spallation is a process in which a light projectile particle (such as a proton, neutron or light nucleus) strikes an atomic nucleus with high energy and causes the emission of a large number of nucleons or light fragments. It typically occurs when the kinetic energy of the incoming particle is at least a few hundred MeV, well above the energies of ordinary nuclear reactions.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds in stages. First, the projectile collides with individual nucleons inside the nucleus in an intranuclear cascade, ejecting fast particles and leaving the residual nucleus in a highly excited state. The excited nucleus then de-excites by evaporating neutrons and other light particles, or by fissioning if it is heavy enough. A single high-energy proton striking a heavy target such as lead, tungsten or mercury can produce tens of neutrons, making spallation an efficient source of neutrons.

Applications

Spallation neutron sources — such as the ISIS facility in the United Kingdom, the Spallation Neutron Source at Oak Ridge, the European Spallation Source, and SINQ in Switzerland — use proton accelerators to produce intense pulsed neutron beams for materials science. Spallation is also studied for accelerator-driven systems that could transmute long-lived nuclear waste, and it occurs naturally when cosmic rays strike the atmosphere and planetary surfaces, producing cosmogenic nuclides such as carbon-14, beryllium-10 and aluminium-26 that are used in dating and exposure studies.
