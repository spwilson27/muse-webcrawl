Redshift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of spectral lines shifting toward the red end of the spectrum for a receding galaxy]
Absorption lines redshifted in the spectrum of a distant galaxy.

In physics, a redshift is an increase in the wavelength, and corresponding decrease in the frequency and photon energy, of electromagnetic radiation (such as light). The opposite change, a decrease in wavelength, is called a blueshift. Redshift is quantified by z = (λ_observed − λ_emitted)/λ_emitted.

Three mechanisms produce it: the Doppler effect (sources moving away), gravitational redshift (light climbing out of a gravitational well), and cosmological redshift (the expansion of space stretching light in transit — the basis of Hubble's law). Vesto Slipher's measurements (1912–17) that most spiral nebulae are strongly redshifted were the first evidence of cosmic expansion; today redshifts measure the universe's history — the most distant confirmed galaxies and quasars exceed z = 13, seen within a few hundred million years of the Big Bang — and precision redshift surveys (DESI, Euclid) map cosmic structure to weigh Dark energy and neutrinos.

Uses

Spectroscopic redshift is astronomy's tape measure and clock: it converts directly to distance (via the expansion history) and lookback time. Photometric redshifts estimate it from galaxy colours in imaging surveys. Within galaxies, redshift differences measure rotation (the data behind Vera Rubin's dark-matter evidence); within the Solar System, Doppler shifts navigate spacecraft.
