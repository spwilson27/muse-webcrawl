The characteristic cross-laminated strands of OSB.

Oriented strand board (OSB) is a type of engineered wood similar to particle board, formed by adding adhesives and then compressing layers of wood strands (flakes) in specific orientations. It was invented by Armin Elmendorf in California in 1963 and is now one of the most widely used structural panels in North American construction, where it largely displaced plywood for wall sheathing, roof decking, and subflooring.

Manufacture

OSB is made from fast-growing, small-diameter trees such as aspen poplar, southern yellow pine, and mixed hardwoods. Logs are debarked and sliced into strands roughly 75–150 mm long, which are dried, blended with resin (commonly phenol-formaldehyde or polymeric MDI) and wax, and formed into mats in which the surface layers' strands run along the panel's length and the core layers run across it. The mats are hot-pressed into panels, giving OSB strength and stiffness comparable to plywood in structural applications.

Properties and grades

OSB is rated by exposure and strength grades (in Europe, OSB/1 through OSB/4 under EN 300, from general-purpose to heavy-duty load-bearing for humid conditions). It has no internal voids or knots, can be made in large panel sizes, and uses wood efficiently, but its cut edges are vulnerable to water absorption and swelling, and it is generally less moisture-resistant than exterior-glue plywood.

Uses

Beyond construction sheathing, OSB is used for the webs of I-joists, in furniture and packaging, and as a substrate in structural insulated panels.
