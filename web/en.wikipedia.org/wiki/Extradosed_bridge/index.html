Extradosed bridge

An extradosed bridge employs a structure that combines the main elements of both a prestressed box girder bridge and a cable-stayed bridge. The name comes from the word extrados, the exterior curve of an arch, and refers to how the stay cables pass over saddle supports on low towers above the deck — appearing to run along the extrados of the structure.

Design

In an extradosed bridge the towers are much lower than on a cable-stayed bridge — the stay cables meet the deck at a shallow angle — so the cables act primarily as external prestressing tendons rather than as the main load-carrying system, and the deck girder itself is stiffer and carries most of the load in bending. Because the stress range in the stays under live load is small, the cables can be stressed far higher than ordinary stay cables and need less fatigue protection, allowing simpler anchorages and saddles.

History

The concept was proposed by the French engineer Jacques Mathivat in 1988, who coined the term prestressing by extradosed cables. The first example was Japan's Odawara Blueway Bridge (1994), and the type became especially popular in Japan; notable examples elsewhere include the Sunniberg Bridge in Switzerland (designed by Christian Menn) and the Barton Creek Bridge in Texas.

Advantages

Extradosed bridges suit medium spans where a plain girder would be too deep and a full cable-stayed bridge too costly; they offer a shallower deck than a girder bridge, lower towers and less visual intrusion than a cable-stayed bridge, and comparatively simple construction and maintenance.
