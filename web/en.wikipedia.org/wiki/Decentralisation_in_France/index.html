Decentralisation in France refers to the transfer of powers and responsibilities from the central state to elected local and regional authorities (collectivités territoriales) in a country long known for its centralized, Jacobin tradition. Modern decentralisation began with the reforms of 1982–83 under President François Mitterrand and has proceeded in successive "acts" since.

Background

From the Revolution and Napoleon onward, France was administered through prefects appointed by Paris, who supervised departments and communes. Local councils existed but were subject to state tutelle (supervisory control). De Gaulle created regions in the 1960s–70s, initially as state agencies rather than elected authorities.

The Defferre laws (Act I)

The laws of 1982–83, associated with Interior Minister Gaston Defferre, abolished prefectural prior control over local decisions, transferred executive power in departments and regions from prefects to the presidents of elected councils, made regions full territorial authorities with directly elected councils (first elections in 1986), and devolved responsibilities such as urban planning, vocational training, and school buildings.

Act II and later reforms

A constitutional revision in 2003 inscribed decentralisation in the Constitution, describing France's organisation as "decentralised," and new transfers followed (roads, school support staff, parts of social welfare). The NOTRe law of 2015 redrew the map—reducing metropolitan regions from 22 to 13—and clarified competences, giving regions the lead on economic development and transport while departments focus on social solidarity. Debates continue over overlapping responsibilities (the millefeuille territorial), local finance, and the very large number of communes (around 35,000) and intercommunal bodies.
