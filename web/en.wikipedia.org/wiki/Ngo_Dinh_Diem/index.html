Ngo Dinh Diem

Ngo Dinh Diem (1901–1963) was a Vietnamese politician who served as the first president of the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam) from 1955 until his assassination in 1963. A devout Catholic mandarin from a prominent family of central Vietnam, he became the central figure of the American-backed state during the early Vietnam War.

Early life and rise

Born in Quảng Bình province into a Catholic family that had served the Nguyễn court, Diem rose in the colonial administration and briefly served as interior minister to Emperor Bảo Đại in 1933 before resigning in protest at French interference. He spent years in exile, largely in the United States, before Bảo Đại appointed him prime minister of the State of Vietnam in 1954, after the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu. In a 1955 referendum widely regarded as fraudulent, Diem deposed Bảo Đại and proclaimed the Republic of Vietnam with himself as president.

Presidency and downfall

Diem's regime, heavily supported by the United States, refused to hold the unification elections envisaged by the 1954 Geneva Accords and persecuted communists and other opponents. His brother Ngo Dinh Nhu ran the secret police and the regime's political apparatus. Discrimination in favour of Catholics alienated the Buddhist majority; the 1963 Buddhist crisis, sparked by the shooting of protesters in Huế and the self-immolation of Thích Quảng Đức, destroyed American confidence. On 1–2 November 1963 Diem and Nhu were overthrown and killed in a military coup tacitly encouraged by Washington.
