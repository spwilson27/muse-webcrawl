Jujutsu (Japanese: 柔術, jūjutsu), also known as jujitsu or jiu-jitsu, is a family of Japanese martial arts and a system of close combat that can be used in a defensive or offensive manner to kill or subdue one or more weaponless or armed and armoured opponents. A subset of techniques from certain styles of jujutsu was used to develop many modern martial arts and combat sports, such as judo, aikido, sambo, ARB, Brazilian jiu-jitsu, and mixed martial arts.

Etymology

Jū can be translated as "gentle, soft, supple, flexible, or yielding", and jutsu can be translated as "art or technique". "Jujutsu" thus has the meaning of "yielding art", as its core philosophy stresses manipulating the opponent's force against themselves rather than confronting it with one's own force, and techniques that subdue opponents without excessive injury.

History

Origins

Jujutsu developed among the samurai of feudal Japan as a method for defeating an armed and armoured opponent in close combat in which the use of weapons was ineffective or impractical. Because striking against an armoured opponent proved ineffective, practitioners learned that the most efficient methods for neutralising an enemy took the form of pins, joint locks, and throws. These techniques were developed around the principle of using an attacker's energy against them, rather than directly opposing it.

Development of the schools

Numerous distinct schools (ryū) of jujutsu arose over the centuries, each emphasising different combinations of striking, throwing, joint manipulation, weapon retention, and battlefield grappling. Well-known historical schools include Takenouchi-ryū (founded 1532), Tenshin Shōden Katori Shintō-ryū, and Yoshin-ryū. During the Edo period, when open warfare declined, many schools adapted their techniques for unarmed civilian defence and law enforcement.

Modern era

In the late 19th century, Kanō Jigorō synthesised techniques from several jujutsu schools into judo, emphasising safe randori (free practice) and removing the most dangerous techniques from general practice. Other modern arts derived from jujutsu include aikido, developed by Morihei Ueshiba, and Brazilian jiu-jitsu, which developed after Mitsuyo Maeda taught the art to the Gracie family in Brazil.

Techniques

Jujutsu techniques are commonly grouped into striking (atemi-waza), throwing (nage-waza), joint locking (kansetsu-waza), choking and strangling (shime-waza), pinning (osaekomi-waza), and weapon defences. Traditional schools also trained in the use of small weapons and in resuscitation methods (kappō).
