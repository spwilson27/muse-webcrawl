Logarithmic spiral

Logarithmic spiral
Polar equationr = a·ebθ
Also calledEquiangular spiral, growth spiral, spira mirabilis
First described byRené Descartes (1638); studied by Jacob Bernoulli
Defining propertyConstant angle between the radius vector and the tangent

A logarithmic spiral, equiangular spiral, or growth spiral is a self-similar spiral curve that appears often in nature. It is defined in polar coordinates by r = a·ebθ, where a and b are real constants. The defining property is that the angle between the tangent to the curve and the radial line at any point is constant; hence "equiangular". Because the radius grows exponentially with angle, the spiral maintains the same shape at every scale — rotations of the curve are equivalent to scalings.

History

The curve was first described by René Descartes in 1638 in correspondence with Marin Mersenne, and was independently investigated by Evangelista Torricelli. Jacob Bernoulli studied it so extensively that he called it the spira mirabilis ("miraculous spiral") and requested it be engraved on his tombstone with the motto Eadem mutata resurgo ("Although changed, I arise the same") — referring to its self-similarity. (The stonemason carved an Archimedean spiral instead.)

Properties

  • The distance between successive turnings increases in geometric progression.
  • The arc length from any point to the centre is finite, despite infinitely many turns.
  • The evolute and involute of a logarithmic spiral are also logarithmic spirals.
  • The golden spiral is the special case whose growth factor per quarter turn involves the golden ratio φ; it is often (loosely) associated with shells and the Fibonacci sequence.

In nature and applications

Approximations to logarithmic spirals occur in the shells of molluscs such as the nautilus, the arms of spiral galaxies, the bands of tropical cyclones, the flight path of a hawk approaching prey (which keeps a constant angle to its target), and the arrangement of seeds and florets. In engineering, the curve underlies certain antenna designs (log-spiral antennas) whose performance is frequency-independent, and cam profiles when constant pressure angle is required.
