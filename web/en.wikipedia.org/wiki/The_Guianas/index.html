The Guianas

The Guianas, also known by its Spanish and Portuguese names Las Guayanas and As Guianas, is a region on the northeastern Atlantic coast of South America. It comprises the independent states of Guyana and Suriname and the French overseas department of French Guiana, and in broader geographical usage also takes in parts of neighbouring Venezuela (Guayana Region) and Brazil (Amapá).

Geography

The region lies between the Orinoco delta in the west and the Amazon delta in the east, backed inland by the ancient, deeply weathered Guiana Shield, one of the oldest geological formations on Earth. The interior is dominated by tropical rainforest and table-top mountains (tepuis), including Mount Roraima. The coastal plain, where most of the population lives, is largely below sea level in places and protected by dykes, a legacy of Dutch colonial engineering.

History

The coast was explored by Europeans from 1500 and contested by Spanish, Dutch, English, French and Portuguese colonisers; the plantation colonies of Essequibo, Demerara, Berbice, Suriname and Cayenne traded hands repeatedly. The present political map was settled in the 19th century: British Guiana (now Guyana, independent in 1966), Dutch Guiana (Suriname, independent in 1975), and French Guiana, which remains an overseas department of France and hosts the Guiana Space Centre at Kourou, Europe's principal spaceport.

Politics and disputes

Several territorial disputes persist, most prominently Venezuela's claim to the Essequibo region west of the Essequibo River — about two thirds of Guyana — revived after major offshore oil discoveries in Guyanese waters. French Guiana, as part of France, is the only territory of the European Union on the South American mainland.
