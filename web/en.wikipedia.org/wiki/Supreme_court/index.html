Supreme court

A supreme court is the highest court in a jurisdiction's hierarchy of courts.

In most legal jurisdictions, a supreme court, also known as a court of last resort, is the highest court within the hierarchy of courts. Broadly speaking, the decisions of a supreme court are not subject to further review by any other court. Supreme courts typically function primarily as appellate courts, hearing appeals from decisions of lower trial courts, or from intermediate-level appellate courts.

Functions

A supreme court usually has final authority over questions of law within its jurisdiction. In many countries its docket is discretionary: litigants must petition for review, and the court selects cases raising significant or unsettled legal questions. Many supreme courts also exercise constitutional review, invalidating legislation inconsistent with the constitution; in some systems, a separate constitutional court performs that role instead.

Examples

Examples include the Supreme Court of the United States, the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom (established in 2009, succeeding the Appellate Committee of the House of Lords), the Supreme Court of India — one of the world's busiest apex courts — the Supreme Court of Japan, and the High Court of Australia, which serves as Australia's court of last resort. The specific Supreme Court of New South Wales is, by contrast, a state-level superior court within Australia, subject to appeal to the High Court.

Appointment and tenure

Supreme court judges are variously appointed by heads of state, confirmed by legislatures, or selected by judicial commissions. Tenure protections — such as appointment until a mandatory retirement age or for life during good behaviour — are intended to secure judicial independence.
