Decentralization is the process by which the activities of an organization, particularly regarding planning and decision-making, are distributed or delegated away from a central, authoritative location or group. The concept is applied in government, public administration, economics, management, and technology, and is usually discussed in contrast with centralization.

In government

Political decentralization transfers authority to elected subnational governments. Its main forms are devolution (transfer of powers to autonomous units, as in federal systems or devolved administrations), delegation (transfer to semi-autonomous agencies accountable to the center), and deconcentration (shifting workloads to field offices of the central state). Fiscal decentralization concerns the revenue-raising and spending powers of local governments. Advocates argue decentralization improves responsiveness, local knowledge, participation, and experimentation; critics cite risks of inequality between regions, duplication, weaker coordination, and local capture.

In organizations and economics

Firms decentralize by pushing decisions to divisions, plants, or front-line workers, balancing local responsiveness against control. In markets, decentralization describes allocation through many independent actors rather than central planning, a theme running from classical economics to Friedrich Hayek's argument about dispersed knowledge.

In technology

Decentralized computing distributes processing and data across many nodes rather than a central server: peer-to-peer networks, federated protocols, and blockchains are prominent examples. Decentralized systems are valued for resilience, censorship resistance, and lack of a single point of failure, but can face challenges in efficiency, governance, and security.
