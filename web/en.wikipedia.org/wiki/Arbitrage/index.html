Arbitrage


Arbitrage exploits a price difference between two or more markets.

Arbitrage is the practice of taking advantage of a price difference between two or more markets: striking a combination of matching deals that capitalize upon the difference, the profit being the difference between the market prices at which the unit is traded. When used by academics, an arbitrage is a transaction that involves no negative cash flow at any probabilistic or temporal state and a positive cash flow in at least one state; in simple terms, it is the possibility of a risk-free profit.

People who engage in arbitrage are called arbitrageurs. The term is mainly applied to trading in financial instruments, such as bonds, stocks, derivatives, commodities and currencies. If the market prices do not allow for profitable arbitrage, the prices are said to constitute an arbitrage equilibrium, or a market that is arbitrage-free. Such an equilibrium is implied by the assumption that market participants will not allow persistent mispricings to go unexploited. The concept is closely related to the law of one price and to the theory of efficient markets.

Conditions for arbitrage

Arbitrage can take place when one or more of the following conditions are met:

  • The same asset does not trade at the same price on all markets.
  • Two assets with identical cash flows do not trade at the same price.
  • An asset with a known price in the future does not today trade at that future price discounted at the risk-free interest rate (or the asset has significant costs of storage).

In reality, pure arbitrage opportunities are rare, short-lived, and typically available only in small sizes, since the very act of exploiting them moves prices toward equilibrium.

Price convergence

Arbitrage has the effect of causing prices in different markets to converge. As a result of arbitrage, the currency exchange rates, the price of commodities, and the price of securities in different markets tend to converge. The speed at which prices converge is a measure of market efficiency. Arbitrage tends to reduce price discrimination by encouraging people to buy an item where the price is low and resell it where the price is high.

Types of arbitrage

Risk arbitrage

Risk arbitrage, also known as merger arbitrage, involves purchasing the stock of a company that is the target of a takeover while shorting the stock of the acquirer. Because the deal may fail, the profit is not certain, and the strategy is therefore only loosely described as arbitrage.

Convertible arbitrage

A convertible bond is a bond that an investor can return to the issuing company in exchange for a predetermined number of shares. Convertible arbitrage involves buying the convertible bond and hedging two of the three factors in order to gain exposure to the third factor at a very attractive price.

Statistical arbitrage

Statistical arbitrage is a class of short-term financial trading strategies that employ mean-reversion models involving broadly diversified securities portfolios, often implemented by quantitative and algorithmic trading systems. It differs from classical arbitrage in that losses are possible, but the strategy is designed so that on average the trades are profitable.

Regulatory and tax arbitrage

Regulatory arbitrage occurs when institutions take advantage of differences or loopholes in regulatory regimes, while tax arbitrage exploits differences between tax systems, such as differing treatments of income in different jurisdictions.

Risks

In practice, arbitrage trades carry execution risk (prices may move before both legs are completed), liquidity risk, counterparty risk, and, for strategies such as merger arbitrage, event risk that the anticipated transaction does not occur. During financial crises, convergence trades can suffer severe losses when funding dries up, famously illustrated by the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management.
