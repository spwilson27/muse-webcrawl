Seneca Village

Seneca Village was a 19th-century settlement of mostly African American landowners in the borough of Manhattan in New York City, within what is now Central Park. Founded in 1825, it was demolished in 1857 when the city took the land through eminent domain to create the park.

History

The community began when trustees of the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church and individual buyers such as Andrew Williams and Epiphany Davis purchased lots from John and Elizabeth Whitehead, who subdivided their farmland. By the 1850s the village had roughly 225 residents, about two-thirds Black, with Irish and German immigrant neighbours, three churches, a school, and cemeteries. Land ownership was politically significant: under New York's 1821 constitution, Black men needed $250 in property to vote, and Seneca Village's property owners formed a notable share of the city's Black electorate.

Demolition and legacy

Residents resisted the taking through petitions and lawsuits but were evicted by 1857. The village was largely forgotten until historians and archaeologists revived interest in the late 20th century; a 2011 excavation by Columbia University and the City University of New York recovered artefacts, and the Central Park Conservancy has installed interpretive signage near the former site between 81st and 89th Streets on the park's west side.
