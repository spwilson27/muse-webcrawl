Midnight movie

The term midnight movie describes a phenomenon that originated in the United States in the 1950s with late-night television broadcasts of low-budget films, and developed in the 1970s into a popular cultural practice: the screening of cult and countercultural films at cinemas at midnight, typically on Friday and Saturday nights, before devoted repeat audiences.

Television origins

In the 1950s, local television stations began programming cheap horror and science-fiction films in late-night slots, often presented by costumed hosts such as Vampira in Los Angeles and Zacherley in Philadelphia and New York. These broadcasts built cult followings for films that had been ignored or dismissed on their original theatrical releases.

Theatrical phenomenon

The theatrical midnight movie is usually dated to 1970–71 at New York's Elgin Theater, where Alejandro Jodorowsky's surrealist western El Topo (1970) played to sold-out midnight audiences. It was followed by John Waters's Pink Flamingos (1972), the zombie film Night of the Living Dead (1968), the reggae film The Harder They Come (1972), and David Lynch's Eraserhead (1977).

The most famous midnight movie is The Rocky Horror Picture Show (1975), an initial flop that from 1976 developed an elaborate participatory culture of audience callbacks, props and shadow-cast performances, and remains in limited release decades later — the longest theatrical run in film history.

Later developments

Home video and streaming eroded the exclusivity of the midnight slot from the 1980s onward, but repertory cinemas continue to programme midnight screenings, and the term has broadened to describe the aesthetics of cult cinema generally: transgressive, strange, low-budget or camp films that find their audience outside the mainstream.
