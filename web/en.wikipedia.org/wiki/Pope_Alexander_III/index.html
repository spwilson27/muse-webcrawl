Pope Alexander III (c. 1100/1105 – 30 August 1181), born Roland of Siena (Rolando Bandinelli), was head of the Catholic Church from 7 September 1159 until his death in 1181. His long pontificate was dominated by conflict with the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick Barbarossa and by the schism created by imperial antipopes.

Early life

Roland Bandinelli was born in Siena and became a distinguished canon lawyer, teaching at Bologna. He authored the Stroma and the Sententiae Rolandi, an influential commentary on Gratian's Decretum. Pope Eugenius III made him cardinal deacon in 1150 and later cardinal priest and papal chancellor.

Pontificate

Alexander's election in 1159 was contested by a minority who chose Victor IV, the first of several antipopes supported by Frederick Barbarossa. Alexander spent much of his reign in exile from Rome, residing in France and in cities of the Lombard League. The struggle culminated in the League's victory at the Battle of Legnano in 1176 and the Peace of Venice in 1177, in which Frederick recognised Alexander as legitimate pope.

Alexander supported Thomas Becket in his dispute with Henry II of England and canonised Becket in 1173, three years after his murder. In 1179 he presided over the Third Lateran Council, which decreed that a two-thirds majority of cardinals was required for a valid papal election — a rule that endures today — and condemned the Cathar heresy. He also laid the foundation stone of Notre-Dame de Paris in 1163, according to tradition.

Legacy

Alexander III is remembered as one of the great lawyer-popes of the Middle Ages, whose decretals shaped canon law and whose defence of ecclesiastical liberty against imperial power strengthened the medieval papacy. The city of Alessandria in Piedmont was named in his honour.
