Melanesia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Melanesia is a subregion of Oceania extending from New Guinea and the Bismarck Archipelago eastward through the Solomon Islands and Vanuatu to Fiji and New Caledonia. Its name (from Greek melas, black, + nesos, islands) was coined by nineteenth-century Europeans for the dark skin of its peoples — a racial geography now used as a neutral regional label, alongside Polynesia and Micronesia.

Peoples and history

Melanesia holds some of humanity's oldest and most diverse cultures: New Guinea was settled at least 40,000–50,000 years ago and independently invented agriculture (Kuk Swamp, UNESCO-listed), and the region speaks over a fifth of the world's languages — Papua New Guinea alone has more than 800 living tongues. The Lapita culture spread through island Melanesia from about 1500 BC, carrying pottery, obsidian and the ancestors of Polynesians eastward.

Colonial division among Germany, Britain, France, the Netherlands and Australia left today's states — Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands, Vanuatu, Fiji — and the French territory of New Caledonia, whose Kanak independence question remains unresolved after the referendums of 2018–2021. The region was a major theatre of the Pacific War (Guadalcanal, Kokoda), and its politics today pivot on logging, deep-sea mining, climate vulnerability shared with the atolls, and the strategic courtship of China, Australia and the United States.
