Computer-aided design

Computer-aided design (CAD) is the use of computers to aid in the creation, modification, analysis, or optimisation of a design. CAD software is used to increase the productivity of the designer, improve the quality of design, improve communications through documentation, and create a database for manufacturing. Designs are made in two dimensions (2D drafting) or three dimensions (3D modelling using wireframe, surface, or solid modelling), and outputs — drawings, models, and toolpath data — feed into computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) and engineering analysis (CAE).

History

The origins of CAD lie in the 1960s: Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963) demonstrated interactive computer graphics, and early commercial systems were developed at large aerospace and automotive firms and by companies such as Hanratty's ADAM and the French firm Dassault (CATIA). Patrick Hanratty is often called the "father of CAD/CAM". The spread of personal computers in the 1980s — notably AutoCAD (1982) — democratised 2D drafting, while parametric solid modelling (Pro/ENGINEER, 1988) changed 3D practice by representing designs as editable feature histories. Modern systems such as SolidWorks, Autodesk Inventor and Fusion, CATIA, NX, and Creo are standard in manufacturing industries.

Uses

CAD is used throughout engineering and design: mechanical parts and assemblies, architecture and construction (where it merges into building information modelling, BIM), civil engineering, electronics (electronic design automation for circuits and PCBs), industrial and product design, animation, and jewellery and fashion. Typical functions include geometric modelling, assembly and interference checking, rendering, technical drawing generation, tolerance analysis, and interfaces to simulation (finite element analysis, computational fluid dynamics) and to CAM for CNC machining and 3D printing.

Parametric and direct modelling

Parametric modelling defines geometry through parameters and constraints, so a design can be changed by editing values while preserving design intent; direct modelling manipulates geometry without a feature history. Contemporary tools increasingly combine both, and add generative design — algorithms that propose optimised forms from specified loads and constraints — and cloud-based collaboration.
