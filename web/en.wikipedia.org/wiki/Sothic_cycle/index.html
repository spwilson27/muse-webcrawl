Sothic cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sothic cycle or Canicular period is a period of 1,461 Egyptian civil years (or 1,460 Julian years) after which the heliacal rising of the star Sirius (Egyptian: Sopdet, Greek: Sothis) again coincides with New Year's Day in the ancient Egyptian civil calendar.

Mechanism

The Egyptian civil year consisted of exactly 365 days—twelve months of thirty days plus five epagomenal days—with no leap year. It therefore drifted against the solar year by about one day every four years. The heliacal rising of Sirius, which in antiquity roughly coincided with the onset of the Nile flood, slipped through all the seasons over the cycle before returning to the same calendar date. Recorded dates of the rising in dated documents thus allow Egyptologists to anchor the floating Egyptian chronology to absolute dates.

Historical records

The Roman author Censorinus reported that Sirius rose on Egyptian New Year's Day in AD 139, implying earlier coincidences around 1321–1322 BCE and 2781–2782 BCE. A small number of dated Egyptian texts record Sothic observations—most famously the Illahun papyrus from the reign of Senusret III and a reference from the reign of Amenhotep I—which provide key fixed points for dating the Middle and New Kingdoms, though their interpretation (including the place of observation) is debated.

Significance

Because the cycle supplies rare absolute dates in Egyptian history, arguments over Sothic dating have outsized effects on the chronology of the entire ancient Near East. Proposed reforms of the calendar, such as that attributed to the Canopus Decree of 238 BCE, would have added a leap day every four years and ended the drift, but they were not generally adopted until the Augustan reform of the Alexandrian calendar.
