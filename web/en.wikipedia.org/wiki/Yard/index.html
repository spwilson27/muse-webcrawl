The yard (symbol: yd) is an English unit of length in both the British imperial and US customary systems of measurement, equal to 3 feet or 36 inches. Since 1959 it has been defined by international agreement as exactly 0.9144 metres. A corresponding unit of area is the square yard, and of volume the cubic yard.

History

The yard's origin is uncertain. Its name derives from the Old English gerd or gyrd, a rod or measuring stick. Tradition held that Henry I of England defined the yard as the distance from the tip of his nose to the end of his outstretched thumb, though this is likely legend. The yard was standardised in statute under Edward I and Edward III, and a physical standard — the imperial standard yard — was created in 1845 after the earlier standard was destroyed in the 1834 fire of the Palace of Westminster.

In 1866 the United States legalised the use of the metric system and defined the yard in terms of the metre (1 yard = 3600/3937 metres under the Mendenhall Order of 1893). On 1 July 1959 the English-speaking nations adopted the international yard of exactly 0.9144 metres. A slightly different "survey yard" persisted in US geodetic surveying until its deprecation at the end of 2022.

Current use

The yard remains in everyday use in the United States for fabric, landscaping, and many sports, and is the unit of American and Canadian football fields (100 yards between goal lines). In the United Kingdom it is used on road signs for short distances and in some informal contexts, though the metre predominates officially. Fabric and ribbon are still commonly sold by the yard in many countries.

Equivalents

  • 1 yard = 3 feet = 36 inches
  • 1 yard = 0.9144 metres (exact)
  • 1,760 yards = 1 statute mile
  • 220 yards = 1 furlong; 22 yards = 1 chain (the length of a cricket pitch)
