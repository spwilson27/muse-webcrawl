Angle trisection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Angle trisection is the construction, using only a compass and an unmarked straightedge, of an angle that is one third of a given arbitrary angle. It is one of the three famous problems of ancient Greek geometry, alongside squaring the circle and doubling the cube, and it was proved impossible in 1837 by Pierre Wantzel.

The problem

The Greeks knew how to bisect any angle and to construct many particular angles, and some angles can be trisected (for example a right angle, since a 30° angle is constructible). The question was whether a universal method exists for an arbitrary angle. Using the triple-angle formula, trisecting an angle θ leads to solving a cubic equation in cos(θ/3); for θ = 60° this reduces to an irreducible cubic with three real roots.

Impossibility proof

Wantzel showed that numbers constructible with straightedge and compass are exactly those obtainable by successive quadratic extensions of the rationals, so a constructible number must have degree a power of two over the rationals. Because the cubic for cos(20°) is irreducible, cos(20°) has degree three, is therefore not constructible, and a 60° angle cannot be trisected. The proof relies on field theory developed in the nineteenth century.

Methods with other tools

Trisection becomes possible if other devices are allowed. Archimedes gave a construction using a marked straightedge (neusis); Nicomedes used the conchoid curve; others used the quadratrix of Hippias, the Archimedean spiral, or conic sections. Approximate trisections, and trisections by paper folding (origami), are also possible, since origami can solve cubic equations.

Legacy

Despite the proof, "angle trisectors" continue to submit purported constructions to mathematicians, making the problem a famous example of a mathematical crank topic. The impossibility result is now standard undergraduate material in courses on Galois theory.
