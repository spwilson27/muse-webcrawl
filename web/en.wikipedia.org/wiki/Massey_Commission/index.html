Massey Commission

The Massey Commission, officially the Royal Commission on National Development in the Arts, Letters and Sciences, was a Canadian royal commission established in 1949 by Prime Minister Louis St. Laurent. Chaired by Vincent Massey, the commission examined the state of Canadian culture and the role of the federal government in supporting the arts, humanities, social sciences, broadcasting, and national institutions. Its report, issued in 1951, is widely regarded as one of the most influential documents in Canadian cultural policy.

Background

In the aftermath of the Second World War, Canadian cultural life was dominated by American and British imports, and federal support for the arts was minimal and unsystematic. The commission was appointed on 8 April 1949, with commissioners including social scientist Georges-Henri Lévesque, historian Hilda Neatby, and businessman Norman Mackenzie. It held hearings across the country and received hundreds of briefs.

Recommendations and outcomes

The Massey Report recommended sustained federal funding of culture and scholarship. Its recommendations led directly or indirectly to the creation of the Canada Council for the Arts (1957), the establishment of the National Library of Canada (1953), increased support for universities through federal grants, the strengthening of the CBC and the National Film Board, and support for the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation's television service. The report famously declared that it was "desirable that the initiative continue to come from the universities themselves" while insisting that Canadian cultural life required public investment to survive.
