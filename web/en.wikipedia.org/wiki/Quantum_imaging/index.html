Quantum imaging

Quantum imaging is a field of quantum optics that uses quantum correlations of light, such as entanglement between photons, to image objects with resolution, sensitivity, or functionality beyond what is possible with classical optics.

In ghost imaging, an image is formed from correlations between a photon that interacted with the object and a partner photon that never did.

Ghost imaging

The best-known quantum imaging protocol is ghost imaging, first demonstrated in the 1990s using entangled photon pairs produced by spontaneous parametric down-conversion. One photon of each pair passes through (or is reflected from) the object and is collected by a bucket detector with no spatial resolution, while the other is recorded by a spatially resolving detector that never sees the object. Coincidence measurements between the two detectors reconstruct an image of the object.

Sub-shot-noise and super-resolution imaging

Quantum states of light such as squeezed states and N00N states can reduce measurement noise below the shot-noise limit or achieve spatial resolution finer than the Rayleigh diffraction limit. Quantum lithography, proposed by Boto and collaborators in 2000, suggested patterning features at a fraction of the classical wavelength using entangled multiphoton states, though practical implementations remain difficult.

Imaging with undetected photons

Another technique, imaging with undetected photons, uses induced coherence between two down-conversion sources. The object is probed by photons at one wavelength that are never detected, while the image is formed with partner photons at a different wavelength that are detected, enabling imaging at infrared or terahertz wavelengths with visible-light cameras.
