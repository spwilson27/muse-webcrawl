George Gilbert Scott

Sir George Gilbert Scott RA (13 July 1811 – 27 March 1878) was an English Gothic Revival architect.

Born: Gawcott, Buckinghamshire
Died: London
Notable works: Midland Grand Hotel; Albert Memorial; St Pancras station

Sir George Gilbert Scott was a prolific English architect of the Victorian era, principally associated with the design, building, and renovation of churches and cathedrals, workhouses, and public buildings in the Gothic Revival style.

Career

Born in Gawcott, Buckinghamshire, the son of a clergyman, Scott was articled to an architect and formed a partnership designing workhouses. Influenced by Augustus Pugin, he became a leading advocate of Gothic architecture. His practice became the largest in Victorian England: he designed or restored some 800 buildings, including work on Westminster Abbey, and the cathedrals of Ely, Salisbury, Chichester, and St Davids.

Major works

Scott's best-known secular works include the Albert Memorial in Kensington Gardens (completed 1872), the Midland Grand Hotel at St Pancras station in London (opened 1873), the main building of the University of Glasgow (1870), and the Foreign Office in Whitehall — the last a famous battleground in which Lord Palmerston forced Scott to abandon Gothic for an Italianate design. Abroad, he designed St Nicholas' Church in Hamburg, then the tallest building in the world.

Legacy

Knighted in 1872, Scott was president of the Royal Institute of British Architects and is buried in Westminster Abbey. His sons George Gilbert Scott Jr. and John Oldrid Scott, and his grandson Giles Gilbert Scott (designer of Liverpool Cathedral and the red telephone box), continued the family's architectural dynasty. Critics have debated his heavy-handed restoration of medieval churches — the "restoration" controversy helped provoke William Morris to found the Society for the Protection of Ancient Buildings in 1877.
