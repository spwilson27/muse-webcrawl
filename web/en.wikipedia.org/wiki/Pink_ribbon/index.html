The pink ribbon is an international symbol of breast cancer awareness. It is used to express moral support for women and men with breast cancer, to raise funds for research, education, screening, and treatment, and to honour those affected by the disease. Pink ribbons are most prominently displayed during Breast Cancer Awareness Month in October.

History

Awareness ribbons had earlier precedents, including the yellow ribbon for hostages and the red ribbon for HIV/AIDS awareness. In 1991, the Susan G. Komen Foundation distributed pink ribbons to participants in its Race for the Cure in New York City. The symbol was popularised nationally in 1992 when Alexandra Penney, editor of Self magazine, working with Evelyn Lauder of Estée Lauder, created a pink ribbon for distribution at cosmetics counters; peach-coloured ribbons had earlier been promoted independently by activist Charlotte Haley, who declined to commercialise her symbol.

Use and campaigns

The ribbon appears on clothing, cosmetics, household products, sporting events, and public buildings illuminated in pink. Professional sports leagues, including the NFL, have adopted pink accessories during October. Landmarks from the White House to the Sydney Opera House have been lit pink for awareness campaigns.

Criticism

The commercialisation of the ribbon has drawn criticism under the label "pinkwashing" — applied to companies that market pink products while contributing little to the cause or while selling products linked to carcinogens. Organisations such as Breast Cancer Action have campaigned for greater transparency about how much of a product's price actually reaches breast-cancer charities, urging consumers to "think before you pink."

Related symbols

Other coloured ribbons denote other causes: red for HIV/AIDS, teal for ovarian cancer, and blue for prostate cancer awareness, among many others.
