Jean Bodin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean Bodin (c. 1530 – 1596) was a French jurist and political philosopher, a member of the Parlement of Paris and professor of law at Toulouse. His Six Books of the Commonwealth (Les Six Livres de la République, 1576) gave the classic early-modern theory of sovereignty: “the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth”, indivisible and inalienable, whose chief mark is the power to make law binding on all subjects without their consent.

Bodin wrote amid the French Wars of Religion and sought a theoretical foundation for strong royal authority that could end civil strife; yet he held the sovereign bound by divine and natural law and by the fundamental laws of the realm (such as the Salic succession rule), and he condemned confiscation and arbitrary taxation without consent. He also wrote a notorious work on witchcraft (Démonomanie des sorciers, 1580) urging vigorous prosecution — a reminder of the distance between early-modern and modern thought — and a comparative study of religions (the Colloquium Heptaplomeres) remarkable for its tolerance. His sovereignty doctrine shaped Hobbes and the modern state concept.
