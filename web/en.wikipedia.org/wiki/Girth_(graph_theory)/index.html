Girth (graph theory)

In graph theory, the girth of an undirected graph is the length of its shortest cycle. If the graph contains no cycles at all (that is, it is a forest), its girth is defined to be infinity. For example, a 4-cycle (a square) has girth 4, and a complete graph on three vertices (a triangle) has girth 3.

Properties

Every planar graph with girth at least k and minimum degree conditions satisfies constraints related to Euler's formula; for instance, a planar triangle-free graph (girth at least 4) on n vertices has at most 2n − 4 edges. Graphs with large girth and large chromatic number exist — a celebrated result first demonstrated by Paul Erdős using the probabilistic method — showing that local sparseness does not imply simple global colouring behaviour.

Cage graphs

A cage is a regular graph with the fewest possible vertices for a given combination of degree and girth. Well-known cages include the Petersen graph, which is the unique (3,5)-cage, and the Heawood graph, the (3,6)-cage. Cage graphs are central to the study of extremal graph theory.

Computation

The girth of a graph can be computed by running a breadth-first search from each vertex and detecting the shortest cycle through it; naive algorithms run in polynomial time, though faster algorithms exist for sparse graphs. Related notions include the odd girth and even girth, the lengths of the shortest odd and even cycles respectively.
