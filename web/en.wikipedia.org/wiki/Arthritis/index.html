Arthritis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arthritis is a term for disorders that affect the joints, with symptoms including pain, stiffness, swelling and reduced range of motion. It is not one disease: over 100 types are recognised. The two most common are osteoarthritis — degenerative breakdown of joint cartilage, driven by age, wear, injury and obesity — and rheumatoid arthritis, an autoimmune disease in which the Immune system attacks the joint lining, causing erosive inflammation and deformity.

Arthritis is a leading cause of disability, particularly in older adults. Gout (crystal deposition from excess uric acid), psoriatic arthritis, lupus-related arthritis, septic arthritis and childhood (juvenile idiopathic) arthritis are other major forms.

Treatment

Osteoarthritis care emphasises exercise, weight management, physiotherapy, analgesics and, for end-stage joints, replacement surgery (hip and knee replacements are among the most successful operations in medicine). Rheumatoid arthritis is treated early and aggressively with disease-modifying drugs (methotrexate and biologic agents such as TNF inhibitors), which have transformed its prognosis.

Diagnosis

History, examination, X-rays and blood tests (inflammatory markers, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP antibodies, uric acid) distinguish the types — an important step, as treatments differ radically.
