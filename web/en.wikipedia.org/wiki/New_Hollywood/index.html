New Hollywood
Director-driven filmmaking in 1970s Hollywood
Periodc. 1967 – early 1980s
Key directorsScorsese, Coppola, Altman, Friedkin, Kubrick, Spielberg, Lucas
Landmark filmsBonnie and Clyde (1967); The Godfather (1972); Taxi Driver (1976)
InfluencesFrench New Wave; auteur theory

New Hollywood, also called the American New Wave or the Hollywood Renaissance, was a movement in American film history from the mid-1960s to the early 1980s, when a new generation of young filmmakers came to prominence. They influenced the types of films produced, their production and marketing, and the way major studios approached filmmaking. In New Hollywood films, the film director, rather than the studio, took on a key auteur role.

Origins

The collapse of the old studio system, the success of European art cinema, and the example of the French New Wave — championed by Cahiers du Cinéma — created space for young, film-school-trained or cinephile directors. Arthur Penn's Bonnie and Clyde (1967), Mike Nichols's The Graduate (1967) and Dennis Hopper's Easy Rider (1969) proved that low-budget, morally ambiguous, formally daring films could succeed with young audiences; the crime-gangster lineage these films reworked ran back through film noir to Hammett and Cain.

Peak and decline

The 1970s brought a string of director-driven landmarks: The Godfather (1972), The Exorcist (1973), Chinatown (1974), One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest (1975), Taxi Driver (1976) and Apocalypse Now (1979). The blockbuster successes of Jaws (1975) and Star Wars (1977), and the financial disaster of Heaven's Gate (1980), swung power back to the studios and towards high-concept, market-tested filmmaking, ending the era's unusual creative freedom.
