Capillary length

Image placeholder: Droplets smaller than the capillary length are nearly spherical.

The capillary length or capillary constant is a length scaling factor that relates gravitational forces and surface tension. It is a fundamental physical parameter used in the study of capillary action and interfacial phenomena, and it characterises the scale at which the curvature of a meniscus matters.

Definition

The capillary length, usually denoted λc or κ−1, is defined as:

λc = √( γ / (ρg) )

where γ is the surface tension of the fluid interface, ρ is the density of the fluid, and g is the gravitational acceleration. For water at room temperature, the capillary length is approximately 2.7 mm.

Significance

Objects much smaller than the capillary length are dominated by surface tension: a water droplet smaller than a few millimetres is nearly spherical, while larger puddles are flattened by gravity and have a maximum height of about twice the capillary length. The capillary length also sets the size of capillary waves and appears in the analysis of menisci, sessile drops, and the Rayleigh–Taylor instability. In scaling terms, the capillary length is the length at which the Bond number equals unity.
