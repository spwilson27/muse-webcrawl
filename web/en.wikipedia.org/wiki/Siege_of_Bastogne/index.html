The siege of Bastogne was an engagement in December 1944 between American and German forces at the Belgian town of Bastogne, a critical road junction during the German Ardennes offensive, the Battle of the Bulge. Elements of the US 101st Airborne Division and supporting armour, artillery and engineers held the encircled town for a week against repeated attacks until relieved by Patton's Third Army, thwarting the German plan to race to the Meuse.

Background

Seven roads converged at Bastogne, making it essential to the German advance on Antwerp. When Germany launched Operation Watch on the Rhine on 16 December 1944, the 101st Airborne, resting near Reims after Operation Market Garden, was rushed by truck to Bastogne, arriving 18–19 December short of winter clothing, ammunition and equipment. The defence incorporated remnants of the 9th and 10th Armored Divisions (notably Combat Command B of the 10th Armored under Colonel William Roberts), the 705th Tank Destroyer Battalion and divisional artillery, under the acting command of Brigadier General Anthony McAuliffe while Major General Maxwell Taylor was in Washington.

The siege

German forces of the XLVII Panzer Corps surrounded Bastogne by 20–21 December, but attacks came piecemeal and the perimeter held. On 22 December German envoys demanded surrender; McAuliffe's written reply — "NUTS!" — became one of the most famous quotations of the war. Supply by parachute and glider, and C-47 drops on 23, 24 and 26 December, sustained the garrison, while artillery observers inflicted heavy losses on German attacks, notably at Champs and around Noville, where Team Desobry had earlier delayed the advance. On 26 December, tanks of the 4th Armored Division under Lieutenant Colonel Creighton Abrams broke through and opened a narrow corridor, evacuating the wounded.

Aftermath

Fighting around Bastogne continued as the corridor was widened in January 1945. The defence cost the 101st Airborne over 3,000 casualties but tied down several German divisions and denied the offensive its key road hub, contributing materially to its failure. Bastogne and the "NUTS!" reply remain central to American memory of the Battle of the Bulge; the town hosts the Bastogne War Museum and the Mardasson Memorial.
