Molecular self-assembly

Molecular self-assembly
FieldSupramolecular chemistry, nanotechnology
Driven byNon-covalent interactions
ExamplesMicelles, lipid bilayers, SAMs, DNA origami
RelatedSelf-organization, bottom-up nanotechnology

Molecular self-assembly is a process in which a disordered system of pre-existing components forms an organized structure or pattern as a consequence of specific, local interactions among the components themselves, without external direction. It is driven by non-covalent forces — hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, π–π stacking, hydrophobic effects, and metal coordination — and occurs throughout nature, from the formation of lipid bilayers and viral capsids to the crystallisation of minerals.

Principles

Self-assembly may be static, producing equilibrium structures such as micelles and self-assembled monolayers (SAMs), or dynamic (dissipative), requiring a continuous supply of energy, as in many biological structures. Molecular recognition between complementary components directs the assembly, and reversible bonding allows error correction.

Key design elements include amphiphiles that form micelles and vesicles, hydrogen-bonding motifs, and DNA nanotechnology, in which complementary strands assemble into tiles, origami shapes, and complex three-dimensional objects.

Applications

Self-assembly is a cornerstone of bottom-up nanotechnology, enabling fabrication of nanostructures too small for lithography. Applications under development include drug-delivery vehicles, sensors, molecular electronics, smart coatings, and tissue-engineering scaffolds.

In biology, self-assembly explains the formation of membranes, ribosomes, and cytoskeletal filaments, and the study of these systems informs efforts to understand the origin of life.
