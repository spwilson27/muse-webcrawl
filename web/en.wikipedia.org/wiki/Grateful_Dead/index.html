The Grateful Dead was an American rock band formed in 1965 in Palo Alto, California. Known for their eclectic style — fusing rock, folk, country, jazz, bluegrass and psychedelia — and for their lengthy live improvisations, the band became a central institution of the San Francisco counterculture and developed a devoted fan following known as "Deadheads".

History

Formation and early years (1965–1969)

The band grew out of a jug band, Mother McCree's Uptown Jug Champions, formed by Jerry Garcia, Bob Weir and Ron "Pigpen" McKernan. Renamed the Warlocks, with bassist Phil Lesh and drummer Bill Kreutzmann, they became the house band for Ken Kesey's Acid Tests in 1965–66, and took the name Grateful Dead — a phrase Garcia found in a dictionary, referring to a motif in folk tales in which a traveller pays for the burial of a stranger and is later rewarded by the dead man's spirit. Their self-titled debut album appeared in 1967, and Anthem of the Sun (1968) and Aoxomoxoa (1969) mixed studio experimentation with live recording.

Workingman's Dead era (1970–1975)

The acoustic, country-tinged albums Workingman's Dead and American Beauty (both 1970), built around the songwriting partnership of Garcia and lyricist Robert Hunter, produced enduring songs such as "Casey Jones", "Uncle John's Band", "Ripple" and "Truckin'". The triple live album Europe '72 documented their celebrated European tour. Drummer Mickey Hart had joined in 1967; Pigpen, whose blues singing anchored the early band, died in 1973, and Keith and Donna Jean Godchaux joined on keyboards and vocals. The band founded its own label, releasing Wake of the Flood (1973) and Blues for Allah (1975), and built the enormous "Wall of Sound" PA system in 1974.

Later years (1976–1995)

Signing to Arista, the band scored its only top-ten single with "Touch of Grey" (1987), from the album In the Dark, which brought a new generation of fans. Keyboardist Brent Mydland died in 1990 and was succeeded by Vince Welnick. Garcia's health declined through the 1990s, and he died on 9 August 1995; the remaining members announced the band's retirement that December.

Musical style and live culture

The Dead's concerts featured two sets, extended improvisational "jams", segueing songs, and a rotating repertoire that made no two shows alike. Uniquely, the band encouraged fans to record concerts — the practice of "taping" — and sold tickets directly by mail, anticipating modern fan-relations marketing. Deadheads followed the band on tour, creating a travelling community and marketplace.

Legacy

Ranked among the greatest live acts in rock history, the Grateful Dead were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1994, and their 8 May 1977 concert at Cornell University was selected for the National Recording Registry. Surviving members have continued in projects including Furthur and Dead & Company. In 2024 the band received Kennedy Center Honors.
