X-inactivation

X-inactivation (also called lyonization, after the English geneticist Mary Lyon) is a process by which one of the two copies of the X chromosome is inactivated in therian female mammals. The inactive copy is packaged into heterochromatin and forms a Barr body, a dense structure visible in the cell nucleus. Because the choice of which X chromosome is silenced is random in most placental mammals, females are genetic mosaics — different cells express either the maternal or paternal X chromosome — which explains, among other things, the patchwork coat colour of calico cats. X-inactivation achieves dosage compensation, equalising the expression of X-linked genes between XX females and XY males.

History

In 1949 Murray Barr and Ewart Bertram observed a dense chromatin body in the nuclei of female cat neurons. Mary Lyon proposed in 1961 that one X chromosome is randomly inactivated early in embryonic development, and that all descendant cells keep the same X inactive. The hypothesis was soon confirmed by studies of mosaic coat patterns and X-linked enzyme variants, and "lyonization" became a foundational example of epigenetic regulation.

Mechanism

Early in embryogenesis, cells count their X chromosomes and inactivate all but one. The long non-coding RNA Xist, transcribed from the X-inactivation centre (Xic) of the future inactive X, coats that chromosome in cis and recruits chromatin-modifying complexes that deposit repressive marks (such as H3K27me3 by Polycomb complexes) and DNA methylation, locking in the silent state. Its antisense transcript Tsix helps regulate the choice. In marsupials and in the extra-embryonic tissues of mice, inactivation is "imprinted": the paternal X is preferentially silenced.

Not all genes are silenced: roughly 15–25% of human X-linked genes "escape" inactivation to some degree, including the pseudoautosomal regions, which contributes to sex differences in some traits and diseases.

Clinical relevance

Because females are mosaics, carriers of X-linked recessive disorders such as haemophilia or Duchenne muscular dystrophy are usually protected, but skewed X-inactivation can produce symptoms in carriers. X-inactivation also influences the presentation of Rett syndrome and various X-linked intellectual disabilities.
