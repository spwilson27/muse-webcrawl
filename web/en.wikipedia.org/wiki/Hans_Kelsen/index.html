Hans Kelsen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hans Kelsen (11 October 1881 – 19 April 1973) was an Austrian jurist, legal philosopher and political philosopher, author of the Pure Theory of Law (Reine Rechtslehre, 1934; second edition 1960) — the most influential statement of legal positivism in the civil-law world. The Pure Theory analyses law as a hierarchical system of norms (Stufenbau) authorised by higher norms up to a presupposed basic norm (Grundnorm), rigorously excluding morality, politics and sociology from the science of law's validity.

Kelsen was also a constitution-maker: he drafted the Austrian Constitution of 1920 and designed its Constitutional Court — the first specialised constitutional court, the “Kelsenian model” later adopted across Europe (Germany's Federal Constitutional Court, Italy, Spain and the post-1989 courts). His debate with Carl Schmitt over “who shall be the guardian of the constitution” (1931) — the court, Kelsen argued, against Schmitt's president — remains the canonical exchange on judicial review. Dismissed under Nazism for his Jewish ancestry, he emigrated to Geneva and then the United States (Berkeley), contributing to the legal architecture of the UN era and to the theory of international law as a primitive but genuine legal order.
