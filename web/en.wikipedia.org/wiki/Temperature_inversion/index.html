Temperature inversion

A temperature inversion is a layer in the atmosphere in which air temperature increases with altitude, contrary to the normal tropospheric pattern in which temperature decreases with height. Because the warm air above acts as a stable "lid" that suppresses vertical mixing, inversions have major effects on weather, air pollution, and sound and radio propagation.

Types

  • Radiation inversions form near the ground on clear, calm nights when the surface loses heat by radiation and cools the air in contact with it; they typically break up after sunrise.
  • Subsidence inversions form aloft when air in a high-pressure system sinks and warms adiabatically, creating a warm layer above cooler surface air — a persistent feature of subtropical anticyclones and coastal cities such as Los Angeles.
  • Frontal inversions occur along weather fronts, where warm air overrides a wedge of cold air.
  • Marine inversions arise when warm air passes over a cold sea surface, common along western continental coasts.

Effects

Inversions suppress convection: clouds struggle to grow, turbulence is damped, and smoke, dust, and pollutants accumulate in the shallow layer beneath the inversion, producing smog episodes. Historically severe cases include the Great Smog of London in 1952 and the Donora smog of 1948, in which a stagnant inversion trapped industrial emissions with deadly results. Inversions also cause frost pockets in valleys, affecting agriculture, and create atmospheric ducts that bend radio waves and sound, producing anomalous long-distance reception and carrying distant noises. The tropopause itself is a permanent inversion that caps tropospheric weather.
