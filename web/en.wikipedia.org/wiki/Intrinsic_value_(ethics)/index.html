Intrinsic value (ethics)

In ethics, intrinsic value is the value that something has in itself, for its own sake, independently of its usefulness to anything else. It is contrasted with instrumental (or extrinsic) value, the value a thing has as a means to an end. Money, for example, is standardly treated as valuable only instrumentally, whereas happiness, knowledge, virtue, or human life itself are frequently held to be valuable intrinsically.

Concept

Philosophers distinguish between something being valued as an end and something having intrinsic value as a matter of its own nature: a person might value stamp collecting as an end without the activity thereby possessing objective intrinsic value. Discussions therefore separate the psychological question of what people prize for its own sake from the normative question of what is genuinely worth valuing. G. E. Moore's "isolation test" in Principia Ethica (1903) proposed judging a thing's intrinsic value by considering it in absolute isolation, stripped of all its effects and relations.

Historical views

Plato and Aristotle treated the good life, and in particular contemplation and virtue, as choiceworthy in themselves. Immanuel Kant argued that rational beings — humanity — possess dignity rather than price: persons must always be treated as ends in themselves and never merely as means. Utilitarians such as Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill located intrinsic value in pleasure or happiness alone, treating everything else as instrumental. Moore, by contrast, was a pluralist, holding that friendship and aesthetic appreciation were among the greatest intrinsic goods.

Monism and pluralism

Value monists hold that exactly one kind of thing is intrinsically good (for hedonists, pleasure; for some Kantians, rational nature). Pluralists hold that several irreducibly different things — knowledge, beauty, friendship, autonomy — are each intrinsically valuable, which raises problems of comparison when values conflict. Related debates concern whether intrinsic value comes in degrees, whether it can belong to states of affairs as well as objects, and whether bad things, such as suffering, possess intrinsic disvalue.

Applications

The concept is central to environmental ethics, where the claim that species, ecosystems, or wilderness possess intrinsic value — defended in the "deep ecology" movement and in Aldo Leopold's land ethic — is used to argue they merit protection regardless of human use. In animal ethics, the assertion that sentient animals have intrinsic value underpins arguments against treating them as mere resources. In bioethics, disputes over the moral status of embryos often turn on whether human life has intrinsic value from conception.
