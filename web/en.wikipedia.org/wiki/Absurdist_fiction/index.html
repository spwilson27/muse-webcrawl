Absurdist fiction is a genre of novels, plays, poems, and other works that focus on the experiences of characters in situations where they cannot find any inherent purpose in life. Through forms of nonsense, parody, and black humour, absurdist fiction explores the theme of absurdity in human existence.

Characteristics

The genre is characterised by non-sequiturs, circular reasoning, illogical or dreamlike plots, and characters who feel purposeless and bewildered in an indifferent world. Where traditional fiction resolves conflict, absurdist fiction often leaves it unresolved, mirroring the philosophical position associated with Albert Camus.

Examples

Franz Kafka's The Metamorphosis and The Trial, Camus's The Stranger, Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot, Joseph Heller's Catch-22, and the novels of Kurt Vonnegut are frequently cited as central examples. Later practitioners include Thomas Pynchon and Donald Barthelme.
