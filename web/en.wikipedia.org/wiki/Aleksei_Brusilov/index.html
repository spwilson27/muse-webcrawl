Aleksei Alekseyevich Brusilov (Russian: Алексей Алексеевич Брусилов; 19 August 1853 – 17 March 1926) was a Russian and later Soviet general most noted for the development of new offensive tactics used in the 1916 Brusilov Offensive, the most successful Russian campaign of the First World War and one of the most lethal offensives in world history.

Early career

Brusilov was born in Tiflis (now Tbilisi, Georgia) into a family with a long military tradition. Page Corps educated, he served in the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 in the Caucasus, rose through cavalry postings, ran the Cavalry Officers' School in St Petersburg, and by 1914 commanded the 8th Army.

First World War

In the opening campaigns Brusilov's 8th Army advanced deep into Galicia against Austria-Hungary. Appointed commander of the Southwestern Front in March 1916, he prepared the offensive that bears his name: launched on 4 June 1916, it attacked along a broad front with short, intense artillery preparation, shock troops and attacks prepared in full view of the enemy in sapped-forward trenches. The offensive shattered Austro-Hungarian armies, took hundreds of thousands of prisoners, forced Germany to divert forces from Verdun, and induced Romania to join the Allies — though at enormous Russian cost and without decisive strategic result.

Revolution and later life

After the February Revolution of 1917, Brusilov became Commander-in-Chief of the Russian Army under the Provisional Government, but was dismissed after the failure of the Kerensky Offensive. In 1920, during the Polish–Soviet War, he issued an appeal to former Tsarist officers to serve the Red Army and worked as an adviser and inspector of cavalry. He died in Moscow in 1926; his memoirs, A Soldier's Note-Book 1914–1918, remain an important source on the Eastern Front.
