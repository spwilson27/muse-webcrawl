Integrin

Integrins are transmembrane receptors that facilitate cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion. Upon ligand binding, integrins activate signal transduction pathways such as the FAK and Src kinase pathways, mediating bidirectional signalling — "inside-out" signals that regulate the receptor's affinity and "outside-in" signals triggered by ligand binding — that control cell survival, proliferation, migration and differentiation. Integrins are obligate heterodimers composed of one α and one β subunit; in mammals, combinations of 18 α and 8 β subunits form at least 24 distinct receptors.

An integrin heterodimer: large extracellular head, two transmembrane helices, short cytoplasmic tails.

Structure

Each integrin consists of a large extracellular domain formed by the α and β subunits (the ligand-binding "head" and supporting "legs"), single transmembrane helices, and short cytoplasmic tails that link — via adaptor proteins such as talin and kindlin — to the actin cytoskeleton. In the inactive state the receptor is bent and has low affinity; activation extends the molecule into an upright, high-affinity conformation. Cytoplasmic assemblies at integrin adhesions, including focal adhesions, integrate chemical and mechanical signals.

Classes and functions

Integrins are grouped by their β subunit or ligand specificity. β1 integrins (such as α5β1, the classic fibronectin receptor, and collagen-binding α1β1/α2β1) anchor most cells to the ECM. β2 integrins are restricted to leukocytes and mediate immune-cell trafficking and phagocytosis. αIIbβ3 on platelets binds fibrinogen and is essential for platelet aggregation. αv integrins bind RGD-containing ligands and activate latent TGF-β. Several integrins recognise the tripeptide sequence RGD in ligands such as fibronectin and vitronectin.

Clinical significance

Mutations cause human disease: defects in β2 integrins cause leukocyte adhesion deficiency, and defects in αIIbβ3 cause Glanzmann's thrombasthenia, a bleeding disorder. Integrins are important drug targets: approved agents include the antiplatelet drugs abciximab, eptifibatide and tirofiban (αIIbβ3 antagonists), the anti-α4 antibodies natalizumab (multiple sclerosis, Crohn's disease) and vedolizumab (gut-selective, for inflammatory bowel disease), and lifitegrast (αLβ2 antagonist, for dry-eye disease). Their role in angiogenesis and metastasis has made them long-standing targets in cancer research, though the αvβ3/αvβ5 inhibitor cilengitide failed in phase III trials for glioblastoma.

History

The name "integrin" was proposed by Richard Hynes in 1987 for receptors that integrate the extracellular matrix with the cytoskeleton, following the identification of the fibronectin receptor and the chicken integrin complex in the mid-1980s.
