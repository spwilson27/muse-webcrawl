Clinical microbiology

Clinical microbiology, also called medical microbiology in its diagnostic application, is the branch of laboratory medicine concerned with the diagnosis, management, and prevention of infectious diseases. Clinical microbiologists identify the bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites causing infection and determine which antimicrobial agents are likely to treat them.

[Image: A microbiologist examining bacterial colonies on agar plates]

Scope

The discipline is usually divided into bacteriology, virology, mycology, and parasitology sections. Work begins with specimen collection and transport, followed by microscopy (including Gram staining), culture on selective and differential media, biochemical and automated identification, serological tests, and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Modern methods

Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionisation time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) has largely replaced conventional biochemical identification of cultured organisms. Molecular methods — polymerase chain reaction (PCR), syndromic multiplex panels, and whole-genome sequencing — are used for fastidious or uncultivable pathogens, outbreak investigation, and detection of resistance genes.

Role in the hospital

Beyond diagnosis, clinical microbiology services advise on empirical and targeted antibiotic therapy, support antimicrobial stewardship programmes, and underpin infection prevention and control through surveillance of healthcare-associated infections and resistant organisms such as MRSA and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales.
