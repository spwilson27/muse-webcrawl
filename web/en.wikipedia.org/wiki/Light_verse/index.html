Light verse is poetry that attempts to be humorous, whimsical, or gently satirical, typically written in crisp, regular metres with inventive rhymes and a strong element of wordplay. Though its tone is light, the form often demands great technical skill, and its subjects range from pure nonsense to pointed social commentary.

History

Comic and occasional verse is ancient — the Greek Anthology is full of witty epigrams, and Martial perfected the form in Latin. In English, light verse flourished with the Restoration wits, John Gay's fables, and the parodies of the 19th century, when magazines such as Punch made it widely popular. Edward Lear's A Book of Nonsense (1846) popularised the limerick, Lewis Carroll mixed verse parody into Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, and W. S. Gilbert's Bab Ballads and his librettos for Gilbert and Sullivan set a standard for metrical ingenuity.

Twentieth century and beyond

The 20th century produced masters such as Hilaire Belloc (the Cautionary Tales), Dorothy Parker, Ogden Nash — famous for his deliberately lopsided lines and comic rhymes — and John Betjeman. Later exponents include Wendy Cope, whose parodies and triolets revived popular interest, and the comic poets of the New Yorker tradition. The clerihew, a whimsical pseudo-biographical quatrain invented by Edmund Clerihew Bentley, is a characteristic light-verse form. Critics sometimes dismiss light verse as minor, but poets such as W. H. Auden — himself a practitioner — argued that its technical demands and its freedom to address serious subjects in a comic register make it a genuine art.
