Phytophthora (from Greek phytón, "plant", and phthorá, "destruction"; "the plant-destroyer") is a genus of plant-damaging oomycetes (water moulds), whose member species cause enormous economic losses on crops worldwide, as well as environmental damage in natural ecosystems. Despite their fungus-like appearance, oomycetes are more closely related to brown algae and diatoms than to true fungi. The cell walls of Phytophthora are mainly cellulose.

Notable species

  • Phytophthora infestans — causes late blight of potato and tomato; responsible for the Great Famine of Ireland in the 1840s.
  • Phytophthora ramorum — causes sudden oak death in North America and sudden larch death in the United Kingdom.
  • Phytophthora cinnamomi — causes root rot and dieback in hundreds of host species, devastating natural ecosystems in Australia.
  • Phytophthora sojae — causes root and stem rot of soybean.
  • Phytophthora capsici — infects cucurbits, peppers and other vegetables.

Reproduction and spread

Phytophthora species reproduce both sexually (forming thick-walled survival spores called oospores) and asexually, producing sporangia that release motile zoospores which swim in water films toward host roots and leaves. They spread readily in wet soil and irrigation water, which is why waterlogging favours disease. Management combines resistant varieties, sanitation, drainage and fungicides such as metalaxyl and phosphonates.
