Shalimar Gardens, Lahore

The Shalimar Gardens (Punjabi: شالیمار باغ; Urdu: شالیمار باغات), sometimes written Shalamar Gardens, are a Mughal garden complex in Lahore, Pakistan. Commissioned by the emperor Shah Jahan in 1641–42, they are the most celebrated of the Mughal pleasure gardens and represent the zenith of Mughal horticulture. In 1981 the gardens were inscribed, together with the Lahore Fort, on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Layout

The gardens cover about 16 hectares (40 acres) and are enclosed by a high brick wall famous for its intricate fretwork. They descend on three terraces along a north–south axis, named in Urdu — the upper terrace Farah Baksh ("Bestower of Pleasure"), the middle Faiz Baksh ("Bestower of Goodness"), and the lower Hayat Baksh ("Bestower of Life"). Water is supplied from the Ravi River via the Shah Nahar canal, engineered partly by Ali Mardan Khan, and is distributed to some 410 fountains, great tanks, marble pavilions, cascades and water channels that cool the summer air.

Elegant pavilions of white marble and red sandstone, many once inlaid with pietra dura and fresco work, punctuate the terraces; among them are the Sawan Bhadun pavilions, Shah Jahan's Khwabgah (sleeping chamber), bath houses (hammams), and the Naqqar Khana gateway. The gardens were planted with fruit trees, roses, and plane (chinar) trees in the formal charbagh pattern of Persian-derived geometry.

History

The site belonged to the Arain Mian family, who were granted the hereditary title Mian by the emperor for their services and who in legend supplied the site and its waterworks. The gardens served as an imperial retreat during Shah Jahan's reign and afterwards. Under Sikh rule in the early 19th century, Maharaja Ranjit Singh removed marble and other materials for his own building projects, and some pavilions were damaged. During the British period and after the independence of Pakistan the gardens were restored as a public park and venue for official receptions and state visits.

Conservation

Threats to the site have included encroachments, air pollution from the adjacent Grand Trunk Road, and the lowering of the water table. The gardens were placed on UNESCO's List of World Heritage in Danger from 2000 to 2012 while conservation measures — including removal of encroachments and restoration of hydraulic systems — were carried out.
