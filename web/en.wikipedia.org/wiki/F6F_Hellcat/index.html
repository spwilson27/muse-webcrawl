The Grumman F6F Hellcat is an American carrier-based fighter aircraft of World War II. Designed to succeed the F4F Wildcat and to counter the Japanese Mitsubishi A6M Zero, the Hellcat was a rugged, powerful and forgiving aircraft that became the dominant US Navy fighter of the second half of the Pacific War. Hellcat pilots were credited with destroying 5,223 enemy aircraft — more than any other Allied naval aircraft — and 305 of them became aces.

Design and development

Grumman designed the F6F around the 2,000 hp Pratt & Whitney R-2800 Double Wasp engine, incorporating combat lessons from the Wildcat and, reportedly, examination of a captured Zero. The prototype flew on 26 June 1942, and production aircraft reached squadrons in early 1943 — an unusually rapid development. The Hellcat emphasised pilot protection (armour and self-sealing fuel tanks), heavy armament of six 12.7 mm machine guns, docile carrier-landing characteristics and the structural strength typical of Grumman's "ironworks" reputation.

Operational history

The Hellcat's combat debut came on 1 September 1943 over Marcus Island. It fought through the great carrier battles of 1944, including the Philippine Sea — the "Great Marianas Turkey Shoot" — and Leyte Gulf, achieving a kill-to-loss ratio of about 19:1 against the Zero specifically and roughly 13:1 overall. Night-fighter (F6F-3N/-5N) and photo-reconnaissance variants also served. In total, 12,275 Hellcats were built in just over two years. After the war the type was quickly replaced by jets in front-line US service but flew on with France in Indochina, and with Uruguay until 1960.
