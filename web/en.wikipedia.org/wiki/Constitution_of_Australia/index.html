Constitution of Australia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Constitution of Australia is the supreme law under which the Commonwealth of Australia operates, coming into force on 1 January 1901 after approval by referendums in the six colonies. It establishes a federal constitutional monarchy: legislative power in a bicameral parliament (House of Representatives and Senate, the latter with equal state representation), executive power formally in the governor-general exercised by ministers responsible to parliament, and judicial power in the High Court (whose federal and constitutional jurisprudence — from the Engineers' case of 1920 to the external-affairs expansion in the Tasmanian Dam case of 1983 — centralised the federation considerably).

The Constitution contains few express rights (freedom of religion in s.116, trial by jury, just terms for acquisitions), leaving rights to statute and common law, though the High Court has implied a freedom of political communication (1992/1997). Amendment requires a referendum with a double majority (national and in a majority of states): of 44 proposals put to 1999, only eight had passed — the 1967 referendum on Aboriginal affairs (over 90 per cent yes) the most famous — and the 2023 Voice proposal failed, continuing the record that change is deliberately hard. The document's silence on the prime minister, cabinet and parties (which rest on convention) makes it the classic case of a codified frame filled by Westminster practice.
