Jonathan Dickinson (1663–1722) was an English Quaker merchant whose journal of a 1696 shipwreck on the coast of Florida became one of the most widely read captivity narratives of colonial America.

The wreck of the Reformation

Dickinson had prospered as a merchant in Jamaica. In 1696 he sailed with his family and other passengers aboard the bark Reformation, bound for Philadelphia. On 23 September 1696 the vessel was wrecked in a storm near present-day Jupiter Island, Florida, in the territory of the Ais and Jaega peoples, close to the modern site called Jonathan Dickinson State Park in his memory.

Captivity and journey

The castaways were seized by the local Ais Indians, stripped and held captive, and feared for their lives; Dickinson's journal records hunger, violence and the death of several of the party, alongside acts of kindness. After weeks in captivity the survivors were released and undertook an arduous journey of some 230 miles up the coast — by foot and canoe — to the Spanish settlement of St. Augustine, and eventually reached Philadelphia in April 1697.

The Journal

Dickinson published his account in 1699 as God's Protecting Providence Being the Most Remarkable Account of the Indians or Cannibals of Florida. Framed as evidence of divine deliverance, it went through many editions in English, Dutch and German, and remains a primary source for the peoples and coast of early Florida. Dickinson later became a prosperous Philadelphia merchant and served as mayor of the city in 1717–1719.
