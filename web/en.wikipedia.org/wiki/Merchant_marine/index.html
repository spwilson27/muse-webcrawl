Merchant marine

A merchant marine (also called a merchant navy) is the fleet of merchant vessels registered in a country, together with the civilian officers and crew who operate them. Merchant ships carry cargo and passengers during peacetime; in wartime they may be called upon as auxiliaries of the navy, transporting troops and military supplies.

Fleets

As of the 2020s, the world merchant fleet numbers well over 100,000 vessels, with the largest registries by tonnage including Panama, Liberia and the Marshall Islands — so-called flags of convenience — while the largest beneficial-owner fleets belong to countries such as Greece, China and Japan. The United States Merchant Marine, administered under the Jones Act framework for domestic trade, plays a notable strategic role.

Personnel and training

Merchant mariners are licensed through national maritime administrations in accordance with the International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers (STCW). Careers progress from ratings and cadets through officer grades to master (captain). Maritime academies and training ships prepare cadets for service.

History

Merchant fleets date to antiquity, but the modern merchant marine took shape with steam propulsion in the 19th century. In both World Wars, merchant mariners suffered heavy losses to submarine attack while keeping Allied supply lines open; the Liberty ships of the Second World War are emblematic of that effort.
