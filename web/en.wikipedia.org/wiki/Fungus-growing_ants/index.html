Fungus-growing ants

Fungus-growing ants comprise the ant tribe Attini, a group of more than 200 described species distributed mainly in the New World tropics. All attine ants are obligate fungus farmers: colonies cultivate symbiotic fungi in underground gardens and feed on them. The tribe includes the leafcutter ants (Atta and Acromyrmex) as well as many small, inconspicuous species that farm fungi on insect frass, seeds, or decaying plant material.

Classification

Attini belongs to the subfamily Myrmicinae. Genera include Atta, Acromyrmex, Trachymyrmex, Sericomyrmex, Apterostigma, Cyphomyrmex, and Myrmicocrypta, among others. Molecular studies place the origin of the tribe in the Amazonian region of South America.

Systems of agriculture

Researchers distinguish several agricultural systems: lower agriculture, in which ants cultivate fungi that can also live independently of ants; coral-fungus agriculture in Apterostigma; yeast agriculture; and higher agriculture, including leafcutter agriculture, in which the cultivar is fully domesticated and produces specialized nutrient-rich structures (gongylidia) eaten by the ants.

Symbionts and parasites

The gardens are attacked by the parasitic fungus Escovopsis. Ants counter it through grooming and through antibiotic compounds produced by actinobacteria, especially Pseudonocardia, grown on their own cuticles. This multi-partner symbiosis is a model system for the study of coevolution and conflict within mutualisms.

Ecological role

Fungus-growing ants move large quantities of plant material and soil, aerating tropical soils and redistributing nutrients. Leafcutter colonies are among the dominant herbivores of the Neotropics and can defoliate crops, making some species agricultural pests.
