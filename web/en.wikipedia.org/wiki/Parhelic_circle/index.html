A parhelic circle is an atmospheric optical phenomenon: a white, horizontal band of light that passes through the Sun at the Sun's exact elevation and, in complete displays, encircles the entire sky at that altitude. It belongs to the family of halos produced by sunlight interacting with ice crystals high in cirrostratus clouds.

Formation

Parhelic circles are caused mainly by external reflection of sunlight from the vertical side faces of hexagonal ice crystals — both plate crystals floating with their faces horizontal and column crystals in suitable orientations. Because reflection preserves the light's colour rather than dispersing it, the circle appears white, in contrast to the rainbow-tinted refraction halos. The circle is always at the same elevation as the Sun, so when the Sun is low it runs low in the sky, and its completeness depends on crystal orientation and cloud coverage.

Associated phenomena

Bright spots where the parhelic circle crosses the 22° halo region are the sun dogs or parhelia, among the most familiar of all halo phenomena; other intensifications along the circle include the 120° parhelia and the anthelion opposite the Sun. Exceptional displays — such as the famous Stockholm "sun dog painting" event of 1535, among the earliest depicted — can show parhelic circles together with multiple halos, arcs and mock suns, and were historically read as omens.

Observation

Parhelic circles are less frequently noticed than sun dogs because they are usually faint; they are best seen when the Sun is low, using sunglasses and shielding the Sun itself. Photographers use fisheye lenses to capture complete circles, which span the full 360° of azimuth.
