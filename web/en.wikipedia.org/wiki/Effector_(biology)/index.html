Effector (biology)

An effector molecule binding to an enzyme's allosteric site

In biochemistry and physiology, an effector is something that carries out or modifies a biological response. The term is used in several related senses across molecular biology, immunology, neuroscience and plant pathology.

Effector molecules

An effector molecule is a small molecule or protein that binds to a regulatory protein and alters its activity, typically by binding to an allosteric site distinct from the active site. Positive (activator) and negative (inhibitor) effectors tune enzyme activity and gene expression; classic examples include lactose and the lac repressor system, and allosteric effectors of hemoglobin such as 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate.

Effector cells and organs

In immunology, effector cells are the activated cells that directly execute an immune response — cytotoxic T cells, plasma cells secreting antibodies, and activated macrophages — as opposed to memory or regulatory cells. In physiology more broadly, an effector is any organ or tissue, such as a muscle or gland, that responds to a nerve impulse in a reflex arc, complementing the receptor that detects the stimulus.

Pathogen effectors

In plant and animal pathology, effectors are proteins secreted by pathogens — bacteria, fungi, oomycetes and nematodes — into host cells to suppress immunity and manipulate host physiology. Plants in turn evolve resistance (R) proteins that recognize specific effectors, a dynamic captured by the gene-for-gene model and the zigzag model of plant immunity.
