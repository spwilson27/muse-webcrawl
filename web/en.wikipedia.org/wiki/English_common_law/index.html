English common law

English common law is the body of law developed in England from the Norman Conquest onwards, characterised by judge-made precedent and administered originally through the royal courts. It forms the basis of the legal systems of many countries, particularly former British colonies, and is often contrasted with civil law systems derived from Roman law.

Origins

After 1066 the Norman kings gradually replaced the patchwork of local customs with a law common to the whole kingdom. Royal justices travelling on circuit heard disputes and applied decisions that, over time, hardened into binding precedent under the doctrine of stare decisis. The treatise attributed to Ranulf de Glanvill (c. 1189) and the work of Henry de Bracton in the thirteenth century were early systematising texts.

Common law and equity

The rigidity of the common law writ system led petitioners to appeal to the Lord Chancellor, whose Court of Chancery developed the separate body of rules known as equity. Equity tempered the strictness of the common law with principles of fairness and remedies such as injunctions and specific performance. The two systems were fused administratively by the Judicature Acts of 1873–1875, though their distinct principles survive.

Global influence

Through colonisation, the English common law spread to North America, Australia, India and much of Africa and the Caribbean. Today roughly a third of the world's population lives in common law jurisdictions or mixed systems influenced by it. The United States, Canada (except Quebec), Australia and India all retain precedent-based common law traditions, though heavily supplemented by statute.

Modern position

In England and Wales, statute law enacted by Parliament is supreme, and much of the common law has been codified or reformed, but judge-made law remains important in fields such as contract, tort and judicial review. The highest court is the Supreme Court of the United Kingdom, established in 2009.
