Cloud point

In liquids, the cloud point is the temperature below which a transparent solution undergoes a liquid-liquid phase separation to become turbid, or the temperature at which dissolved solids in a hydrocarbon, such as wax in diesel fuel or petroleum, begin to form crystals and make the liquid appear cloudy. The term is used in both chemistry and the petroleum and surfactant industries, with slightly different specific meanings.

In petroleum

For crude oils, diesel, and other petroleum products, the cloud point is the temperature at which wax crystals first become visible as the sample is cooled under prescribed conditions. It is an important specification for winter-grade diesel: below the cloud point, wax can clog fuel filters and lines. Cloud point is generally a few degrees above the pour point, the temperature at which the oil ceases to flow. Refiners adjust cloud points by dewaxing and by blending additives that modify crystal growth.

In surfactant solutions

For non-ionic surfactants, the cloud point marks the temperature at which the solution separates into a surfactant-rich phase and a surfactant-poor phase on heating, becoming cloudy. It depends on the surfactant's hydrophilic-lipophilic balance, concentration, and the presence of salts. The phenomenon, caused by dehydration of the surfactant's polyoxyethylene chains at higher temperatures, is exploited in cloud point extraction, a separation technique for preconcentrating analytes and removing contaminants.

In polymers

Solutions of some polymers exhibit a cloud point at their lower critical solution temperature (LCST), becoming turbid when warmed above it. Poly(N-isopropylacrylamide), with an LCST near 32 °C, is a well-known example used in smart materials and drug delivery research.
