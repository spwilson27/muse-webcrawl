Peritoneal dialysis

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is a treatment for patients with severe chronic kidney disease that uses the patient's own peritoneum — the membrane lining the abdomen — as a semipermeable filter. Dialysis fluid is introduced through a permanent catheter into the peritoneal cavity, waste products and excess fluid pass from the blood across the peritoneum into the fluid, and the fluid is then drained and replaced.

Methods

In continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD), the patient manually exchanges bags of fluid several times a day while going about normal activities. In automated peritoneal dialysis (APD), a machine called a cycler performs the exchanges overnight while the patient sleeps. PD is a home-based therapy, offering many patients greater independence than in-centre haemodialysis.

Complications

The most important complication is peritonitis — infection of the peritoneal cavity — which usually presents with cloudy fluid and abdominal pain and requires antibiotics. Other problems include catheter infections, hernias, weight gain from glucose absorbed from the dialysate, and, rarely, encapsulating peritoneal sclerosis after many years of treatment. Over time the peritoneal membrane may lose its filtering efficiency.

Comparison with haemodialysis

Survival outcomes are broadly comparable between PD and haemodialysis for most patients, and PD better preserves residual kidney function in the first years of treatment. PD is used in a minority of dialysis patients in many Western countries but is the mainstay therapy in some health systems, such as Hong Kong's "PD first" policy.
