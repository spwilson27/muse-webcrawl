Battle of Antietam

The Battle of Antietam (also known in the South as the Battle of Sharpsburg) was fought on 17 September 1862, near Sharpsburg, Maryland, and Antietam Creek, during the American Civil War. It was the first field army-level engagement of the war fought on Union soil and remains the bloodiest single day in American military history, with combined casualties of about 22,700 killed, wounded and missing.

Fighting around the Dunker Church, morning of 17 September 1862.

Background

Confederate General Robert E. Lee's Army of Northern Virginia had crossed the Potomac into Maryland, hoping to win a victory on Northern soil that might secure foreign recognition and relieve pressure on Virginia. Union Major General George B. McClellan's Army of the Potomac, greatly reinforced by the accidental discovery of Lee's lost Special Order 191, moved to intercept. Lee chose to make a stand at Sharpsburg with his back to the Potomac River.

The battle

The battle unfolded in three phases. In the morning, Union corps attacked Lee's left through the Cornfield and the West Woods around the Dunker Church. At midday, fighting centred on the Sunken Road — "Bloody Lane" — where Union divisions pierced the Confederate centre but failed to exploit the breakthrough. In the afternoon, Major General Ambrose Burnside's corps finally forced a crossing of Antietam Creek at what became known as Burnside Bridge and threatened the Confederate right, until A. P. Hill's division arrived from Harpers Ferry and drove them back.

Aftermath

Lee held his ground on 18 September, then withdrew across the Potomac on the night of 18–19 September, ending the Maryland Campaign. The battle was tactically inconclusive, but it was a strategic Union success: it halted Lee's invasion and gave President Abraham Lincoln the military platform he needed to issue the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on 22 September 1862.
