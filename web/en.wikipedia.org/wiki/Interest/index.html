Interest


Growth of a principal sum under compound interest

In finance and economics, interest is payment from a borrower or deposit-taking financial institution to a lender or depositor of an amount above repayment of the principal sum (that is, the amount borrowed), at a particular rate. It is distinct from a fee which the borrower may pay to the lender or some third party. It is also distinct from dividend, which is paid by a company to its shareholders from its profit.

Interest is typically expressed as an annual percentage of the principal, the interest rate. Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal, while compound interest is calculated on the principal plus accumulated interest, so that the amount owed grows exponentially over time.

History

The charging of interest on loans is ancient. Records from Mesopotamia show interest-bearing loans of grain and silver thousands of years ago, and the Code of Hammurabi regulated maximum rates. In the medieval period, the Catholic Church condemned usury — lending at interest — as a sin, and Islamic law continues to prohibit riba (interest), which has given rise to Islamic finance structures that avoid explicit interest. From the Renaissance onwards, commercial practice and legal reform normalised interest in Europe, and the development of banking made interest rates a central variable of modern economies.

Types of interest

  • Simple interest — computed on principal only: I = P × r × t.
  • Compound interest — computed on principal plus accrued interest; the amount grows as A = P(1 + r)t with periodic compounding.
  • Fixed vs. variable (floating) rates — rates that stay constant, or change with a benchmark such as a central bank policy rate.
  • Nominal vs. real interest — the real rate adjusts the nominal rate for inflation, as described by the Fisher equation.

Economic theory

Economists explain interest in terms of the time value of money: people prefer consumption now to later, so lenders require compensation for deferring consumption, for the risk of default, and for expected inflation. Central banks influence short-term interest rates through monetary policy, and market rates across maturities form the yield curve.
