Scapegoat

A scapegoat is a person or group blamed for the wrongdoings, mistakes, or faults of others, often as a convenient target for negative feelings, political expediency, or social tension. The term originates in the ritual described in the Book of Leviticus, in which the sins of Israel were symbolically laid on a goat that was driven into the wilderness on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement.

"The Scapegoat" — the goat bearing the sins of the people, sent into the desert.

Biblical origin

Leviticus 16 prescribes that on the Day of Atonement the high priest Aaron should take two goats: one sacrificed as a sin offering, and the other — the "goat for Azazel" — presented alive, burdened by confession with all the iniquities of Israel, and sent away into the wilderness. William Tyndale's English Bible translation (1530) rendered this the "scapegoat" (the goat that escapes), giving the language the word.

Scapegoating as a social phenomenon

In social psychology, scapegoating is explained by frustration–aggression theory and by theories of intergroup conflict: frustrated or threatened groups displace aggression onto relatively powerless outsiders, especially minorities, immigrants or dissenters. History offers grim examples, from medieval accusations that Jews caused the Black Death to modern political campaigns against minority groups. The philosopher René Girard made the "scapegoat mechanism" central to his theory of culture, arguing that communities restore unity by converging on a victim whose expulsion or death is then sacralised in myth and ritual.
