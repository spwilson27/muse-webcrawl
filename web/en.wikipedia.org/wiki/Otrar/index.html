Otrar was an ancient oasis city on the middle Syr Darya river in present-day southern Kazakhstan, a major trading centre on the Silk Road between Central Asia, Persia and China. The nearby settlement of Farab was the home town of the philosopher al-Farabi. Today the site survives as the great ruined mound of Otrar Tobe, one of the most important archaeological sites in Kazakhstan.

History

Otrar grew as the centre of a fertile irrigated oasis where routes from the Eurasian steppe met the caravan roads of Transoxiana. It passed through the hands of Turkic khanates, the Karakhanids and the Khwarazmian Empire, minting its own coins and supporting famous libraries and bathhouses.

The Otrar incident and Mongol siege

In 1218 the city's governor, Inalchuq, seized a Mongol-sponsored Muslim caravan of some 450 merchants, accusing them of espionage, and—with the approval of Shah Muhammad II of Khwarazm—had them executed. When Genghis Khan's envoys demanding redress were killed or humiliated, the Mongols invaded Khwarazmia. Otrar resisted a siege of about five months in 1219–1220 before being stormed and destroyed; Inalchuq was captured and executed, and much of the population was killed or enslaved.

Later history and archaeology

The city was partially rebuilt and continued to be inhabited under the Timurids and the Kazakh Khanate, but declined as irrigation systems failed, and was finally abandoned by the early 19th century. Excavations by Soviet and Kazakh archaeologists have uncovered citadel walls, mosques, bathhouses and craft quarters, revealing the layout of a major medieval Central Asian city.
