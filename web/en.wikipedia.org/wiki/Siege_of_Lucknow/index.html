Siege of Lucknow

The Siege of Lucknow was the prolonged defence of the Residency compound at Lucknow, the capital of Oudh (Awadh), during the Indian Rebellion of 1857. From 2 July to 16 November 1857, Indian sepoy mutineers and rebel forces besieged a garrison of British troops, civilians, and loyal sepoys commanded first by Sir Henry Lawrence, then by John Inglis. The siege became one of the most celebrated episodes of the rebellion in British memory, symbolising endurance, and the ruined Residency was preserved as a memorial.

Background

Oudh had been annexed by the East India Company in 1856, a deeply resented act. When the rebellion broke out in May 1857, the Chief Commissioner, Sir Henry Lawrence, prepared the Residency grounds as a refuge. After the British defeat at the Battle of Chinhat on 30 June, the surviving force withdrew into the Residency, and the siege proper began on 2 July. Lawrence was mortally wounded by a shell on 2 July and died on 4 July, leaving command to Brigadier John Inglis. The garrison numbered around 1,700 fighting men plus some 1,300 women, children, and non-combatants, defended by a hastily built perimeter of earthworks and loopholed buildings.

The siege and reliefs

The besiegers, vastly more numerous, attacked repeatedly and drove mines under the defences, countered by British counter-mining under Captain John Fulton of the Engineers. Disease and shellfire steadily reduced the garrison. A first relief column under Henry Havelock and James Outram fought its way into the Residency on 25 September 1857 but was too weak to evacuate the civilians, so it reinforced the defence instead — the "first relief." A larger force under the new Commander-in-Chief, Sir Colin Campbell, broke through in mid-November (the second relief), storming Sikandar Bagh on 16 November, and evacuated the women, children, wounded, and garrison by 22 November, abandoning the Residency itself. British forces under Campbell finally recaptured the whole city in March 1858 after heavy fighting.

Aftermath and memory

Of the original garrison, hundreds died, including most of the senior officers. Twenty-four Victoria Crosses were awarded for actions during the defence and reliefs — the action at Sikandar Bagh remains one of the most decorated single engagements in British military history. The Residency ruins, still bearing shell and bullet marks, were kept unrestored as a monument; its cemetery contains Lawrence's grave, with his self-composed epitaph: "Here lies the son of a soldier who tried to do his duty."
