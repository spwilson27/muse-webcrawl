Michel Serres

Born: 1 September 1930, Agen, France

Died: 1 June 2019 (aged 88), Paris, France

Occupation: Philosopher, historian of science

Institutions: Sorbonne; Stanford University; Académie française (seat 18, from 1990)

Michel Serres (French: [miʃɛl sɛʁ]; 1 September 1930 – 1 June 2019) was a French philosopher, theorist, and writer, and a member of the Académie française. Trained in mathematics, philosophy, and the classics, and beginning his career as a naval officer, Serres wrote widely read and stylistically distinctive works crossing the boundary between the sciences and the humanities.

Career

Serres entered the École navale in 1949 and served as a naval officer before turning to philosophy, completing his doctorate in 1968 with a thesis on Leibniz. He taught at Clermont-Ferrand and Vincennes before becoming professor of the history of science at the Sorbonne (Paris I), and he held a long-term visiting professorship at Stanford University. He was elected to the Académie française in 1990.

Work

Serres's early five-volume Hermès series (1969–1980) explored communication, translation, and interference between disciplines, taking the messenger god Hermes as a figure for the circulation of knowledge. The Parasite (1980) developed a general theory of relations based on the parasitic third party that interrupts and redirects exchanges — a concept extended into social theory. The Natural Contract (1990) argued that humanity must extend its social contract to the Earth itself, an influential text in environmental philosophy. His trilogy Les Origines de la géométrie and works on Lucretius, statuary, angels, and the body reflect his interest in the deep history of science and myth.

In his later years Serres reached a mass audience with short meditative books, above all Petite Poucette ("Thumbelina", 2012), a bestseller celebrating the generation formed by mobile phones and the Internet and calling for a reinvention of education and knowledge. He died in Paris on 1 June 2019 at the age of 88.
