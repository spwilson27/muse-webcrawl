Chincha Islands

The Chincha Islands (Spanish: Islas Chincha) are a group of three small islands 21 km (13 mi) offshore from Pisco, Peru, in the Pacific Ocean. They are known as Isla Chincha Norte, Isla Chincha Centro and Isla Chincha Sur. The islands were once famous for their vast deposits of guano — seabird droppings used as fertiliser — which in the mid-19th century generated enormous revenues for Peru and became the object of international conflict, including the Chincha Islands War (1864–1866) between Spain and its former colonies Peru and Chile.

Guano era

The dry climate of Peru's coast allowed guano from vast colonies of guanay cormorants, boobies and pelicans to accumulate in deposits tens of metres thick. Exported from the 1840s, Chincha guano was prized in Europe and North America as a fertiliser and a source of nitrates for explosives. At its peak, guano accounted for the majority of Peruvian state revenue. The deposits were worked largely by Chinese indentured labourers under brutal conditions. Spain's seizure of the islands in April 1864, during a dispute over an incident at a Peruvian hacienda, precipitated the Chincha Islands War, in which Peruvian and Chilean forces resisted Spanish naval attacks including the bombardment of Valparaíso and the Battle of Callao.

Later history

By the early 20th century the Chincha deposits were largely exhausted, and the sustainable harvesting of guano from Peru's other islands passed under state management. Today the islands are protected as part of Peru's national reserve system for guano islands and capes, with restricted access to preserve seabird colonies, and guano is still collected periodically by hand on a rotational basis.
