Buddhism in Sri Lanka is predominantly of the Theravāda tradition and has been practised on the island since the 3rd century BCE, making Sri Lanka one of the oldest continuously Buddhist countries in the world. About 70 percent of Sri Lankans — chiefly among the Sinhalese majority — identify as Buddhist. The Sri Lankan Saṅgha (monastic order) preserved the Pali Canon in writing and played a central role in the island's history, culture, and politics; the constitution of Sri Lanka gives Buddhism "the foremost place" while assuring the rights of other religions.

Introduction and early history

According to the island chronicles (Dīpavaṁsa and Mahāvaṁsa), Buddhism was brought to Sri Lanka around 250 BCE by Mahinda, son of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka, who converted King Devānampiya Tissa at Anuradhapura. Mahinda's sister Saṅghamittā brought a sapling of the Bodhi tree, planted at Anuradhapura as the Sri Maha Bodhi — the oldest historically documented tree in the world — and founded the order of nuns. The Mahāvihāra monastery became the seat of Theravāda orthodoxy. In the 1st century BCE, during a period of invasion and famine, the Pali Canon was committed to writing at Aluvihāra, a decisive event in Buddhist textual history.

Medieval period

Rival fraternities — the Abhayagiri and Jetavana vihāras — flourished alongside the Mahāvihāra and admitted Mahāyāna influences. King Parākramabāhu I unified the Saṅgha under Mahāvihāra leadership in the 12th century. The relic of the Buddha's Tooth, brought to the island in the 4th century, became the palladium of Sinhalese kingship; its temple in Kandy, the Sri Dalada Maligawa, remains Sri Lanka's most sacred Buddhist site.

Colonial decline and revival

Under Portuguese, Dutch, and British rule (1505–1948), Christian missions and colonial policy eroded Buddhism; the Kandyan kingdom's fall in 1815 and lapses in higher ordination required missions to Burma and Thailand to restore the ordination lineage, giving rise to the Amarapura (1803) and Rāmañña (1864) fraternities alongside the older Siam Nikāya (1753). A 19th-century Buddhist revival — led by figures such as Migettuwatte Gunananda Thera, whose 1873 Panadura debate with Christian missionaries attracted international attention, and supported by the Theosophist Henry Steel Olcott — founded Buddhist schools and the flag now used worldwide. Anagārika Dharmapāla carried the revivalist message abroad and founded the Mahā Bodhi Society.

Modern Sri Lanka

Since independence in 1948 Buddhism has been intertwined with Sinhalese nationalism and state politics, a relationship that has also been a factor in ethnic tensions with the Tamil minority. At the same time Sri Lanka exports Theravāda to the West through meditation teachers and monasteries, and its forest tradition and scholarship retain international standing. Annual festivals such as Vesak and the Esala Perahera in Kandy are major public events.
