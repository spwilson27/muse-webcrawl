World history studies the past on a planetary scale.

World history, also called global history, is a field of historical study that examines history from a global perspective, looking beyond individual nations and regions to trace patterns, connections and comparisons across the whole of the human past. It asks how large-scale processes — migration, trade, empire, disease, technology, religion and environmental change — have linked societies together, and it treats humanity's shared history, from the origins of Homo sapiens to the present, as its subject.

Development of the field

Universal histories are ancient — from Herodotus and Sima Qian to Ibn Khaldun and Enlightenment philosophes — but world history as a distinct academic field emerged in the mid-twentieth century, particularly in the United States, where it was promoted as an alternative to the traditional Western civilisation survey. A landmark was William H. McNeill's The Rise of the West (1963), which argued that contact between civilisations was the principal driver of historical change. The World History Association was founded in 1982 and the Journal of World History in 1990 under Jerry H. Bentley. Leften Stavrianos's The Global Rift and later works by historians such as Kenneth Pomeranz, whose The Great Divergence (2000) compared industrialisation in Europe and China, further shaped the field. The College Board's Advanced Placement World History course, launched in 2001, brought the approach to millions of school students.

Themes and methods

World historians organise the past around transnational themes rather than national narratives. Recurring subjects include the spread of agriculture, the growth of Afro-Eurasian trade networks such as the Silk Road and the Indian Ocean trade, the Mongol Empire's integration of Eurasia, the Columbian Exchange linking the Old and New Worlds after 1492, the Atlantic slave trade, industrialisation, imperialism, world wars, decolonisation and modern globalisation. Methodologically the field draws on comparison, connection-tracing and increasingly on environmental and scientific evidence; its outer reaches overlap with Big History, popularised by David Christian, which extends the timeframe to the Big Bang.

Debates

Scholars continue to debate whether world history should emphasise the unity of global processes or the diversity of local experiences, and whether older narratives were Eurocentric in privileging Europe's trajectory. The related "global history" movement, strong in Europe, often focuses on globalisation and global integration since the eighteenth century, while American world history covers the full span of the human past. Despite these differences the field has become a standard framework for teaching and researching history at the largest scales.
