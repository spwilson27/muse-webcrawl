Tzadik

Tzadik (Hebrew: צַדִּיק, ṣaddīq, "righteous [one]"; also transliterated tzaddik; plural tzadikim) is a title in Judaism given to people considered righteous, such as biblical figures and later spiritual masters. The word is derived from the Hebrew root ṣ-d-q, meaning justice or righteousness. In Hasidic Judaism the term took on a special meaning for the hereditary leader (rebbe) of a Hasidic dynasty.

In the Bible and rabbinic thought

The Hebrew Bible applies tzadik to figures such as Noah, "a righteous man in his generation". Rabbinic tradition developed the idea of the tzadik nistar—the hidden righteous—and the legend of the Lamed Vav Tzadikim, thirty-six hidden saints for whose sake the world is preserved, based on passages such as Proverbs 10:25.

In Hasidism

From the 18th century, Hasidic communities centred on a tzadik, often called the Rebbe or Admor, regarded as an intermediary between the community and God. Followers seek his blessing and counsel, gather at his court, and celebrate at his tish (table). After a tzadik's death, his tomb (ohel) may become a site of pilgrimage. Dynasties founded by famous tzadikim include those of the Baal Shem Tov's disciples; notable tzadikim include Rabbi Nachman of Breslov, founder of the Breslov movement, and the Lubavitcher Rebbes of Chabad.

Grades of righteousness

Classical texts distinguish the complete tzadik (tzadik gamur), whose good inclination wholly prevails, from intermediate figures; the Tanya, a foundational Chabad text, develops these categories at length.
