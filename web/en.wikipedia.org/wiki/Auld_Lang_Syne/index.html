"Auld Lang Syne" (Scots for "old long since", or roughly "times long past") is a Scottish folk song whose words were adapted by the poet Robert Burns in 1788 from an older ballad. It is traditionally sung at the stroke of midnight on New Year's Eve (Hogmanay in Scotland) and at farewells, funerals (in some countries), graduations and other partings, and is one of the most widely recognised songs in the English-speaking world.

Singing "Auld Lang Syne" at midnight on Hogmanay.

Text and melody

Burns sent the poem to the Scots Musical Museum in 1788 claiming he had taken it down "from an old man". Its opening asks:

Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
and never brought to mind?
Should auld acquaintance be forgot,
and auld lang syne?

The chorus — "For auld lang syne, my dear, for auld lang syne, we'll tak' a cup o' kindness yet, for auld lang syne" — is the best-known part. The melody now standard (a pentatonic tune printed by George Thomson) differs from the one Burns originally paired with the words.

Use

In Scotland the song closes Hogmanay celebrations, with singers joining hands in a circle and crossing arms at the final verse. The custom spread worldwide through Scottish emigration and was popularised in North America by Guy Lombardo and His Royal Canadians, whose New Year's Eve broadcasts from 1929 made it the anthem of the American New Year. Variants are sung — often to the same tune — in many languages, from Japan (where "Hotaru no Hikari" closes school ceremonies and the NHK New Year broadcast) to South Korea and China.
