Yongle Emperor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Forbidden City in Beijing as built under the Yongle Emperor]

The Yongle Emperor (1360–1424), personal name Zhu Di, was the third emperor of China's Ming dynasty (r. 1402–1424), who seized the throne from his nephew in a civil war and remade the empire in his image. He moved the capital to Beijing, building the Forbidden City; launched Zheng He's treasure voyages and campaigns into Vietnam and Mongolia; compiled the vast Yongle Encyclopedia; and restored the Grand Canal. His reign is regarded as a second founding of the Ming — energetic, expansionist and authoritarian.

Usurpation and consolidation

Zhu Di, fourth son of the founding Hongwu Emperor and Prince of Yan based at Beiping (Beijing), rebelled in 1399 when the young Jianwen Emperor moved against the frontier princes; after three years of war he took Nanjing in 1402, where Jianwen vanished in the palace fire (legends of his survival as a monk persisted for centuries). Yongle executed resisters and their families — the scholar Fang Xiaoru's kin and associates famously — while building a loyal administration and promoting the eunuch secretariat that would plague later Ming government.

Achievements

The emperor transferred the capital to his northern base (renamed Beijing, 'northern capital') in 1420–21 to face the Mongols, personally leading five steppe campaigns and dying on the last, at Yumuchuan in 1424. He annexed Vietnam (1407–1427 occupation, ultimately unsustainable), received embassies from across Asia, sponsored the Yongle Encyclopedia (1403–1408: 11,095 volumes, the largest encyclopedia of the pre-modern world), and patronised the porcelain and architecture that define Ming splendour.
