Coat of plates

A coat of plates, showing the textile covering to which metal plates were riveted

Type: Body armour

Period: c. 1250–1450

Materials: Iron or steel plates, fabric or leather covering, rivets

A coat of plates is a form of torso armour of the medieval period, consisting of metal plates riveted inside a covering of fabric or leather and worn over a padded garment and often a mail hauberk. It emerged in Europe in the mid-13th century as armourers sought better protection against lances, arrows and bladed weapons than mail alone provided, and it remained in use, especially among infantry and less wealthy men-at-arms, into the 15th century. The coat of plates is an ancestor of the later brigandine and, indirectly, of the solid plate cuirass.

Construction

A coat of plates typically consisted of a poncho-like or vest-like textile foundation — linen, canvas or leather — to the inside of which a series of overlapping rectangular or shaped iron plates were attached with rivets whose heads showed on the outside as neat rows of studs. The plates overlapped downwards to deflect blows, and the garment fastened at the back or side with straps and buckles. Because the plates were concealed, surviving descriptions and art usually show only the fabric exterior and rivet heads.

Historical examples

The most famous finds come from the mass graves of the Battle of Visby (1361) on the Swedish island of Gotland, excavated in the early 20th century, which yielded more than twenty coats of plates in varying states of preservation — an extraordinary survival of organic-covered armour. Archaeological evidence and effigies from across Europe, from England to Italy and Germany, show the type was widespread. German examples are often called a Plattenrock. As plate armour for the torso developed into single-piece breastplates in the later 14th century, the coat of plates evolved into the finer, smaller-plated brigandine favoured by both soldiers and wealthy civilians.

Relation to other armour

The coat of plates is distinguished from the brigandine by its larger plates, and from the jack of plates — a later, usually cheaper garment made with plates often recycled from old armour sewn into a canvas jacket. It differs from lamellar and scale armour in that its plates are riveted to a foundation garment rather than laced directly to one another or sewn overlapping on the outside.
