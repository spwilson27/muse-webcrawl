Tauromachy

Tauromachy (from Greek tauromachia, "bull-fighting") is a term that encompasses the various forms of bull-related spectacle, most notably bullfighting as practised in Spain, Portugal, southern France, and parts of Latin America. It also extends to other bull sports such as Portuguese forcados, bloodless bullfighting, bull-leaping, and the ancient bull games of Crete and Thessaly.

History

The origins of tauromachy are often traced to prehistoric bull worship and sacrifice in the Mediterranean world. In ancient Crete, acrobats performed bull-leaping (taurokathapsia), grasping the horns of a charging bull and vaulting over its back. Roman venationes featured combat between men and wild animals, including bulls. In medieval Iberia, knights fought bulls on horseback as a form of military training and display of nobility.

Modern Spanish bullfighting on foot developed in the 18th century, with Francisco Romero of Ronda credited with introducing the use of the muleta (red cape) and sword. The three-act structure of the corrida — the tercios of lances, banderillas, and death — was codified during this period.

Forms

  • Spanish bullfighting (corrida de toros): the bull is killed in the ring by the matador.
  • Portuguese bullfighting: the bull is fought by a cavaleiro on horseback and seized by forcados; the bull is not killed in the ring.
  • French forms: including the course landaise and course camarguaise, in which the animals are not harmed.
  • Recortes: acrobatic evasion and leaping over bulls, popular in Spain.
  • Bloodless bullfighting: practised in parts of California and elsewhere, using Velcro-tipped banderillas.

Cultural significance and controversy

Tauromachy has inspired major works of art and literature, including Goya's etchings La Tauromaquia (1816), Bizet's opera Carmen, Picasso's bull imagery, and Hemingway's Death in the Afternoon (1932). Supporters defend bullfighting as an art form and cultural heritage. Opponents criticise it as cruelty to animals, and several jurisdictions have restricted or banned it; the Spanish region of Catalonia banned bullfighting in 2010, though the ban was later overturned by Spain's Constitutional Court.
