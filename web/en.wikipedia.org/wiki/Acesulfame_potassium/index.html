Acesulfame potassium, also known as acesulfame K or Ace K, is a calorie-free artificial sweetener discovered in 1967 by the German chemist Karl Clauss at Hoechst AG, who noticed a sweet taste after licking his finger while working with related compounds. It is about 200 times sweeter than sucrose, is not metabolised by the body, and is excreted unchanged by the kidneys, contributing no calories or glycaemic effect. Its E number is E950.

Properties and uses

Acesulfame potassium is a white crystalline powder, highly soluble in water and stable under heat and in mildly acidic or basic conditions — unlike aspartame it survives baking and long storage in acidic soft drinks, making it suitable for baked goods, beverages and shelf-stable products. On its own it can have a slight bitter aftertaste, so it is very commonly blended with other sweeteners such as aspartame, sucralose or sucralose alternatives like steviol glycosides; the blends taste more sugar-like and the sweeteners mutually mask off-notes and show synergy (the blend is sweeter than the sum of parts). Each sweetener in the blend is also used at lower absolute levels. Acesulfame K is used in thousands of products worldwide: diet soft drinks (often with aspartame), chewing gum, yoghurts, tabletop sweeteners (Sunett, Sweet One), pharmaceuticals and oral-hygiene products — it does not promote tooth decay.

Safety and regulation

Acesulfame potassium was approved in the United Kingdom and Germany in the early 1980s, in the United States by the FDA for dry products in 1988 and for beverages in 1998 (general-purpose approval followed in 2003), and across the European Union under the Sweeteners Directive. The acceptable daily intake is set at 9 mg/kg body weight by EFSA and 15 mg/kg by the FDA/JECFA. Although periodic controversies have alleged carcinogenicity based on early studies criticised for methodological weaknesses, repeated reviews by the FDA, EFSA and other regulators have concluded that acesulfame K is safe at permitted levels; EFSA's comprehensive re-evaluation of food additives has kept it under review as part of its programme on sweeteners. Because it passes through wastewater treatment largely intact, acesulfame is also used by environmental scientists as a persistent tracer of domestic wastewater in rivers and groundwater.
