Prose

Prose is the ordinary form of written language, distinct from poetry in that it follows the natural flow of speech, grammatical structures, and paragraphs rather than the metrical and lineated patterns of verse. The word comes from the Latin expression prosa oratio, "straightforward speech." Prose is the vehicle of the novel, the essay, history, biography, journalism, and most exposition and argument of every kind.

Characteristics

Prose is organised into sentences and paragraphs rather than lines and stanzas. It has no obligatory metre or rhyme scheme, though prose may still employ rhythm, repetition, imagery, and every other figure of speech — the boundary with poetry is one of form rather than artistry. Forms such as the prose poem deliberately blur the distinction, using poetic devices within unmetred, unlineated paragraphs. Molière's Monsieur Jourdain famously discovers, to his delight, that he has been speaking prose all his life without knowing it.

History

Although early literature was usually composed in verse — for mnemonic reasons in oral cultures — prose developed early for inscriptions, laws, annals, and ritual texts. Greek prose matured in the fifth and fourth centuries BC with the historians Herodotus and Thucydides and the orators and philosophers; Latin prose reached classical standards in Cicero and Caesar. In many literatures — medieval romances, the Icelandic sagas, and the Chinese classical tradition — prose narrative developed distinctive styles. The rise of printing and an expanding reading public made prose dominant, and the novel became its flagship form from the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries onward.

Types of prose

  • Fiction: novels, novellas, short stories, fables.
  • Non-fiction: essays, histories, biographies, memoirs, criticism, science writing.
  • Heroic and saga prose: narrative prose of legendary material.
  • Poetic prose / prose poetry: prose written with heightened poetic effect.
