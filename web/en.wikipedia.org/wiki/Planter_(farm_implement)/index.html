Planter (farm implement)

Row crop planter
TypeAgricultural machinery
FunctionPrecise sowing of large seeds in rows
Typical cropsMaize (corn), soybean, cotton, sunflower, peanut
Unit widthCommonly 6–48 rows

A planter is a farm implement, usually towed behind a tractor, that sows (plants) seeds in rows throughout a field. It is distinguished from a seed drill (grain drill) by its greater precision: its metering mechanisms place individual seeds at exact spacing and depth in each row, which suits large seeds such as maize, beans, cotton and sunflowers whose final plant spacing strongly affects yield.

Design

A planter consists of a toolbar carrying multiple independent row units. Each unit has a seed hopper, a metering device (mechanical finger pickups, or modern vacuum or positive-air-pressure meters that singulate seeds), a furrow-opening disc or runner, and closing wheels that press soil over the seed. Many units also carry fertiliser hoppers and applicators, residue managers, and down-pressure springs or pneumatic systems to keep depth constant. GPS guidance, electric meter drives and per-row sensors in contemporary "precision planters" adjust seeding rates on the move and map emergence.

History

Hand seed drills were described in China from the Han dynasty and in Europe by the 16th century; Jethro Tull's horse-drawn drill (1701) is the famous English example. Horse-drawn corn planters spread in the 19th-century United States, and check-row planters that deposited seeds in a grid enabled cross-cultivation. Twentieth-century mechanisation brought plate meters, then vacuum metering (1970s onward) and today's high-speed precision planters capable of accurate spacing at 15 km/h or more.

Related implements

Related machines include the seed drill for small grains, the air seeder for broadacre sowing, transplanters for seedlings, and specialised potato planters. The term "planter" may also denote a person — historically, in the American South and Caribbean, the owner of a plantation.
