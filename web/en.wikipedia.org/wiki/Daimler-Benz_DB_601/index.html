The Daimler-Benz DB 601 was a German aircraft engine built during World War II. It was a liquid-cooled inverted V-12 engine, developed from the earlier DB 600, and powered several of the Luftwaffe's most important aircraft, most famously the Messerschmitt Bf 109 fighter.

Design and development

The DB 601 differed from the DB 600 principally in using direct fuel injection instead of a carburettor. Fuel injection allowed the engine to keep running under negative-g manoeuvres that would starve a float carburettor — a significant tactical advantage over early-war Allied engines such as the Rolls-Royce Merlin. The engine also featured a variable-speed, hydraulically driven supercharger that gave good performance at altitude.

The inverted V layout lowered the engine's centre of gravity, improved the pilot's view over the nose, and allowed a cannon (Motorkanone) to be mounted firing through the hollow propeller shaft. Displacement was 33.9 litres; the DB 601A produced about 1,100 hp, and later variants such as the DB 601N and DB 601E reached roughly 1,270–1,350 hp using higher-octane fuel and increased boost.

License production

The DB 601 was built under licence in Italy by Alfa Romeo (as the R.A.1000 R.C.41-I Monsone) for the Macchi C.202 Folgore and Reggiane Re.2001, and in Japan by Aichi (Atsuta) and Kawasaki (Ha-40) for the Kawasaki Ki-61 Hien.
