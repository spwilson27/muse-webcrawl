British peerage

The British peerage is the system of hereditary and life titles of nobility in the United Kingdom. It comprises the peerages of England, Scotland, Ireland, Great Britain and the United Kingdom. Peers are ranked in five degrees: duke, marquess, earl, viscount and baron. The peerage historically formed the core of the House of Lords, the upper house of Parliament.

History

The peerage developed from the feudal baronage summoned to advise the king. The peerages of England and Scotland were united in 1707 into the Peerage of Great Britain, and the Irish peerage joined in 1801 with the creation of the Peerage of the United Kingdom. Hereditary peerages were routinely created until 1964; since then, almost all new creations have been life peerages under the Life Peerages Act 1958.

Until 1999, most hereditary peers were entitled to sit in the House of Lords. The House of Lords Act 1999 removed that automatic right, allowing only 92 hereditary peers to remain, elected from among their number. Life peers, appointed on the advice of the prime minister, now form the bulk of the chamber.

Creation and succession

Peerages are created by the sovereign by letters patent. Hereditary peerages usually descend in the male line according to primogeniture, though some older Scottish and English baronies by writ can pass through female lines and may fall into abeyance between co-heirs. A peer may disclaim a hereditary title for life under the Peerage Act 1963.
