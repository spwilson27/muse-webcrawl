Circuit switching

Circuit switching is a method of implementing a telecommunications network in which a dedicated communication path (circuit) between two endpoints is established before data is exchanged and is maintained for the duration of the session. The classic example is the traditional public switched telephone network (PSTN), in which a call reserves a fixed channel end-to-end.

Characteristics

Because bandwidth is reserved, circuit switching offers a constant bit rate and predictable latency, well suited to continuous voice. The trade-off is inefficiency: the reserved capacity is wasted during pauses in conversation, and call setup takes time before any data flows. If any link along the path is congested, the call is blocked rather than delayed.

Comparison with packet switching

Packet-switched networks such as the Internet divide data into packets routed independently, sharing capacity statistically and degrading gracefully under load. Modern telephony increasingly emulates circuit-like guarantees over packet networks (for example VoIP with quality-of-service mechanisms), while the underlying transport is almost entirely packet-switched; legacy circuit-switched networks are being retired in many countries.
