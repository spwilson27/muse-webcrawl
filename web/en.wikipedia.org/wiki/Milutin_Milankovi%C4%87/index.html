Milutin Milanković

Born: 28 May 1879, Dalj, Austria-Hungary
Died: 12 December 1958, Belgrade, Yugoslavia
Fields: Mathematics, astronomy, climatology, geophysics
Known for: Milankovitch cycles, insolation theory of ice ages

Milutin Milanković (Serbian Cyrillic: Милутин Миланковић; 28 May 1879 – 12 December 1958) was a Serbian mathematician, astronomer, climatologist, geophysicist, civil engineer, and popularizer of science. Milanković is best known for developing one of the most significant scientific theories relating Earth motions and long-term climate change: the theory of Milankovitch cycles, which explains the ice ages by relating variations in the Earth's orbital parameters — eccentricity, axial tilt, and precession — to changes in solar radiation (insolation) received at high northern latitudes.

Life and career

Milanković was born in the village of Dalj, on the Danube, then in the Kingdom of Croatia-Slavonia within Austria-Hungary. He studied civil engineering at the Vienna University of Technology (then the Technische Hochschule), graduating in 1902 and earning a doctorate in 1904. After working as a construction engineer in Vienna, he accepted a chair of applied mathematics at the University of Belgrade in 1909, where he spent the rest of his career. During the First World War he was interned in Budapest, where, with access to a library, he worked on the mathematical theory of climate. He became a member of the Serbian Academy of Sciences and Arts and a vice-president of the Yugoslav Academy.

Work on climate

Milanković computed how variations in the Earth's orbit change the distribution of solar radiation by latitude and season. He proposed that ice ages occur when northern high-latitude summers are cool enough — because of orbital configuration — for winter snow to survive and accumulate into ice sheets. He published his calculations in works such as Théorie mathématique des phénomènes thermiques produits par la radiation solaire (1920) and the comprehensive Kanon der Erdbestrahlung und seine Anwendung auf das Eiszeitenproblem ("Canon of Insolation and the Ice-Age Problem", 1941). His theory was initially controversial but was vindicated in the 1970s when deep-sea sediment cores showed climate oscillations matching his predicted cycles.

Other contributions and legacy

Milanković also proposed a reform of the Julian calendar, adopted by several Eastern Orthodox churches in 1923, which is currently the most accurate calendar in use. Craters on the Moon and Mars and an asteroid, 1605 Milankovitch, are named after him. The European Geosciences Union awards a Milutin Milanković Medal for contributions to climate science.
