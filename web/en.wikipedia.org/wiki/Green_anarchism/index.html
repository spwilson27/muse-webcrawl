Green anarchism

Green anarchism, also known as ecological anarchism or eco-anarchism, is a school of thought within anarchism that puts a particular emphasis on environmental issues. A green anarchist theory is normally one that extends anarchist ideology beyond a critique of human interactions to include a critique of the interactions between humans and non-humans as well. This often culminates in an anarchist revolutionary praxis that is not merely dedicated to human liberation but also to some form of ecological liberation, and that can be either social (consciously sustainable, decentralised and localised) or individualist (primitivist) in nature.

Origins

Early influences include the thought of Henry David Thoreau and his book Walden, the French geographer and anarchist Élisée Reclus, and the naturist movements of the early 20th century. In the mid-20th century, Murray Bookchin developed social ecology, arguing that the domination of nature by humans grew out of social domination, and advocating decentralised, ecological societies. Bookchin's ideas, though influential in green politics broadly, were later contested by proponents of deep ecology and by anarcho-primitivists such as John Zerzan, who argue that civilisation itself is the root of domination and advocate a return to hunter-gatherer modes of life.

Currents and practice

Green anarchism encompasses strains such as social ecology, anarcho-primitivism, ecofeminist and "green syndicalist" positions, and individualist "rewilding" projects. In practice, green anarchists have been prominent in movements including Earth First!, the anti-roads protests of the 1990s in Britain, the animal liberation movement, anti-globalisation mobilisations, and publications such as Green Anarchy. Critics within and outside anarchism dispute primitivism's rejection of technology and question the feasibility of its proposed social forms.
