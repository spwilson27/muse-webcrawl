Liturgical drama

The Easter trope Quem quaeritis is regarded as the seed of medieval liturgical drama.

Liturgical drama refers to medieval dramatic performances that grew out of the Christian liturgy, principally between the tenth and sixteenth centuries. Sung in Latin and performed by clergy within churches as part of worship, these plays dramatized biblical episodes — above all the Resurrection and the Nativity — and are regarded by theatre historians as a bridge between ancient drama and the vernacular mystery plays of the later Middle Ages.

Origins: the Quem quaeritis trope

The genre's origin is conventionally traced to the Quem quaeritis ("Whom do you seek?") trope, a short sung dialogue added to the Easter introit in which an angel questions the three Marys at the empty tomb. The earliest surviving rubrics come from the Regularis Concordia (c. 970) compiled under Æthelwold of Winchester, which describes how a brother should enact the angel at the sepulchre. Related dialogues developed for Christmas (the shepherds at the crib) and other feasts.

Development

Over the following centuries the scenes expanded: the Visitatio Sepulchri (Visit to the Sepulchre) acquired processions, the lamentations of the Marys, and the episode of Peter and John racing to the tomb. Twelfth-century works such as the Ordo representacionis Ade and the plays associated with Hildegard of Bingen (the Ordo Virtutum, c. 1151) show increasing literary sophistication. Large collections like the Benedictbeuern manuscript (which includes the Carmina Burana) preserve Passion plays, saints' plays, and the playful Danielis ludus (Play of Daniel) composed at Beauvais.

Decline and legacy

As the plays grew longer and more elaborate they migrated from the sanctuary to the church porch, then to market squares, and passed from clergy to lay guilds, becoming the vernacular mystery and miracle plays of England, France, and Germany. Ecclesiastical authorities periodically objected to theatrical excess within worship — Pope Innocent III restricted plays in church in 1207 — and the Reformation and Counter-Reformation largely ended the tradition, though survivals such as the Oberammergau Passion Play continue a related devotional practice.
