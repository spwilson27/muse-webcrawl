Washington Naval Treaty

The Washington Naval Treaty, also known as the Five-Power Treaty, was a treaty signed in 1922 among the major Allied powers of the First World War — the United States, the British Empire, Japan, France and Italy — to limit naval armaments and prevent a costly battleship-building race. It emerged from the Washington Naval Conference of 1921–22.

Terms

The treaty fixed capital ship tonnage in the famous ratio 5:5:3:1.75:1.75 for the United States, Britain, Japan, France and Italy respectively, imposed a ten-year "holiday" on new battleship construction, capped individual capital ships at 35,000 tons with guns no larger than 16 inches, and limited aircraft carrier tonnage. Several existing ships, including American battlecruisers converted into the carriers Lexington and Saratoga and Japanese hulls converted into Akagi and Kaga, were scrapped or converted under its terms.

Effects and collapse

The treaty, supplemented by the London Naval Treaty of 1930, constrained naval construction through the 1920s but left cruisers and other classes as new fields of competition. Japan, resentful of the inferior ratio, gave notice of withdrawal in 1934, and the treaty system lapsed at the end of 1936, opening the way to the naval arms race preceding the Second World War.
