Semelparity and iteroparity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Semelparity: a single reproductive episode before death. Iteroparity: multiple reproductive cycles over a lifetime.

Semelparity and iteroparity are two contrasting reproductive strategies available to living organisms. A species is semelparous if its individuals reproduce only once before dying, and iteroparous if its individuals reproduce repeatedly over the course of their lifetimes. The terms were coined in the 1950s by the ecologist Lamont Cole, from the Latin semel ("once") and iterum ("again"), combined with pario ("to beget").

Semelparity

Semelparous organisms put all of their reproductive effort into a single, massive episode of reproduction and then die. Classic examples include most species of Pacific salmon, which migrate upstream, spawn once, and die; annual plants such as wheat and maize; many insects; and the female octopus, which starves while guarding her eggs. The strategy can be favoured when the probability of surviving to breed again is low, or when a single large reproductive effort yields more surviving offspring than several smaller ones.

Iteroparity

Iteroparous organisms reproduce in multiple cycles. Most mammals, birds, reptiles, and perennial plants are iteroparous. Iteroparity spreads reproductive risk across seasons and years — a "bet-hedging" advantage in unpredictable environments — and allows parents to gain experience and larger body size between breeding attempts, at the cost of lower investment in each individual episode.

Trade-offs and evolution

The choice between the strategies is a central problem in life history theory. Cole's paradox noted that, all else being equal, an annual species needs only a tiny increase in fecundity to match the fitness of a perennial, which raises the question of why iteroparity is so common; the resolution involves differences in juvenile and adult mortality. Semelparity is associated with highly variable or seasonal environments and with life histories in which adult survival between breeding seasons is poor.
