Boxing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Boxing is a combat sport in which two opponents throw punches at each other, usually with gloved hands, inside a roped ring, under the supervision of a referee. Bouts are won by knockout, by the referee's or corner's stoppage, or by judges' decision after a set number of rounds.

History

Fist-fighting is depicted in ancient art from Egypt and Greece, where boxing was an Olympic sport; Roman gladiators fought with metal-studded cestus gloves. Modern boxing was codified in England: Broughton's rules (1743) introduced gloves and rounds, and the Marquess of Queensberry Rules (1867) established the modern framework — three-minute rounds, gloves mandatory, ten-second counts.

Professional and amateur scenes

Professional boxing is organised by weight divisions (from minimumweight to heavyweight) and fragmented among sanctioning bodies (WBA, WBC, IBF, WBO), each crowning its own champions. Its defining figures include Jack Johnson, Joe Louis, Muhammad Ali — whose bouts with Joe Frazier and George Foreman ("Rumble in the Jungle", 1974) were global events — and Mike Tyson. Amateur (Olympic) boxing uses shorter formats and has launched many professional stars. The sport's dangers — chronic brain injury above all — drive continuing debates about safety and regulation, and its athletic benchmarks include sprinters-turned-fighters and icons such as Usain Bolt in the wider arena of sporting celebrity.
