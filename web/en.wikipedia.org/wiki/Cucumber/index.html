Cucumber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Green cucumbers on the vine, one sliced into crisp rounds]

The cucumber (Cucumis sativus) is a creeping vine of the gourd family and its cylindrical green fruit — eaten immature, at about 95% water, with a cool, mild flesh. Cucumbers were domesticated in India at least 3,000 years ago and spread along trade routes to Egypt, Greece and Rome; the Emperor Tiberius is said to have demanded them daily, out of season, from wheeled frames that Roman writers describe like primitive greenhouses. Most of the world's crop is now grown in China. Cucumbers divide into slicing types for salads and pickling types — gherkins, cornichons and dill pickles — preserved in brine or vinegar.

History and culture

"Cool as a cucumber" is measurement, not metaphor: the inside of a cucumber can sit noticeably cooler than the surrounding air, which made it precious in hot climates long before refrigeration — cucumbers appear in the Epic of Gilgamesh's world, in Egyptian tomb offerings, and in the biblical listing of the foods Israelites missed in the desert. The Victorian cucumber sandwich became a symbol of refined idleness (and of Oscar Wilde's wit in mocking it). In Japan, kappa-maki sushi rolls honour a water spirit fond of cucumbers. Unripe is the point: a ripe cucumber is yellow, bloated and bitter with cucurbitacins.

Varieties and use

Slicing cucumbers are bred long, thin-skinned and mild — "burpless" types reduce the bitter compounds concentrated in the skin and stem end. Pickling transformed the cucumber into a preserved food of global reach: lacto-fermented kosher dills in New York delis, vinegar cornichons with French steak, sweet bread-and-butter pickles, and the small spiky pickles of Indian and Middle Eastern tables. Beyond food, cucumber is a spa cliché (slices on eyelids) and a flavouring — in gin, in sodas, and in the British summer drink Pimm's.
