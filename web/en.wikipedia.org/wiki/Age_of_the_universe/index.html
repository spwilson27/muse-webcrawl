Age of the universe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Timeline graphic of the universe's history from the Big Bang to the present, marked 13.8 billion years]
13.8 billion years from the Big Bang to now.

The age of the universe is the time elapsed since the Big Bang: according to the standard ΛCDM model and measurements of the Cosmic microwave background by the Planck satellite (final analysis 2020), 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years. Independent checks agree: the oldest globular clusters' stars are slightly younger than this; radioactive dating of ancient stars and meteorites gives compatible bounds; and the expansion history integrates to the same figure.

The value depends on the expansion history — hence on the Hubble constant and the densities of matter and Dark energy. Early estimates swung wildly (Hubble's original constant implied an age of only ~2 billion years, embarrassingly less than Earth's rocks — a crisis resolved by recalibrating the cosmic distance ladder in the 1950s). The modern figure's precision (~0.2%) makes it one of cosmology's triumphs, though the Hubble tension implies that either the local or the early-universe inference chain contains an error that a revised age may one day expose.

Historical estimates

Before cosmology, ages were the province of theology and geology (Ussher's 4004 BC; Kelvin's tens of millions of years for Earth, refuted by radioactivity). The twentieth century successively enlarged the canvas: 1929 expansion (billions of years), 1965 the microwave background (a hot beginning), 1998 acceleration (an age comfortably exceeding the oldest stars — removing the 'age crisis' of the 1990s, when some globular clusters seemed older than the universe).
