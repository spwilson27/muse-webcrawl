The Kalmyk language (Калмык келн, Kalmyk keln) is a Mongolic language spoken by the Kalmyk people of the Republic of Kalmykia in southern Russia, the only Buddhist-majority region of Europe. It is the literary form of Oirat as spoken by the descendants of the Oirat Mongols who migrated to the Volga steppe in the seventeenth century, and it is classified as definitely endangered by UNESCO.

History

In 1648 the monk Zaya Pandita devised the Clear Script (todo bichig), a vertical Mongolian-derived alphabet adapted precisely to Oirat phonology, in which a rich literature of Buddhist texts, epics and chronicles was written. In the Soviet period the script was replaced first by Latin (1930s) and then by Cyrillic (1938). In 1943 the entire Kalmyk population was deported to Siberia and Central Asia; the language's transmission was severely damaged before the Kalmyks were permitted to return in 1957.

Features

Kalmyk is agglutinative, with vowel harmony and a subject–object–verb word order. Its phonology preserves distinctions lost in Khalkha Mongolian, including long vowels arising from contraction. Today most Kalmyks are bilingual in Russian, and language revitalisation efforts include Kalmyk-language media, teaching in schools, and revival interest in the Clear Script.
