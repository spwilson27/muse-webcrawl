Substitution bias

Substitution bias is a possible bias in the measurement of a consumer price index (CPI). It describes the tendency of a fixed-basket price index to overstate changes in the cost of living because it does not account for the ability of consumers to substitute cheaper alternatives for goods and services whose prices rise relatively faster.

Mechanism

A traditional Laspeyres-type price index prices a fixed basket of goods over time. When the price of one item rises relative to others, consumers typically buy less of it and more of substitutes — switching, for example, from beef to chicken when beef prices spike. Because the fixed basket keeps weighting the now-dearer item as before, the index records a higher cost of living than consumers actually experience by adjusting their purchases. The bias therefore tends to make measured inflation higher than the change in the true cost of maintaining a given standard of living.

Evidence and remedies

The 1996 Boskin Commission report in the United States estimated that substitution bias contributed roughly 0.4 percentage points per year to the overstatement of CPI inflation, split between lower-level (within-category) and upper-level (across-category) substitution. Statistical agencies have responded by using geometric-mean formulas at the lower level, updating basket weights more frequently, and publishing chained, superlative indexes — such as the US Chained CPI (C-CPI-U) — that incorporate expenditure data from both periods and thereby capture substitution more fully.
