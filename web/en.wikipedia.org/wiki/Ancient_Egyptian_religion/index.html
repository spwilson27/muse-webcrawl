Ancient Egyptian religion was a complex system of polytheistic beliefs and rituals that formed an integral part of ancient Egyptian culture and state for more than three thousand years, from the unification of Egypt around 3100 BC until the suppression of the last temples under Christian Roman rule in the 4th–6th centuries AD. It centred on the Egyptians' interactions with a multitude of deities believed to control the forces of nature and to be present in the world.

Beliefs

The cosmos was held to have emerged from a watery chaos (Nu), with the creator god — Atum at Heliopolis, Ptah at Memphis, Amun at Thebes in later theology — bringing order into being. Central to Egyptian thought was ma'at, the principle of truth, justice, and cosmic order, opposed by isfet (chaos). The sun god Ra was said to journey across the sky by day and through the underworld (the Duat) by night, battling the serpent Apophis. The myth of Osiris — murdered and dismembered by his brother Set, reassembled by his wife Isis, and succeeded by his son Horus — underpinned ideas of death, kingship, and renewal.

Gods

Egyptians worshipped hundreds of gods, many depicted with human bodies and animal heads: falcon-headed Horus, jackal-headed Anubis (god of embalming), ibis-headed Thoth (wisdom and writing), lioness Sekhmet, cow-eared Hathor, and cat goddess Bastet. Gods had local strongholds and could merge into composite forms such as Amun-Ra, "king of the gods" during the New Kingdom.

The king and the temples

The pharaoh was regarded as a divine intermediary — the living Horus — responsible for maintaining ma'at. In theory all temple rituals were performed by the king; in practice priests acted on his behalf. Great temple complexes such as Karnak and Luxor housed cult statues, received offerings, and staged festivals, including the Opet Festival and processions in which the god's image travelled in a sacred barque.

Death and the afterlife

Egyptians believed the person comprised several elements — including the ka (life force) and ba (personality) — that could survive death if the body was preserved through mummification and supplied with grave goods, spells, and offerings. The dead were judged in the Hall of Two Truths, where the heart was weighed against the feather of Ma'at; the worthy entered the Field of Reeds, an idealised mirror of earthly life. Funerary literature evolved from the Old Kingdom Pyramid Texts through the Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts to the New Kingdom Book of the Dead.

The Amarna period and decline

In the 14th century BC the pharaoh Akhenaten promoted the sun-disc Aten above all other gods — sometimes described as an early monotheism — and suppressed the cult of Amun; the reform was reversed after his death. Under Greek and Roman rule Egyptian cults, especially that of Isis, spread across the Mediterranean world. The last hieroglyphic inscription dates to AD 394 at Philae, whose temple of Isis was finally closed under Justinian in the 6th century AD.
