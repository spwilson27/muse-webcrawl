Heir apparent

An heir apparent is a person who is first in an order of succession and cannot be displaced from inheriting by the birth of another person; the term contrasts with an heir presumptive, whose position can be displaced by the birth of someone with a stronger claim. The concept applies chiefly to hereditary monarchies and to hereditary titles, though it is also used loosely for designated successors in other contexts.

Distinction from heir presumptive

An heir apparent's claim is indefeasible under the applicable succession law: for example, the eldest child of a monarch in a system of absolute primogeniture. An heir presumptive is currently first in line but could be supplanted — for instance, a younger brother of a childless monarch, who would be displaced if the monarch had a child. In the United Kingdom, the eldest son of the sovereign is typically created Prince of Wales and is heir apparent; historically a daughter could only be heir presumptive (before the Succession to the Crown Act 2013 introduced absolute primogeniture for those born after 2011).

Titles

Heirs apparent to thrones often bear special titles: Crown Prince (or Crown Princess) in many monarchies, Prince of Wales in the United Kingdom, Prince of Asturias in Spain, Dauphin in pre-revolutionary France, and Tsesarevich in imperial Russia. An heir apparent who dies before the monarch is succeeded in line by their own heir, and never reigns.
