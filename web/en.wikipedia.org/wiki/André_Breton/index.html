André Robert Breton (19 February 1896 – 28 September 1966) was a French writer and poet, best known as the founder of Surrealism. His Manifesto of Surrealism (1924) defined the movement as "pure psychic automatism" — the expression of thought free from reason, morality and aesthetic convention — and he remained its dominant theorist and often authoritarian organiser for four decades.

Early life

Breton was born in Tinchebray, Normandy, and studied medicine and psychiatry in Paris. During the First World War he worked in neurological wards, encountering shell-shocked soldiers and the theories of Freud, and met the poet Jacques Vaché, whose nihilistic humour profoundly influenced him. After the war he joined the Paris Dada group around Tristan Tzara, co-founding the journal Littérature with Louis Aragon and Philippe Soupault.

Surrealism

With Soupault, Breton wrote Les Champs magnétiques (1920), the first work of automatic writing. Breaking with Dada in 1922–24, he launched Surrealism with the 1924 Manifesto and the journal La Révolution surréaliste. Around him gathered poets and artists including Paul Éluard, Max Ernst, Salvador Dalí, Man Ray and Joan Miró. His novel Nadja (1928) and the essay-novel L'Amour fou (1937) are central surrealist texts. Breton's efforts to align the movement with communism, and his frequent excommunications of members, caused repeated schisms.

Exile and later years

In 1941 Breton fled occupied France for the United States, where he organised surrealist exhibitions in New York and broadcast for Voice of America. He returned to Paris in 1946 and continued to lead the movement until his death there in 1966. The "Pope of Surrealism" — a title he both attracted and resented — is buried in the Batignolles cemetery.
