FormulaC₁₂H₁₉Cl₃O₈

Sucralose is an artificial sweetener and sugar substitute, about 600 times sweeter than sucrose, twice as sweet as saccharin and three times as sweet as aspartame. It is made from sucrose by replacing three hydroxyl groups with chlorine atoms; the resulting organochloride is not broken down by the body and passes through essentially unmetabolised, so it contributes no calories. Sucralose is marketed, most famously in North America under the brand name Splenda, and has the E number E955.

Discovery

Sucralose was discovered in 1976 by scientists at Tate & Lyle working with researchers Leslie Hough and Shashikant Phadnis at Queen Elizabeth College, University of London, during a programme on chlorinated sugars. The oft-retold story holds that Phadnis, asked to "test" a chlorinated compound, misheard the instruction as "taste" — and tasted it, finding it intensely sweet. Tate & Lyle patented the compound and developed it in partnership with Johnson & Johnson's McNeil Specialty Products.

Properties and uses

Sucralose is a white, free-flowing powder, freely soluble in water and stable across a wide pH range and at cooking temperatures, which allows its use in baking — an advantage over aspartame. Most ingested sucralose is excreted unchanged in the faeces, and the small absorbed fraction is excreted in urine. It is used in more than 4,500 products: soft drinks, baked goods, chewing gum, dairy products, canned fruit and tabletop sweeteners. Retail Splenda is mostly bulking agents (maltodextrin and dextrose) with a small amount of sucralose, and so is not calorie-free per packet in the way pure sucralose is. Sucralose is frequently blended with acesulfame potassium and other sweeteners for a rounder sweetness profile.

Safety and controversies

Canada first approved sucralose in 1991, followed by Australia, the European Union (2004) and the United States FDA (1998–1999, for broad categories of food). Reviews by the FDA, EFSA and JECFA have repeatedly concluded it is safe, with an acceptable daily intake of 5 mg/kg body weight (FDA) and 15 mg/kg (EFSA/JECFA). Debate has nonetheless persisted: rodent studies have been cited both by critics (claiming effects on gut bacteria and, in one contested Italian study, leukaemia) and by manufacturers; regulators have generally found the critical studies methodologically flawed. At very high temperatures some research indicates sucralose in baked fats can degrade to chlorinated by-products, a topic of continuing study. In 2023 a widely publicised in-vitro study reported that sucralose-6-acetate, a trace impurity and metabolite, damaged DNA in cultured cells; food-safety agencies responded that the findings did not alter existing assessments at dietary exposure levels.
