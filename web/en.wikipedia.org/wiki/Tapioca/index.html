Tapioca

Tapioca is a starch extracted from the storage roots of the cassava plant (Manihot esculenta), a species native to South America. Nearly pure carbohydrate and almost free of protein, tapioca is a staple food in many tropical regions and is used worldwide as a thickening agent in cooking. It is sold in forms including flakes, sticks, flour and the familiar spherical "pearls" of various sizes and colours.

Production and uses

To make tapioca, the cassava root is washed, crushed and the starch washed out and dried. Moist starch mass forced through a sieve and heated forms the hard, opaque pearls that turn translucent when cooked. Tapioca pudding is a traditional sweet dish in Britain and elsewhere; large pearls ("boba") are the signature ingredient of Taiwanese bubble tea; and in Brazil the starch is used for dishes such as the crêpe-like beiju. Because it is naturally gluten-free, tapioca flour is common in gluten-free baking, and the starch is also used industrially in paper, textiles and adhesives.

Nutrition

Tapioca is essentially pure starch: it provides energy but few nutrients, containing negligible protein, fibre, vitamins or minerals. Cassava must be properly processed to remove naturally occurring cyanogenic compounds, a step built into commercial tapioca production.
