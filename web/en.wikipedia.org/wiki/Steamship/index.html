Steamship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Victorian paddle steamer with two funnels leaving harbour]
A 19th-century paddle steamer.

A steamship (often SS, PS or MV in other propulsion eras) is a ship propelled by one or more steam engines — reciprocating engines driving paddles or propellers at first, later steam turbines. Steamships freed shipping from wind and tide, made schedules reliable, and dominated sea trade from the mid-19th century until diesel took over after the Second World War. Landmarks include Robert Fulton\'s Clermont (1807), the first Atlantic crossing under steam assistance by Savannah (1819), and Brunel\'s iron-hulled SS Great Britain (1843).

Paddle steamers

Early steamships used side or stern paddle wheels — ideal on rivers such as the Mississippi, where shallow-draught sternwheelers became icons of 19th-century America. Trevithick and others experimented from the 1780s; Henry Bell\'s Comet (1812) began Europe\'s first commercial steam service on the Clyde.

Screw propulsion and iron hulls

The screw propeller, proven in the 1840s (famously by a tug-of-war between HMS Rattler and Alecto in 1845), plus iron and then steel hulls, enabled ever-larger ocean liners. Compound and triple-expansion engines cut coal consumption, making long routes like London–Australia economic.

Turbines and decline

Parsons\' Turbinia (1894) demonstrated the steam turbine, which powered the great express liners (Lusitania, Queen Mary) and most warships. Diesel\'s efficiency ended reciprocating steam by the 1950s; turbines lingered in LNG carriers and nuclear ships (where reactors raise steam) into the modern era.
