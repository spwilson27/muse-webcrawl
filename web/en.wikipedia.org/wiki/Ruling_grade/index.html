Ruling grade

The ruling grade (or ruling gradient) of a section of railway is the steepest sustained gradient that a train must surmount over a significant distance, and which therefore governs — "rules" — the maximum load a locomotive of a given power can haul over that section, or the speed at which a given load can be hauled.

Definition and use

Short, steeper pitches (sometimes called momentum grades) can be climbed using the kinetic energy of a moving train and do not determine hauling capacity in the same way. The ruling grade is the gradient that limits the tonnage rating of locomotives for the whole route: timetable planners and operating departments set locomotive loads so that trains can restart and proceed on the ruling grade under adverse conditions.

Ruling grades on main lines are commonly between 0.5% and 1% (1 in 200 to 1 in 100) on older routes, while dedicated freight or mountain lines may exceed 2%. Famous examples include the 2.2% grade of Saluda Grade in North Carolina and the sustained gradients of the Transcontinental routes over the Sierra Nevada and the Rockies. On rack railways and funiculars, gradients are far steeper but are handled by special equipment.

Related measures

The compensated gradient adjusts a nominal grade for the additional resistance of curvature, so that the total resistance on curved steep sections stays within limits. Engineers designing new lines balance the capital cost of earthworks and tunnels against the operating cost imposed by a steeper ruling grade.
