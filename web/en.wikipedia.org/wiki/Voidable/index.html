Voidable

A voidable contract, in law, is a contract that is valid and enforceable unless and until one of the parties — the party with the power of avoidance — elects to rescind it. Unlike a void contract, which is a nullity from the beginning, a voidable contract takes effect immediately and produces legal consequences unless it is avoided.

Grounds for voidability

Contracts are commonly voidable on grounds including:

  • Misrepresentation — where one party was induced to contract by a false statement of fact made by the other party.
  • Duress and undue influence — where consent was obtained by illegitimate pressure or an abuse of a position of trust.
  • Incapacity — contracts entered into by minors are typically voidable at the minor's option (with exceptions for necessaries), as are, in some circumstances, contracts of persons lacking mental capacity.
  • Mistake — certain non-fundamental mistakes render a contract voidable rather than void.
  • Non-disclosure — in contracts uberrimae fidei (of the utmost good faith), such as insurance contracts, material non-disclosure may entitle the insurer to avoid the policy.

Effects of avoidance

When a voidable contract is avoided, the parties are, so far as possible, restored to their pre-contractual positions (restitutio in integrum). The right to rescind may be lost — the contract is then said to be affirmed — if the entitled party, with knowledge of the facts, elects to continue with the contract, if there is undue delay, if restitution becomes impossible, or if third parties have acquired rights in good faith.

Voidable marriages and transactions

The concept also applies outside contract law. A voidable marriage is valid unless annulled by a court decree. In company and insolvency law, certain transactions — such as preferences or transfers at an undervalue — are voidable on the application of a liquidator, meaning they can be unwound if challenged but stand if not.
