Crimean Gothic

The Crimean Goths survived in the mountainous south of Crimea into the early modern period

Crimean Gothic was an East Germanic language spoken by the Crimean Goths, descendants of Gothic tribes who settled around the Black Sea and remained in the mountains and southern coast of Crimea after the fall of the Gothic kingdoms elsewhere. It is the latest attested Germanic language of the Gothic branch, surviving into the sixteenth century — and possibly later — long after Gothic had died out in western Europe.

Attestation

Knowledge of the language rests largely on a single document: a list of about eighty words, a fragment of a song, and grammatical notes recorded around 1562 by Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq, the Habsburg ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, from two informants in Constantinople, published in his Turkish Letters. Earlier references include a ninth-century mention of Gothic used liturgically in Crimea and reports by the Franciscan William of Rubruck in the thirteenth century. A handful of names and possible loanwords in other sources supplement the record.

Features

Busbecq's vocabulary (for example broe "bread", plut "blood", stega "goat", reghen "rain") shows clear kinship with the Gothic of Wulfila's Bible translation while also showing developments of its own, including changes in vowels and consonants that have led some scholars to detect similarities with West Germanic languages. The fragmentary song Busbecq recorded has resisted convincing interpretation.

History of the speakers

The Crimean Goths maintained a principality (Gothia, centred on Mangup) under Byzantine, then Genoese and Ottoman influence. After the Ottoman conquest of 1475 the community gradually assimilated linguistically to Greek and Crimean Tatar; travellers' reports suggest Gothic speech may have lingered in remote villages into the seventeenth or even eighteenth century before disappearing.
