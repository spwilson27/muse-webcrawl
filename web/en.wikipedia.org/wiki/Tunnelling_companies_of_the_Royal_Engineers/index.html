The tunnelling companies of the Royal Engineers were specialist units of the British Army formed during the First World War to conduct offensive and defensive mining operations beneath the trenches of the Western Front. Proposed by Major (later Major-General Sir) John Norton-Griffiths, a civil engineer and MP, and authorised in early 1915, the companies recruited civilian tunnellers, miners, and "clay-kickers" from collieries and from sewer and underground-railway works across Britain and the Empire.

Operations

Tunnellers dug galleries under no man's land to plant explosive mines beneath German positions, and fought a clandestine underground war against their German counterparts, listening for enemy digging and detonating defensive camouflets. Their most famous operation was the Battle of Messines in June 1917, when 19 enormous mines — among the largest non-nuclear explosions ever recorded — were fired simultaneously under the German lines on Messines Ridge. Other notable mines were fired at the Somme in 1916, including the Lochnagar and Hawthorn Ridge mines, and at Vimy Ridge and Hill 60. Australian, Canadian and New Zealand tunnelling companies served alongside the British units.

Legacy

The tunnellers' war was later popularised by Sebastian Faulks's novel Birdsong (1993) and the film Beneath Hill 60 (2010). Several of the great mine craters survive as memorials, and the La Boisselle Study Group and others continue archaeological work on the tunnel systems.
