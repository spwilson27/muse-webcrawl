The guqin (Chinese: 古琴; pinyin: gǔqín) is a seven-stringed Chinese musical instrument of the zither family. It has been played since ancient times and is traditionally the most esteemed instrument of the Chinese scholar-gentleman, closely associated with Confucius, who was said to be an accomplished player. Its name literally means "ancient qin" or "old instrument"; it is sometimes simply called the qin.

Unlike the more popular guzheng, the guqin has no bridges or frets: the player presses the strings directly against the soundboard, allowing slides, vibrato, and harmonics. Its quiet, refined sound suits solitary meditation, and its repertoire uses a distinctive tablature notation (jianzipu) that records fingerings rather than pitches.

History

Legend attributes the qin's invention to culture heroes of remote antiquity such as Fuxi and Shennong, and tradition holds that it originally had five strings, with King Wen and King Wu of Zhou adding the sixth and seventh. Surviving instruments date back more than a thousand years; famous antiques include the Tang-dynasty instrument "Jiu Xiao Huan Pei". The guqin was central to the ideal of the scholar, one of the "four arts" (qin, weiqi, calligraphy, painting) expected of the cultivated person.

In 2003, guqin music was proclaimed a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity by UNESCO. In 1977 a recording of the piece "Flowing Streams" (Liu Shui) performed by Guan Pinghu was included on the Voyager Golden Record carried into space.

Construction

The guqin is a long, hollow wooden box, roughly 120 cm long, lacquered and often inlaid with thirteen mother-of-pearl markers (hui) indicating harmonic positions. Traditionally its strings were of silk; modern players often use steel-nylon strings. The body is made from two boards, typically paulownia or catalpa on top and a harder wood below, and its shape is laden with cosmological symbolism — the instrument is said to mirror the human body or a phoenix.

Playing technique

The right hand plucks with the fingernails and fingertips while the left hand stops, slides, and presses the strings. Three classes of sound are distinguished: san yin (open-string tones), an yin (stopped tones), and fan yin (harmonics), which players compare to the sounds of heaven, earth, and humankind. Guqin music is intimate and often unmetered, unfolding in long phrases shaped by many subtle types of vibrato and slide.
