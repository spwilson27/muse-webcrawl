Paddington Bear (though his Peruvian name is Pastuso) is a fictional character in children's literature, created by British author Michael Bond. He first appeared on 13 October 1958 in the children's book A Bear Called Paddington and has been featured in more than twenty books, illustrated by artists including Peggy Fortnum and R. W. Alley. An immigrant bear from "darkest Peru," Paddington is found by the Brown family at London Paddington station — from which he takes his name — wearing a tag reading "Please look after this bear. Thank you."

Character and story

Paddington was sent to England by his Aunt Lucy, who had retired to the Home for Retired Bears in Lima. He travels as a stowaway, surviving on marmalade, and is taken in by Henry and Mary Brown at 32 Windsor Gardens, Notting Hill. Paddington is unfailingly polite, well-meaning, and optimistic, but his literal-mindedness and mishaps — flooding bathrooms, chaotic shopping trips, and disastrous DIY — drive the stories' gentle comedy. He is known for his battered suitcase (with a secret compartment), his blue duffel coat and wide-brimmed hat, his love of marmalade sandwiches (kept under his hat "for emergencies"), elevenses of buns and cocoa at Mr Gruber's antique shop, and his famous "hard stare" for those who offend him.

Books and adaptations

Michael Bond wrote the Paddington stories for nearly sixty years; the final book, Paddington at St Paul's, appeared posthumously in 2018. The character has starred in several television series (including the BBC's 1976 stop-motion series and The Adventures of Paddington, 2019–) and in the critically and commercially successful films Paddington (2014), Paddington 2 (2017), and Paddington in Peru (2024), with Ben Whishaw voicing Paddington. A statue of Paddington stands at Paddington station, and the character has become a beloved symbol of welcome and kindness; in 2022 a sketch featuring Paddington taking tea with Queen Elizabeth II opened the Platinum Party at the Palace.

Origins

Bond was inspired by a lone teddy bear he bought for his wife from a London shop shelf on Christmas Eve 1956, and by memories of child evacuees and refugees arriving in Britain during the Second World War — an origin that has made Paddington an enduring emblem of the refugee experience.
