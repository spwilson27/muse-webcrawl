Cord blood (also called umbilical cord blood) is blood that remains in the placenta and the attached umbilical cord after childbirth. It is collected because it contains haematopoietic stem cells, which can be used to treat haematopoietic and genetic disorders of the blood and immune system.

Medical uses

The first successful cord blood transplant was performed in 1988 in Paris, when a child with Fanconi anaemia received cord blood from his newborn sister. Since then, tens of thousands of unrelated cord blood transplants have been performed worldwide for leukaemias, lymphomas, bone marrow failure syndromes, haemoglobinopathies such as thalassaemia and sickle cell disease, and inherited immunodeficiencies and metabolic disorders. Cord blood has advantages over bone marrow: it is readily available, can tolerate greater HLA mismatch, and carries a lower risk of graft-versus-host disease. Its main limitation is the small volume collected, which may provide too few cells for larger adult recipients; techniques such as double-unit transplants and ex vivo expansion address this.

Collection and banking

After the baby is delivered and the cord is clamped and cut, blood is drained from the cord into a sterile bag, tested, typed for HLA, processed to concentrate stem cells, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Parents may donate to a public bank, where units are listed on registries for any matching patient, or pay a private bank to store the unit exclusively for family use. Medical organisations generally recommend public donation, since the chance a child will need its own stored cord blood is very low; debates continue over delayed cord clamping, which benefits the newborn but reduces collection volume.

Research

Cord blood is also investigated as a source of mesenchymal and other progenitor cells, and in trials for conditions such as cerebral palsy and type 1 diabetes, though such uses remain experimental.
