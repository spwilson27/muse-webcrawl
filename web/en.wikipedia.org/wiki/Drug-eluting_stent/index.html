Drug-eluting stent

A drug-eluting stent (DES) is a tube-like device placed in a narrowed, diseased coronary artery that slowly releases a drug to block cell proliferation. This prevents fibrosis that, together with clots (thrombi), could otherwise block the stented artery, a process called restenosis. A drug-eluting stent places the stent and a drug coating into a artery through a catheter inserted into a peripheral artery, usually the femoral or radial artery.

Overview

Coronary stents are used in a procedure called percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), most commonly to treat coronary artery disease, in which fatty deposits called plaque narrow the arteries supplying the heart muscle. After a stent is placed, the vessel wall heals around it; in some patients, excessive tissue growth inside the stent (in-stent restenosis) re-narrows the artery. Drug-eluting stents were developed to counter this problem by releasing antiproliferative drugs from a polymer coating on the stent surface.

Drugs used

The first drug-eluting stents released sirolimus (rapamycin) or paclitaxel. Later generations use "limus" family drugs such as everolimus, zotarolimus, and biolimus, which inhibit the mTOR pathway and reduce smooth-muscle cell proliferation. The stent platform is typically made of stainless steel or a cobalt-chromium or platinum-chromium alloy.

Clinical use

Randomized trials in the early 2000s showed that drug-eluting stents substantially reduced restenosis and the need for repeat revascularization compared with bare-metal stents. Because the drug delays healing of the vessel lining, patients receiving a drug-eluting stent are prescribed dual antiplatelet therapy — typically aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor such as clopidogrel — for a period of months to reduce the risk of stent thrombosis. Newer-generation stents with thinner struts and more biocompatible or biodegradable polymers have improved safety outcomes, including lower rates of late stent thrombosis.

History

The first drug-eluting stent, the sirolimus-eluting Cypher stent, received European approval in 2002 and United States FDA approval in 2003. The paclitaxel-eluting Taxus stent followed in 2004. Second-generation devices, including the everolimus-eluting Xience and the zotarolimus-eluting Resolute stents, largely replaced first-generation products in clinical practice.
