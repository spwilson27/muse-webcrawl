The piano is a keyboard instrument that produces sound when its keys are pressed, causing hammers inside to strike strings. The strings are affixed to a resonant soundboard, which amplifies the sound. Invented in Florence around 1700 by Bartolomeo Cristofori, the piano developed from the harpsichord and clavichord but allowed the player to vary volume by touch — hence its original name, gravicembalo col piano e forte ("harpsichord with soft and loud"), shortened to pianoforte and then piano.

History

Cristofori's key innovation was an escapement action in which the hammer is thrown at the string and falls away, letting the string vibrate freely, while a damper silences it when the key is released. During the 18th and 19th centuries the instrument grew in range (from about five octaves to the modern 7¼), string tension and size; iron frames, cross-stringing and felt-covered hammers enabled the powerful modern grand. Firms such as Steinway & Sons (founded 1853) industrialised production. The piano became the central instrument of Romantic music and of middle-class domestic life, and remains fundamental to composition, teaching and performance across classical, jazz and popular music.

Electric / digital pianoAmplified or sampled instruments imitating the acoustic piano's sound and touch.

A standard piano has 88 keys — 52 white and 36 black — spanning A0 to C8. Pressing a key lifts a damper and drives a felt hammer to strike one to three steel strings tuned to the note; releasing the key re-applies the damper. Three pedals are usual: the sustaining (damper) pedal, the soft (una corda) pedal, and the sostenuto pedal.

Role in music

Because it can supply melody, harmony and rhythm at once, the piano became the composer's working instrument and the most common vehicle for music education. Its repertoire spans from Bach and Mozart through Chopin, Liszt and Rachmaninoff to jazz innovators such as Art Tatum, Thelonious Monk and Bill Evans, and it anchors countless ensembles and songwriting traditions.
