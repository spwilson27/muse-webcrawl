The Gallipoli peninsula (Turkish: Gelibolu Yarımadası; Greek: Χερσόνησος της Καλλίπολης, "Thracian Chersonese") is a peninsula in East Thrace, the European part of modern Turkey, forming the northwestern shore of the Dardanelles strait. It is bounded by the Aegean Sea to the west and the Gulf of Saros to the north, and takes its name from the town of Gallipoli (Gelibolu), from the Greek Kallipolis, "beautiful city".

History

In antiquity the Thracian Chersonese was colonised by Greeks and later passed under Persian, Macedonian, Roman and Byzantine rule, guarding the route between the Aegean and the Black Sea. It was among the first European territories taken by the Ottoman Turks in the 14th century.

The peninsula is best known for the Gallipoli campaign of the First World War. From April 1915 to January 1916, British, French and imperial forces — including the Australian and New Zealand Army Corps (ANZAC) — landed on the peninsula in an attempt to force the Dardanelles and capture Constantinople. Ottoman forces under commanders including Mustafa Kemal successfully defended the peninsula, and the Allies evacuated after heavy casualties on both sides. The campaign is commemorated each year on Anzac Day, 25 April, and the battlefields are preserved as the Gallipoli Peninsula Historical Site.
