King Kong is a 1933 American pre-Code adventure fantasy monster film directed and produced by Merian C. Cooper and Ernest B. Schoedsack, with special effects by Willis H. O'Brien. The film stars Fay Wray, Robert Armstrong, and Bruce Cabot, and tells the story of a giant ape creature called Kong who is captured from a remote island and brought to New York City, where he escapes and climbs the Empire State Building before being shot down by airplanes.

The film was produced and distributed by RKO Radio Pictures. It premiered in New York City on March 2, 1933, and opened to widespread critical and commercial success. It is widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential films of all time, pioneering techniques in stop-motion animation, model work, and musical scoring.

Plot

In 1933, filmmaker Carl Denham seeks to complete his new picture and engages the ship Wanderer, captained by Jack Driscoll, bound for the mysterious Skull Island in the Indian Ocean. Denham hires Ann Darrow, a destitute vaudeville actress, to be his leading lady. On the island, the crew encounters a native village whose wall separates them from a colossal ape, Kong, to whom the natives offer sacrifices. Ann is kidnapped and offered to Kong, who, instead of killing her, becomes infatuated and protects her from the island's dinosaurs and other prehistoric creatures.

Denham and Driscoll lead a rescue party; most are killed, but Driscoll rescues Ann, while Denham captures Kong alive using gas bombs. Kong is transported to New York and exhibited in chains as "Kong, the Eighth Wonder of the World." He breaks free during the exhibition, rampages through the city in search of Ann, and ultimately climbs the Empire State Building, where he is attacked by Navy biplanes and falls to his death. Standing over the body, Denham delivers the film's famous closing line: "Oh no, it wasn't the airplanes. It was Beauty killed the Beast."

Production

Cooper conceived the story from his boyhood fascination with gorillas and his experiences filming wildlife documentaries such as Grass (1925) and Chang (1927). Animator Willis O'Brien, who had pioneered stop-motion dinosaurs in The Lost World (1925), was brought in to animate Kong and the prehistoric creatures using articulated metal armatures covered in rabbit fur. Kong's size shifts between scenes — from roughly 18 feet tall on the island to 24 feet in New York — to suit dramatic needs.

Max Steiner's score is considered one of the first significant feature film scores in Hollywood, using a full orchestra and leitmotifs to heighten the action. The film employed rear projection, matte paintings, and miniature work extensively, combining techniques that would define special effects for decades.

Reception and legacy

King Kong was an immediate box office success and has been re-released multiple times. In 1991, it was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry by the Library of Congress as "culturally, historically, or aesthetically significant." It regularly appears on lists of the greatest films ever made, including those of the American Film Institute.

The film spawned a sequel, The Son of Kong, released the same year, and a long series of remakes and reimaginings, including the 1976 remake produced by Dino De Laurentiis and Peter Jackson's 2005 remake. The character of King Kong became one of the most iconic movie monsters in cinema history and a cornerstone of the later "MonsterVerse" franchise.
