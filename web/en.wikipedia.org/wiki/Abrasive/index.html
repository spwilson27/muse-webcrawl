Abrasive

An abrasive is a material, often a mineral, used to wear away the surface of a workpiece through rubbing, grinding, polishing or cutting. Abrasives are among humanity's oldest tools — sandstone was used to shape stone implements — and remain essential to manufacturing, construction and dentistry.

Materials

Natural abrasives include emery, corundum, garnet, pumice, flint and diamond. Synthetic abrasives, generally more uniform and harder, include aluminium oxide (alumina), silicon carbide (carborundum), cubic boron nitride and synthetic diamond. Hardness is commonly gauged on the Mohs scale; the abrasive must be harder than the workpiece, and its grains must fracture to expose fresh sharp edges (friability) rather than dull in place.

Forms and uses

Bonded abrasives — grains held in a resin, vitrified or metal matrix — form grinding wheels, cut-off discs and honing stones. Coated abrasives glue grains to paper, cloth or fibre backing, as in sandpaper and abrasive belts, graded by grit size. Loose abrasives are used in slurries for lapping, polishing, sandblasting and waterjet cutting, where garnet or alumina is entrained in a high-pressure jet. Abrasive machining processes include grinding, honing, lapping, superfinishing and abrasive flow machining, chosen according to the tolerance and finish required.
