Type approval

Type approval is the confirmation that a product design or type meets specified technical and regulatory requirements, given by a designated authority or approved body after testing and assessment of representative samples. Once a type is approved, individual items manufactured in conformity with it may be sold or used without each unit being re-tested, provided production continues to conform. Type approval is used for products where safety, interoperability, or environmental performance is regulated — notably motor vehicles, radio and telecommunications equipment, ships, aircraft components, and medical devices.

Process

Typically the manufacturer submits samples and technical documentation to a certification body, which tests the samples against the applicable standards and issues a type-approval certificate describing the approved configuration. Ongoing obligations usually include notifying changes to the design, "conformity of production" checks, and market-surveillance powers for the authority, including withdrawal of approval.

Vehicle type approval

The best-known system is the UNECE framework under the 1958 Agreement, administered by the World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations (WP.29): a vehicle type or component (lights, tyres, brakes) approved under a UN Regulation by one contracting party is recognised by the others, and approved parts carry an E-mark in a circle identifying the approving country. The European Union operates a parallel whole-vehicle type approval system under framework regulations such as (EU) 2018/858, under which a vehicle type approved in one member state may be registered throughout the EU. In the United States, by contrast, motor vehicles are generally brought to market under a self-certification system, with manufacturers certifying their own compliance with Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standards, subject to enforcement and recall by NHTSA.

Telecommunications

Radio equipment and terminals connected to public networks require type approval in most jurisdictions to control spectrum use and interference: examples include FCC equipment authorisation in the United States and the EU Radio Equipment Directive's conformity assessment, allowing equipment bearing the CE mark to be sold across the European Economic Area.
