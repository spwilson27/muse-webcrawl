Strike fighter

In current military parlance, a strike fighter is a multirole combat aircraft designed to operate primarily as an attack aircraft while retaining the performance and armament of a fighter, able to conduct both air-to-ground strikes and air-to-air combat. The term came into common use in the 1980s, notably with the McDonnell Douglas F/A-18 Hornet, the "F/A" designation itself denoting fighter/attack.

Concept

Earlier practice separated pure interceptors and fighters from dedicated attack aircraft. Strike fighters merge the roles: they can deliver precision-guided munitions against ground and sea targets, then defend themselves with air-to-air missiles and a cannon after releasing their stores. Modern examples include the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet, the F-15E Strike Eagle (whose name popularised the term), the F-35 Lightning II, the Dassault Rafale, and Sukhoi's Su-30/Su-34 family.

History

The concept has antecedents in Second World War fighter-bombers such as the P-47 Thunderbolt and F4U Corsair, and in Cold War aircraft like the F-4 Phantom II, which served in both roles. Dedicated variable-geometry strike aircraft (F-111, Tornado IDS) emphasised low-level penetration, while true multirole strike fighters emerged as sensors, guided weapons, and fly-by-wire controls matured.
