Pandeism, or pan-deism, is a theological doctrine that combines aspects of pantheism and deism. It holds that a creator deity, in creating the universe, ceased to exist as a separate and conscious entity and merged with — or became — the universe itself. The pandeist God therefore does not intervene in the world, does not answer prayer, and is not a personal being distinct from creation; the universe is the God, unfolded, but the God does not presently exist in any form apart from the universe.

Definition and distinctions

Pandeism differs from pantheism, which need not posit any act of creation at all — for the pantheist, God simply is the universe, eternally. It differs from deism in that the classical deist God remains separate from the creation, a watchmaker who set the machine running and withdrew; the pandeist God is not separate but has been wholly absorbed into the machine. It differs from panentheism, in which the universe exists within God while God also exceeds it.

History of the idea

Idea-elements resembling pandeism appear in the philosophy of Giordano Bruno and in some readings of Spinoza, and in Hindu concepts of the world as the body or manifestation of Brahman. The term itself appears in 1859, when Moritz Lazarus and Heymann Steinthal used "Pandeismus" in criticism of aspects of contemporary philosophy. The notion resurfaced in 20th- and 21st-century speculative theology and cosmology, including the suggestion that the Big Bang represents the transformation of the creator into the cosmos, and in Scott Adams's book God's Debris (2001), whose thought experiment — God annihilating himself in the Big Bang — is frequently cited as a modern pandeist parable.

Arguments

Advocates argue pandeism reconciles the apparent design of a created universe with the absence of ongoing divine intervention: a universe that is the residue of a creator would exhibit elegant, self-sufficient laws. Critics respond that the doctrine is empirically indistinguishable from naturalism and raises the classical problem of evil in acute form: if God became the universe, then all suffering is, in a sense, God's own — a consequence some pandeists embrace as a theodicy, holding that the creator chose to experience finitude.
