Estate tax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Estate tax (United States)

Type: Excise tax on the transfer of a decedent's estate
Levied by: Federal government; some states levy their own estate or inheritance taxes
Exemption (2024): $13.61 million per individual (scheduled to fall after 2025 absent legislation)
Top rate: 40%
Introduced: 1916 (modern federal estate tax)

An estate tax is a tax on the transfer of the estate of a deceased person. The tax applies to the total value of the decedent's assets — cash, securities, real estate, businesses, trusts, and other property — above an exemption threshold, and is paid by the estate before distribution to heirs. It is distinguished from an inheritance tax, which is levied on each beneficiary's share and paid by the recipient. Critics of the estate tax often call it a "death tax"; supporters point to its role in limiting dynastic wealth concentration.

United States federal estate tax

The modern U.S. federal estate tax was enacted in the Revenue Act of 1916. It is codified in the Internal Revenue Code and reported on Form 706. Key features include:

  • Unified credit / exemption: only estates above a large per-person exemption owe tax. The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 roughly doubled the basic exclusion to $10 million (indexed for inflation), reaching $13.61 million in 2024 — with a statutory sunset that would halve it after 2025.
  • Marital deduction: property passing to a surviving U.S.-citizen spouse is generally deductible in full, and "portability" lets a surviving spouse use a deceased spouse's unused exclusion.
  • Stepped-up basis: inherited assets generally take a basis equal to their value at death, erasing built-in capital gains.
  • Gift tax integration: lifetime taxable gifts share the same unified credit, and the generation-skipping transfer tax applies to transfers to grandchildren and beyond.

Because of the high exemption, only a small fraction of estates — roughly one in a thousand in recent years — owe any federal estate tax, and the tax raises well under 1% of federal revenue.

State and international taxes

Several U.S. states, including Massachusetts, Oregon, Washington, New York, and Illinois, levy their own estate taxes with lower exemptions, and a few (such as Pennsylvania, Nebraska, and Kentucky) impose inheritance taxes instead. Internationally, inheritance and estate taxes are common in OECD countries: Japan and South Korea have top rates above 50%, France and the United Kingdom (inheritance tax, 40% above its nil-rate band) also tax transfers at death, while countries such as Australia, Canada, and Sweden have abolished death taxes (Canada instead taxes deemed capital gains at death).

Debate

Opponents argue the tax penalises saving, threatens family farms and small businesses, and amounts to double taxation. Defenders respond that exemptions protect nearly all farms and businesses in practice, that much estate wealth consists of never-taxed capital gains, and that the tax is one of the most progressive components of the revenue system. The issue has been a recurring theme in U.S. tax politics since the phased repeal enacted in 2001 and the one-year repeal in 2010.
