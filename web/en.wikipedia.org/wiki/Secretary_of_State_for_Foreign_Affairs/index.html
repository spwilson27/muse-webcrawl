The Secretary of State for Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Affairs, commonly called the Foreign Secretary, is a senior minister of the Crown in the Government of the United Kingdom and head of the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO). Together with the Chancellor of the Exchequer and the Home Secretary, the office is one of the three Great Offices of State below the prime minister. The Foreign Secretary is responsible for British foreign policy, relations with other countries and international organisations, the overseas territories, and — since the 2020 merger with the Department for International Development — international development policy.

History

The office was created in 1782, when the secretaries of state for the Northern and Southern Departments were reorganised into the Home Office and Foreign Office; the first Foreign Secretary was Charles James Fox, who held the post only months before his death in 1806 during a later tenure. In 1968 the Foreign Office merged with the Commonwealth Office to form the Foreign and Commonwealth Office, and in 2020 the FCDO was created. Famous holders include Lord Palmerston, Lord Salisbury, Sir Edward Grey (Foreign Secretary at the outbreak of the First World War), Anthony Eden, Ernest Bevin (architect of NATO and the Marshall Plan's British counterpart), and Margaret Thatcher's foreign secretaries Lord Carrington and Geoffrey Howe.

Responsibilities and residence

The Foreign Secretary directs the UK's diplomatic service and its network of embassies and high commissions, oversees the Secret Intelligence Service (MI6) and GCHQ jointly with the prime minister's overall authority, and represents the UK in bodies such as the UN Security Council and the G7. The official London residence is 1 Carlton Gardens, and the Foreign Secretary also enjoys use of Chevening House in Kent, shared by tradition with no one else among ministers since 1981.
