Voltage scaling

Voltage scaling is a power management technique in computer architecture, where the voltage used in a component is increased or decreased, depending upon circumstances. Dynamic voltage scaling is a technique where the voltage is dynamically adjusted to increase performance (overvolting) or to conserve energy (undervolting).

FieldComputer engineering
Related conceptsDynamic frequency scaling, DVFS, CMOS power dissipation

Background

The dynamic power consumed by a CMOS chip is approximately proportional to the square of its supply voltage and to its operating frequency: P ≈ αCV²f. Because voltage enters quadratically, lowering the supply voltage is the most effective single lever for reducing dynamic power, although it also slows transistor switching and therefore limits the maximum clock frequency.

Dynamic voltage scaling

Modern processors combine voltage scaling with frequency scaling in a technique known as DVFS (dynamic voltage and frequency scaling). Operating systems select performance states (P-states) that pair a frequency with the minimum voltage needed to sustain it, trading throughput for battery life and reduced heat.

Undervolting and overvolting

Undervolting reduces the supply voltage below its nominal specification to save power and reduce heat, at some risk of instability. Overvolting raises it to support higher clock speeds in overclocking, at the cost of greater power draw, heat, and potentially reduced component lifetime due to effects such as electromigration.
