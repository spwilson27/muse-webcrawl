Johann Joseph Ignaz von Döllinger (28 February 1799 – 14 January 1890) was a German Catholic theologian and church historian, professor at the University of Munich, and the most prominent scholarly critic of the dogma of papal infallibility. His refusal to accept the decrees of the First Vatican Council led to his excommunication in 1871 and made him the intellectual leader of the Old Catholic Churches.

Life and career

Born in Bamberg, the son of a physician, Döllinger studied at Würzburg, was ordained a priest in 1822, and in 1826 became professor of church history and canon law at Munich, where he taught for nearly half a century. His early work defended the Catholic Church against Protestant critics, but his historical studies progressively distanced him from the ultramontane party that favoured maximal papal authority. He served in the Frankfurt Parliament of 1848 and later in the Bavarian legislature.

Conflict over infallibility

Döllinger's The Pope and the Council (1869), published under the pen-name Janus, argued that papal infallibility lacked support in early church history. When the First Vatican Council nevertheless defined the dogma in 1870, Döllinger refused to submit, and the Archbishop of Munich excommunicated him in April 1871. He never formally joined the Old Catholic Church that his supporters organised, but he presided over its early congresses and led the Bonn Union Conferences of 1874–75, which sought reunion with Anglicans and Orthodox Christians. Lord Acton was among his best-known students and correspondents.

Works and legacy

Döllinger wrote extensively on church history, the Reformation, and the history of moral theology. The Bavarian Academy of Sciences, of which he was president, and scholars across Europe honoured him on his ninetieth birthday, a year before his death in Munich in 1890.
