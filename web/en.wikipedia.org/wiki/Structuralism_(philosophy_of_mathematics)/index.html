Structuralism (philosophy of mathematics)

In the philosophy of mathematics, structuralism is the view that mathematical theory describes structures, and that mathematical objects are exhaustively defined in terms of their places in such structures, so that mathematical objects have no intrinsic properties, only the properties they have by virtue of their places in the structure.

Structuralism grew out of the observation that different constructions of, for example, the natural numbers — as von Neumann ordinals, as Zermelo ordinals, or as Dedekind cuts — are all equally good realizations of the same structure. What matters is not what the numbers are as sets, but the pattern of relations they stand in: the successor relation, addition, multiplication, and so on.

Varieties

Several varieties of structuralism have been distinguished:

  • Ante rem structuralism, defended by Stewart Shapiro, holds that structures exist independently of any systems that exemplify them, much as universals exist on a Platonic view.
  • In re structuralism, associated with Michael Resnik, holds that structures exist only in the systems that instantiate them.
  • Eliminative structuralism, developed by Geoffrey Hellman, treats mathematical statements as generalizations over all possible systems satisfying the relevant structural conditions, avoiding commitment to structures as objects.
  • Category-theoretic structuralism takes category theory as the foundational framework in which structures are characterized up to isomorphism.

Motivation

A central motivation is Benacerraf's identification problem: since the natural numbers can be reduced to sets in many incompatible ways, no single reduction can be the "correct" one, suggesting that numbers are not sets at all but positions in the natural-number structure. Structuralism also aims to explain the applicability of mathematics and the objectivity of mathematical knowledge without positing mysterious abstract objects with intrinsic natures.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether structuralism can account for the identity of places within a structure, whether it avoids the very ontology it seeks to replace, and whether modal versions can recover full mathematical truth. The relation between structuralism and Platonism and nominalism remains contested.
