Morion (helmet)

A morion (Spanish: morrión) is a type of open-faced combat helmet originally from the Kingdom of Castile in Spain, used from the beginning of the 16th century to the early 17th century, usually having a flat brim and a crest, or "comb", from front to back. Its introduction was contemporary with the exploration of North, Central and South America. Explorers such as Hernán Cortés and Francisco Pizarro are famously associated with the morion, though the helmets were not in widespread use among conquistadors until after the major conquests.

Design

The morion's distinctive shape — a broad, flat brim turned up at front and back into points, with a high central comb — offered protection against downward sword cuts and against falling debris, while the brim shaded the wearer's eyes and guarded against blows and projectiles from above. The comb strengthened the crown against sword strokes and gave the helmet a martial silhouette. The morion is derived from the earlier Spanish capacete and is sometimes confused with it; the related cabasset, with a teardrop-shaped crown and narrow brim, is a distinct type.

Most morions were raised from a single plate of steel, a considerable feat of armouring; two-piece riveted examples also exist. Many were richly etched, gilt, or damascened for officers and ceremonial guards, while plain "munition" morions armed pikemen and arquebusiers across Europe.

Use

The morion became the emblematic helmet of the Spanish tercios and was adopted or copied in England, France, the Italian states, and the Holy Roman Empire. It is now inseparably linked in popular culture with the Spanish conquest of the Americas and the Armada period. Surviving examples are held by the Royal Armouries, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and the Wallace Collection, among others.
