Acid rain

Forest damage attributed to acid deposition in central Europe.

Acid rain is rain or any other form of precipitation that is unusually acidic, meaning it has elevated levels of hydrogen ions (low pH). It is caused mainly by emissions of sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides (NOx), which react in the atmosphere with water and oxygen to form sulfuric and nitric acids. Most of these emissions come from burning fossil fuels in power stations, factories and vehicles.

Effects

Acid deposition acidifies lakes and streams, harming fish and aquatic life; damages forests by leaching nutrients from soil and releasing toxic aluminium; and corrodes buildings and monuments made of limestone and marble. Normal rainwater is slightly acidic (pH ≈ 5.6) because of dissolved carbon dioxide, but acid rain can reach pH 4 or lower.

History and control

The term was coined in 1872 by the Scottish chemist Robert Angus Smith. Acid rain became a major environmental issue in the 1970s and 1980s, prompting controls such as the US Clean Air Act amendments of 1990, whose cap-and-trade programme for SO2 substantially reduced emissions, and similar European protocols. Flue-gas desulfurisation ("scrubbers") and catalytic converters also reduced the pollutants responsible.
