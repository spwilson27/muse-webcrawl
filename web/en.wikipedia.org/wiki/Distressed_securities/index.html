Distressed securities

Distressed securities are securities of a company or government that is experiencing significant financial or operational difficulty, typically trading at a substantial discount to par or face value. Distressed investing is the strategy of purchasing such securities — most often corporate bonds, bank debt, or equity of companies in or near bankruptcy — in the expectation of profiting from a restructuring, recovery, or liquidation.

Definition and market

A security is often classed as distressed when it yields more than 1,000 basis points above comparable risk-free government securities, or trades below 80 percent of par; ratings agencies typically classify such issuers in the CCC range or below. The market expands during recessions and credit crises, when defaults rise.

Strategies

Distressed investors include specialist hedge funds and private equity firms. Strategies range from passive purchase of discounted bonds, to "loan-to-own" tactics in which an investor accumulates debt in order to gain control of a company through a debt-for-equity swap in restructuring. Critics sometimes label aggressive practitioners "vulture funds", particularly in the context of sovereign debt disputes, such as those involving Argentina.

Risks

Returns depend on legal and negotiated outcomes in bankruptcy proceedings, asset values in liquidation, and the seniority of the claim held. Securities are often illiquid and difficult to value, and investors may lose their entire stake if a restructuring burdens their class of claims.
