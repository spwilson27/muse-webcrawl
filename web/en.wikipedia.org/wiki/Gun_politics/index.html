Gun politics

Gun politics is an area of politics and law dealing with the regulation of civilian ownership and use of firearms. In many countries, gun politics is a polarised debate between those who advocate stricter controls and those who defend broad rights of ownership, though the specific legal frameworks, historical traditions and levels of firearm ownership vary enormously between jurisdictions.

Public debate over firearms regulation

By country

In the United States, gun politics centres on the Second Amendment to the Constitution, which protects an individual right to keep and bear arms, as affirmed by the Supreme Court in District of Columbia v. Heller (2008). Federal law sets minimum standards, while states vary widely in their licensing, carrying and weapon-type rules. Advocacy organisations on both sides, such as gun-rights groups and gun-control campaigns, are prominent political actors.

Many European countries, as well as Japan, Australia and Canada, impose licensing, registration, safe-storage requirements and restrictions on certain categories of weapons. Australia's National Firearms Agreement of 1996, enacted after the Port Arthur massacre, combined a buyback scheme with uniform licensing and is frequently cited in international debate.

Arguments

Supporters of stricter regulation argue that firearm availability is associated with higher rates of homicide, suicide and accidental injury, and point to lower firearm death rates in countries with tighter controls. Opponents argue that lawful ownership is a civil liberty, that firearms are used defensively, and that restrictions primarily burden law-abiding owners while doing little to deter determined criminals. Both sides dispute the interpretation of crime statistics and cross-national comparisons.
