The Kimura lock, also called the chicken wing or double wristlock, is a grappling submission hold that torques the opponent's shoulder joint by controlling the arm with a figure-four grip: the attacker grasps the opponent's wrist with one hand while the other arm loops over and under the opponent's arm to grab the attacker's own wrist, then rotates the trapped arm behind the opponent's back to hyperextend and internally rotate the shoulder.

Name and origin

In judo the technique is known as gyaku ude-garami ("reverse arm entanglement"). The name "Kimura" spread through Brazilian jiu-jitsu after the famous 1951 match in Rio de Janeiro in which judoka Masahiko Kimura defeated Hélio Gracie with the hold, breaking Gracie's arm when he refused to submit. The Gracie family adopted the technique's name in Kimura's honour. In catch wrestling and professional wrestling the same hold is called the double wristlock or chicken wing.

Application

The Kimura is applied from guard, half guard, side control, north–south and standing positions, and in modern grappling and mixed martial arts it also serves as a control and sweeping system ("Kimura trap") leading to back takes and armbars. Defences focus on gripping one's own belt, thigh or hands to prevent the arm being rotated, straightening the arm, or rolling with the pressure. Because it attacks the shoulder with great leverage, it can cause serious injury if not released promptly after a tap.
