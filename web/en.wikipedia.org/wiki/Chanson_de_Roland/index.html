The Song of Roland

La Chanson de Roland (The Song of Roland) is an 11th-century chanson de geste, the oldest surviving major work of French literature. It dramatises the Battle of Roncevaux Pass in 778, in which the rearguard of Charlemagne's army, commanded by Roland, was destroyed — transforming a historical ambush by Basque raiders into a heroic last stand against a vast Muslim host.

Synopsis

After seven years of war in Spain, Charlemagne receives a treacherous peace offer from the Muslim king Marsile of Saragossa. Roland nominates his stepfather Ganelon as envoy; Ganelon, enraged, betrays the Franks by revealing how to ambush the rearguard. Roland, commanding the rearguard with his friend Oliver and Archbishop Turpin, refuses out of pride to blow the olifant horn for help until the battle is lost. He dies facing the enemy after shattering his sword Durendal against a rock to keep it from capture. Charlemagne avenges him, and Ganelon is tried and executed.

Historical basis

The historical Roland was prefect of the Breton March, killed in 778 when Basques ambushed Charlemagne's baggage train in the Pyrenees, as recorded by Einhard. Over three centuries of oral tradition, the Basques became Saracens, and Roland became the model of the loyal, self-sacrificing knight.

Significance

The poem is the archetype of the chanson de geste and a cornerstone of the Matter of France. Its ideals of feudal loyalty, Christian warfare and heroic death shaped medieval chivalric culture, and it remains a national epic of France.
