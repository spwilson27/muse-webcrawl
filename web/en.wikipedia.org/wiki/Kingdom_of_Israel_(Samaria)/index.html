The Kingdom of Israel (Hebrew: מַמְלֶכֶת יִשְׂרָאֵל), also called the Northern Kingdom or the Kingdom of Samaria, was an Israelite kingdom of the Southern Levant during the Iron Age. According to the Hebrew Bible it split from the United Monarchy around 930 BC, when the ten northern tribes rejected Solomon's son Rehoboam and made Jeroboam their king, leaving the Kingdom of Judah in the south. It endured for about two centuries until its conquest by Assyria in 722 BC.

History

The kingdom's early capitals were Shechem and then Tirzah. The dynasty founded by Omri (r. c. 885–874 BC) gave the kingdom its lasting capital at Samaria, built on a commanding hill, and Assyrian records long called Israel "the House of Omri". Omri's son Ahab fought the Arameans, appeared at the Battle of Qarqar (853 BC) against Shalmaneser III — the first recorded contact between Israel and Assyria — and, with his wife Jezebel, was denounced by the prophets Elijah and Elisha for promoting the cult of Baal.

The coup of Jehu (841 BC) installed a dynasty that lasted a century; under Jeroboam II (r. c. 793–753 BC) Israel recovered territory and prosperity, denounced by the prophets Amos and Hosea for social injustice. After Jeroboam's death the kingdom fell into anarchy and became an Assyrian vassal. The last king, Hoshea, rebelled and withheld tribute; Shalmaneser V besieged Samaria, and in 722/720 BC Sargon II captured the city, deporting — by his account — 27,290 inhabitants, including much of the elite, to Mesopotamia and Media. These deportees are the origin of the legend of the Ten Lost Tribes.

Archaeology and sources

Besides the biblical books of Kings and the northern prophets, the kingdom is documented in Assyrian royal inscriptions and monuments such as the Black Obelisk (depicting Jehu's tribute) and the Mesha Stele from Moab. Excavations at Samaria, Megiddo, Hazor and Dan have uncovered its palaces, fortifications and the famous Samaria ivories.
