Urushibara nickel is a nickel-based hydrogenation catalyst developed by the Japanese chemist Yoshiyuki Urushibara in the 1950s. It is prepared by adding zinc (or aluminium) dust to an aqueous solution of nickel chloride, which precipitates nickel onto the metal; subsequent treatment with acid or alkali removes residual zinc and activates the catalyst. The resulting finely divided nickel is used as an economical substitute for Raney nickel in many hydrogenation reactions.

Preparation and varieties

Two classic variants are described. U-Ni-A is prepared by digesting the precipitated nickel–zinc material with aqueous sodium hydroxide, which dissolves excess zinc and zinc oxide. U-Ni-B is instead washed with acetic acid (or dilute acid), giving a catalyst of different activity and selectivity. Because the catalyst is prepared in situ and used as an aqueous or alcoholic slurry, it avoids some of the handling hazards of dry pyrophoric Raney nickel, though wet Urushibara nickel can also ignite when allowed to dry in air.

Applications

Urushibara nickel catalyses the hydrogenation of carbon–carbon double and triple bonds, and has been applied to the reduction of oximes, nitriles and other functional groups, as well as to desulfurisation-type transformations. Its activity is often comparable to that of Raney nickel, and in some semi-hydrogenations of alkynes its selectivity can be tuned by the choice of digestion conditions and added modifiers (poisons) such as amines or lead salts, analogous to the Lindlar catalyst.
