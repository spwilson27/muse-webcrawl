Hadalpelagic zone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The hadalpelagic zone is the deepest layer of the ocean.

The hadalpelagic zone is the deepest region of the ocean, lying within oceanic trenches below about 6,000 metres (20,000 ft) and extending to the bottom of the deepest trench, the Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench at nearly 11,000 metres (36,000 ft). The name derives from Hades, the ancient Greek god of the underworld. It lies below the abyssopelagic zone and constitutes the least explored environment on Earth.

Conditions

The hadal zone is characterized by complete darkness, near-freezing temperatures (1–4 °C), and crushing hydrostatic pressure exceeding 1,000 atmospheres at full depth. It is confined to the world's 27 or so deep-sea trenches, which together amount to only 1–2% of the ocean floor area, mostly along subduction zones in the Pacific. Despite the pressure, trenches are not sterile: they act as traps for sinking organic matter, which can support surprisingly rich communities.

Life

Hadal fauna includes amphipods (such as the supergiant Alicella gigantea), isopods, holothurians (sea cucumbers), bivalves, and snailfish — the Mariana snailfish (Pseudoliparis swirei) is among the deepest-living fish, found below 8,000 m. Xenophyophores (giant single-celled organisms) dot the trench floor. Animals are adapted to pressure at the molecular level, e.g., by accumulating trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) to stabilize proteins; fish are not found below roughly 8,400 m, plausibly due to osmotic limits.

Exploration

The zone was first systematically sampled by the Danish Galathea II expedition (1950–52), which hauled animals from over 10,000 m in the Philippine Trench, proving life existed at full ocean depth. In 1960 Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh reached the Challenger Deep in the bathyscaphe Trieste; subsequent visits include James Cameron's solo dive in Deepsea Challenger (2012) and Victor Vescovo's repeated dives in Limiting Factor (2019). Modern research uses free-falling "lander" instruments and full-ocean-depth submersibles. Pollution, including microplastics and persistent organic pollutants, has been documented even here.
