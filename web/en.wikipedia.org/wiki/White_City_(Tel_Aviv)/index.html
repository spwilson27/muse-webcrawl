The White City (Hebrew: העיר הלבנה, Ha-Ir ha-Levana) is a collection of over 4,000 buildings in Tel Aviv, Israel, built in the Bauhaus or International Style during the 1930s and 1940s, the largest such concentration in the world. In 2003 UNESCO proclaimed the White City a World Cultural Heritage Site, describing it as "an outstanding example of new town planning and architecture in the early 20th century".

History

The buildings were designed largely by Jewish architects who had immigrated from Germany and elsewhere in Europe during the British Mandate period, bringing the modernist ideas of the Bauhaus school (closed by the Nazis in 1933). They adapted the International Style to local conditions: white plaster walls to reflect the Mediterranean sun, long ribbon balconies providing shade, pilotis lifting buildings above gardens, flat roofs, and "thermometer" stairwell windows. The town plan itself followed Patrick Geddes's garden-city master plan of 1925–29.

Preservation

Neglected for decades and threatened by demolition, the area was protected by municipal preservation plans from the 1990s onward, culminating in the UNESCO listing. Centred on Rothschild Boulevard, Bialik Street, and Dizengoff Square, the White City remains a working residential district; preservation rules complicate renovation, and the Bauhaus Center Tel Aviv conducts tours and exhibitions on the architecture.
