The menorah (Hebrew: מְנוֹרָה, menōrā) is a seven-branched ancient Hebrew lampstand made of pure gold and used in the Tabernacle and later in the Temple in Jerusalem. Described in detail in the Book of Exodus, it is one of the oldest symbols of Judaism and of the Jewish people; today it appears in the emblem of the State of Israel. It is distinct from the nine-branched menorah (hanukkiah) used at Hanukkah.

Biblical account

According to Exodus 25, God showed Moses a pattern for the menorah on Mount Sinai: it was to be hammered from a single talent of pure gold, with a central shaft and six branches — three on each side — decorated with cups shaped like almond blossoms, and seven lamps kept burning with pure olive oil. Aaron and his sons, the priests, were charged with tending it in the sanctuary from evening to morning.

History

The menorah stood in Solomon's Temple and, after the return from Babylonian exile, in the Second Temple. When the Maccabees rededicated the Temple in 164 BC — the event commemorated by Hanukkah — tradition holds that a single cruse of undefiled oil burned miraculously for eight days. After the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, the menorah was carried to Rome in Titus's triumph; a famous relief on the Arch of Titus (c. AD 81) depicts it in the triumphal procession, carried on the shoulders of captives. Its later fate is unknown; legends place it in Rome, Constantinople, or lost at sea. A large bronze menorah modelled on the Arch of Titus relief stands outside the Knesset in Jerusalem, a gift from Britain (1956).

Symbolism

Interpretations of the seven branches have included the seven days of creation, the sun, moon and five visible planets of antiquity, and the seven continents or seven branches of knowledge. The first-century historian Josephus wrote that the seven lamps signified the planets. In modern Israel the menorah is the centrepiece of the national emblem, flanked by olive branches.
