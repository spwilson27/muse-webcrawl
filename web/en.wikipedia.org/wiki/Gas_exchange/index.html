Gas exchange

Gas exchange is the physical process by which gases move passively by diffusion across a surface. In biology it is the process in which gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide move between an organism and its environment, and between the blood and tissues. Gas exchange takes place across specialised respiratory surfaces including the alveoli of lungs, the gills of fish, and the skin of amphibians, and it is essential for cellular respiration.

Principles

Diffusion is fastest across surfaces that are large in area, thin, moist, and supplied with a steep concentration gradient — properties shared by effective respiratory surfaces. Ventilation maintains the gradient by bringing in fresh air or water, while blood flow removes oxygenated blood and delivers carbon-dioxide-rich blood. Fick's law states that the rate of diffusion is proportional to surface area and the difference in partial pressures, and inversely proportional to the thickness of the barrier.

In humans

In the human lungs, gas exchange occurs in roughly 300 million alveoli, providing a surface area of about 70 square metres. Oxygen from inhaled air diffuses across the alveolar–capillary membrane into the blood, where most is carried by haemoglobin in red blood cells. Carbon dioxide, produced by the tissues, diffuses in the opposite direction and is exhaled. Exchange in the tissues ("internal respiration") is the reverse: oxygen leaves the blood and carbon dioxide enters it.

In other organisms

Fish extract oxygen from water using gills, where countercurrent flow between water and blood maximises uptake. Insects deliver air directly to tissues through a network of tracheae opening at spiracles. Many amphibians exchange gases across moist skin as well as lungs. In plants, gas exchange for photosynthesis and respiration occurs through stomata in leaves, whose opening is regulated by guard cells.

Disorders

Impaired gas exchange results from diseases that thicken the diffusion barrier (such as pulmonary fibrosis or oedema), reduce surface area (emphysema), or mismatch ventilation and perfusion, leading to hypoxaemia and, in severe cases, respiratory failure.
