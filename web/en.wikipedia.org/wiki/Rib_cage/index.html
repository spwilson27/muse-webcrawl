Rib cage

Part ofAxial skeleton
SystemSkeletal system
ArticulationsThoracic vertebrae, sternum (via costal cartilages)
IdentifiersLatin: cavea thoracis

The rib cage, also known as the thoracic cage, is an endoskeletal enclosure in the thorax of most vertebrates that consists of the ribs, thoracic vertebrae and sternum. In humans it is composed of 12 pairs of ribs and their associated costal cartilages, the sternum, and the 12 thoracic vertebrae. Together with the skin, associated fascia and muscles, it forms the thoracic wall.

Structure

The ribs are classified into three groups based on their anterior attachments:

  • True ribs (pairs 1–7), whose costal cartilages attach directly to the sternum.
  • False ribs (pairs 8–10), whose cartilages join the cartilage of the rib above rather than the sternum itself.
  • Floating ribs (pairs 11–12), which have no anterior attachment and end freely in the abdominal wall musculature.

Posteriorly, each rib articulates with the thoracic vertebrae at the costovertebral joints. The spaces between adjacent ribs, the intercostal spaces, contain the intercostal muscles, nerves and vessels, which assist in breathing.

Function

The rib cage protects the vital organs of the thorax — principally the heart and lungs — and, to a lesser extent, organs of the upper abdomen such as the liver and spleen. Its elastic, semi-rigid structure also enables respiration: movements of the ribs, driven by the intercostal muscles and the diaphragm, change the volume of the thoracic cavity, drawing air into and expelling it from the lungs. The upper ribs also provide attachment for muscles of the shoulder girdle and upper limb.

Development and variation

The ribs develop from the costal processes of the thoracic vertebrae during embryogenesis. Variations are common: about one in two hundred people has a cervical rib arising from the seventh cervical vertebra, and some individuals have 11 or 13 pairs of ribs rather than 12.

Clinical significance

Rib fractures are among the most common bone injuries, usually resulting from trauma; a "flail chest" occurs when several adjacent ribs are broken in two places, compromising ventilation. The first rib is protected by the clavicle and rarely fractured, but when broken it signals severe trauma. Costochondritis — inflammation of the costal cartilages — is a common cause of chest-wall pain. Surgical access to the thoracic organs may require removal or division of ribs.

In other animals

Rib cages occur throughout the tetrapod vertebrates and vary widely in form: snakes have hundreds of rib pairs extending along most of the body, while turtles have ribs fused to the carapace. In birds, the ribs bear uncinate processes that brace the thorax for flight.
