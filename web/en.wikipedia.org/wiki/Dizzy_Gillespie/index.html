John Birks "Dizzy" Gillespie (21 October 1917 – 6 January 1993) was an American jazz trumpeter, bandleader, composer and singer. With Charlie Parker, he was a central figure in the development of bebop and modern jazz in the 1940s, and he later pioneered Afro-Cuban jazz. He was instantly recognisable by his trumpet with its bell bent upward at a 45-degree angle — the result of an accident at a 1953 party that he discovered improved the sound — his ballooning, scarred cheeks when playing, his beret and horn-rimmed glasses, and his exuberant showmanship and scat singing.

Early life and career

Born the youngest of nine children in Cheraw, South Carolina, Gillespie taught himself trombone and then trumpet, won a scholarship to the Laurinburg Institute in North Carolina, and moved to Philadelphia in 1935. He joined Frankie Fairfax's band, where he acquired the nickname "Dizzy" for his clowning, then moved to New York and played with Teddy Hill, in whose band he deputised for his idol Roy Eldridge, and Cab Calloway, who fired him in 1941 after a spitball incident the two men remembered differently.

Bebop

In the early 1940s, after-hours sessions at Minton's Playhouse and Monroe's Uptown House in Harlem brought Gillespie together with Charlie Parker, Thelonious Monk, Kenny Clarke and others experimenting with fast tempos, complex harmonies and virtuosic improvisation. With Parker he recorded bebop landmarks such as "Groovin' High," "Salt Peanuts," "A Night in Tunisia" and "Ko-Ko" in 1945. His big bands of the late 1940s carried bebop to wider audiences despite commercial headwinds.

Afro-Cuban jazz and later years

In 1947 Gillespie hired the Cuban conguero Chano Pozo, with whom he created "Manteca" and "Tin Tin Deo," fusing bebop with Afro-Cuban rhythms. Pozo was murdered in 1948, but the fusion endured as a permanent stream in jazz. In 1956 Gillespie led a State Department tour of the Middle East, becoming a celebrated musical ambassador; he later ran a tongue-in-cheek campaign for President in 1964, promising to rename the White House the Blues House. A convert to the Baháʼí Faith in 1968, he remained an active performer and mentor into his seventies. He died of pancreatic cancer in 1993 and was honoured with a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award and induction into the Big Band and Jazz Hall of Fame.
