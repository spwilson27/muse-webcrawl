Prehistoric Britain covers the period from the earliest evidence of human occupation of what is now Great Britain — roughly 900,000 years ago — until the Roman invasion of AD 43, after which written history begins. During this long span Britain was repeatedly colonised and abandoned as Ice Age glaciers advanced and retreated; for most of it, Britain was a peninsula of continental Europe, joined to the mainland by the now-submerged landscape of Doggerland, which was finally inundated by rising sea levels around 6500–6200 BC.

Palaeolithic

The earliest evidence of human presence comes from Happisburgh in Norfolk, where flint tools and fossilised footprints dated to around 900,000–850,000 years ago indicate visits by Homo antecessor or a related species. At Boxgrove in Sussex, remains of Homo heidelbergensis date to about 500,000 years ago. Neanderthals occupied Britain intermittently between about 400,000 and 40,000 years ago; anatomically modern humans arrived around 45,000 years ago. The celebrated "Red Lady of Paviland" (actually a young man) in Wales, dated to about 33,000 years ago, is among the earliest ceremonial burials in Europe. Cave art at Creswell Crags and the Cheddar Gorge skeleton known as Cheddar Man (c. 7100 BC, Mesolithic) are among later landmarks of the period.

Mesolithic

After the end of the last Ice Age around 9700 BC, Mesolithic hunter-gatherers recolonised Britain. The site of Star Carr in Yorkshire (c. 9000 BC) preserves some of the earliest evidence of carpentry and ritual headdresses made from red deer skulls. Rising seas cut Britain off from the continent; the Storegga Slide tsunami (c. 6200 BC) may have hastened the flooding of Doggerland.

Neolithic

Farming arrived around 4000 BC, probably through migration from continental Europe — ancient DNA indicates that Britain's Neolithic farmers were largely descended from Anatolian populations who travelled via the Mediterranean. Monument building flourished: causewayed enclosures, chambered tombs, cursus monuments and stone circles. Stonehenge was begun around 3000 BC, and Avebury contains the largest prehistoric stone circle in the world. The remarkably preserved village of Skara Brae in Orkney dates to about 3180 BC. Around 2450 BC the arrival of the Beaker culture brought substantial genetic change, with steppe-related ancestry largely replacing earlier Neolithic ancestry within a few centuries.

Bronze Age

The Bronze Age (c. 2500–800 BC) saw the spread of metalworking, initially copper and gold and later tin-bronze, exploiting Cornwall's tin deposits. Round barrows, stone circles and field systems multiplied; individual burials with rich grave goods, such as those of the Wessex culture, suggest growing social hierarchy. The Dover Bronze Age boat (c. 1500 BC) attests to cross-Channel trade.

Iron Age

From about 800 BC iron technology spread and Celtic languages and culture became established. Britain was divided among numerous tribal kingdoms — known from coins, classical sources and archaeology — including the Catuvellauni, Iceni, Brigantes and Atrebates. Hillforts such as Maiden Castle dominated the landscape. Julius Caesar's expeditions of 55 and 54 BC brought Britain into closer contact with the Roman world, and trade intensified over the following century. The period ends with Claudius's invasion of AD 43.
