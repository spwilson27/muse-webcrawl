Ground rent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ground rent is a regular payment required under a leasehold arrangement, in which a leaseholder pays rent to the freeholder (landowner) for the use of the land on which a building stands. It arises where land is let on a long lease — commonly 99, 125, or 999 years — while ownership of the land itself remains with the freeholder.

Nature and terms

Traditionally, ground rents were set at a nominal or "peppercorn" level, or at a modest fixed sum. Some leases provide for the rent to double or to be reviewed at intervals, which can produce substantial increases over a long term. Ground rent is distinct from a service charge, which covers the cost of maintaining common parts and services; it is payment for the land itself and no service need be provided in return for it.

Reform

In England and Wales, the sale of new leasehold houses and escalating ground rents became a public controversy in the 2010s. The Leasehold Reform (Ground Rent) Act 2022 restricted ground rent on most new long residential leases to a peppercorn (zero financial value). In the United States, ground rent systems survive notably in Baltimore, Maryland, and in Hawaii, where leasehold land tenure has deep historical roots. In the Netherlands, the related erfpacht system lets municipalities retain land ownership and charge a canon (ground rent) on long leases.

Economic theory

Economists since David Ricardo and Henry George have analysed rent on land — the surplus attributable to the land itself rather than to improvements — and proposals for land value taxation draw on the idea that ground rent is an unearned increment that may be taxed without distorting production.
