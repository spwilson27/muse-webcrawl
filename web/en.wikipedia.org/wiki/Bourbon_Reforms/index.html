Bourbon Reforms

The Bourbon Reforms were a series of political, economic, fiscal and military measures introduced across the Spanish Empire during the eighteenth century under the Bourbon monarchs, especially Charles III (reigned 1759–1788). Modelled in part on French administrative practice, the reforms aimed to modernise and centralise imperial government, increase revenue, revive trade, and strengthen the empire's defences in the face of British and Portuguese rivalry.

Measures

Key administrative changes included the creation of new viceroyalties — New Granada (1717, permanently restored 1739) and the Río de la Plata (1776) — and the introduction of French-style intendancies in Spain and the Americas, replacing many of the older corregidores. Peninsular Spaniards were favoured over American-born Creoles in appointments. Economically, the Casa de Contratación's monopoly system was loosened: the 1778 Reglamento de Libre Comercio opened trade among most Spanish and American ports, customs were reformed, royal monopolies (estancos) were extended to tobacco and other goods, and taxes such as the alcabala were raised and more rigorously collected. The Crown also expelled the Jesuits from the empire in 1767, seizing their missions and properties.

Effects and legacy

The reforms increased royal revenue and stimulated commerce and population growth, but they also burdened colonial populations with heavier taxation and displaced Creole elites from positions of influence. Historians commonly regard the resentments they generated, together with the political collapse of the Spanish monarchy in 1808, as among the long-term causes of the Spanish American wars of independence.
