Charles I of Naples

Charles I (early 1226/1227 – 7 January 1285), commonly known as Charles of Anjou, was a French prince, the youngest son of King Louis VIII of France and brother of Louis IX. He was Count of Provence and of Anjou and Maine, and became the first Angevin king of Sicily and Naples after conquering the Hohenstaufen kingdom in 1266. His ambition to build a Mediterranean empire made him one of the most powerful rulers of thirteenth-century Europe, but his harsh rule provoked the Sicilian Vespers revolt of 1282, which cost him the island of Sicily.

Rise

Through his marriage to Beatrice of Provence in 1246 Charles inherited the county of Provence, and his brother invested him with Anjou and Maine. He accompanied Louis IX on the Seventh Crusade to Egypt in 1248. Ambitious for a crown, he accepted the papacy's offer of the Kingdom of Sicily, then ruled by the Hohenstaufen. Crowned in Rome in January 1266, he defeated and killed Manfred of Sicily at Benevento (1266) and defeated the last Hohenstaufen claimant, Conradin, at Tagliacozzo (1268), afterwards having the sixteen-year-old prince executed.

Mediterranean ambitions

Charles assembled a sprawling sphere of influence: he acquired claims in the Latin East, became King of Albania, bought the title King of Jerusalem in 1277, and by the Treaty of Viterbo (1267) prepared a campaign to conquer the Byzantine Empire and restore Latin rule in Constantinople. His domination of Italy alarmed both the papacy's rivals and his own subjects; heavy taxation and the behaviour of his French officials fuelled resentment, especially in Sicily.

Sicilian Vespers and death

On Easter Monday, 30 March 1282, an incident at vespers outside Palermo ignited a general massacre of the French on the island. The rebels offered the crown to Peter III of Aragon, and the ensuing War of the Sicilian Vespers shattered Charles's plans: the crusade against Constantinople was abandoned, his fleet was repeatedly beaten by Roger of Lauria, and his son and heir was captured in 1284. Charles died at Foggia on 7 January 1285, retaining only the mainland kingdom — thereafter called Naples — while Sicily passed to the house of Aragon.
