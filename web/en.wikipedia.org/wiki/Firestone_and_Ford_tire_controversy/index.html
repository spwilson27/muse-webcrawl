The Firestone and Ford tire controversy was a major product-safety crisis of 2000–2001 involving tread separation failures of Firestone tires fitted as original equipment on the Ford Explorer sport utility vehicle. When the tires failed at speed, the tread could peel off, causing drivers to lose control; the Explorer's high centre of gravity made rollovers — often fatal — more likely. The affair led to one of the largest tire recalls in history, congressional hearings, the end of the nearly century-old business relationship between Ford and Firestone, and new U.S. legislation.

Background

Firestone had supplied Ford since 1906, and the two companies' founding families were linked by marriage. The Explorer, launched in 1990, became the best-selling SUV in the United States. From the mid-1990s, reports accumulated of Firestone ATX and Wilderness AT tires — especially those made at Firestone's Decatur, Illinois plant — suffering tread separations, initially most visibly in hot climates and in Venezuela and Saudi Arabia, where Ford and Firestone quietly replaced tires before U.S. regulators acted.

Recall and investigation

In May 2000 the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) opened an investigation. On August 9, 2000, Bridgestone/Firestone announced a recall of 6.5 million ATX, ATX II and Wilderness AT tires. Ford later unilaterally replaced an additional 13 million Wilderness AT tires in 2001 at a cost of about US$3 billion, a move Firestone criticised as shifting blame. In total some 14.4 million tires were taken out of service. NHTSA ultimately linked the defects to 271 deaths and over 800 injuries in the United States.

Dispute over blame

Firestone argued the Explorer's design and Ford's recommended inflation pressure of 26 psi — lower than Firestone's suggested 30 psi — contributed to failures. Ford maintained the tires were defective regardless of vehicle. Congressional hearings in September 2000 aired evidence that both companies had early knowledge of failures abroad. In May 2001 Bridgestone/Firestone announced it would end its supply relationship with Ford, closing a 95-year partnership.

Consequences

The controversy prompted the U.S. Transportation Recall Enhancement, Accountability, and Documentation (TREAD) Act of 2000, which increased reporting requirements and penalties and mandated tire-pressure monitoring systems on new vehicles. Firestone paid substantial settlements and its CEO resigned; Ford's CEO Jacques Nasser departed in 2001 amid the crisis's costs and fallout. The Explorer was redesigned with a wider track and, later, electronic stability control.
