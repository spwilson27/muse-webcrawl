Self-regulation

Self-regulation is the ability to manage one's behaviour, emotions, thoughts, and impulses in the pursuit of long-term goals. More precisely, it is the capacity to alter one's responses — to override impulses, delay gratification, sustain attention, and persist at tasks — in line with standards such as ideals, values, social expectations, and plans.

Psychological theory

Self-regulation is usually modelled as a feedback process: a person monitors their current state, compares it with a standard, and acts to reduce any discrepancy (the TOTE and cybernetic traditions, formalised by Carver and Scheier). Roy Baumeister's strength model likened self-control to a muscle that can be fatigued — the controversial "ego depletion" hypothesis — and trained, though large replication attempts in the 2010s cast doubt on the size and robustness of depletion effects, prompting revised accounts in terms of motivation and attention rather than a literal resource. Self-regulation develops through childhood with the maturation of prefrontal executive functions, and deficits are implicated in ADHD, addiction, obesity, and conduct problems. Related concepts include self-control, emotion regulation, willpower, executive function, and grit.

Other uses

Outside psychology, self-regulation denotes the governance of an industry or profession by its own members rather than by the state — for example press self-regulation through professional codes and councils, or advertising standards bodies. Such arrangements are praised for flexibility and expertise and criticised for weak sanctions and conflicts of interest, and are often compared with statutory regulation or co-regulation. In biology and systems theory, self-regulation refers to homeostatic feedback processes that keep a system within viable bounds.
