Situational irony

Situational irony is a figure of speech and a literary device in which the outcome of a situation is strikingly different from — often the opposite of — what was expected. A situation is often described as situationally ironic when actions produce an effect exactly contrary to what was intended, so that the result appears as a kind of mockery of human plans. It is one of the three main types of irony, alongside verbal irony (saying the opposite of what one means) and dramatic irony (in which the audience knows more than the characters).

Characteristics

Situational irony typically involves a sharp incongruity between expectation and reality: a fire station burning down, a traffic enforcement officer receiving a parking ticket, or a marriage counsellor filing for divorce. Literary examples include O. Henry's "The Gift of the Magi", in which a husband sells his watch to buy combs for his wife's hair while she sells her hair to buy a chain for his watch. In common usage "irony" is sometimes loosely applied to mere coincidence or bad luck, a usage that usage writers frequently dispute; the key element is the thwarted expectation or reversed outcome, not simple misfortune.

Related concepts

Situational irony overlaps with poetic justice, in which virtue is rewarded and vice punished with fitting reversal, and with peripeteia, the sudden reversal of fortune in classical drama. It is also distinct from cosmic irony, in which fate or the gods are imagined as deliberately frustrating human intentions.
