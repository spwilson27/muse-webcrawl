Spanish Netherlands

The Spanish Netherlands (Spanish: Países Bajos Españoles; Dutch: Spaanse Nederlanden) were the Habsburg Netherlands ruled by the Spanish branch of the Habsburgs from 1556 to 1714. They initially comprised the Seventeen Provinces — roughly modern Belgium, Luxembourg, the Netherlands and parts of northern France. After the Dutch Revolt, the northern provinces seceded as the Dutch Republic (recognised in 1648), and the Spanish Netherlands thereafter denoted the Catholic south, approximately present-day Belgium and Luxembourg.

History

The provinces passed to Philip II of Spain on the abdication of his father Charles V in 1556. Religious persecution, taxation and centralisation provoked the Dutch Revolt from 1568, opening the Eighty Years' War. The Duke of Alba's "Council of Troubles", the Spanish Fury at Antwerp (1576), and Alexander Farnese's campaigns recovered the south for Spain, while the Union of Utrecht (1579) consolidated the rebellious north. Under the Archdukes Albert and Isabella (1598–1621) the south enjoyed a measure of autonomy and a Catholic cultural flowering associated with Rubens; the Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621) paused the war.

Thereafter the territory became a battleground in Spain's wars with France, losing Artois and parts of Flanders and Hainaut. By the Treaties of Utrecht and Rastatt (1713–14), following the War of the Spanish Succession, the Spanish Netherlands passed to the Austrian Habsburgs, becoming the Austrian Netherlands.
