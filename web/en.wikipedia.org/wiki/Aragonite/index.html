Aragonite

Aragonite

Formula: CaCO3

Crystal system: Orthorhombic

Hardness: 3.5–4 (Mohs)

Named for: Molina de Aragón, Spain

Aragonite is a carbonate mineral with the chemical formula CaCO3, one of the three naturally occurring crystalline forms of calcium carbonate, the others being calcite and vaterite. It is named after Molina de Aragón in Spain, near where it was first described in 1797. Although chemically identical to calcite, aragonite crystallises in the orthorhombic system and is slightly harder and denser; at surface conditions it is metastable and slowly converts to calcite over geological time.

Occurrence

Aragonite forms in marine and cave environments, in hot springs, and in the metamorphic blueschist rocks where high pressures stabilise it. It commonly grows as acicular (needle-like) crystals, columnar aggregates, and the stalactitic "flos-ferri" of iron mines. Needle-mud sediments of aragonite form the famous oolitic shoals of the Bahamas.

Biological role

Aragonite is the mineral of most mollusc shells and of the skeletons of reef-building corals; the iridescent nacre (mother of pearl) lining many shells is a composite of aragonite platelets and protein. Fish otoliths — the ear stones used by scientists to age fish — are also aragonite. Because aragonite is more soluble than calcite, organisms that build with it are considered especially vulnerable to ocean acidification.
