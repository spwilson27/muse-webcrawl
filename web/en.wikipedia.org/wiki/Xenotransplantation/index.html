Xenotransplantation

Xenotransplantation is the transplantation of living cells, tissues or organs from one species to another. Such grafts are called xenografts or xenotransplants, in contrast to allografts (between members of the same species). Because of the severe shortage of human donor organs, research has focused on pigs as source animals, whose organs are similar in size and physiology to humans.

Barriers

The principal obstacle is rejection. Hyperacute rejection occurs within minutes when naturally occurring human antibodies bind the alpha-gal sugar on pig endothelium, activating complement and destroying the graft. Genetic engineering has produced pigs lacking the alpha-gal epitope (GGTA1 knockouts) and expressing human complement-regulatory and anticoagulant proteins, along with many additional edits. Other concerns include physiological incompatibilities, organ growth, and the risk of transmitting porcine viruses — notably porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERVs) — to recipients and potentially the wider population, which has prompted calls for strict monitoring and regulation.

History and milestones

Early attempts included chimpanzee kidney grafts in the 1960s and the 1984 "Baby Fae" case, in which an infant received a baboon heart and survived 21 days. In 2021, surgeons at NYU attached a genetically modified pig kidney to a brain-dead human recipient, where it functioned for several days. In January 2022, David Bennett at the University of Maryland received the first genetically modified pig heart, surviving two months. In March 2024, Richard Slayman received the first pig kidney transplant in a living patient at Massachusetts General Hospital. Regulators such as the FDA have authorised these procedures case-by-case under expanded access, and formal clinical trials have since been approved.

Ethics

Xenotransplantation raises ethical debate about animal welfare and the breeding of pigs as organ sources, informed consent for recipients facing lifelong surveillance, equitable access, and the societal risk of cross-species infection.
