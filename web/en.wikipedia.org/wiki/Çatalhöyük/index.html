Çatalhöyük

LocationKonya Plain, Central Anatolia, Turkey
PeriodNeolithic, c. 7500 – 6400 BCE
Discovered1958 (James Mellaart)
Excavations1961–1965 (Mellaart); 1993–2017 (Ian Hodder)
StatusUNESCO World Heritage Site (2012)

Çatalhöyük (Turkish pronunciation: [tʃaˈtaɫhœjyc], also Çatal Höyük and Çatal Hüyük; from Turkish çatal "fork" + höyük "tumulus") was a very large Neolithic and Chalcolithic proto-city settlement in southern Anatolia, which existed from approximately 7500 BCE to 6400 BCE, and flourished around 7000 BCE. In July 2012 it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Çatalhöyük is one of the earliest known large settled communities and a key site for understanding the transition to farming life.

The settlement covered about 13 hectares and may have housed between 3,500 and 8,000 people at its peak. Its most striking feature is its layout: mudbrick houses were packed tightly together with no streets, and inhabitants moved across rooftops and entered their homes through openings in the roof by ladder. Houses were repeatedly rebuilt on the same footprint, producing up to 18 occupation layers, and the dead were buried beneath the floors, sometimes with crania removed, plastered and painted.

Art and economy

Çatalhöyük is famous for its symbolic art: wall paintings (including what may be the earliest map or landscape), reliefs, bull heads (bucrania) installed on walls and benches, and figurines, among them the well-known seated woman flanked by two leopards, sometimes interpreted as a mother goddess — an interpretation the later excavation director, Ian Hodder, treated with caution. The inhabitants practised farming (emmer and einkorn wheat, barley, lentils, sheep and goats) alongside hunting of wild cattle, and traded in obsidian from Cappadocian sources.

Excavation

The site was identified in 1958 by James Mellaart, who excavated it from 1961 to 1965. From 1993 to 2017 an international project directed by Ian Hodder applied reflexive, multi-disciplinary methods, examining questions of household organisation, ritual and social equality; the evidence suggests a remarkably egalitarian society with little hierarchy.
