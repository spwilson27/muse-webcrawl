The Nordic Bronze Age (also Northern Bronze Age, or Scandinavian Bronze Age) is a period of Scandinavian prehistory from c. 1750 to 500 BC. It succeeded the Nordic Stone Age and was followed by the Pre-Roman Iron Age. Despite the name, bronze was imported — Scandinavia has no native tin and little copper — which made the culture dependent on long-distance trade with the bronze-producing regions of central Europe, exchanged for goods such as amber.

Culture

The period is renowned for its richly furnished burials in oak coffins within great mound barrows, most famously the Egtved Girl and the other well-preserved Danish coffins preserved by waterlogged conditions. Weapons, razors, tweezers and ornaments show skilled local bronze casting, typically using imported metal. Large bronze lurs (ceremonial trumpets), the golden Trundholm sun chariot, and rock carvings (petroglyphs) depicting ships, sun wheels, weapons and processions give evidence of a solar religion.

Periodisation

The Danish archaeologist Oscar Montelius divided the Nordic Bronze Age into six periods (I–VI) based on typological changes in artefacts, a scheme still in use. Periods I–III form the Early Bronze Age and IV–VI the Late Bronze Age, during which cremation largely replaced inhumation.

Climate and decline

The period enjoyed a climate warmer than today's, supporting a relatively dense population. From around 500 BC the climate deteriorated and iron — locally available from bog iron — replaced imported bronze, ushering in the Pre-Roman Iron Age.
