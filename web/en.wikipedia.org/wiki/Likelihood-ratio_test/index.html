Likelihood-ratio test

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In statistics, the likelihood-ratio test assesses the goodness of fit of a statistical model based on the ratio of maximum likelihood values. It compares the likelihood of the observed data under a more complex (or unrestricted) model against that under a simpler (nested, restricted) model. If the simpler model fits almost as well, the restriction is considered acceptable; if the likelihood drops substantially, the restriction is rejected.

Definition

The likelihood ratio is

Λ = L(θ̂₀) / L(θ̂)

where L(θ̂₀) is the maximum of the likelihood function over the restricted parameter space and L(θ̂) the maximum over the full space. The test statistic commonly used is −2 ln Λ, which is always non-negative. The test was developed by Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson in the 1920s and 1930s.

Distribution

By Wilks' theorem, under regularity conditions and when the null hypothesis is true, −2 ln Λ converges in distribution to a chi-squared distribution whose degrees of freedom equal the difference in the number of free parameters between the two models. This makes the test convenient for comparing nested models — for example, in logistic regression, analysis of deviance, and model selection generally.

Related tests

The likelihood-ratio test, the Wald test and the score (Lagrange multiplier) test form the classical "holy trinity" of asymptotically equivalent tests in maximum likelihood inference; they differ in which model's estimates they require.
