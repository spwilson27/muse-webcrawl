Yarikh (Ugaritic: 𐎊𐎗𐎃, yrḫ, "moon"; also Yerah, Yarih, Jarah, or Jerah) was the moon god worshipped in the ancient Syrian city of Ugarit and more broadly in Canaanite religion. His name is cognate with the Semitic word for "moon" or "month" (compare Hebrew yareaḥ, ירח). He was described in Ugaritic texts with the epithet "illuminator of the heavens" and "lamp of the gods".

Mythology

Yarikh is best known from the Ugaritic poem usually called The Marriage of Nikkal and Yarikh (KTU 1.24), a text written partly in Ugaritic and partly in Hurrian. In it, the moon god seeks to marry Nikkal, goddess of orchards and fruit, daughter of the summer king Khirikhibi. Yarikh negotiates the bride-price with her father, promising a thousand pieces of silver, ten thousand of gold, and lapis lazuli, and threatens — or is threatened — that if refused he will ask for another bride instead. The marriage is concluded and the poem ends with a blessing of fertility on the union.

Yarikh's father is not named in surviving texts, and some scholars have suggested a connection between him and the god El, the head of the Ugaritic pantheon. A dog appears to have been associated with him in one fragmentary passage.

Worship

Theophoric names containing Yarikh's name are attested across the Levant, and the city of Jericho may preserve his name in its own (from yareakh, "moon"). In later Phoenician religion his role diminished, though moon worship continued in the region in figures such as the Mesopotamian Sin, whose cult centre at Harran was long-lived.
