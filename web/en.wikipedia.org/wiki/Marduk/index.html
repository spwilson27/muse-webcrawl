Marduk (Akkadian: 𒀭𒀫𒌓, AMAR.UTU; "calf of the sun") is the supreme god of ancient Babylonia, patron deity of the city of Babylon, and hero of the creation epic Enūma Eliš. Originally a local, probably agricultural and storm-related deity of modest standing, he rose with Babylon itself to become king of the gods in Mesopotamian religion.

Rise to supremacy

Marduk's elevation tracked Babylon's politics: under Hammurabi (18th century BCE) he was promoted to the head of the pantheon in fact, and by the late second millennium the Enūma Eliš supplied his mythic charter — the gods, helpless before Tiamat's monster army, grant the young Marduk absolute kingship; he destroys Tiamat, orders the cosmos, creates mankind, and receives fifty names of power. His temple in Babylon, the Esagila, adjoined the great ziggurat Etemenanki, a likely inspiration for the Tower of Babel.

Cult and festivals

The high point of Marduk's cult was the Akitu, Babylon's twelve-day New Year festival, during which his statue processed along the Processional Way through the Ishtar Gate, the Enūma Eliš was recited, and the king's rule was ritually renewed — the high priest struck the king's cheek before the god, and good rule was assured if the king wept. The capture or removal of Marduk's statue by foreign conquerors was remembered as catastrophe; its restorations, by kings from Agum-Kakrime to Nebuchadnezzar, were celebrated as cosmic restorations. His sacred animal was the mušḫuššu, a serpent-dragon depicted on the Ishtar Gate, and his emblem the triangular spade.

Later history

Marduk — called Bel ("lord") in later periods — remained central through the Persian and Hellenistic eras; Berossus wrote Babylonian history around his cult for a Greek audience. As Babylon declined in the Parthian and Sasanian periods, his worship faded, leaving his name embedded in the planet Jupiter's Babylonian astronomy and in biblical references to "Merodach".
