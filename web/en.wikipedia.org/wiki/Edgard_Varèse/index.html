Edgard Varèse

Edgard Victor Achille Charles Varèse (22 December 1883 – 6 November 1965) was a French-born composer who became a naturalised American citizen and whose music emphasised timbre and rhythm. He is often called the "father of electronic music" for his pioneering use of new instruments and electronic resources, and he famously defined music as "organised sound".

Life

Varèse was born in Paris and studied with Albert Roussel, Charles-Marie Widor and others, absorbing influences from early music, Debussy and Stravinsky. After years in Paris and Berlin, he emigrated to the United States in 1915. In New York he co-founded the International Composers' Guild (1921) and later the Pan American Association of Composers (1928), promoting new music by composers of the Americas.

Music

Nearly all of Varèse's early works were lost or destroyed, so his reputation rests on a small body of works from the 1920s onward: Amériques (1921), Hyperprism (1923), Octandre (1923), Intégrales (1925), Arcana (1927) and Ionisation (1931), the last scored for percussion ensemble alone and a landmark in that medium. Later works include Ecuatorial (1934), Déserts (1954), which interpolates electronic tape, and the Poème électronique (1958), created for Le Corbusier's Philips Pavilion at the Brussels World's Fair and played through hundreds of loudspeakers.

Influence

Although performances were rare in his lifetime, Varèse profoundly influenced later composers and musicians, from the post-war avant-garde to Frank Zappa, who idolised him. His vision of music liberated from conventional pitch systems anticipated much of the electronic and experimental music of the later twentieth century.
