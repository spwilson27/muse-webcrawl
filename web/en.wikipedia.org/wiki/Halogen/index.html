The halogens are the elements of group 17 of the periodic table: fluorine (F), chlorine (Cl), bromine (Br), iodine (I), astatine (At) and tennessine (Ts). Their name, coined by Berzelius, means 'salt-producers', because they react with metals to form salts such as sodium chloride.

Halogens are the most reactive nonmetals, with seven electrons in their outer shell, one short of a full shell. They form diatomic molecules (F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂) and their reactivity decreases down the group as the outer shell gets farther from the nucleus and more shielded.

Trends

State at room temperature changes down the group: fluorine and chlorine are gases, bromine a liquid, iodine a solid — reflecting strengthening intermolecular forces. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salts, a standard test-tube reaction in school chemistry.

Compounds

Hydrogen halides (HCl, HF) are acidic gases; halide salts are widespread; organic halogen compounds include PVC (a chlorine polymer), Teflon (a fluorine polymer) and the ozone-damaging CFCs now phased out under the Montreal Protocol.
