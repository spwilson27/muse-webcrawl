Direct effect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In European Union law, direct effect is the principle that provisions of EU law, if they are sufficiently clear, precise, and unconditional, confer rights on individuals which national courts must enforce, without the need for implementing legislation by the member states. Together with the principle of primacy (supremacy), it is one of the foundational doctrines of the EU legal order and a principal means by which EU law penetrates national legal systems.

Van Gend en Loos

The doctrine was established by the European Court of Justice in Van Gend en Loos v Nederlandse Administratie der Belastingen (1963), where the Court held that the European Economic Community constituted "a new legal order of international law" whose subjects include not only states but also their nationals, and that a treaty prohibition on new customs duties could be invoked by a company before a Dutch court.

Categories

  • Vertical direct effect — individuals may rely on EU law against the state (including emanations of the state).
  • Horizontal direct effect — in certain cases, individuals may rely on EU law against other private parties. Treaty articles and regulations can have horizontal effect; directives, however, cannot of themselves impose obligations on private parties (Marshall, 1986; Faccini Dori, 1994), though national courts must interpret domestic law consistently with them ("indirect effect") and the state may be liable in damages for failure to implement them (Francovich, 1991).

Sources capable of direct effect

Regulations are directly applicable and can have direct effect; treaty provisions can have direct effect if they meet the criteria of clarity, precision, and unconditionality; directives can have vertical direct effect after their implementation deadline has passed; and decisions addressed to a state can likewise be invoked. Certain provisions of the Charter of Fundamental Rights have also been held to be sufficient in themselves to confer invocable rights.
