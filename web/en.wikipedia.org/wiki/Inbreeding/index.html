Inbreeding

Inbreeding
FieldPopulation genetics
MeasureInbreeding coefficient (F)
ConsequencesIncreased homozygosity; inbreeding depression
OppositeOutbreeding; heterosis (hybrid vigour)

Inbreeding is the production of offspring from the mating or breeding of individuals or organisms that are closely related genetically. By analogy, the term is also used in human reproduction, though it more specifically denotes unions between close kin; mating between relatives of any degree constitutes consanguinity. Inbreeding increases homozygosity — the chance that offspring inherit identical alleles from both parents — thereby exposing recessive deleterious alleles.

Effects

The principal biological consequence is inbreeding depression: reduced fitness through lower survival, fertility and growth, and a higher incidence of recessive genetic disorders. In humans, offspring of first-cousin unions show a modestly increased risk of congenital anomalies, and populations with long histories of consanguinity show elevated rates of certain rare recessive diseases. Historically inbred dynasties, such as the Spanish Habsburgs, accumulated striking hereditary problems, including the "Habsburg jaw".

Measurement and management

The inbreeding coefficient, introduced by Sewall Wright, quantifies the probability that two alleles at a locus are identical by descent; for offspring of first cousins F = 1/16. Plant and animal breeders deliberately use inbreeding (line-breeding) to fix desirable traits, balanced against loss of vigour, while conservation biologists manage small populations to minimise inbreeding and preserve genetic diversity. Inbreeding also increases the expression of rare recessive conditions of the kind catalogued among the inborn errors of metabolism.
