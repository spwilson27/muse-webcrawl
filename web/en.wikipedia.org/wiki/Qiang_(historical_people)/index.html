The Qiang (Chinese: 羌; pinyin: qiāng) were a nomadic and semi-agricultural people of the western borders of ancient China, attested from Shang dynasty oracle bones (c. 1200 BCE) through the early Tang dynasty. Their name survives in the modern Qiang ethnic minority of western Sichuan, although the historical Qiang were a broader grouping than the modern one.

History

Oracle bones mention the Qiang as captives and as "barbarian" tribes beyond the Shang frontier. During the Han dynasty, the term covered a shifting mosaic of tribes in the upper Yellow River valley. The Later Han government moved many Qiang into the Wei valley, where rebellions—the "Qiang revolts" of the 2nd century CE—proved devastating and contributed to the dynasty's decline. In the 4th and 5th centuries, Qiang-led states emerged during the Sixteen Kingdoms period, most notably the Later Qin founded by Yao Chang, a Qiang chieftain, which controlled Shaanxi and Gansu from 384 to 417.

Ethnic connections

The Qiang are conventionally considered one of the earliest recorded Tibeto-Burman-speaking peoples, ancestral to various groups of the eastern Tibetan plateau. The Tangut people, who founded the Western Xia dynasty (1038–1227), were of Qiang descent. Because early Chinese texts used the character qiang loosely for many western tribes, historians distinguish between the historically documented groups and the modern Qiang people of Maoxian County, Sichuan, a recognised ethnic minority of about 300,000.
