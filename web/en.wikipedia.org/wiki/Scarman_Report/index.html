The Scarman Report is the report of the public inquiry conducted by the judge Lord Scarman into the Brixton riots of April 1981 in south London. Commissioned by the Home Secretary under the Police Act 1964, it was published on 25 November 1981 and remains a landmark document in British debates about policing, race, and urban deprivation.

The riots

The Brixton disorders of 10–12 April 1981 followed tensions between the Metropolitan Police and the local Afro-Caribbean community, inflamed by "Operation Swamp 81", a stop-and-search campaign using the "sus" power under the Vagrancy Act 1824. Over the weekend, hundreds were injured, vehicles were burned, and buildings damaged.

Findings

Scarman rejected the idea that the riots were planned or conspiratorial, attributing them instead to a spontaneous outburst rooted in "racial disadvantage" — a cluster of unemployment, poor housing, educational underachievement, and hostile policing. He criticised police methods and the collapse of trust, but rejected the charge of institutional racism as then framed, arguing that Britain's institutions were not deliberately racist, though "racial disadvantage and its felt consequences" demanded urgent action.

Recommendations and legacy

The report recommended reform of police training, recruitment of more ethnic-minority officers, independent investigation of complaints, statutory consultation between police and communities, and concerted government action against racial disadvantage. Its aftermath included the repeal of the "sus" law and reforms consolidated in the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE). Scarman's denial of institutional racism was revisited by the Macpherson Report (1999) into the murder of Stephen Lawrence, which reached a different conclusion.
