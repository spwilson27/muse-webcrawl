Carbon suboxide

Carbon suboxide, or tricarbon dioxide, is an oxide of carbon with the formula C3O2. Its molecule is a chain of three carbon atoms joined by cumulative double bonds to terminal oxygens, O=C=C=C=O, making it a cumulene and formally the anhydride of malonic acid. It is a pungent, lachrymatory gas that polymerises spontaneously at room temperature to a red, yellow or black solid.

History

Carbon suboxide was first characterised in 1873 by Benjamin Brodie, who obtained it by passing an electric discharge through carbon monoxide, and was prepared in quantity by Otto Diels and Bruno Wolf in 1906 by dehydrating malonic acid with phosphorus pentoxide — still the standard preparation. Small amounts are also produced biochemically during heme oxidation, alongside carbon monoxide.

Structure

The molecule is nominally linear with D∞h symmetry, but it is famously "quasilinear": the bending potential at the central carbon is extremely flat, so the molecule bends with almost no energy cost and the effective structure depends on vibrational state. It is isoelectronic with the carbon dioxide dimer fragment and related cumulenes, and its bonding is often described using zwitterionic resonance forms.

Reactions

Carbon suboxide reacts with water to give malonic acid, with ammonia to give malonamide, and with hydrogen chloride to give malonyl chloride. On standing it polymerises to a coloured solid (sometimes called "red carbon"), whose structure consists of fused lactone rings; the polymer was once studied as a possible component of interstellar and cometary material. The gas is flammable and toxic in a manner similar to carbon monoxide.
