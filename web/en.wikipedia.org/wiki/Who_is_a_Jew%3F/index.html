Who is a Jew?

"Who is a Jew?" (Hebrew: מיהו יהודי, Mihu Yehudi) is a basic question about Jewish identity and considerations of Jewish self-identification. The question is based on ideas about Jewish personhood, which have cultural, ethnic, religious, political, genealogical, and personal dimensions. Answers differ among the movements of Judaism and have significant legal and political consequences, particularly in the State of Israel, where the definition determines eligibility under the Law of Return and matters of personal status administered by the rabbinate.

Traditional definition

Orthodox and Conservative Judaism maintain the traditional halakhic definition: a person is Jewish if born to a Jewish mother, or if he or she has formally converted to Judaism under rabbinic supervision. Matrilineal descent has been the normative rule in rabbinic Judaism since late antiquity, although the Hebrew Bible traced descent patrilineally for tribal and priestly status.

Differences among movements

Reform and Reconstructionist Judaism also accept patrilineal descent, recognizing a child of one Jewish parent—mother or father—as Jewish if the child is raised and identifies as Jewish. Karaite Judaism traditionally follows patrilineal descent. Secular and cultural definitions emphasize self-identification and participation in Jewish culture rather than religious law. The question has been the subject of landmark Israeli Supreme Court cases, including the Brother Daniel case (1962) and the Shalit case (1970), and recurring legislative debate over the conversion provisions of the Law of Return.
