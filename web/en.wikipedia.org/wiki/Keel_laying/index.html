Keel laying

Keel laying, or laying the keel, is the formal recognition of the start of a ship's construction. It is often marked with a ceremony attended by dignitaries from the shipbuilding company and the ultimate owners of the ship. In the modern era, the event is usually the placement of the first modular section, or keel block, of the hull in the building dock or slipway.

History

The keel is the central structural member running along the bottom of a ship's hull, traditionally the first part of the vessel to be built, from which the frames, stem and sternpost were erected. Because construction literally began with the keel, laying it became a milestone celebrated by shipbuilders. In wooden-ship construction, a coin was traditionally placed under the keel block — a custom related to the mast-stepping coin ceremony — as a token of good fortune.

Modern practice

Modern ships are assembled from prefabricated modules, so there may be no single continuous keel laid in the old sense. Shipyards and navies nonetheless preserve the keel-laying ceremony: the module is lowered into place, and in United States Navy tradition the keel layer's initials may be welded onto a plate that is fixed to the keel. The ceremony is the first of four traditional milestones in a warship's life, followed by launching (or christening), commissioning and decommissioning.

For record-keeping purposes, the keel-laying date is often treated as a ship's nominal "birth date", and appears in registers of shipping and in lists of warships.
