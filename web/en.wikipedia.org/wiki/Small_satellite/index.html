A small satellite, miniaturized satellite or smallsat is an artificial satellite of low mass and size, usually under 1,200 kg by NASA's classification (many authors use 500 kg). Advances in miniaturised electronics, commercial off-the-shelf components and standardised form factors — above all the CubeSat standard (1999) — have made smallsats the dominant category of spacecraft launched since the 2010s, enabling university projects, startups and large commercial constellations.

CubeSats are specified in units (1U = 10 cm cube, ~1–1.3 kg); common sizes are 1U, 3U, 6U and 12U.

Characteristics

Smallsats trade capability for cost and speed: they use commercial electronics, accept higher risk, are built in months rather than years, and launch as secondary payloads ("rideshare") or on dedicated small launch vehicles such as Rocket Lab's Electron. Their low individual cost enables constellations — Planet Labs' several hundred Dove microsatellites image the entire Earth daily; SpaceX's Starlink satellites (~260–300 kg each) number in the thousands; and OneWeb and Iridium NEXT illustrate the model at larger masses.

Applications and concerns

Applications include remote sensing, AIS ship tracking, IoT connectivity, astronomy (e.g., the ASTERIA CubeSat), deep-space firsts such as the MarCO CubeSats that flew to Mars in 2018, and education. The proliferation of smallsats has intensified concerns over orbital debris, collision risk and the brightness of mega-constellations affecting astronomy, prompting stricter deorbit rules (e.g., the FCC's five-year post-mission disposal rule adopted in 2022).
