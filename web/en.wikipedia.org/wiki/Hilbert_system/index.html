Hilbert system

In logic, a Hilbert system or Hilbert–Ackermann system is a type of system of formal deduction attributed to Gottlob Frege and David Hilbert. It is defined by a large or even infinite number of axiom schemes and a small set of inference rules — most commonly a single rule, modus ponens.

Structure

A Hilbert system for classical propositional logic typically takes as axiom schemes the three patterns:

  • A → (B → A)
  • (A → (B → C)) → ((A → B) → (A → C))
  • (¬A → ¬B) → (B → A)

together with the rule that from A and A → B one may infer B. Proofs in such systems are sequences of formulae, each either an axiom instance or obtained from earlier lines by the inference rules. Because deductions are made from assumptions without discharging them, Hilbert systems are usually used in combination with the deduction theorem.

Characteristics

Hilbert systems are convenient for metatheoretical study — completeness and consistency proofs are relatively simple — but proofs of even elementary results can be long and unintuitive compared with natural deduction or sequent calculus. Many equivalent variants exist, differing in the choice of primitive connectives and axiom schemes.
