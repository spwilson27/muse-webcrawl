Soda bread

Soda bread is a variety of quick bread in which baking soda (sodium bicarbonate) is used as the leavening agent instead of yeast. The basic ingredients are flour, salt, baking soda and a liquid acid — traditionally soured milk or buttermilk — whose reaction with the soda releases carbon dioxide and makes the dough rise. Soda breads have a mild flavour, a tender crumb and a thick crust, and are best eaten on the day they are baked.

History and regional forms

Soda bread became widespread in the nineteenth century after bicarbonate of soda became commercially available; it is most associated with Ireland, where soft wheat flour made yeast breads difficult, but similar breads are traditional in Scotland, Serbia and among Native American and Australian ("damper") traditions. In Ireland the loaf is often marked with a cross — said in folklore to let the fairies out — and may be baked as a round farl on a griddle or as a pan loaf; wholemeal versions are common, and richer "spotted" versions contain raisins or currants. Scottish soda farls and scones are cooked on a girdle (griddle), while Serbian česnica is a festive soda bread.

Preparation

The dough is mixed briefly and kneaded minimally, since overworking makes it tough; it is shaped, scored and baked at once because the leavening begins as soon as the wet and dry ingredients meet. Traditional Irish soda bread contains nothing but flour, salt, soda and buttermilk; added sugar, eggs, butter or dried fruit produce tea breads such as the spotted dog.
