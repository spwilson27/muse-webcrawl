Colluvium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Colluvium is loose, unconsolidated sediment that has been transported downslope by gravity and accumulates at the base of slopes, hillsides and cliffs. Unlike alluvium, which is moved by running water, colluvium is deposited mainly by gravity-driven processes such as soil creep, landslides, rockfall, debris flows and rainwash over short distances.

Characteristics

Colluvial deposits are typically poorly sorted, containing angular fragments of the bedrock found upslope mixed with soil material. Because they travel only a short distance, clasts remain angular rather than rounded. Colluvium often forms wedge-shaped aprons at slope bases; where it is derived largely from rockfall below a cliff it may form a talus or scree slope.

Formation and significance

Weathering breaks down bedrock on slopes, and the loosened material gradually moves downward. Colluvial layers can bury and preserve archaeological sites and old land surfaces, making them important in geoarchaeology and Quaternary studies: buried soils within colluvium can record past episodes of slope stability, climate change and human land use such as deforestation and ploughing, which often accelerate colluviation. In engineering and hazard assessment, thick colluvial deposits are significant because they may be unstable and prone to renewed landsliding, especially when saturated by heavy rain.
