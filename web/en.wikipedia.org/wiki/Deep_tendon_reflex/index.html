Deep tendon reflex

The deep tendon reflexes, also called muscle stretch reflexes or myotatic reflexes, are involuntary muscle contractions elicited by briskly stretching a muscle, classically by tapping its tendon with a reflex hammer. They are among the most important components of the neurological examination, because their presence, absence, or exaggeration helps localize lesions of the nervous system.

Mechanism

When a tendon is tapped, the muscle is briefly stretched, activating muscle spindle sensory receptors. Type Ia afferent fibres carry the signal to the spinal cord, where they synapse directly on alpha motor neurons supplying the same muscle — making this the classic monosynaptic reflex. The motor neurons fire, the muscle contracts, and at the same time interneurons inhibit the antagonist muscle (reciprocal inhibition). The best-known example is the knee-jerk or patellar reflex: tapping the patellar tendon extends the leg via the quadriceps (spinal levels L2–L4).

Reflexes commonly tested

  • Biceps reflex — C5–C6
  • Brachioradialis reflex — C6
  • Triceps reflex — C7
  • Patellar (knee-jerk) reflex — L2–L4
  • Achilles (ankle-jerk) reflex — S1–S2
  • Jaw jerk — trigeminal nerve (pons)

Grading and clinical significance

Deep tendon reflexes are commonly graded on a scale from 0 (absent) to 4+ (markedly hyperactive, often with clonus), with 2+ considered normal. Hyporeflexia (diminished or absent reflexes) suggests a lower motor neuron lesion, peripheral neuropathy, or involvement of the relevant spinal segment, while hyperreflexia (brisk reflexes, spread of reflexes, clonus) suggests an upper motor neuron lesion above the level of the reflex arc. Asymmetry between the two sides of the body is often as informative as the absolute grade. Reinforcement manoeuvres such as the Jendrassik manoeuvre can be used to elicit reflexes that appear absent.
