Abacus

An abacus (plural abaci or abacuses), also called a counting frame, is a calculation tool used for arithmetic processes. In the form of a frame holding rods or grooves on which beads or stones are slid, the abacus was used in the ancient Near East, Europe, China and Russia for millennia, long before the adoption of the Hindu–Arabic numeral system, and remains in use in some parts of the world today.

History

Counting boards were used in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Persia, Greece and Rome; the marble Salamis Tablet (c. 300 BC) is the oldest surviving counting board. The Roman hand abacus used pebbles (calculi, the origin of the word "calculate"). In Han-dynasty China the bead abacus, the suanpan, developed, with two beads above and five below a dividing bar in its classic form. The Japanese soroban, imported from China in the 14th century, was refined to a 1:4 bead arrangement. In Russia, the schoty uses ten beads per wire and remained common in shops into the late 20th century. Indigenous American devices such as the Inca yupana and the Aztec nepohualtzintzin served similar purposes.

Use

On a bead abacus each column represents a decimal place. Beads are moved toward the bar to register digits, and skilled users perform addition, subtraction, multiplication, division and even square and cube roots rapidly — in a famous 1946 contest in Tokyo, soroban champion Kiyoshi Matsuzaki defeated an American soldier using an electric calculator in most events. The abacus is still taught in East Asian schools as a foundation for mental arithmetic ("anzan"), and adapted abacuses such as the Cranmer abacus are used in teaching mathematics to blind students.
