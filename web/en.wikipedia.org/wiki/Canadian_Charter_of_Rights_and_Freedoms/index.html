Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms is the first part of the Constitution Act, 1982, which forms part of the constitution of Canada. Proclaimed on 17 April 1982 as part of the patriation of the constitution under Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau, the Charter guarantees fundamental freedoms (conscience, expression, assembly, association), democratic rights, mobility rights, legal rights (life, liberty and security of the person; fair trial; equality of language rights in federal institutions), and equality rights (s.15, effective 1985), subject to “such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society” (s.1, applied through the Oakes proportionality test, 1986).

The Charter transformed Canadian law and politics: courts struck down abortion restrictions (Morgentaler, 1988), Sunday-closing and hate-propaganda laws were tested, same-sex marriage was achieved through Charter litigation and reference, and Indigenous rights (protected separately by s.35) interact with it. Its distinctive safety valve is the notwithstanding clause (s.33), allowing legislatures to override most rights for renewable five-year terms — used rarely outside Quebec historically, more assertively in recent years, and perpetually debated as either democratic correction or rights erosion. Section 28 guarantees Charter rights equally to men and women “notwithstanding any other provision”.
