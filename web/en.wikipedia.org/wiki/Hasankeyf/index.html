Hasankeyf (Arabic: حصن كيفا; Kurdish: Heskîf) is an ancient town in Batman Province in southeastern Turkey, on the banks of the Tigris River. Settled continuously for thousands of years, it served as a Roman frontier post, a Byzantine bishopric and the capital of an Artuqid principality, and its cliffs are honeycombed with thousands of human-made caves. Much of the historic town was submerged in 2020 by the reservoir of the Ilısu Dam, despite a long international campaign to save it.

History

In Roman times the town, known as Cepha or Kiphas, guarded a crossing of the Tigris on the frontier with Persia, and the Romans built a fort and bridge there. Under the Artuqids in the twelfth century Hasankeyf flourished as a capital and trading city; the surviving ruins of the great bridge over the Tigris (built c. 1116) — once among the largest of the medieval world — and the citadel date largely from this and the succeeding Ayyubid and Ak Koyunlu periods. The El-Rızk Mosque (1409) with its finely carved minaret and the cylindrical tomb of Zeynel Bey are notable monuments. The town passed to the Ottoman Empire in the sixteenth century.

Ilısu Dam and submergence

The Ilısu Dam, part of Turkey's Southeastern Anatolia Project (GAP), was planned from the 1950s and built in the 2000s–2010s. Its reservoir flooded old Hasankeyf in 2019–2020. Residents were moved to a new settlement, "New Hasankeyf", on higher ground, and several monuments — including the Zeynel Bey tomb and parts of the El-Rızk mosque — were transported whole to a cultural park there. Heritage organisations argued that Hasankeyf met nine of UNESCO's ten criteria for World Heritage status, but it was never inscribed, and legal challenges in Turkey and at the European Court of Human Rights failed to halt the project.
