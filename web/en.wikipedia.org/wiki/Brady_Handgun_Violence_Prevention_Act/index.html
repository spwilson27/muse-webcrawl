Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act

Brady Act
Enacted: 30 November 1993
Signed by: President Bill Clinton
Public Law: 103-159
Signing of the Brady Bill, 30 November 1993

The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act (Pub. L. 103-159), often called the Brady Act or Brady Bill, is an Act of the United States Congress that mandated federal background checks on firearm purchasers in the United States and imposed a five-day waiting period on purchases until the National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS) was implemented in 1998.

Background

The act is named for James Brady, press secretary to President Ronald Reagan, who was shot and permanently disabled during the attempted assassination of Reagan on 30 March 1981 by John Hinckley Jr., who had purchased his revolver at a Dallas pawn shop despite a history of mental illness and arrests. Brady and his wife Sarah became prominent campaigners for gun control, and the bill was debated in Congress for seven years before passing.

Provisions

The act required licensed firearms dealers to perform background checks before completing a sale. Its interim provisions — a waiting period of up to five business days and checks by local chief law enforcement officers — were challenged by several sheriffs; in Printz v. United States (1997) the Supreme Court struck down the requirement that state and local officers conduct the checks as a violation of the Tenth Amendment. On 30 November 1998 the waiting period expired and the FBI-operated NICS took over instant checks nationwide.
