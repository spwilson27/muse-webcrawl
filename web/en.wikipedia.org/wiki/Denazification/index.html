Denazification

Denazification (German: Entnazifizierung) was an Allied initiative to rid German and Austrian society, culture, press, economy, judiciary, and politics of any remnants of the National Socialist (Nazi) ideology after World War II. It was carried out by removing those who had been Nazi Party or SS members from positions of power and influence, by disbanding or rendering impotent organisations associated with Nazism, and by trying prominent Nazis for war crimes in the Nuremberg trials.

Implementation

The programme was set out at the Potsdam Conference in 1945 and implemented differently in the four occupation zones. In the American zone, adults filled out questionnaires (Fragebogen), and German tribunals (Spruchkammern) classified people into five categories from "major offender" to "exonerated". The Soviet zone combined purges with social restructuring; the British and French zones were generally less extensive.

Wind-down and legacy

As the Cold War intensified, the Western Allies curtailed denazification; amnesty laws in the young Federal Republic of Germany reintegrated many former party members, and Article 131 of the Basic Law restored rights to dismissed officials. Historians debate the programme's thoroughness, noting both genuine reckoning and widespread evasion (the notorious Persilschein, or whitewash certificate), while later German memory culture — including the student movement of 1968 — revisited unfinished denazification.
