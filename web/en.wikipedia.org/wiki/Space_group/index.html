Space group

In mathematics, physics and chemistry, a space group is the symmetry group of a repeating pattern in space, usually in three dimensions. The elements of a space group are the rigid transformations — translations, rotations, reflections and their combinations, including screw rotations and glide reflections — that leave the pattern unchanged.

Classification

In three dimensions there are exactly 230 distinct space groups. This number was derived independently around 1890–1891 by Evgraf Fedorov, Arthur Moritz Schoenflies and William Barlow. By comparison, there are 2 space groups (wallpaper groups aside) — more precisely, 17 wallpaper groups in two dimensions and 7 frieze groups in one dimension.

Every space group is a combination of a Bravais lattice (one of 14) and a point group (one of 32 crystallographic point groups). Of the 230 groups, 73 are symmorphic, obtainable by combining the point group with the lattice translations directly; the remaining 157 are nonsymmorphic, containing screw axes or glide planes whose rotational or reflective parts are coupled to fractional translations.

Notation

Space groups are most often named in Hermann–Mauguin (international) notation, such as P21/c — the most common space group for organic molecular crystals, adopted by roughly a third of known structures. Schoenflies notation is also used, especially in spectroscopy. The definitive reference is the International Tables for Crystallography.

Applications

Space groups are fundamental to crystallography: determining a crystal structure from X-ray, neutron or electron diffraction amounts largely to identifying the space group and the positions of the atoms within the unit cell. They also classify magnetic structures (with extensions to 1,651 magnetic space groups) and appear in condensed-matter physics in the study of band structure and symmetry-protected phenomena.
