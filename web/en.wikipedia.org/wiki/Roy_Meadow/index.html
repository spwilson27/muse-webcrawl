Roy Meadow

Sir Samuel Roy Meadow (born 9 June 1933) is a British retired paediatrician who became one of the country's most prominent experts on child abuse, but whose career ended in controversy after his expert testimony in several criminal trials of mothers accused of murdering their babies was discredited.

Career

Meadow qualified in medicine and specialised in paediatrics, becoming professor of paediatrics and child health at the University of Leeds. In 1977 he coined the term "Munchausen syndrome by proxy" (later called fabricated or induced illness) to describe cases in which a carer fabricates or induces illness in a child. His textbook ABC of Child Abuse became a standard reference, and he was knighted in 1998 for services to paediatrics.

The cot death trials

Meadow frequently appeared as an expert witness for the prosecution in cases involving the unexpected deaths of infants. He popularised the saying known as "Meadow's law": "One sudden infant death is a tragedy, two is suspicious and three is murder, until proved otherwise."

His testimony was central to the conviction of Sally Clark, a solicitor convicted in 1999 of murdering her two baby sons. In that trial Meadow cited a statistic that the chance of two natural cot deaths in an affluent family was "one in 73 million"—a figure derived by squaring the probability of a single cot death, a method widely criticised by statisticians because it wrongly assumes the events are independent. The Royal Statistical Society took the unusual step of publicly criticising the use of the statistic. Clark's conviction was quashed on appeal in 2003. Other convictions in which Meadow had testified, including those of Angela Cannings and Trupti Patel, were also overturned or resulted in acquittal, prompting a wider review of such prosecutions.

GMC proceedings

In 2005 the General Medical Council found Meadow guilty of serious professional misconduct over the evidence he gave in the Clark trial and struck him off the medical register. On appeal, the High Court overturned the erasure in 2006, holding that while his evidence had been flawed, it did not amount to serious professional misconduct warranting removal. Meadow retired from practice and his reputation was severely damaged by the affair, which led to lasting changes in how expert statistical evidence is handled in British courts.
