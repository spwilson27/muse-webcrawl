Institutional ethnography
A sociological method developed by Dorothy E. Smith.

Institutional ethnography (IE) is a method of social inquiry developed by Canadian sociologist Dorothy E. Smith, beginning in the 1970s and elaborated in works such as The Everyday World as Problematic (1987) and Institutional Ethnography: A Sociology for People (2005). Rather than studying institutions as bounded organizations, IE investigates how people's everyday activities are coordinated — "ruled" — by text-mediated relations that extend beyond local settings.

Approach

An institutional ethnography begins not with a theoretical problem but with a problematic: a disjuncture experienced in everyday life, for example between patients' experiences of care and the categories of a medical chart. The researcher starts from the standpoint of people in a particular place in the social organization, observes their actual work (broadly conceived), and traces how that work is coordinated by texts — forms, files, policies, records, professional discourses — which replicate the same categories and instructions across many local sites. These translocal coordinations are called ruling relations.

Key concepts

Central concepts include work knowledge (what people know from doing), text-reader conversations, bifurcation of consciousness (the split between lived experience and institutional modes of knowing), and the mapping of sequences of action across settings to make visible how institutions operate. The aim is an account useful to the people whose standpoint the inquiry begins from — originally, in Smith's feminist sociology, women.

Applications

IE has been applied extensively in nursing and health care, education, social work, and studies of bureaucracy, development, and the audit culture of universities and hospitals, where the method reveals how accountability regimes reorganize frontline work.
