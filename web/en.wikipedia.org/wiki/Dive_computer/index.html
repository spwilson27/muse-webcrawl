Dive computer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A wrist-mounted dive computer

A dive computer, personal decompression computer or decompression meter is a device used by an underwater diver to measure the elapsed time and depth during a dive and use this data to calculate and display an ascent profile which, according to the programmed decompression algorithm, will give a low risk of decompression sickness.

Most dive computers use real-time ambient pressure input to a decompression algorithm to indicate the remaining time to the no-decompression limit, and after that has passed, the minimum decompression required to surface with an acceptable risk of decompression sickness. Several algorithms have been used, and various personal conservatism factors may be available.

Functions

Modern dive computers typically display current depth, maximum depth, elapsed dive time, no-stop time remaining, water temperature, and ascent rate, and provide audible and visual alarms for excessive ascent rate or missed decompression stops. Many also support nitrox and trimix gas mixtures, integrate with electronic compasses and tank pressure sensors, and log dives for later download to a personal computer.

History

Analog pneumatic decompression meters were used from the 1950s. The first electronic dive computer widely available was the Orca Edge, introduced in 1983. Dive computers became mainstream in recreational diving during the 1990s and are now standard equipment for most divers, having largely replaced decompression tables for recreational use.
