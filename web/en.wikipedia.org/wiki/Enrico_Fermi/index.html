Enrico Fermi (29 September 1901 – 28 November 1954) was an Italian-American physicist, creator of the world's first nuclear reactor (Chicago Pile-1, 1942) and a central figure in the Manhattan Project. He made major contributions to statistical mechanics (Fermi–Dirac statistics), weak-interaction theory (beta decay), and the physics of slow neutrons, winning the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1938.

Fermi emigrated to the United States in 1938, partly because his wife Laura was Jewish and Italy had enacted racial laws. Known for back-of-the-envelope estimation ('Fermi problems') and for being both a brilliant theorist and experimentalist — a combination rare even in his day — he also posed the 'Fermi paradox' about the apparent absence of extraterrestrial civilisations. The fermi (femtometre), fermion and Fermilab commemorate him.

Work

In Rome in the 1930s, Fermi's group bombarded elements with neutrons, finding slow neutrons especially effective at inducing radioactivity. At Chicago his team achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction on 2 December 1942, beneath the university's Stagg Field. He died of stomach cancer in 1954, possibly linked to radiation exposure.
