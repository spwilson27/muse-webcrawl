The Scottish Deerhound, or simply the Deerhound, is a large breed of sighthound, bred to hunt red deer by coursing. In outward appearance it resembles a rough-coated Greyhound but is taller and more heavily built, with a wiry coat in shades of grey. Once the dog of Scottish chieftains and a near-exclusive companion of Highland nobility, the breed was sometimes described as "the royal dog of Scotland".

History

Large rough-coated sighthounds have been known in Scotland since at least the sixteenth century, used to course and pull down deer in the Highlands. The breed's numbers collapsed with the decline of the clan system and the break-up of great estates after the Jacobite rising of 1745 and the Highland Clearances. In the nineteenth century, breeders including Archibald and Duncan McNeill restored the breed, and its popularity rose with deer-stalking as a Victorian sport and the patronage of Queen Victoria. A breed standard was drawn up in the 1880s and the Deerhound Club was founded in 1886.

Characteristics

The Deerhound is gentle, dignified, and famously quiet indoors, though it requires considerable exercise and room. It is a true coursing hound, hunting by sight with great speed and endurance over rough ground. Like other deep-chested large breeds it can be prone to gastric dilatation-volvulus (bloat), and also to bone cancer (osteosarcoma) and cardiomyopathy; its lifespan is typically around 8 to 11 years. Deerhounds appear in literature from Sir Walter Scott — whose own Deerhound Maida was a favourite — onwards.
