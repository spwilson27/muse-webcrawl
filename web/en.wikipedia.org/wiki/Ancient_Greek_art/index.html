Ancient Greek art

Ancient Greek art stands out among that of other ancient cultures for its development of naturalistic but idealised depictions of the human body, in which largely nude male figures were the focus of innovation. Produced from roughly the ninth century BCE until the Roman conquest and beyond, Greek art — in pottery, sculpture, architecture, and metalwork — exerted an enormous influence on Roman art and, through it, on the entire Western tradition.

Periods

  • Geometric period (c. 900–700 BCE): pottery painted with bands of meanders and stylised figures; small bronze votives.
  • Archaic period (c. 700–480 BCE): black-figure and then red-figure vase painting; the kouros and kore statues with their characteristic "Archaic smile".
  • Classical period (c. 480–323 BCE): the age of Phidias, Polykleitos, and Praxiteles; the Parthenon and its sculptures; the canon of ideal proportions and the contrapposto stance.
  • Hellenistic period (c. 323–31 BCE): art of the successor kingdoms, marked by dramatic movement, emotion, and virtuosity — the Pergamon Altar, the Laocoön group, the Venus de Milo.

Pottery

Painted pottery is the best-preserved and most abundant Greek art form. Athenian workshops perfected the black-figure technique, in which figures were painted in slip that fired black, and its successor red-figure, which allowed finer interior detail. Vases depicted myths, athletics, warfare, and scenes of daily life, and were exported throughout the Mediterranean.

Sculpture

Greek sculptors moved from the rigid frontality of Egyptian-influenced kouroi to fully naturalistic anatomy. Polykleitos codified ideal proportions in his treatise the Canon, embodied in the Doryphoros. In the fourth century Praxiteles introduced the sensuous Aphrodite of Knidos, the first monumental female nude. Most famous bronzes survive only in Roman marble copies, but originals such as the Delphi Charioteer and the Artemision Bronze preserve the medium in its glory.
