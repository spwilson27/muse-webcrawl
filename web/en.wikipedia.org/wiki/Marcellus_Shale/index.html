The Marcellus Shale is a Middle Devonian (approximately 390 million years old) sedimentary rock formation, predominantly black shale rich in organic matter, underlying much of the Appalachian Basin in the eastern United States — principally Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Ohio, and New York. Since the late 2000s it has become one of the most productive natural gas fields in the world.

Geology

The Marcellus Formation was deposited in the Appalachian Basin during the Devonian, when the region lay beneath a shallow inland sea. It consists largely of dark, organic-rich marine shales that are both the source rock for hydrocarbons and, in places, a reservoir in their own right. The formation is typically only a few tens of metres thick but extends over some 250,000 square kilometres, lying between about 1,200 and 2,600 metres below the surface in the productive areas of Pennsylvania and West Virginia.

Development

The Marcellus had long been considered uneconomic because its shale is of very low permeability. The combination of horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing (fracking), first proven commercially in the Barnett Shale of Texas, changed that calculus. The first successful Marcellus well using the combination was drilled in Washington County, Pennsylvania, in 2004, and drilling expanded rapidly after 2008. By the mid-2010s the Marcellus was supplying roughly a fifth of United States natural gas production, and it remains the largest gas-producing formation in the country.

Controversy

Development has brought substantial economic benefits to parts of Appalachia, along with controversy over groundwater contamination, induced seismicity, methane emissions, road traffic, and the use of large volumes of water and chemicals in fracturing fluids. New York State banned high-volume hydraulic fracturing in 2014, a decision upheld by the courts, effectively closing its portion of the Marcellus to development, while Pennsylvania and West Virginia have continued large-scale production.
