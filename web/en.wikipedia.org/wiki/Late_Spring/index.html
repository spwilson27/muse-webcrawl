Late Spring

Late Spring (Japanese: 晩春, Banshun) is a 1949 Japanese drama film directed by Yasujirō Ozu, written by Ozu and Kogo Noda and adapted from the novel Chichi to musume (Father and Daughter) by Kazuo Hirotsu. It stars Chishū Ryū as widowed professor Shūkichi Somiya and Setsuko Hara as his devoted daughter Noriko, whose impending arranged marriage drives the story. The film is the first of Ozu's celebrated "Noriko trilogy", followed by Early Summer (1951) and Tokyo Story (1953), in each of which Hara plays a different character named Noriko.

Plot

Noriko Somiya, a 27-year-old woman, lives contentedly with her widowed father Shūkichi in Kamakura, caring for him and doing translations and housework. Her family and friends increasingly press the subject of marriage: her aunt Masa arranges a meeting with a prospective husband, and her friend Aya, a divorcee, embodies both the freedom and the stigma attached to a woman outside marriage. Shūkichi, persuaded that Noriko sacrifices her happiness for him — and stung by a friend's remarriage — pretends that he himself intends to remarry, freeing Noriko to accept the match. On a final father-daughter trip to Kyoto, the two share quiet, bittersweet days. Noriko marries; Shūkichi returns to an empty house and, in the film's famous closing image, sits alone peeling an apple, the peel falling in a single coil.

Themes and style

The film exemplifies Ozu's mature style: a camera positioned low at tatami level, elliptical storytelling in which major events such as the wedding occur off-screen, and "pillow shots" of objects and landscapes — most famously a vase in the Kyoto inn scene, over which Noriko's expression shifts from a smile to tears. The film reflects postwar Japanese anxieties about the decline of the traditional family, the changing position of women, and the bittersweet necessity of letting children go. Censors of the Allied occupation reportedly objected to the film's treatment of arranged marriage, yet allowed it largely uncut.

Reception and legacy

Late Spring was a major critical and commercial success in Japan and is consistently ranked among the greatest films ever made; it appeared in the Sight and Sound critics' polls. It established the template for Ozu's later family dramas and remains one of the key works of the shōshimin-eiga (home drama) genre.
