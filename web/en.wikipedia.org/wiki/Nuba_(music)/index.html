Nuba (music)

A nuba (Arabic: نوبة‎) is a musical form and suite structure central to the classical music traditions of North Africa, particularly the Andalusi music of Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya. Each nuba is a large-scale composition built on a particular melodic mode (tab) and unfolds through a fixed sequence of movements with contrasting rhythms and tempos.

Structure

A complete nuba can last several hours and traditionally comprises five principal rhythmic movements, beginning with slow, rhythmically free instrumental and vocal preludes and progressing through increasingly animated sections. The movements include instrumental overtures (such as the mishaliyya or touchia), sung poems drawn from classical Arabic muwashshah and zajal poetry, and concluding fast sections. Orchestras typically feature the oud, rabab, violin, viola, qanun, nay, hand percussion such as the tar and darbuka, and a chorus of singers.

History

The nuba tradition traces its origins to the music of al-Andalus (Muslim Spain), where court musicians in Córdoba and later Granada developed elaborate suite forms. After the fall of Granada in 1492, musicians carried the repertoire to North African cities such as Fez, Tlemcen, Algiers, Tunis and Tripoli, where distinct regional schools preserved and reshaped it. Moroccan tradition speaks of an original cycle of twenty-four nubas, one for each hour of the day, of which eleven complete or partial nubas survive; Algerian and Tunisian traditions preserve their own related cycles.
