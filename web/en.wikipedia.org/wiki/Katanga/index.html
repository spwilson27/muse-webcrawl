Katanga

Katanga is a region in the south-east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, named after the former province of Katanga, which existed in various forms until it was divided in 2015 into the provinces of Tanganyika, Haut-Lomami, Lualaba and Haut-Katanga. Rich in copper, cobalt and other minerals of the Central African Copperbelt, Katanga has played an outsized role in Congolese history, including a secessionist episode at independence.

Geography and resources

The region occupies a plateau of around 1,200–1,500 m elevation drained by the Lualaba (upper Congo) and Luapula rivers. In the south lies the Congolese portion of the Copperbelt, a belt of sedimentary copper and cobalt deposits that has been mined intensively since the colonial period. Kolwezi, Likasi and Lubumbashi (the former Élisabethville) are the principal mining cities; the region also holds deposits of uranium (the Shinkolobwe mine supplied uranium for the Manhattan Project), zinc, cadmium, tin and germanium.

History

Before colonization the area saw the Luba and Lunda empires and the trading kingdom of Msiri, whose copper trade with both coasts ended with his killing by Belgian agents in 1891. Under the Congo Free State and later the Belgian Congo, the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga developed the mines from 1906, recruiting labour from across Central Africa.

Eleven days after Congolese independence in 1960, Katanga declared its secession under Moise Tshombe, supported by Belgian mining interests; the ensuing Katanga Crisis drew in the United Nations in its first major peace-enforcement operation and cost the life of UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld in a plane crash near Ndola in 1961. Secession ended in January 1963. Under Mobutu the province was renamed Shaba; "Shaba invasions" by Katangese exiles in 1977 and 1978 were repelled with foreign support. In 1997 the province's name reverted to Katanga, and in 2015 it was split into four provinces as required by the 2006 constitution.

Identity and politics

Katanga retains a strong regional identity centred on the Luba-Katanga and Lunda peoples and on the Swahili-speaking mining towns. Disputes over "autochthonous" Katangese identity versus Kasai-origin Luba residents erupted in ethnic violence in the early 1990s. Control of mining revenues remains central to Congolese politics.
