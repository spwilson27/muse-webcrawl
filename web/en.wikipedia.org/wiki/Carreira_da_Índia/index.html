Carreira da Índia

The Carreira da Índia ("India Run") was the annual maritime route sailed by Portuguese fleets between Lisbon and Goa, the capital of Portuguese India, from shortly after Vasco da Gama's pioneering voyage of 1497–99 until the mid-19th century. It was the lifeline of the Portuguese seaborne empire in Asia and for a century the principal conduit of the Asian spice trade to Europe.

The route

Fleets usually left Lisbon in March or April to catch the monsoon across the Indian Ocean. The outward voyage swung far into the Atlantic to pick up the westerlies around the Cape of Good Hope, then proceeded up the East African coast, often calling at Mozambique Island, before crossing to India with the southwest monsoon, arriving in September. The return voyage left Goa in December or January on the northeast monsoon. A full round trip took roughly a year and a half, and departures and arrivals were tightly governed by the monsoon windows.

Organisation and cargo

The run was a crown monopoly administered by the Casa da Índia in Lisbon. The great carracks, or naus, grew to 1,000 tons and more; they carried silver, copper, wine, and olive oil eastward and returned laden with pepper, spices, silk, porcelain, and other Asian goods. Offices and shares of cargo space in the fleet were valuable royal favours.

Decline

Shipwrecks on the unforgiving Natal coast were frequent — the wrecks of ships such as the São João (1552) and São Bento (1554) became famous in Portuguese literature. From the early 1600s Dutch and English competition eroded the monopoly, and Portuguese India trade shrank; the Carreira continued on a reduced scale into the age of steam before finally ending in the mid-19th century.
