The Livonian War (1558–1583) was a prolonged conflict over control of Old Livonia — present-day Estonia and Latvia — fought between the Tsardom of Russia under Ivan the Terrible and a shifting coalition of Denmark–Norway, Sweden and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. It destroyed the medieval Livonian Confederation and redrew the map of the Baltic for a century.

Course of the war

Ivan IV invaded in January 1558, demanding tribute and a corridor to the Baltic; Russian forces quickly took Narva and Dorpat (Tartu). Unable to resist, the Livonian estates sought foreign protectors: Reval (Tallinn) and northern Estonia submitted to Sweden in 1561, while Gotthard Kettler, last Master of the Livonian Order, secularised his state as the Duchy of Courland under Polish suzerainty. Denmark purchased the bishopric of Ösel–Wiek.

From 1570 Ivan sponsored a puppet "Kingdom of Livonia" under the Danish prince Magnus. Polish–Lithuanian intervention under Stephen Báthory after 1578 reversed Russian gains: Báthory's campaigns retook Polotsk and besieged Pskov, while Sweden seized Narva and Ingria in 1581. The Truce of Yam-Zapolsky (1582) with Poland and the Treaty of Plussa (1583) with Sweden ended the war; Russia lost all its Livonian conquests and its Ingrian coast, and was cut off from the Baltic.

Consequences

The war devastated Livonia through siege, famine and plague. It bankrupted Russia's resources, contributed to the chaos of the Time of Troubles, and established Sweden and Poland–Lithuania as the dominant Baltic powers until the Great Northern War.
