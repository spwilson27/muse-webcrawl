Infant respiratory distress syndrome (IRDS), also called respiratory distress syndrome of newborn, or increasingly surfactant deficiency disorder, and previously called hyaline membrane disease, is a syndrome in premature infants caused by developmental insufficiency of pulmonary surfactant production and structural immaturity of the lungs.

Pathophysiology

Surfactant, produced by type II alveolar cells from about the 24th week of gestation, lowers surface tension in the alveoli and prevents them from collapsing at the end of expiration. Without enough surfactant, alveoli collapse (atelectasis), the lungs become stiff, and the work of breathing rises sharply. The resulting hypoxemia and acidosis further impair surfactant production, creating a worsening cycle. Protein-rich fluid leaking into damaged alveoli forms the "hyaline membranes" seen histologically.

Signs and diagnosis

Symptoms appear within minutes to hours of birth: rapid breathing (tachypnoea), grunting, nasal flaring, chest wall recession, and cyanosis. Chest X-ray classically shows a diffuse "ground-glass" appearance with air bronchograms and low lung volumes. The risk rises steeply with decreasing gestational age; nearly all infants born before 28 weeks are affected without preventive treatment.

Treatment and prevention

Antenatal corticosteroids given to mothers at risk of preterm delivery greatly reduce incidence and severity by accelerating lung maturity. Treatment includes supplemental oxygen, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), mechanical ventilation when needed, and intratracheal exogenous surfactant — animal-derived preparations such as poractant alfa — which transformed outcomes after its introduction in the late 1980s. Patrick Bouvier Kennedy, son of US President John F. Kennedy, died of the disease in 1963 at two days old, a case that spurred research and the development of neonatology.
