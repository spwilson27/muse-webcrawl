Christian denomination

A Christian denomination is a distinct religious body within Christianity that comprises all church congregations of the same kind, identifiable by traits such as a name, a particular history, organization, leadership, theological doctrine, worship style, and sometimes a founder. It is a sect that has become a self-governing body with a widely established identity.

The term is frequently used in a broader sense to refer to the whole of a religion's family of churches, sometimes including historic schisms. Christian denominations generally share a common heritage and set of essential doctrines, but may differ on secondary issues.

Major branches

Christianity can be taxonomically divided into several major branches, which themselves include many denominations:

  • Catholicism — the largest branch, centred on communion with the Bishop of Rome (the Pope); includes the Latin Church and 23 Eastern Catholic Churches.
  • Eastern Orthodoxy — the second-largest, comprising autocephalous national churches in communion with one another.
  • Oriental Orthodoxy — churches that accept only the first three ecumenical councils.
  • Protestantism — originating in the 16th-century Reformation, including Lutherans, Reformed, Anglicans, Baptists, Methodists, Adventists, and Pentecostals.
  • Restorationism — movements such as the Latter Day Saint movement and Jehovah's Witnesses that seek to restore primitive Christianity.

Numbers

Estimates of the number of distinct Christian denominations worldwide vary widely, with figures in the tens of thousands often cited by reference works such as the World Christian Encyclopedia, though such counts depend heavily on definitions and treat many independent congregations as separate denominations.
