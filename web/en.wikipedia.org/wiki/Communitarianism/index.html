Communitarianism
Key figures: Michael Sandel, Alasdair MacIntyre, Charles Taylor, Michael Walzer, Amitai Etzioni

Communitarianism is a philosophy emphasising the connection between the individual and the community, arguing that a person's identity and moral outlook are shaped by social, cultural and historical contexts, in contrast to liberal theories that begin from an unencumbered self.

Origins

The academic movement emerged in the 1980s as a critique of John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971). Michael Sandel's Liberalism and the Limits of Justice (1982), Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue (1981), Charles Taylor's essays on atomism and recognition, and Michael Walzer's Spheres of Justice (1983) are its founding texts.

Responsive communitarianism

In the 1990s Amitai Etzioni and others founded a more policy-oriented responsive communitarian movement, balancing rights with responsibilities and promoting civil society, character education and community policing. It influenced the "Third Way" politics of the Clinton and Blair eras.

Criticism

Critics warn communitarianism can justify majority tyranny, conservatism, or relativism, and question whether appeals to community values resolve hard moral disagreements.
