Webbed toes

Webbed toes are the common name for syndactyly affecting the feet. The condition results when two or more toes fail to separate fully during fetal development, leaving them joined by skin, and in some cases by soft tissue or bone. It is one of the most common congenital limb differences, occurring in roughly 1 in every 2,000–3,000 live births.

Presentation and causes

The webbing most often affects the second and third toes and may be partial or complete. Syndactyly usually occurs as an isolated trait, often inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with variable expression, but it can also be part of genetic syndromes such as Apert syndrome or Poland syndrome. In normal development the webbing between digits regresses by programmed cell death; failure of this process leaves the fused digits.

Treatment

Webbed toes rarely impair walking and, unlike webbed fingers, usually require no treatment. Surgery is sometimes performed for cosmetic reasons or when the fusion causes deformity, using skin grafts or local flaps to deepen the web space. In animals, webbed feet are a normal adaptation for swimming in species such as ducks, otters and frogs.
