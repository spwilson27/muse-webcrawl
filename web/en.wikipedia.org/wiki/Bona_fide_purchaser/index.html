Bona fide purchaser

Equity protects a bona fide purchaser for value without notice against prior equitable interests.
Bona fide purchaser
FieldProperty law, equity, commercial law
Also calledBFP; innocent purchaser for value; "equity's darling"
RequirementsGood faith, value given, no notice of the prior claim
EffectTakes free of certain prior equitable interests or defects in title

A bona fide purchaser (BFP) — referred to in full as a bona fide purchaser for value without notice — is a person who purchases property in good faith, for valuable consideration, and without notice of a prior claim or equitable interest in the property. Because equity regards such a purchaser as blameless, a BFP generally takes the property free of the earlier equitable interest; the doctrine is summarised by the maxim that the bona fide purchaser is "equity's darling".

Elements

Three elements are required. Good faith: the purchase must be honest, without fraud or collusion. Value: the purchaser must give valuable consideration — money or money's worth — though adequacy of the price is usually judged only as evidence of good faith. Absence of notice: the purchaser must not have actual notice, constructive notice (what a reasonable inspection or inquiry would have revealed, including matters on a public register) or imputed notice (knowledge of the purchaser's agent) of the prior interest, at the time the purchase money is paid.

Applications

In land law the doctrine protects buyers against unregistered equitable interests such as trusts and equitable mortgages, subject in registered-land systems (such as Torrens title and the English Land Registration Act 2002) to statutory rules on overriding interests. In commercial law the analogous holder-in-due-course doctrine protects good-faith purchasers of negotiable instruments, and the UCC protects buyers in the ordinary course of business. A recurring exception to the general "nemo dat" rule (no one can give a better title than they have), BFP protection promotes security of transactions; conversely, purchasers of stolen goods or from a seller with a void (as opposed to voidable) title usually gain no protection at common law.
