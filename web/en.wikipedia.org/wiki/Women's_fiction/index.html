Women's fiction

Women's fiction is an umbrella term and publishing category for novels marketed primarily to women, generally centred on the emotional lives, relationships and personal growth of (usually female) protagonists. As defined by organisations such as the Women's Fiction Writers Association, it is a commercial category in which the protagonist's emotional journey and transformation drive the plot, whether or not a romantic subplot is present; a happy romantic resolution is not required, which distinguishes it from the romance genre proper.

Scope and definitions

The label covers a broad range of tones and sub-genres: contemporary family dramas, literary novels about women's lives, historical fiction about female experience, "book club fiction", and at the lighter end "chick lit" — a term coined in the mid-1990s around works such as Helen Fielding's Bridget Jones's Diary (1996). Bestselling modern practitioners frequently associated with the category include Jojo Moyes, Liane Moriarty, Kristin Hannah, Jodi Picoult, Celeste Ng and Taylor Jenkins Reid.

History

Women readers were the principal market for the novel from its 18th-century beginnings, and writers such as Jane Austen, the Brontës and George Eliot shaped the form while sometimes being dismissed by critics as producers of "lady's novels". In the 1970s, second-wave feminism inspired both reissues of forgotten women writers (e.g. by Virago Press, founded 1973) and popular novels of female self-discovery such as Erica Jong's Fear of Flying (1973) and Marilyn French's The Women's Room (1977).

Debate over the term

The category is commercially powerful — women buy the large majority of fiction — but contested. Critics and many authors argue that "women's fiction" ghettoises serious work by women while comparable novels by men are marketed simply as "fiction", and that no equivalent "men's fiction" shelf category exists. Others defend the label as a useful discovery tool for readers and a recognition of women's experiences as central subject matter rather than niche material.
