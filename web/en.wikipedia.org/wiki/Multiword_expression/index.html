Multiword expression

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Multiword expression (MWE)
FieldLinguistics; natural language processing
DefinitionA sequence of words behaving as a single lexical or semantic unit
Examples"kick the bucket"; "by and large"; "traffic light"; "take into account"
Key propertyNon-compositionality (full or partial)

A multiword expression (MWE) is a sequence of two or more words that behaves, in some respect, as a single unit — lexically, syntactically or semantically. Classic examples include idioms such as kick the bucket ("die"), fixed phrases like by and large, compound nouns such as traffic light, phrasal verbs like give up, and proper-name-like collocations. Their defining puzzle is non-compositionality: the meaning or behaviour of the whole cannot be straightforwardly predicted from the meanings of the parts, so a language user's vocabulary must store them, in some sense, as entries in their own right.

Types and properties

MWEs form a spectrum. At one end stand completely frozen idioms, which resist modification (*the bucket was kicked does not mean "died"); at the other, productive collocations whose parts remain partly independent. Scholars classify them into lexicalised phrases (idioms, compounds, irreversible binomials such as salt and pepper) and institutionalised phrases (statistically frequent but compositional combinations such as traffic jam). Different tests — substitutability, internal modification, syntactic flexibility — reveal different degrees of "unit-hood", and borderline cases are endemic.

In language technology

Multiword expressions are a notorious pain point for natural language processing: machine translation, parsing and information retrieval systems that treat each word independently mistranslate idioms, mis-segment compounds and miss named entities. Dedicated MWE identification and annotation (for example in the PARSEME shared tasks) has therefore become a recognised subfield of computational linguistics, and modern large language models are often evaluated on how gracefully they handle idiomatic and formulaic language.
