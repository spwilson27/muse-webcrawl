South Tyrol (German: Südtirol; Italian: Alto Adige; Ladin: Südtirol) is an autonomous province in northern Italy, the northernmost province of the country. Together with the province of Trento it forms the autonomous region of Trentino-Alto Adige/Südtirol. Its capital is Bolzano.

Geography and population

The province occupies the southern slopes of the main Alpine ridge, including parts of the Dolomites in the east and the Ortler massif in the west. It is one of Italy's wealthiest provinces, with an economy based on tourism, agriculture (notably apples and wine) and small industry. A majority of inhabitants — roughly 62 percent in the 2011 census — declare German as their first language; about 23 percent speak Italian, concentrated in the cities, and about 4 percent speak Ladin in the Dolomite valleys.

History

Historically part of the Austrian County of Tyrol, the territory was annexed by Italy after the First World War under the Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919), despite its overwhelmingly German-speaking population. The Fascist regime pursued forced Italianisation, banning German in schools and public life and encouraging Italian immigration. The 1939 "Option Agreement" between Hitler and Mussolini offered German speakers emigration to the Reich or assimilation; most who opted to leave never did, or returned after the war.

After 1945 the Gruber–De Gasperi Agreement promised autonomy, but its initial implementation through the combined region with Italian-majority Trentino disappointed German speakers, fuelling protest and, in the 1960s, bombings by separatist activists. The Second Autonomy Statute of 1972 granted far-reaching self-government to the province itself — legislative powers, proportional allocation of public jobs and housing among language groups, and mother-tongue schooling — and is widely regarded as a successful model of minority protection and conflict resolution. The dispute was formally declared closed before the United Nations in 1992.
