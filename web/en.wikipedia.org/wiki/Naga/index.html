Naga

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism, a Naga (Sanskrit: नाग, romanized: Nāga) is a divine or semi-divine race of half-human, half-serpent beings that reside in the netherworld (Patala) and can occasionally take human or part-human form, or are depicted wholly as snakes. A female naga is a Nagi, or Nagini.

Nagas are potent, handsome, proud, and dangerous beings. They can be benevolent, neutral, or malevolent toward humans; they are often associated with waters — rivers, lakes, seas, and wells — and are guardians of treasure. Their domain is the enchanted underworld realm called Naga-loka or Patala, filled with gems, gold, and other earthly treasures.

In Hinduism

Nagas are prominently featured in Hindu epics and Puranas. Shesha, the great serpent on whom the god Vishnu reclines, is the king of the nagas in many traditions. Vasuki, the serpent king, formed the churning rope in the Samudra Manthana, the churning of the ocean of milk. Manasa is the goddess of snakes, worshipped mainly in Bengal and northeastern India. The epic Mahabharata describes the enmity between the nagas, led by Takshaka, and the Kuru king Janamejaya, who performed a great snake sacrifice (sarpa satra) to avenge his father's death.

In Buddhism

In Buddhist tradition, the naga king Mucalinda protected the Buddha from a storm by sheltering him beneath his hoods in the weeks after his enlightenment. Nagas appear in the Jataka tales and are regarded as guardians of the Dharma; the Buddha is said to have converted many nagas. In Tibetan and East Asian Buddhism, nagas are often conflated with the Chinese dragon (long).

In Southeast Asia

Nagas are deeply embedded in the cultures of Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and Myanmar. In Cambodia, the multi-headed naga bridges at Angkor Wat recall the mythic Naga princess, considered the ancestress of the Khmer people. In Thailand and Laos, nagas are believed to inhabit the Mekong River and are associated with water and fertility; the annual Naga fireball phenomenon on the Mekong is attributed to them. In Malay folklore, nagas are many-headed dragons, and in Javanese tradition, the naga is a great green serpent deity associated with the goddess of the southern sea.

In Jainism

Nagas appear frequently in Jain iconography, most notably as the protective canopy over the Tirthankara Parshvanatha, who is sheltered by the serpent king Dharanendra.
