A planetary nebula is a type of emission nebula consisting of an expanding, glowing shell of ionised gas ejected from red giant stars late in their lives. The term "planetary nebula" is a misnomer: these objects have nothing to do with planets, but were so named because their round, greenish disks reminded eighteenth-century astronomers of the planets Uranus and Neptune as seen through small telescopes.

Formation

Planetary nebulae form when a low- to intermediate-mass star (roughly 0.8 to 8 solar masses) exhausts the hydrogen in its core and passes through the red giant and asymptotic giant branch (AGB) phases. Pulsations and a strong stellar wind expel the star's outer envelope. As the exposed core contracts and heats up — eventually reaching temperatures above 30,000 K — its ultraviolet radiation ionises the ejected gas, making it glow. The exposed core becomes a white dwarf at the centre of the expanding shell.

Characteristics

A typical planetary nebula is roughly one light-year in diameter and expands at about 20–50 km/s. The phase is brief by astronomical standards, lasting only several tens of thousands of years before the gas disperses into the interstellar medium and the central star cools. Planetary nebulae are important sources of carbon, nitrogen, and other elements, returning enriched material from which new stars and planets can form. Well-known examples include the Ring Nebula (M57), the Dumbbell Nebula (M27), the Helix Nebula, and the Cat's Eye Nebula.

Observation history

The first planetary nebula discovered was the Dumbbell Nebula, recorded by Charles Messier in 1764. William Herschel coined the term after observing the Saturn Nebula, whose appearance reminded him of a planet. Spectroscopic work by William Huggins in the 1860s showed that these nebulae are glowing gas rather than unresolved star clusters, and in the twentieth century their connection to dying stars and white dwarfs became clear.

Shapes

Although many planetary nebulae appear round, a large fraction are elliptical or bipolar, shaped by binary companions, magnetic fields, and fast stellar winds colliding with the slower AGB wind. Complex structures such as jets, knots, and faint outer halos are common in deep images from the Hubble Space Telescope.
