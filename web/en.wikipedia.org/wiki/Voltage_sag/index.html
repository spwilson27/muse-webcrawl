Voltage sag

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An RMS voltage waveform exhibiting a short-duration sag.

A voltage sag (in British English, voltage dip) is a short-duration reduction in the root-mean-square (RMS) voltage of an electrical power system, typically lasting from half a cycle to a few seconds. According to IEEE Standard 1159, a sag is a decrease to between 0.1 and 0.9 per-unit of nominal voltage for durations of 0.5 cycles to 1 minute; longer or deeper reductions are classified differently.

Causes

Voltage sags are most often caused by short-circuit faults on the power system — for example from lightning strikes, equipment failures or contact with trees — which are cleared by protective devices within a few cycles. They can also be caused by the starting of large motors and other heavy loads drawing high inrush currents.

Effects

Although a sag may last only a fraction of a second, sensitive equipment can malfunction: contactors may drop out, adjustable-speed drives and industrial processes may trip, and computers may reset. Sags are generally considered the most costly power quality problem for industrial customers, because a single event can halt an entire production line.

Mitigation

Mitigation measures include uninterruptible power supplies (UPS), dynamic voltage restorers, ferroresonant transformers, and designing equipment to ride through sags in accordance with immunity curves such as the ITIC (CBEMA) curve and the SEMI F47 standard for semiconductor manufacturing tools.
