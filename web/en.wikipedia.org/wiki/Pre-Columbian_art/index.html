Pre-Columbian art

Pre-Columbian art refers to the visual arts of the indigenous peoples of the Americas before the arrival of Christopher Columbus in 1492 and the ensuing European colonisation. It encompasses the art of Mesoamerica, the Andes, the Caribbean, North America and the intermediate regions, produced over several millennia in media including stone, ceramic, gold, textiles, feathers and jade.

Mesoamerica

The Olmec of the Gulf Coast carved colossal basalt heads and jade figurines from around 1200 BC. Later cultures included the Maya, renowned for painted ceramics, stucco sculpture, carved stelae and murals such as those at Bonampak; the Zapotec and Mixtec of Oaxaca, masters of goldwork and illuminated codices; Teotihuacan, with its monumental pyramids and mural painting; and the Aztec (Mexica), whose sculptors produced massive stone works such as the Sun Stone and the Coatlicue statue.

Andean art

In the Andes, the Chavín, Paracas, Nazca, Moche, Tiwanaku, Wari, Chimú and Inca cultures produced fine ceramics, metalwork and some of the world's most sophisticated textiles. Moche portrait vessels, Nazca polychrome pottery and lines, and Inca gold and silver objects are among the best-known examples. Textiles, preserved by the dry coastal climate, were a central medium of status and ritual.

North America and other regions

North American traditions include Ancestral Pueblo pottery and architecture, Mississippian shell and copper work, Northwest Coast carved totem poles and masks, and the ceramics of the Intermediate Area and Amazonia.

Legacy

Much pre-Columbian art was destroyed or looted during the conquest, and questions of repatriation and museum ownership remain active. Major collections are held in Mexico City's National Museum of Anthropology, the Museo de América in Madrid and the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.
