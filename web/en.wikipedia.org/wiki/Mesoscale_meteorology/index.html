Mesoscale meteorology

Mesoscale meteorology is the study of weather systems smaller in size than synoptic scale systems but larger than microscale and storm-scale cumulus systems. Horizontal dimensions generally range from around 5 kilometres to several hundred kilometres. Examples of mesoscale weather systems include sea breezes, squall lines, and mesoscale convective complexes.

Radar view of mesoscale weather systems

Scale divisions

Following the classification proposed by Fujita and refined by Orlanski, the mesoscale is subdivided into meso-alpha (200–2,000 km, sometimes considered partly synoptic), meso-beta (20–200 km), and meso-gamma (2–20 km) ranges. Phenomena studied include thunderstorms and supercells, mesoscale convective systems, land–sea breeze circulations, mountain waves and gap winds, fronts at fine scale, and urban heat island circulations.

Methods

Mesoscale research relies heavily on remote sensing — Doppler weather radar, satellite imagery, wind profilers, and surface mesonetworks — because conventional synoptic observing networks are too coarse to resolve mesoscale features. Since the 1980s, field campaigns such as VORTEX (studying tornadoes) and numerical models with kilometre-scale grid spacing, such as the WRF model, have transformed the field. Mesoscale analysis underpins modern severe-weather forecasting and nowcasting.
