Multi-junction solar cell

A multi-junction (MJ) solar cell is a solar cell with multiple p–n junctions made of different semiconductor materials. Each material's p–n junction produces electric current in response to different wavelengths of light. By stacking subcells with decreasing band gaps, a multi-junction cell can capture a larger portion of the solar spectrum than a single-junction cell, achieving substantially higher conversion efficiencies.

Principle of operation

In a single-junction cell, photons with energy below the band gap pass through unused, while photons with energy far above the band gap lose their excess energy as heat (thermalization). These two losses limit single-junction cells to the Shockley–Queisser limit of roughly 33% efficiency under unconcentrated sunlight. A multi-junction cell divides the spectrum among subcells: the top cell, with the largest band gap, absorbs high-energy photons, and each successive cell absorbs progressively lower-energy photons. This reduces both thermalization losses and below-band-gap transmission losses.

Materials and structure

High-efficiency multi-junction cells are typically built from III–V compound semiconductors such as gallium arsenide (GaAs), indium gallium phosphide (InGaP), and germanium (Ge). A common triple-junction design stacks InGaP (band gap ~1.9 eV), GaAs (~1.4 eV), and Ge (~0.67 eV) subcells connected in series by tunnel junctions. Lattice matching between layers is critical; metamorphic and wafer-bonding techniques allow combinations of materials that cannot be grown directly on one another.

Efficiency records and applications

Multi-junction cells hold the world records for photovoltaic conversion efficiency: devices with four or more junctions have exceeded 47% efficiency under concentrated sunlight. Because III–V cells are expensive to manufacture, they are used mainly where efficiency and reliability outweigh cost — in space applications, powering satellites and spacecraft, and in concentrator photovoltaic (CPV) systems on the ground, where lenses or mirrors focus sunlight onto small, high-efficiency cells.

Multi-junction technology powered many notable spacecraft, including deep-space probes and Mars rovers, where mass and area constraints make efficiency paramount.
