Salter–Harris fracture

A Salter–Harris fracture is a fracture that involves the epiphyseal plate (growth plate) of a long bone in a child or adolescent. Because the growth plate is weaker than surrounding ligaments and bone, such injuries are common in paediatric trauma—accounting for a substantial share of childhood fractures—and require care to avoid disturbing future growth. The classification was described by Robert B. Salter and W. Robert Harris in 1963.

Classification

Type I: a transverse fracture straight through the growth plate, separating epiphysis from metaphysis. Type II: the fracture runs through the plate and exits through the metaphysis, producing the Thurstan-Holland fragment; this is the most common type. Type III: an intra-articular fracture splitting the epiphysis and extending through the plate. Type IV: the fracture crosses metaphysis, plate and epiphysis. Type V: a compression or crush injury of the plate, often diagnosed retrospectively. A mnemonic for the types is "SALTR": Straight across, Above, Lower, Through, Rammed. Higher types carry progressively worse prognoses for growth disturbance.

Treatment and prognosis

Most type I and II fractures are treated by closed reduction and casting. Types III and IV usually require anatomical reduction, often with surgical fixation, because they involve the joint surface. The main complication is premature growth arrest, which can cause limb-length discrepancy or angular deformity and may require later corrective surgery.
