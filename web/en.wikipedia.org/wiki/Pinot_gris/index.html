Pinot gris

Pinot gris (French: [pino ɡʁi]), also sold as Pinot grigio in its Italian style, is a white wine grape variety that originated as a colour mutation of Pinot noir. Its berries are greyish-blue to pinkish-brown, hence "gris" (grey). Two broad styles exist: the rich, spicy, sometimes off-dry wines of Alsace, and the light, crisp, dry Pinot grigio of north-eastern Italy.

History

Pinot gris was known in Burgundy in the Middle Ages and spread to Switzerland and, by the fourteenth century, to Hungary (where it became Szürkebarát, "grey monk"). In the eighteenth century it was planted in Germany as Ruländer. In Alsace it was once called Tokay d'Alsace, a name phased out under EU rules to protect Hungary's Tokaji.

Styles

Alsace Pinot gris is full-bodied and richly textured, with flavours of ripe pear, honey, smoke and spice; it ranges from dry to lusciously sweet in vendanges tardives and sélection de grains nobles bottlings, and is one of the four noble grapes of Alsace. Italian Pinot grigio, especially from Alto Adige and Friuli, is picked earlier to give a light, neutral, crisp dry wine — a style that became hugely popular in export markets, notably the United States, from the 1990s onward.

Regions

Besides Alsace and northern Italy, significant plantings exist in Germany (Grauburgunder), Austria, Hungary, Slovenia, Romania, and in the New World in Oregon, California, New Zealand and Australia, where both styles are made.
