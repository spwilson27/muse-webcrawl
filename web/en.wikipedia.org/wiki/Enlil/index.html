Enlil (Sumerian, from en "lord" + lil "wind, air, spirit") is the god of wind, air, earth and storms in the Mesopotamian pantheon, and for most of the third and second millennia BC the effective head of the pantheon — the executive power who decreed destinies, granted kingship and commanded the gods below the remote sky god Anu. His temple, the Ekur ("Mountain House") at Nippur, was the religious centre of Sumer, and it was Enlil's favour, proclaimed by the priests of Nippur, that legitimised the rulers of successive dynasties.

Myths

In Sumerian cosmology Enlil separated heaven from earth and brought up the first plants; with his hoe he created mankind's tools and, in the Song of the Hoe, fashioned the first humans in the "flesh-growing site." The myth Enlil and Ninlil tells how the young god, banished to the underworld for seizing the goddess Ninlil, follows her in disguise and fathers the moon god Nanna and the underworld deities Nergal and Ninazu. Enlil is also the stern author of the great Flood: in the Atrahasis epic and the Sumerian flood story, enraged by the noise and overpopulation of humankind, he sends plague, drought and finally the deluge itself, and is afterwards reconciled to human survival only through Enki's advocacy and the sacrifice of the flood hero. Other tales show a less majestic side — in Enlil and Sud he woos his wife by letter, and when his emblems of power are stolen by the Anzu bird, his son Ninurta must recover them.

Cult and political role

Enlil's cult at Nippur long outranked that of any city god: Sumerian kings from the Early Dynastic period onward sought investiture at the Ekur, and even Babylon's rise did not wholly displace Nippur's prestige. His sacred number was 50, and his name became a title: Babylon's Marduk assumed the "Enlilship" over the gods and was hymned as the "Enlil of the gods," while Assyria's Assur was similarly styled an Enlil. Hymns praise Enlil as the "Great Mountain," whose decrees cannot be altered and whose word decides the fate of every land.

Decline

As Babylon and then Assyria dominated, Enlil's independent role shrank in favour of Marduk and Assur, though Nippur remained a major cult city into the Hellenistic era. His character — a god both creator and destroyer, dispenser of kingship and of catastrophe — left a lasting imprint on the later storm gods of the ancient Near East.
