Non-refoulement

Non-refoulement (from French refouler, "to drive back") is a fundamental principle of international refugee law which prohibits a country receiving asylum seekers from returning them to a country where they would face persecution or serious harm. It is regarded as a rule of customary international law and a cornerstone of refugee protection.

Legal sources

The principle is stated most prominently in Article 33 of the 1951 Refugee Convention: "No Contracting State shall expel or return ('refouler') a refugee in any manner whatsoever to the frontiers of territories where his life or freedom would be threatened on account of his race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion."

Related prohibitions appear in the Convention Against Torture (Article 3), which bars return to torture without exception, and in human-rights jurisprudence under the European Convention on Human Rights, notably Soering v. United Kingdom (1989) and Hirsi Jamaa v. Italy (2012), the latter covering interdiction at sea.

Exceptions and controversies

Article 33(2) of the Refugee Convention permits return where the refugee is a danger to the security of the host country or has been convicted of a particularly serious crime. Contemporary controversies concern "pushbacks" at land and sea borders, offshore processing, safe-third-country transfers, and whether non-refoulement applies extraterritorially — an issue in Sale v. Haitian Centers Council (1993), where the US Supreme Court held it did not apply to interdiction on the high seas, a reading widely criticised by scholars and UNHCR.
