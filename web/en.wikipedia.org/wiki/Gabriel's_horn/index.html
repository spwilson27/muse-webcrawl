Gabriel's horn

Gabriel's horn (also called Torricelli's trumpet) is a particular geometric figure that has infinite surface area but finite volume. The name refers to the tradition identifying the archangel Gabriel as the angel who blows the horn to announce Judgement Day. The properties of this figure were first studied by Italian mathematician Evangelista Torricelli in the 17th century.

Gabriel's horn is formed by rotating y = 1/x about the x-axis for x ≥ 1.

Definition

Gabriel's horn is formed by taking the graph of y = 1/x on the domain x ≥ 1 and rotating it about the x-axis. Using calculus, its volume can be computed with the disk method:

V = π ∫1∞ (1/x²) dx = π

while its surface area is given by an integral that diverges:

A = 2π ∫1∞ (1/x) √(1 + 1/x⁴) dx > 2π ∫1∞ (1/x) dx = ∞

The painter's paradox

This result gives rise to the so-called painter's paradox: the horn could be filled with π cubic units of paint, yet painting its inner surface would require an infinite amount of paint. The resolution lies in the idealised mathematical nature of the figure — a physical coat of paint has nonzero thickness and the horn's opening narrows below molecular scales.

History

Torricelli's 1641 result, obtained using Cavalieri's method of indivisibles before the invention of calculus, surprised contemporaries and contributed to debates about the nature of infinity involving figures such as Thomas Hobbes and John Wallis.
