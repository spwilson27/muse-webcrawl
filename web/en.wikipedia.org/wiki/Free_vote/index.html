Free vote

A free vote in a legislature is a vote in which legislators are not obliged to vote as directed by their party, but may vote instead according to their own conscience or the preferences of their constituents. It is also known as a conscience vote.

Free votes are the exception to the normal rule of party discipline, under which the whips instruct members how to vote and may punish defiance.

Usage

Free votes are most commonly granted on questions regarded as matters of personal morality or ethics rather than party policy. Subjects that have traditionally been decided by free votes in Westminster-style parliaments include:

  • Abortion law
  • Capital punishment
  • Assisted dying and euthanasia
  • Embryo research
  • Marriage law, including same-sex marriage

By country

United Kingdom

In the Parliament of the United Kingdom, free votes have been held on issues such as the abolition of the death penalty, abortion reform in the 1960s, hunting with dogs, and assisted dying bills. On a free vote the government does not treat defeat as a matter of confidence.

Canada

In Canada, free votes are comparatively rare because of strong party discipline, though governments have occasionally permitted them on moral questions.

Australia and New Zealand

In Australia, conscience votes have been held on euthanasia and abortion. In New Zealand, many alcohol-licensing and social bills have traditionally been treated as conscience issues.
