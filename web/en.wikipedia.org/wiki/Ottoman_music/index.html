Ottoman music

Ottoman music (Turkish: Osmanlı müziği) — also called Ottoman classical music or Turkish classical music — is the courtly art-music tradition developed in the Ottoman Empire from about the 15th century to the empire's end, and continued, transformed, in the Republic of Turkey. It is a monophonic, melodic tradition built on the makam system of melodic modes and the usul system of rhythmic cycles.

Instruments and forms

Core instruments include the tanbur (long-necked lute), ney (end-blown reed flute), oud (short-necked lute), kemençe (bowed fiddle), kanun (plucked zither), and later the Turkish violin and clarinet. The grand concert form is the fasıl, a suite in a single makam progressing through forms such as the instrumental peşrev and saz semaisi and vocal forms including the kâr, beste, ağır semai, şarkı and yürük semai. Military music of the Janissary mehter bands influenced European composers during the 18th-century "alla turca" fashion. Religious music of mosques and Sufi lodges — especially the Mevlevi ayin ceremony — forms a related branch.

Theory and transmission

Makams define characteristic scales, melodic progressions (seyir) and modulation conventions; modern theory, codified by Suphi Ezgi, Hüseyin Sadettin Arel and others in the early 20th century, divides the octave into 53 commas and names about 24 unequal intervals in common use. Historically the music was transmitted orally through the meşk master-pupil system; notation systems were developed by figures such as Prince Cantemir (Dimitrie Cantemir), whose early-18th-century collection is a key source for the repertoire of his era.

Composers and modern history

Noted composers include Itri (Buhurizade Mustafa Itri, 17th century), Dede Efendi, Tanburi Cemil Bey and Münir Nurettin Selçuk. Ottoman music was multi-ethnic, shaped by Turkish, Greek, Armenian, Jewish and Arab musicians. After 1923 the Turkish Republic restricted its broadcasting for a period (1934–36) while promoting Western music, but the tradition survived through conservatories, state ensembles and radio, and it remains a living art in Turkey.
