Maya stelae

[Image: Stela at Copán, Honduras, depicting the ruler Waxaklajuun Ubaah Kʼawiil]
A Classic-period stela at Copán

Maya stelae (singular stela) are monuments that were fashioned by the Maya civilization of ancient Mesoamerica. They consist of tall, sculpted stone shafts and are often associated with low circular stones referred to as altars. Stelae were normally erected in the great plazas of Maya cities, frequently paired with altars and placed before pyramids and palace platforms.

Function

Stelae were essentially royal billboards: they commemorated rulers and legitimised dynasties by depicting kings (and occasionally queens) in elaborate regalia, accompanied by hieroglyphic texts recording births, accessions, wars, marriages and ritual events such as period-ending celebrations in the Long Count calendar. Many stelae were erected to mark the passing of a k'atun (a period of 7,200 days) or its subdivisions. The carved dates on stelae provided the backbone for reconstructing Classic-period Maya history once the script was deciphered in the later twentieth century.

Distribution and notable examples

Stelae are characteristic of the southern Maya lowlands during the Classic period (c. AD 250–900), at cities including Tikal, Calakmul, Copán, Quiriguá, Piedras Negras, Yaxchilán and Palenque. The tallest Maya stela, Stela E at Quiriguá in Guatemala, stands about 10.6 metres above ground and depicts the ruler K'ak' Tiliw Chan Yopaat. The earliest dated lowland stela, Stela 29 at Tikal (AD 292), bears the earliest Long Count date yet found in the Maya lowlands. Some carved stelae traditions reach back into the Late Preclassic, for example at Kaminaljuyu and Takalik Abaj in the highlands and Pacific piedmont.

Materials and style

Stelae were carved from locally available stone — limestone in most of the lowlands, sandstone at Quiriguá, and fine-grained trachyte at Copán, whose sculptors produced exceptionally high-relief, almost three-dimensional portraits. Many were originally painted, often in red pigment. In the northern lowlands of Yucatán, stelae are comparatively plain, while at Puuc sites carved stelae are rarer.
