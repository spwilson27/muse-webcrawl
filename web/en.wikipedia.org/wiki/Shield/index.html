A shield is a piece of personal armour held in the hand, which may or may not be strapped to the wrist or forearm. Shields are used to intercept specific attacks, whether from close-ranging weaponry or projectiles such as arrows, by means of active blocks, as well as to provide passive protection by closing one or more lines of engagement during combat.

Shields vary greatly in size and shape, ranging from large panels that protect the user's whole body to small models that were intended for hand-to-hand combat use only. Shields also vary a great deal in thickness; whereas some shields were made of relatively deep, absorbent, wooden planking to protect soldiers from the impact of spears and crossbow bolts, others were thinner and lighter and designed mainly to deflect blade strikes. Finally, shields range from largely circular designs to rectangular, triangular, and other forms, often with a distinctive boss or umbo at the centre.

History

Prehistory and the ancient world

The oldest surviving shields are those found in bog deposits and burials from Bronze Age Europe, made of wood, hide, or bronze. The large round bronze shields of the Greek aspis type, carried by hoplites, were central to the phalanx formation: each man's shield overlapped and protected his neighbour, making the formation far stronger than the sum of its parts. Roman legionaries carried the curved rectangular scutum, used both individually and in the testudo (tortoise) formation against missile fire.

Medieval shields

In the early Middle Ages, round shields of linden wood reinforced with iron were common across northern Europe. By the 11th and 12th centuries the elongated "kite" shield, which protected a mounted knight's leg as well as his torso, had become standard in western Europe, as depicted in the Bayeux Tapestry. With the advent of heraldry, the shield became the principal surface for displaying a coat of arms, giving rise to the "heater" shield shape familiar from coats of arms today. The tall, free-standing pavise was used by crossbowmen and archers as portable cover.

Decline and modern use

The spread of firearms and of full plate armour made the hand shield largely obsolete in European warfare by the 17th century, although the small buckler survived in fencing for longer, and shields such as the Scottish targe persisted into the 18th century. In the modern era, shields remain in use by police and riot-control units, typically made of transparent polycarbonate, and ballistic shields are employed by special weapons teams. Many cultures outside Europe retained shield traditions much longer, including the hide shields of the Zulu and Maasai, the rattan shields of Southeast Asia, and the painted hide shields of the Plains peoples of North America.

Construction

Shields were most commonly constructed of wood — often several layers of planks glued with the grain running in alternating directions, an early form of plywood — covered with leather, canvas, or metal. Metal reinforcing bands and a central iron boss protected the hand grip. Some shields were faced with bronze or iron, or in prestige examples were decorated with enamel, painting, or precious metal.

Types

  • Buckler – a small round shield held in the fist, popular in the late medieval and Renaissance periods.
  • Heater shield – the flat-topped triangular shield of the high medieval knight.
  • Kite shield – an elongated shield with a rounded top and tapering bottom.
  • Pavise – a large rectangular shield used as portable cover by missile troops.
  • Scutum – the curved rectangular shield of the Roman legionary.
  • Targe – a round shield, particularly associated with Scottish Highlanders.
  • Tower shield – a very large shield covering most of the body.
