Thermal wind


The vertical shear of the geostrophic wind caused by a horizontal temperature gradient.

The thermal wind is the difference in the geostrophic wind between two pressure levels in the atmosphere. It does not itself constitute a wind; rather, it is a measure of the vertical wind shear of the geostrophic flow. The concept is fundamental to synoptic meteorology because it links the pattern of horizontal temperature differences in a layer of the atmosphere to the way the wind changes with height through that layer.

The thermal wind arises from combining the geostrophic wind relation with the hydrostatic equation. Because the thickness of an atmospheric layer between two pressure surfaces is proportional to the layer's mean temperature, a horizontal temperature gradient causes pressure surfaces to tilt increasingly with height, so that the geostrophic wind strengthens or turns with altitude.

Mathematical formulation

In pressure coordinates, the thermal wind VT between pressure levels p1 (below) and p0 (above) is

VT = Vg(p0) − Vg(p1) = −(R/f) ln(p1/p0) k × ∇T̄

where R is the gas constant for dry air, f is the Coriolis parameter, and T̄ is the mean temperature of the layer. Equivalently, in height coordinates, the thermal-wind equations relate the vertical shear of the geostrophic wind components to the horizontal temperature gradient:

∂ug/∂z = −(g/(fT)) ∂T/∂y,    ∂vg/∂z = (g/(fT)) ∂T/∂x

The thermal wind blows parallel to the isotherms (lines of equal layer-mean temperature), with cold air on the left in the Northern Hemisphere (and on the right in the Southern Hemisphere), analogous to the way the geostrophic wind blows parallel to isobars.

Examples and applications

The best-known consequence of the thermal wind is the strengthening of the westerly winds with height in mid-latitudes. Because temperature decreases from the equator towards the poles, the thermal wind through the troposphere is westerly, and superposed on the surface winds it produces the strong upper-level westerlies culminating in the jet streams near the tropopause, where the poleward temperature gradient is concentrated at the polar front.

  • Advection: If the geostrophic wind veers (turns clockwise) with height in the Northern Hemisphere, warm air is being advected; backing (counter-clockwise turning) indicates cold-air advection. Forecasters use this rule to anticipate temperature changes and frontal development.
  • Baroclinic atmosphere: An atmosphere in which the thermal wind is non-zero is called baroclinic; one in which density depends only on pressure, so that the thermal wind vanishes, is barotropic.
  • Oceanography: An analogous "thermal wind" relation is used in physical oceanography, where horizontal density gradients (from temperature and salinity) determine the vertical shear of geostrophic currents; this is a standard method for computing ocean currents from hydrographic sections.
