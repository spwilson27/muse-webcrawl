Table manners are the rules of etiquette used while eating, which may also include the use of utensils. Different cultures observe different rules for table manners. Each family or group sets its own standards for how strictly these rules are to be enforced.

History

Written codes of dining behaviour go back to antiquity, but the modern Western tradition owes much to Erasmus's De civilitate morum puerilium (1530), which instructed boys not to lick greasy fingers, gnaw bones, or drink from the soup bowl. Court culture in France under Louis XIV codified elaborate rules; forks, long resisted, became standard in northern Europe and America by the 18th–19th centuries. In the 20th century, Emily Post's Etiquette (1922) became the standard American authority.

Common rules

Common Western rules include: wash hands before eating; place the napkin on the lap; wait until everyone is served (or the host begins) before starting; chew with the mouth closed and do not talk with the mouth full; keep elbows off the table while eating; use utensils from the outside in with formal place settings; and compliment the cook. Rules differ internationally — for example, in much of East Asia chopsticks must never be stuck upright in rice; in India and parts of the Middle East and Africa, eating is done with the right hand; slurping noodles is acceptable in Japan, and belching may be a compliment in some cultures.

Styles of utensil use

Two main styles of fork handling exist: the European (continental) style keeps the fork in the left hand throughout, while the American style switches the fork to the right hand after cutting. Placement of utensils on the plate signals whether one is resting or finished.
