The Mishnah or Mishna (Hebrew: מִשְׁנָה, "study by repetition", from the verb shanah, "to study and review") is the first major written collection of the Jewish oral traditions known as the Oral Torah. It is also the first major work of rabbinic literature. The Mishnah was redacted by Judah ha-Nasi (Rabbi Judah the Prince) around 200 CE, drawing on the teachings of the Tannaim, the rabbinic sages active from about 10 to 220 CE.

Structure

The Mishnah consists of six orders (sedarim), each containing 7–12 tractates (masekhtot), 63 in total, subdivided into chapters and paragraphs:

  • Zeraim ("Seeds") — prayer and blessings, tithes and agricultural laws
  • Moed ("Festival") — the Sabbath and festivals
  • Nashim ("Women") — marriage, divorce and vows
  • Nezikin ("Damages") — civil and criminal law, courts, oaths
  • Kodashim ("Holy things") — sacrifices and the Temple service
  • Tohorot ("Purities") — ritual purity and impurity

Relationship to the Talmud

The Mishnah became the core text around which the rabbis of Palestine and Babylonia composed the Gemara; Mishnah plus Gemara together make up the Talmud. Material from the Tannaitic period not included in the Mishnah was collected in the Tosefta and the halakhic midrashim, and is cited in the Talmud as baraitot ("outside" teachings). The Mishnah records majority rulings alongside named minority opinions, preserving the disputes — notably between the schools of Hillel and Shammai — that shaped later Jewish law (halakha).
