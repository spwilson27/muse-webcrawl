Field emission microscopy (FEM) is an analytical technique in surface science invented by Erwin Wilhelm Müller in 1936. It was the first microscopy method to achieve near-atomic spatial resolution, and it is used to study the structure and electronic properties of the surface of a sharply pointed metal or semiconductor specimen.

Principle

The specimen is a needle with a tip radius of tens of nanometres, held at a large negative potential (several kilovolts) inside an ultra-high-vacuum chamber facing a fluorescent screen. The intense electric field at the tip — on the order of volts per nanometre — causes electrons to tunnel out of the surface by field emission. The emitted electrons travel nearly radially to the screen, producing a highly magnified projection image of the tip surface. Because the emission current depends exponentially on the local work function, different crystal facets and adsorbed species appear with different brightness.

Applications

FEM has been used to map crystal plane distributions on emitter tips, observe surface diffusion of adsorbates in real time, measure work-function changes on adsorption, and study field-induced surface processes. Müller's later field ion microscope (1951) extended the method to image individual atoms using imaging gas ions.
