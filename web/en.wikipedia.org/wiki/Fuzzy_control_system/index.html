Fuzzy control system

A fuzzy control system is a control system based on fuzzy logic — a mathematical framework for reasoning with imprecise, linguistic information. Instead of exact numeric rules, a fuzzy controller uses statements such as "if the temperature is a little high, then reduce the heating somewhat," formalized through fuzzy sets and membership functions.

Structure

A fuzzy controller typically has three stages. Fuzzification converts crisp sensor inputs into degrees of membership in linguistic categories (for example, "cold", "warm", "hot"). A rule base and inference engine then combine if–then rules to produce fuzzy output sets, and defuzzification converts the result back into a crisp control action, commonly by the centroid method.

History

Fuzzy set theory was introduced by Lotfi Zadeh in 1965. Ebrahim Mamdani and Seto Assilian built the first fuzzy controller, for a steam engine, in 1975, and industrial applications followed in the 1980s — notably the control of cement kilns and, in Japan, the Sendai subway's automatic train operation. Consumer products such as washing machines and camera autofocus systems were widely marketed as "fuzzy logic" devices in the 1990s.

Advantages and criticism

Fuzzy control is attractive when a system is hard to model mathematically but skilled operators can articulate their strategy in words. Critics note that fuzzy controllers can be difficult to analyse for stability and that their performance may be matched by conventional control designs; nevertheless, fuzzy methods remain embedded in industrial and consumer control applications.
