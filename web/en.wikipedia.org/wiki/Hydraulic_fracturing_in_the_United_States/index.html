Hydraulic fracturing in the United States

Hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") in the United States is the process of injecting fluid at high pressure into shale and other tight rock formations to create fractures through which natural gas, petroleum, and brine can flow to a production well. Combined with horizontal drilling, it enabled the early-21st-century "shale revolution", which made the United States the world's largest producer of oil and natural gas.

History

Hydraulic fracturing was first performed experimentally in Kansas in 1947 and commercialised in 1949; more than a million wells had been fractured by the 2010s. The shale era began when Mitchell Energy, led by George P. Mitchell, combined fracturing with horizontal drilling in the Barnett Shale of Texas in the late 1990s and early 2000s. Development then spread to the Marcellus, Eagle Ford, Bakken, Permian Basin, and other plays. By the mid-2010s, hydraulically fractured wells accounted for the majority of U.S. oil and gas production.

Regulation

Regulation is primarily at state level; the federal Safe Drinking Water Act largely exempts fracturing (except with diesel fuels) following amendments in the Energy Policy Act of 2005, a provision critics call the "Halliburton loophole". Some jurisdictions, notably New York State (2014), have banned high-volume fracturing, while federal rules have addressed methane emissions, disclosure on the FracFocus registry (launched 2011), and well construction.

Controversies

Fracturing is controversial because of concerns over groundwater contamination, chemical disclosure, wastewater disposal, air emissions, and induced seismicity — most earthquakes being linked not to fracturing itself but to deep injection of wastewater, notably in Oklahoma in the 2010s. EPA assessments (2016) concluded that fracturing activities can impact drinking water under some circumstances. Supporters emphasise energy security, lower gas prices, reduced coal use, and economic benefits to producing regions.
