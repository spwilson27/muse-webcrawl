Fick's laws of diffusion

Formulated1855
Formulated byAdolf Fick
FieldPhysics, chemistry, biology

Diffusive flux from a region of high concentration to one of low concentration

Fick's laws of diffusion describe diffusion, the movement of particles from regions of higher concentration to regions of lower concentration. They were derived by Adolf Fick in 1855, by analogy with Fourier's law of heat conduction.

First law

Fick's first law relates the diffusive flux to the concentration gradient under steady-state conditions. In one dimension it states that the flux J is proportional to the negative gradient of concentration c: J = −D ∂c/∂x, where D is the diffusion coefficient (diffusivity), with units of area per unit time. Particles move from high to low concentration at a rate proportional to the steepness of the gradient.

Second law

Fick's second law describes how diffusion causes concentration to change with time. It is a partial differential equation, ∂c/∂t = D ∂²c/∂x² in one dimension, whose solutions (often involving error functions or Gaussian distributions) predict spreading profiles. For a point source, the characteristic distance travelled grows with the square root of time.

Applications

The laws are used throughout science and engineering: gas exchange in lungs and across cell membranes, dopant diffusion in semiconductor fabrication, drug delivery, and mass transport in chemical engineering. Analogous equations govern heat conduction and some models of fluid flow.
