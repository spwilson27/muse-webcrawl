Jungle is a genre of electronic dance music that developed in the United Kingdom in the early 1990s out of the breakbeat hardcore scene. Characterised by fast tempos (150–200 BPM), syncopated sampled breakbeats — most famously the Amen break — deep sub-bass lines, and influences from reggae, dub, and dancehall, jungle laid the foundations for drum and bass, into which it had largely evolved by the late 1990s. History

Jungle emerged on London's pirate radio stations and rave circuit in 1991–92, as producers in the breakbeat hardcore scene began emphasising basslines and reggae and dub samples over the euphoric piano riffs of earlier rave music. Soundsystem culture, brought by the Afro-Caribbean diaspora, was a formative influence: tracks drew on dancehall MC chat, dub echoes, and the "rewind" (pull-up) tradition of Jamaican sound systems. Early anthems included A Guy Called Gerald's work and tracks on labels such as Reinforced Records and Moving Shadow.

By 1994 jungle had reached national prominence in Britain, but moral panics in the press and a split in the scene pushed the music in two directions: ragga jungle, with overt dancehall vocals, and the smoother, more melodic "intelligent" style associated with producers such as LTJ Bukem. As tempos rose and production techniques refined the sound around 1996–97, the term "drum and bass" increasingly replaced "jungle" as the genre's name. A revival of interest in the classic jungle sound grew through the 2010s and 2020s, with a new generation of producers returning to chopped breakbeats and period production techniques.

Characteristics

Jungle tracks are built around looped, sped-up funk and soul drum breaks, sliced and rearranged in samplers — the six-second "Amen break" from the Winstons' "Amen, Brother" (1969) being the genre's signature rhythm. Basslines are typically deep, resonant sub-bass patterns derived from reggae and dub, often generated with sine waves or the "Reese" bass sound. Vocals may come from sampled dancehall MCs, soul singers, or live MCs performing over the music in clubs, and time-stretched samples — pitch-shifted without changing duration — are a hallmark of the era's sound.

Etymology

The origin of the name is debated. It is often derived from "Junglists", a term for residents of the Tivoli Gardens district of Kingston, Jamaica — nicknamed "the Jungle" — sampled in early tracks; it also referenced the dense, frenetic layering of the music itself. The name attracted controversy because of possible racist connotations, which some in the scene cited as a reason the industry preferred "drum and bass".
