Geʻez script

The Geʻez script (ግእዝ), also known as Ethiopic, is an abugida used to write several languages of Ethiopia and Eritrea, originally the Geʻez language and today chiefly Amharic and Tigrinya. In the script, each basic character represents a consonant, and modifications to its shape indicate the following vowel.

History

The script derives from the ancient South Arabian alphabet, brought across the Red Sea in the first millennium BC. Early Aksumite inscriptions were written in an unvocalised consonantal form, sometimes in boustrophedon. Around the 4th century AD, under King Ezana, vowel marks were systematised, creating the vocalised abugida still in use. Word division, originally shown by two vertical dots, was later replaced by spaces, and punctuation marks and Ethiopic numerals developed within manuscript practice.

Structure

The traditional inventory comprises 26 basic consonant characters, each occurring in seven forms, called orders, corresponding to the vowels ä, u, i, a, e, ə, and o. Additional characters represent labialised consonants, and languages such as Amharic added characters for sounds not present in classical Geʻez. The characters are traditionally called fidels and are learned in a fixed order beginning hä, lä, ḥä, mä, śä…

Numerals and punctuation

Traditional Ethiopic numerals form a non-positional system adapted from Greek letters, with distinct signs for 1–9, 10–90, 100, and 10,000; modern usage generally prefers Arabic numerals. Traditional punctuation includes the word separator ፡ and sentence and paragraph marks such as ። and ፨.

Modern use

The script is the official writing system for Amharic in Ethiopia and for Tigrinya in Eritrea and Ethiopia's Tigray Region. It is encoded in Unicode in the Ethiopic blocks, is printed and taught throughout the region, and appears on currency, signage, and official documents.
