Hepatic lipase

Hepatic lipase (HL), also called hepatic triglyceride lipase, is an enzyme produced by hepatocytes in the liver that plays a central role in lipoprotein metabolism. Anchored to the surface of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells via heparan sulfate proteoglycans, it hydrolyses triglycerides and phospholipids in circulating lipoproteins, especially HDL and intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL). In humans it is encoded by the LIPC gene on chromosome 15.

Function

Hepatic lipase remodels lipoproteins in several ways: it converts IDL toward LDL, converts large triglyceride-rich HDL2 to smaller HDL3 by removing lipid, and acts as a ligand facilitating hepatic uptake of lipoprotein particles. Unlike lipoprotein lipase, it is not substantially activated by apolipoprotein C-II and is not inhibited by high salt concentrations, properties historically used to distinguish the two enzymes in post-heparin plasma assays.

Clinical significance

Genetic deficiency of hepatic lipase is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterised by elevated cholesterol and triglycerides, accumulation of abnormal triglyceride-rich HDL and LDL remnants, and premature coronary artery disease in some patients. Common variants near the LIPC promoter (such as the −514C/T polymorphism) influence enzyme activity and HDL cholesterol levels across populations. HL activity tends to be higher in men and is influenced by hormones, including androgens, estrogens and thyroid hormone, contributing to sex differences in HDL levels.
