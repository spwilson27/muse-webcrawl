Burma Railway

The Burma Railway, also known as the Death Railway, the Siam–Burma Railway, and the Thailand–Burma Railway, is a 415-kilometre (258 mi) railway between Ban Pong, Thailand, and Thanbyuzayat, Burma (now Myanmar), built by the Empire of Japan during World War II. It was constructed between 1942 and 1943 using the forced labour of Allied prisoners of war and Asian civilian labourers to supply Japanese forces in the Burma campaign.

Background

A rail link between Thailand and Burma had been surveyed by the British in the early 20th century but abandoned because of the difficult terrain of jungle-covered mountains and numerous river crossings. After occupying Thailand and Burma in 1942, Japan needed a supply route to its forces in Burma that avoided the sea lanes around the Malay Peninsula, which were vulnerable to Allied submarines. The overland railway offered a shorter, more secure line of communication.

Construction

Work began in June 1942 from both ends of the line. The labour force consisted of roughly 60,000 Allied prisoners of war — British, Australian, Dutch, and American — and an estimated 180,000 to 250,000 Asian civilian labourers known as rōmusha, recruited or conscripted from across Japanese-occupied Southeast Asia.

Conditions were appalling. Workers suffered from malnutrition, cholera, dysentery, tropical ulcers, and brutal treatment, and were driven to work at an accelerated pace during the notorious "Speedo" period of 1943. Estimates of the death toll commonly cite more than 12,000 Allied prisoners and as many as 90,000 or more Asian labourers. The line was completed in October 1943, in about sixteen months.

The bridge on the River Kwai

The railway's most famous structure is the bridge over the Khwae Yai River at Kanchanaburi in Thailand, immortalised by Pierre Boulle's 1952 novel The Bridge over the River Kwai and David Lean's 1957 film adaptation. The bridge was bombed repeatedly by Allied aircraft and rebuilt after the war; two of its spans are wartime replacements.

After the war

After Japan's surrender, the British removed a section of track near the Burma border, and the line fell into disrepair on the Burmese side. The State Railway of Thailand rebuilt the section from Nong Pla Duk to Nam Tok, which remains in operation and is a popular tourist route. Cemeteries and museums at Kanchanaburi and Thanbyuzayat commemorate the dead; the principal Allied war cemeteries are at Kanchanaburi and Chungkai in Thailand and Thanbyuzayat in Myanmar.
