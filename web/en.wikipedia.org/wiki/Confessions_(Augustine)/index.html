Confessions (Latin: Confessiones) is an autobiographical work by Augustine of Hippo, consisting of 13 books written in Latin between AD 397 and 400. The work outlines Augustine's sinful youth and his conversion to Christianity. Modern English translations of it are sometimes published under the title The Confessions of St. Augustine in order to distinguish the book from other books with similar titles. Its original title being Confessions in English, it is widely regarded as one of the first true autobiographies in Western literature.

Structure

Books 1–9 trace Augustine's life from infancy in Thagaste, through his education, the stealing of pears as a youth (an emblem of gratuitous sin), his concubinage and the birth of his son Adeodatus, his involvement with Manichaeism, his teaching in Carthage, Rome and Milan, and his conversion under the influence of Ambrose and the reading of Romans 13:13–14 — tolle lege ("take up and read") — culminating in his baptism in 387 and the death of his mother Monica. Book 10 examines memory and the self; Books 11–13 meditate on time, creation, and the Book of Genesis.

Legacy

The Confessions has been continuously influential: its analysis of time ("What is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks, I don't") anticipated modern philosophy of time, and its intimate address to God helped create the autobiographical genre. It was one of the most copied texts of the Middle Ages.
