Play therapy

Play therapy refers to a range of methods of capitalising on children's natural urge to explore and harnessing it to meet and respond to their developmental and mental health needs. It is also used for forensic or psychological assessment purposes where the individual is believed to be too young or too traumatised to give a verbal account of adverse, abusive, or potentially criminal circumstances in their life.

Overview

Play therapy is extensively acknowledged by specialists as an effective intervention in complementing children's natural means of expression as a substitute for verbal therapy in resolving internal conflicts and obtaining broader insight into their inner life. Because young children often lack the vocabulary or abstract reasoning to articulate experiences and feelings directly, play serves as their symbolic language: toys are their words and play is their language, as play therapist Garry Landreth has put it.

History

The therapeutic use of play grew out of psychoanalysis. Hermine Hug-Hellmuth described children's play as a route to the unconscious in the early twentieth century, and Melanie Klein and Anna Freud developed psychoanalytic play techniques in the 1920s and 1930s. Virginia Axline, a student of Carl Rogers, adapted client-centered therapy to children in the 1940s; her book Dibs in Search of Self (1964) popularized the approach. Clark Moustakas, Violet Oaklander (Gestalt play therapy), and others broadened the field, and professional associations such as the Association for Play Therapy (founded 1982) helped establish training and credentialing.

Approaches

Non-directive (child-centered) play therapy

In Axline's model, the therapist follows the child's lead, providing an accepting, permissive relationship and reflecting the child's feelings, while setting only necessary limits. The child directs the activity and play, and the therapist does not interpret or direct solutions.

Directive approaches

Other models are more structured. Cognitive-behavioral play therapy teaches coping skills through play; Adlerian play therapy combines lifestyle exploration with encouragement; sandplay therapy (developed by Dora Kalff from Margaret Lowenfeld's "world technique") uses a tray of sand and miniature figures; filial therapy trains parents to conduct therapeutic play sessions with their own children.

Applications and evidence

Play therapy is used with children experiencing anxiety, trauma, grief, divorce, chronic illness, behavioral problems, and developmental disorders, and in hospital and school settings. Meta-analyses have generally found moderate positive effects, though outcomes vary by approach, problem type, and parental involvement. Critics note that the evidence base is less developed than for some other child interventions, and that claims for particular branded models sometimes outrun the research.
