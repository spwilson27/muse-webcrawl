Commuter town

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A commuter town, also known as a dormitory town, bedroom community, or bedroom suburb, is a town whose residents predominantly travel (commute) to work in a nearby larger city or employment centre, while the town itself has few local employers relative to its population. Such towns are predominantly residential, with retail and services oriented toward residents rather than workers.

Development

Commuter towns grew with suburbanisation: first along railway lines in the 19th century (London's "Metroland" suburbs being a classic example), then along motorways in the 20th. Housing is often cheaper or more spacious than in the core city, attracting families who accept longer journeys. The pattern intensified after the Second World War with mass car ownership and highway building in North America, producing settlements at the metropolitan fringe sometimes termed exurbs.

Effects and criticism

Because residents leave during the day, commuter towns may have quiet daytime economies, congested transport links at peak hours, and strong orientation toward the central city's jobs, culture, and media. Long commutes are associated with higher transport emissions and costs to wellbeing. Growth of remote and hybrid work since 2020 has partially weakened the pattern, increasing daytime populations in many such towns. Planners sometimes seek to counter the dormitory character by adding local employment, mixed-use development, and town-centre amenities.
