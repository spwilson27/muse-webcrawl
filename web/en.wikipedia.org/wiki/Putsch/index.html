Putsch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A putsch (from Swiss German Putsch, a push or thrust) is a coup d'état attempt — a sudden, violent overthrow of a government by a small group, typically military or paramilitary. The word acquired its political sense from the Züriputsch of 1839 in Zurich and is indelibly associated with the failed German putsches of the Weimar era: the Kapp Putsch (March 1920, defeated by a general strike) and Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch (Munich, 8–9 November 1923), whose failure sent Hitler to prison (where he dictated Mein Kampf) and taught the Nazis the “legality” route to power.

'Putsch' connotes illegitimacy and amateurish conspiracy as much as violence in ordinary usage, and is often applied retrospectively by the victors (the “July Putsch” of Austrian Nazis in 1934; the Algiers putsch of French generals in 1961). Political scientists generally fold it into coup typology — distinguishing palace coups, military coups, self-coups and pronunciamientos — while the word survives in compound historical names (the August Putsch of Soviet hardliners, 1991).
