Coaxial escapement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Invented: 1974 (patented 1975)
Inventor: George Daniels
Commercialized by: Omega SA, from 1999

The coaxial escapement is a type of modern watch escapement mechanism invented by the English horologist George Daniels. It was patented in 1975 and is considered one of the most significant advances in mechanical watchmaking since the lever escapement, because it operates with greatly reduced sliding friction and therefore needs little or no lubrication on the impulse faces, promising longer service intervals and more stable rate over time.

How it works

Unlike the Swiss lever escapement, in which the escape wheel teeth slide across the pallet faces—requiring oil that degrades—the coaxial design gives impulse radially through two coaxially mounted escape wheels. A large wheel drives a roller jewel attached to the balance directly for one impulse, while a smaller coaxial wheel works through the lever pallets for the other. The geometry produces two virtually friction-free impulses per oscillation, in a "push" rather than a sliding action.

History

Daniels built prototype watches through the 1970s but initially struggled to persuade Swiss manufacturers to adopt a design from an English independent watchmaker. He produced a handful of handmade watches using it for collectors. Eventually Omega acquired the rights and, after years of industrialization work with Nicolas Hayek's support and watchmaker Nicholas Speake-Marin among others, launched the first mass-produced coaxial movement, the Omega calibre 2500, in 1999. Later calibres (8500, 8800, 8900 and Master Chronometer lines) were designed around the escapement from the start, with silicon balance springs and anti-magnetic materials.

Assessment

Horologists praise the design's theoretical elegance, though some note that real-world service intervals and rate stability depend on overall movement quality. Daniels's handmade coaxial watches are among the most collectible modern timepieces.
