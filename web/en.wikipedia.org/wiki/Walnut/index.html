A walnut is the edible seed of any tree of the genus Juglans (family Juglandaceae), particularly the Persian or English walnut, Juglans regia. Technically the walnut is the seed of a drupe or drupaceous nut rather than a true botanical nut. Walnuts are rich in protein and essential fatty acids and are widely eaten raw, roasted, or used in cooking and baking.

The walnut trees are deciduous trees, 10–40 metres tall, with pinnate leaves, native to regions from southeastern Europe through Asia and in the Americas. Important species include the black walnut (Juglans nigra) of eastern North America, valued for its dark timber, and the butternut (Juglans cinerea).

Cultivation and production

Walnuts have been cultivated since ancient times; the English walnut originated in Persia and was spread by the Romans and later traders. Today China is the largest producer of walnuts, followed by the United States — where California produces the great majority of the crop — Iran and Turkey. World production is several million tonnes of in-shell nuts annually.

Uses

Besides the kernels, walnut products include walnut oil, green walnut pickles and liqueurs such as Italian nocino. Walnut timber, especially black walnut, is prized for furniture, cabinetry, panelling and gunstocks because of its rich brown colour and workability. The trees produce the compound juglone, which can inhibit the growth of some other plants nearby (allelopathy).
