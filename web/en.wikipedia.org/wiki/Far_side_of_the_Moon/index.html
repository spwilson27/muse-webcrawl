Far side of the Moon

The far side of the Moon is the hemisphere of the Moon that always faces away from Earth, opposite to the near side, because of synchronous rotation in the Moon's tidally locked orbit with Earth. Despite the name, it is not permanently dark: both hemispheres receive equal amounts of sunlight on average, though the far side is illuminated when the near side experiences a new moon.

Characteristics

The far side differs markedly from the near side. It is densely cratered and possesses almost none of the dark basaltic plains (maria) that dominate the near side; maria cover only about 1% of the far side compared with about 31% of the near side. It contains the South Pole–Aitken basin, one of the largest known impact craters in the Solar System, and the Moon's thickest crust.

Exploration

The far side was first photographed in 1959 by the Soviet Luna 3 probe, revealing its heavily cratered terrain. It was seen directly by human eyes for the first time in December 1968 during Apollo 8. Because radio signals from Earth cannot reach it directly, missions there require a communications relay satellite.

In January 2019, China's Chang'e 4 became the first spacecraft to soft-land on the far side, in Von Kármán crater within the South Pole–Aitken basin, deploying the Yutu-2 rover and communicating via the Queqiao relay satellite. In June 2024, Chang'e 6 returned the first samples ever collected from the far side.

Potential uses

The far side is shielded from Earth's radio emissions, making it an attractive site for radio astronomy, including proposed low-frequency radio telescopes.
