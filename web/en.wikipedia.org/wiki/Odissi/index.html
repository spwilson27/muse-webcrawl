Odissi

Odissi, from the eastern state of Odisha, is among the oldest surviving forms of Indian classical dance.

Odissi is one of the eight major forms of Indian classical dance. It originates from the temples of Odisha, an eastern coastal state of India, and is distinguished by its fluid, sinuous movements, sculptural poses, and devotional themes centred on the god Jagannath, a form of Krishna.

History

Evidence of the dance tradition appears in the rock-cut caves of Udayagiri and Khandagiri, dating to the early centuries BCE. For centuries Odissi was performed in temples by female dancers known as maharis as an act of worship, and outside temples by young male performers called gotipuas, who dressed as women. The tradition declined under colonial rule but was reconstructed and revived in the mid-20th century by gurus including Kelucharan Mohapatra, who is credited with shaping the modern repertoire.

Technique

The signature posture of Odissi is the tribhangi, a three-bend position of the body at the neck, torso, and knees, giving the dance its characteristic S-curve likened to temple sculpture. The basic square stance is called chowka. Dancers wear a sari draped in a distinctive fan at the front, silver jewellery, and a crown-like headdress (tahia); the feet are painted with red dye (alta) and fitted with bells.

Repertoire

A traditional recital opens with mangalacharan, an invocation; proceeds through batu nritya, pure dance items such as pallavi, and expressive narrative pieces (abhinaya) based on texts such as Jayadeva's Gita Govinda; and concludes with moksha, a dance of spiritual liberation. Music is in the Odissi style of classical music, with the mardala drum, flute, violin, and manjira cymbals.
