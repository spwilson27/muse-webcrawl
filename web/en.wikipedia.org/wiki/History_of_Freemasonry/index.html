History of Freemasonry

The history of Freemasonry traces the development of the fraternal organisation from the guilds of operative stonemasons of the late Middle Ages to the worldwide speculative fraternity of today. Freemasonry's own legendary histories once traced its origins to biblical times, but documentary history begins with the records of masons' lodges in Scotland and England around 1600.

Origins

Medieval stonemasons who built the cathedrals organised themselves in lodges and guilds, with charges (rules) preserved in documents such as the Regius Poem (c. 1390). In 16th- and 17th-century Scotland, the Schaw Statutes (1598–1599) regulated the craft, and lodges began admitting "accepted" or speculative masons — gentlemen who were not working stonemasons. The minutes of the Lodge of Edinburgh (Mary's Chapel) begin in 1599, the oldest lodge records in existence.

The first Grand Lodge

On 24 June 1717, four London lodges founded the first Grand Lodge, the Premier Grand Lodge of England, to govern the craft. In 1723 James Anderson published the Constitutions of the Free-Masons, which systematised the fraternity's legendary history and charges. A rival "Antient" Grand Lodge formed in 1751, and the two bodies competed until they merged in 1813 as the United Grand Lodge of England.

Spread and development

Freemasonry spread rapidly through the British Empire, continental Europe and the Americas. In Catholic Europe, papal condemnations from 1738 drove a separation between Anglo-American ("regular") Masonry, which requires belief in a supreme being and excludes women, and the liberal tradition exemplified by the Grand Orient de France. In the United States, Masonry grew with figures such as George Washington and Benjamin Franklin among its members, survived the Anti-Masonic backlash of the 1820s–30s (sparked by the Morgan affair), and developed elaborate appendant bodies such as the Scottish Rite and the Shriners.

Persecution and modern era

Freemasons were persecuted by totalitarian regimes in the 20th century, including Nazi Germany and Francoist Spain, and suppressed in communist states. Membership in many Western countries declined from its mid-20th-century peak, though the fraternity remains one of the world's largest secular fraternal organisations, devoted formally to moral instruction, charity and fellowship rather than religion or politics.
