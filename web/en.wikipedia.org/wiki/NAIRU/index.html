NAIRU

NAIRU is an acronym for non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. It is the level of unemployment at which the rate of inflation does not accelerate or decelerate. If unemployment falls below the NAIRU, labour market tightness bids up wages and prices, and inflation rises; if unemployment is above it, inflation tends to fall. The concept is closely related to the "natural rate of unemployment" proposed by Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps in 1968, but the NAIRU terminology — introduced by Franco Modigliani and Lucas Papademos in 1975 and popularised by James Tobin — deliberately avoids the implication that the rate is natural, immutable, or optimal.

The expectations-augmented Phillips curve: inflation accelerates when unemployment is below the NAIRU.

Theoretical basis

The NAIRU arises in the expectations-augmented Phillips curve. In the long run the Phillips curve is vertical at the NAIRU: attempts to hold unemployment below it with expansionary policy produce ever-accelerating inflation, the "accelerationist hypothesis". The NAIRU itself is determined by real, structural factors — the efficiency of job matching, labour-market institutions, benefit systems, union power, skills, and the degree of competition — rather than by monetary policy.

Estimation and use

The NAIRU is not directly observable and must be estimated econometrically, typically with state-space models that infer it from the joint behaviour of unemployment and inflation. Estimates are imprecise, with wide confidence intervals, and are frequently revised; Douglas Staiger, James Stock, and Mark Watson famously concluded that the NAIRU is estimated too imprecisely to be of much practical use for monetary policy. Central banks nevertheless use NAIRU-type estimates as one gauge of labour-market slack when setting interest rates. Estimates for the United States have generally fallen over time, from around 6% in the 1980s to roughly 4–4.5% in Congressional Budget Office estimates of recent years.

Criticism

Hysteresis

Olivier Blanchard and Lawrence Summers argued that prolonged unemployment can itself raise the NAIRU — through skill loss, discouragement, and insider–outsider dynamics — so that the rate is path-dependent rather than a fixed attractor. This "hysteresis" mechanism was invoked to explain persistently high European unemployment in the 1980s and, conversely, suggests that running a "high-pressure" economy might lower structural unemployment.

Other critiques

Critics note that inflation depends on many factors besides domestic labour-market slack — import prices, supply shocks, globalisation, and expectations anchored by policy — so unemployment below estimated NAIRU levels has often coexisted with stable or falling inflation, notably in the United States in the late 1990s and late 2010s. Post-Keynesian economists question whether a unique NAIRU exists at all.
