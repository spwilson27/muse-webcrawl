Romney Marsh is a sparsely populated wetland area in the counties of Kent and East Sussex in the south-east of England. It covers about 100 square miles (260 km²) of low-lying land reclaimed from the sea over many centuries, protected by sea walls and the great shingle bank at Dungeness. Long celebrated for its sheep, its remote churches and its smuggling past, the Marsh has been called an unofficial "fifth continent" — distinct in character from the rest of Kent.

Geography and reclamation

The Marsh began as a tidal lagoon behind a growing shingle spit. From Roman times onward, and intensively in the Middle Ages, inhabitants embanked and drained the salt marshes ("inning"), creating rich pasture protected by structures such as the Rhee Wall, built to scour the harbour of New Romney. As the coastline advanced, ports that once stood on the sea — including New Romney, one of the original Cinque Ports — were left inland, and the River Rother shifted its mouth to Rye. Today the Royal Military Canal, dug in 1804–1809 as a defence against Napoleonic invasion, marks the Marsh's northern edge beneath the old sea cliff.

Sheep and agriculture

The pastures supported vast flocks of the hardy Romney sheep, a breed exported worldwide for its long wool and ability to thrive on wet ground. Sheep farming shaped the landscape of open grazing marsh divided by drainage ditches (sewers), and a distinctive body of marsh law administered drainage through the Lords, Bailiff and Jurats of Romney Marsh, an authority with roots in the thirteenth century.

Smuggling and legend

The lonely coast and flat, ditch-cut terrain made the Marsh a stronghold of smuggling in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, when gangs landed wool going out ("owlers") and brandy, tea and tobacco coming in. The area inspired Russell Thorndike's Doctor Syn novels, about a smuggling parson. The Marsh is also known for its medieval churches, such as those at Fairfield and Newchurch, standing isolated among the fields, and for the narrow-gauge Romney, Hythe and Dymchurch Railway, opened in 1927.

Wildlife

The wetlands, gravel pits and shingle of Dungeness support important populations of breeding and wintering birds, great crested newts and rare invertebrates, and much of the area is protected as a Site of Special Scientific Interest. Dungeness, at the Marsh's eastern point, is also home to a nuclear power station and a famous garden created by the film-maker Derek Jarman.
