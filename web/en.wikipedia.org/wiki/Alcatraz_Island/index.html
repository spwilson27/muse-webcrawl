Alcatraz Island is a small rocky island in San Francisco Bay, 1.25 miles (2 km) offshore from San Francisco, California. The island was developed with facilities for a lighthouse, a military fortification, a military prison and a federal prison — the last of which, Alcatraz Federal Penitentiary, operated from 1934 to 1963 and made "The Rock" a byword for inescapable imprisonment. Today the island is a major tourist attraction managed by the National Park Service as part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area.

History

The island was named La Isla de los Alcatraces ("The Island of the Pelicans") by Spanish explorer Juan Manuel de Ayala in 1775. The United States acquired it in 1848, built a fortress and the West Coast's first operational lighthouse (1854), and used it as a military prison during and after the Civil War.

Transferred to the Department of Justice in 1933, the island became a maximum-security federal penitentiary for the most troublesome prisoners of the era, including Al Capone, Robert Stroud (the "Birdman of Alcatraz"), Machine Gun Kelly and James "Whitey" Bulger. The cold, swift currents of the bay were considered an insurmountable barrier; of 36 men who attempted escape in 14 incidents, none is known to have succeeded, although the fate of Frank Morris and the Anglin brothers, who vanished in June 1962, remains officially unresolved. High operating costs closed the prison in 1963.

Occupation and park era

From November 1969 to June 1971 the island was occupied by Native American activists claiming it under treaty rights — a landmark of the Red Power movement. Designated a National Historic Landmark in 1986, Alcatraz now receives about 1.5 million visitors a year, drawn by the cellhouse tours, gardens, and large seabird colonies.
