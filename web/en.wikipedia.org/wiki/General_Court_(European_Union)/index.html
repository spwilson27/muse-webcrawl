General Court (European Union)

The General Court, informally known as the European General Court (EGC), is a constituent court of the Court of Justice of the European Union. It hears actions taken against the institutions of the European Union by individuals and member states, deciding cases at first instance in most areas of direct judicial review. Appeals lie to the Court of Justice on points of law only. The General Court sits in Luxembourg alongside the Court of Justice.

History

The court was established by the Single European Act and began work in 1989 as the Court of First Instance, created to relieve the Court of Justice of a growing caseload, initially staff cases and competition cases. Its jurisdiction was progressively extended, and the Treaty of Lisbon renamed it the General Court. The separate European Union Civil Service Tribunal, which had taken over staff cases in 2005, was dissolved in 2016 and its jurisdiction transferred back, while a reform increasing the General Court to two judges per member state was phased in between 2015 and 2019.

Jurisdiction

The General Court principally hears: actions for annulment of EU acts brought by individuals, companies and, in some cases, member states and institutions; actions for failure to act; actions for damages against the Union; disputes under arbitration clauses in EU contracts; EU trade mark and design appeals from the European Union Intellectual Property Office; and staff cases between the EU and its servants. Its competition and state-aid docket — reviewing European Commission decisions imposing large fines — is its most commercially significant work; notable judgments include the 2021 annulment of the Commission's €13 billion Apple state-aid decision (later reversed by the Court of Justice in 2024) and the upholding of the €2.42 billion Google Shopping fine, affirmed on appeal.

Composition and procedure

The General Court consists of two judges per member state (54 in total), appointed by common accord of the member states for renewable six-year terms. Unlike the Court of Justice, it normally sits without advocates general, though a judge may be designated to perform that function in a particular case. Chambers usually comprise three or five judges; particularly important cases may go to a Grand Chamber of 15 judges. Judgments are collegiate, with no dissenting opinions published.
