Code of Ur-Nammu

The Code of Ur-Nammu is the oldest known surviving law code written in a recognizable form. It was composed in Sumerian in the reign of Ur-Nammu, king of Ur (c. 2050 BC) during the Third Dynasty of Ur. Although the better-known Code of Hammurabi is more complete, Ur-Nammu's code predates it by roughly three centuries. The surviving tablets are held in Istanbul (Nippur fragment) and the University of Pennsylvania Museum.

Contents

The code follows a prologue celebrating Ur-Nammu's establishment of justice, then a series of casuistic laws in the form "If (a man...)..., then (...)". Surviving provisions cover murder, robbery, and kidnapping (punishable by death), sexual offences, false accusation, perjury, divorce, slavery, and a schedule of monetary compensation for bodily injuries — for example, fixed payments in silver for severing a foot, breaking a bone, or cutting off a nose.

Character

Unlike Hammurabi's code, which frequently prescribes retaliation ("an eye for an eye"), the Code of Ur-Nammu generally imposes fines in silver for physical injuries, a system seen as comparatively humane. Capital punishment is reserved for the gravest offences. The code also standardises weights and measures and reflects the king's role as guarantor of the "equity of the land".

Discovery

Two fragments found at Nippur were translated by Samuel Noah Kramer in 1952; a further tablet from Ur, translated in 1965, allowed a fuller reconstruction, and another copy was identified at Sippar. Because Ur-Nammu's son Shulgi reigned for much of the relevant period, some scholars attribute the code's promulgation to him in his father's name.
