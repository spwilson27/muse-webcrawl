The Easter Vigil, also called the Paschal Vigil or the Great Vigil of Easter, is a liturgy held in traditional Christian churches as the first official celebration of the Resurrection of Jesus. Historically it is during this service that adults are baptised and adult catechumens are received into full communion with the Church. It is held in the hours of darkness between sunset on Holy Saturday and sunrise on Easter Sunday — most commonly in the evening, though it was traditionally held at night — and is the most important service in the liturgical year of many churches.

Roman Catholic practice

The modern Roman Catholic Easter Vigil, restored by Pope Pius XII in 1951 and revised after the Second Vatican Council, has four parts:

  • The Service of Light — a new fire is blessed outside the church and the Paschal candle lit from it; the candle is carried in procession into the darkened church as the deacon sings "Lumen Christi" ("The Light of Christ"), and the ancient hymn Exsultet proclaims the Resurrection.
  • The Liturgy of the Word — up to seven readings from the Old Testament (the creation, the Exodus, the prophets) trace salvation history, followed by the Gloria (with bells rung for the first time since Holy Thursday), the Epistle and the Gospel of the Resurrection.
  • The Baptismal Liturgy — the Litany of the Saints is sung, baptismal water is blessed, catechumens are baptised and confirmed, and the whole congregation renews its baptismal promises.
  • The Liturgy of the Eucharist — the Mass continues, with the newly baptised receiving Communion for the first time.

Eastern practice

In the Eastern Orthodox Church the corresponding service is the Paschal Vigil beginning late on Holy Saturday night: the Midnight Office, followed by Matins at which the priest announces "Christ is risen!", a candlelit procession around the church, and the Paschal Divine Liturgy of St John Chrysostom, often finishing in the small hours. Byzantine Catholic and Oriental Orthodox churches keep similar all-night vigils.

Other traditions

Many Anglican, Lutheran and Methodist churches have revived the vigil in the twentieth century, following the same broad pattern of light, readings, baptism and Eucharist. The service's dramatic movement from darkness to light makes it the climax of the Paschal Triduum.
