Edward VI (12 October 1537 – 6 July 1553) was King of England and Ireland from 28 January 1547 until his death in 1553. The son of Henry VIII and Jane Seymour, he succeeded at the age of nine and reigned under a regency council dominated first by his uncle Edward Seymour, Duke of Somerset, and then by John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland. Though he never ruled in person, his reign saw the Church of England move decisively toward Protestantism.

Accession and Somerset

Edward was the long-awaited son for whom Henry VIII had broken with Rome; his mother died days after his birth. He was a precocious, rigorously educated child, taught by humanist tutors and raised a convinced Protestant. On Henry's death, a regency council governed, but Edward Seymour manoeuvred himself into the office of Lord Protector. Somerset pursued war against Scotland — winning at Pinkie in 1547 but failing to secure the marriage of Edward to Mary, Queen of Scots ("the Rough Wooing") — and faced rebellion in 1549: Kett's rising in Norfolk and the Prayer Book Rebellion in the West Country, provoked by religious change and by enclosure. In October 1549 Somerset was overthrown by John Dudley.

Protestant reform

Under Edward, the English Reformation advanced beyond Henry's political break with Rome into doctrine and worship. Archbishop Thomas Cranmer produced the Book of Common Prayer in 1549 and its more thoroughly Protestant revision of 1552, the Forty-two Articles of religion, and an Ordinal; images were removed from churches, chantries dissolved, altars replaced by communion tables, and clerical marriage legalised. The king took a close personal interest in these reforms and in sermons at court, keeping a journal (his "Chronicle") of events.

Northumberland and the succession crisis

Northumberland's regime ended the Scottish and French wars, but when Edward fell ill in 1553 — probably with tuberculosis — the prospect of his Catholic half-sister Mary succeeding threatened the entire Protestant settlement. Edward, on his deathbed, approved a "Devise for the Succession" excluding both Mary and Elizabeth and settling the crown on his Protestant cousin Lady Jane Grey, who had conveniently married Northumberland's son Guildford Dudley. Jane was proclaimed queen on 10 July 1553, but support rallied to Mary within days; Jane reigned for nine days before being deposed (and was executed the following year).

Death and legacy

Edward died at Greenwich on 6 July 1553, aged fifteen, and was buried in Westminster Abbey. His short reign fixed Protestant worship in forms that, after Mary's Catholic restoration, returned almost unchanged under the Elizabethan settlement of 1559. Schools founded or refounded in his name ("King Edward's Schools") dot England, and Mark Twain's The Prince and the Pauper fixed his image in popular culture.
