Development of the New Testament canon

The development of the New Testament canon was a long process by which the early Christian churches came to recognise a definite collection of writings — eventually 27 books — as authoritative scripture alongside the Hebrew Bible. There was no single moment of decision: the canon emerged over the first four centuries of the Common Era through usage in worship, theological controversy, and the judgements of bishops and councils.

Early collections

Collections of Paul's letters circulated among churches by the late first century, and the four Gospels were treated as a set by the late second century, when Irenaeus argued that there could be neither more nor fewer than four. Around 144 AD the Roman teacher Marcion proposed the first known Christian canon, consisting of an edited Gospel and ten Pauline letters, and excluding the Old Testament entirely; the church's rejection of Marcionism spurred it to define its own, much larger canon. The Muratorian fragment, usually dated to the later second century, lists most of the books of the present New Testament, while works such as the Shepherd of Hermas and the Didache were read in some churches but ultimately excluded.

Criteria and controversies

Churches weighed criteria of apostolic origin, antiquity, widespread use in worship ("catholicity") and conformity with the rule of faith. Some books were long disputed — Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude and Revelation (the antilegomena) — while others now excluded, such as 1 Clement and the Shepherd, enjoyed near-canonical status in places. Eusebius of Caesarea, in the early fourth century, classified writings into accepted, disputed and spurious categories.

Closure

The earliest surviving list that matches exactly the present 27-book canon is the Easter letter of Athanasius of Alexandria in 367 AD. Councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) endorsed essentially the same list in the West. The canon was never formally closed by an ecumenical council of the undivided church, and differences remain: the Syriac Peshitta tradition long lacked several short epistles, Ethiopian Christianity retains a broader canon, and the Reformers of the sixteenth century debated the disputed books while retaining them.
