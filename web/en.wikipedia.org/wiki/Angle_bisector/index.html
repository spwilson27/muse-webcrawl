Angle bisector

In geometry, an angle bisector is a line, ray or segment that divides an angle into two equal angles. The bisector of an angle is the locus of points inside the angle that are equidistant from its two sides. Angle bisection is one of the classical constructions achievable with compass and straightedge, described in Euclid's Elements.

Construction

To bisect an angle with compass and straightedge: draw an arc centred at the vertex cutting both sides of the angle; from each intersection point draw arcs of equal radius meeting inside the angle; the ray from the vertex through that meeting point is the bisector. Unlike angle trisection, bisection is always possible with these tools, and repeating it divides an angle into 4, 8, 16, … equal parts.

In triangles

The Angle Bisector Theorem states that the bisector of an angle of a triangle divides the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides: if a bisector from vertex A meets BC at D, then BD/DC = AB/AC. The three internal bisectors of a triangle are concurrent at the incentre, the centre of the triangle's incircle, which is equidistant from all three sides. The length of a bisector can be computed from the side lengths; one formula gives ta2 = bc[1 − (a2/(b+c)2)].

External bisectors

The internal and external bisectors at a vertex are perpendicular. External bisectors concur with internal ones at the excentres of the triangle. The bisectors of a pair of intersecting lines form the locus of points equidistant from both lines, a fact used in optics and in the theory of conic sections.
