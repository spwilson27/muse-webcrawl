Chitin (from Greek χιτών, "tunic, envelope") is a long-chain polymer of N-acetylglucosamine, an amide derivative of glucose. It is a primary component of cell walls in fungi, the exoskeletons of arthropods (such as crustaceans and insects), the radulae of molluscs, the beaks and internal shells of cephalopods, and the scales of fish. It is the second most abundant polysaccharide in nature after cellulose.

Chitin's structure is comparable to cellulose, with each glucose monomer bearing an acetylamine group; the chains hydrogen-bond into stiff microfibrils. In crustacean shells the chitin matrix is mineralized with calcium carbonate, and in insects it is composite with proteins such as sclerotin. Pure chitin is translucent, pliable and resilient; in organisms it is modified to be stronger or more elastic as needed.

Uses

Chitin is extracted commercially from shrimp and crab shell waste; its deacetylated derivative chitosan is biodegradable and biocompatible and is used in wound dressings, drug delivery, water treatment, agriculture (seed treatments and biopesticides), and as a dietary supplement. Chitin and chitosan are also researched for tissue engineering, bioplastics and food packaging.
