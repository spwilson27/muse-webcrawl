Zoot Suit is a play by Chicano playwright Luis Valdez, first produced in 1978 at the Mark Taper Forum in Los Angeles. Based on the Sleepy Lagoon murder trial of 1942 and the Zoot Suit Riots of 1943, the play mixes courtroom drama, Brechtian commentary and big-band music to dramatise the discrimination faced by Mexican-American youth in wartime Los Angeles. Its success made it the first Chicano play to be produced on Broadway, where it opened in 1979, and it remains a landmark of Latino theatre in the United States.

Plot and characters

The play centres on Henry Reyna, a pachuco gang leader wrongfully convicted with his friends for a murder at the Sleepy Lagoon reservoir, and on his mythical alter ego, El Pachuco — a strutting figure in a zoot suit who narrates, interrupts and comments on the action, even stopping the play to address the audience. Around the trial, the play dramatises press hysteria, racial profiling, and the riots in which American servicemen attacked zoot-suited youths. The convictions were overturned on appeal in 1944, which the play treats as a bittersweet victory.

Production history

Developed with Valdez's company El Teatro Campesino, Zoot Suit was a sensation in Los Angeles before transferring to Broadway's Winter Garden Theatre in March 1979, where Edward James Olmos's performance as El Pachuco earned a Tony nomination. Valdez directed a film adaptation in 1981, again starring Olmos. Major revivals include the Center Theatre Group's 2017 production at the Mark Taper Forum for the play's approaching 40th anniversary.

Themes

The play explores identity, assimilation, racism and the myth-making of the press, and it reclaimed the pachuco — long stereotyped as a delinquent — as an emblem of cultural resistance. Its bilingual dialogue and corridos helped define Chicano theatre aesthetics.
