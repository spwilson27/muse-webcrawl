Galdr

Galdr (Old Norse: galdr, plural galdrar) is a form of sung or chanted spellcasting in Old Norse tradition — a type of magic performed through incantation, often with a high-pitched or falsetto voice. The word derives from Old Norse gala, "to sing, chant, crow", cognate with Old English galan, the root of "nightingale".

Odin is said to have known eighteen galdrar.

In the Eddas

The poem Hávamál attributes to the god Odin a catalogue of eighteen powerful spells, the Ljóðatal, covering purposes such as healing, blunting enemy weapons, freeing oneself from bonds, calming storms, and speaking with the dead. In Grógaldr, the dead völva Gróa is raised from her grave by her son Svipdagr and sings protective galdrar over him. In Skírnismál, the god Freyr's messenger Skírnir threatens the giantess Gerðr with curses and runes.

Practice and associations

Galdr was closely associated with seiðr, the Norse shamanic magic worked by (mostly female) practitioners called völvas, and with runic inscriptions — several runestones and bracteates may preserve formulaic words connected to chant magic. In the Christian period, performing galdrar was condemned; Icelandic law codes and later witchcraft trials (in which galdr was central to the charges in Iceland's notably male-focused witch persecutions) treated it as illicit sorcery.

Modern Heathen practitioners have revived galdr as the intoning of rune names and charms, though historical details of melody and performance are unrecoverable.
