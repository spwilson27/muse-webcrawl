Spithead and Nore mutinies

The Spithead and Nore mutinies were two major mutinies by British sailors of the Royal Navy in 1797, during the French Revolutionary Wars. The Spithead mutiny, by sailors of the Channel Fleet anchored off Portsmouth, was a largely orderly strike for better pay and conditions. The subsequent mutiny at the Nore, an anchorage in the Thames estuary, took on a more political character and ended in failure and executions.

Background

Naval pay had not risen since 1653, while wartime inflation had eroded its value. Sailors also protested against poor-quality provisions, harsh discipline, and the practice of paying off ships only after long delays. Unrest spread through the fleet in the spring of 1797, fuelled by correspondence between ships and by the example of successful industrial action elsewhere.

Spithead

On 16 April 1797 the Channel Fleet refused orders to sail. The sailors elected delegates, presented petitions, and maintained discipline aboard their ships, returning captured officers ashore with courtesy. The Admiralty conceded the main demands: a pay increase, better provisions, and a royal pardon for all involved. The fleet returned to duty in May.

The Nore

The mutiny at the Nore began on 12 May and adopted broader demands, including the dissolution of Parliament and peace with France. Led by Richard Parker, styling himself "President of the Delegates of the Fleet," the mutineers blockaded London shipping. Support ebbed as authorities cut off supplies, and the mutiny collapsed in June. Parker and around thirty others were hanged.

Legacy

The mutinies shocked the British government at a moment of invasion fears, but the Spithead settlement permanently improved sailors' pay, while the harsher repression after the Nore deterred comparable revolts for decades.
