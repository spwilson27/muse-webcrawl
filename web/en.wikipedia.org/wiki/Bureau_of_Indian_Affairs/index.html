The Bureau of Indian Affairs (BIA) is an agency of the United States federal government within the Department of the Interior. It is responsible for the administration and management of land held in trust by the United States government for Native American tribes and individuals, and for carrying out the federal government's treaty and trust responsibilities to the 574 federally recognised tribes.

History

The BIA was created in 1824 by Secretary of War John C. Calhoun within the War Department, without authorisation from Congress, and was transferred to the new Department of the Interior in 1849. Through the 19th century the bureau administered policies of removal, reservation confinement, and allotment under the Dawes Act of 1887, which broke up tribal lands into individual parcels and cost tribes some 90 million acres. In the 20th century, the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 under Commissioner John Collier ended allotment and encouraged tribal self-government, while the termination policy of the 1950s was in turn reversed by the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, which enabled tribes to contract to run federal programmes themselves.

Responsibilities

The bureau provides services including law enforcement through BIA police, fire management, road maintenance, social services, and support for tribal governments across roughly 55 million acres of trust land. Its sister agency, the Bureau of Indian Education, funds and operates schools serving Native students. Since 1977 the bureau has been headed by an Assistant Secretary for Indian Affairs; its director is a career position. Much of the BIA's modern work concerns land-into-trust decisions, water rights settlements, and the probate and leasing of fractionated trust allotments.
