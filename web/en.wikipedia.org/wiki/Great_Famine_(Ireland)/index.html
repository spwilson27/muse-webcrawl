The Great Famine (Irish: an Gorta Mór [ənˠ ˈɡɔɾˠt̪ˠə ˈmˠoːɾˠ]), also known in Irish as an Drochshaol ("the bad life") and in English as the Great Hunger, was a period of mass starvation and disease in Ireland from 1845 to 1852. Its immediate cause was the potato blight Phytophthora infestans, which destroyed the potato crop on which roughly a third of the population depended for food. Approximately one million people died and a further million emigrated, reducing Ireland's population by 20–25 per cent.

Causes

By the 1840s, Ireland's poorest tenant farmers relied almost entirely on the potato — grown on tiny subdivided plots under a landlord system — because it produced more calories per acre than any alternative. When the blight arrived from the Americas in 1845, successive harvests failed while other food continued to be produced and, controversially, exported. Historians debate the adequacy of the British government's relief response, shaped by laissez-faire ideology, the Poor Law and workhouse system, and mass evictions by landlords.

Consequences

The Famine permanently transformed Ireland. Emigration — to North America, Britain and Australia — continued for decades, creating a vast Irish diaspora; Ireland's population kept falling into the 20th century and has never regained its 1841 peak of about 8.2 million. The catastrophe hastened the decline of the Irish language, reshaped land ownership through the Encumbered Estates Acts and later land reforms, and became a defining grievance in Irish nationalism. It remains commemorated by memorials worldwide.
