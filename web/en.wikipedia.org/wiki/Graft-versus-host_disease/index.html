Graft-versus-host disease

Graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) is a syndrome, characterized by inflammation in different organs, that is caused by donor immune cells attacking the recipient's tissues. It is commonly associated with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (bone marrow transplant), but can also occur after transfusion of non-irradiated blood products to immunocompromised recipients (transfusion-associated GvHD). In GvHD, the immunocompetent T cells present in the graft recognize the recipient's antigens as foreign and mount an immune response against the recipient's skin, liver, gastrointestinal tract and other organs. GvHD occurs in roughly 30–50 percent of recipients of stem cells from a matched related donor, and more often with less-matched donors, and it remains a leading cause of non-relapse mortality after transplantation.

Classification

GvHD is divided by clinical features (following NIH consensus criteria) into:

  • Acute GvHD: classically arising within the first 100 days after transplant (though "late acute" forms occur), affecting skin (maculopapular rash that can progress to blistering), liver (cholestatic jaundice) and gut (secretory diarrhea, pain, bleeding). Severity is graded I–IV by organ staging; grade III–IV disease carries a poor prognosis.
  • Chronic GvHD: a multi-system, often fibrotic and autoimmune-like syndrome typically arising after day 100, affecting skin (lichenoid or sclerotic changes), mouth, eyes (dry eye), lungs (bronchiolitis obliterans), liver, joints and genitalia. It is the leading cause of late non-relapse mortality and impaired quality of life in transplant survivors.
  • Overlap syndrome: features of both acute and chronic disease.

Pathophysiology

Three conditions, formulated by Rupert Billingham in the 1960s, are required: the graft must contain immunocompetent cells; the recipient must express tissue antigens absent in the donor; and the recipient must be incapable of rejecting the graft. In acute GvHD, tissue damage from the conditioning regimen (chemotherapy/radiation) releases inflammatory cytokines that activate recipient antigen-presenting cells, which in turn activate donor T cells; these expand and attack target tissues. Chronic GvHD additionally involves dysregulated B cells, aberrant thymic selection and fibrosis driven by macrophages and TGF-β. A related beneficial phenomenon, the graft-versus-leukemia effect, in which donor cells attack residual cancer, is a major reason allogeneic transplantation can cure hematologic malignancies.

Prevention

Standard prophylaxis combines a calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine or tacrolimus) with methotrexate or mycophenolate. Post-transplant cyclophosphamide (PTCy) — which preferentially depletes alloreactive T cells — has transformed haploidentical (half-matched) transplantation and, following the BMT CTN 1703 trial, became a standard prophylaxis in matched-donor transplantation as well in many centers. Other strategies include anti-thymocyte globulin, abatacept (approved for prophylaxis in certain settings), T-cell depletion of the graft, and irradiation of blood products to prevent transfusion-associated GvHD, which is rare but almost always fatal.

Treatment

First-line treatment of significant acute GvHD is high-dose systemic corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone 2 mg/kg/day), to which roughly half of patients respond. Steroid-refractory acute GvHD is treated with the JAK1/2 inhibitor ruxolitinib, which improved outcomes in the REACH2 trial and was FDA-approved in 2019. For chronic GvHD, corticosteroids remain first line; approved later-line agents include ibrutinib, ruxolitinib and the ROCK2 inhibitor belumosudil, alongside extracorporeal photopheresis and other immunosuppressants. Management also requires infection prophylaxis and supportive care, since both the disease and its treatment are profoundly immunosuppressive.
