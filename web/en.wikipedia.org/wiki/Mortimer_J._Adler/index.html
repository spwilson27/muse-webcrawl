Mortimer Jerome Adler (December 28, 1902 – June 28, 2001) was an American philosopher, educator, encyclopedist, and popular author. As a philosopher he worked within the Aristotelian and Thomistic traditions. He taught at Columbia University and the University of Chicago, served as chairman of the board of editors of Encyclopædia Britannica, and founded the Institute for Philosophical Research.

Life and career

Adler left school at fourteen to work as a copy boy for the New York Sun, but discovered the great books through John Stuart Mill's autobiography and talked his way into Columbia, taking night classes; he never received a bachelor's degree (having refused the swimming requirement) but earned a doctorate in psychology in 1928 and taught there. In 1930 he moved to the University of Chicago, where with Robert Maynard Hutchins he developed the Great Books curriculum built on seminar discussion of classic texts.

His bestseller How to Read a Book (1940) taught generations of readers analytical reading and became the founding document of the Great Books movement. With Hutchins he edited Britannica's 54-volume Great Books of the Western World (1952) and its Syntopicon, an index of the tradition's great ideas. Adler later directed the Paideia Proposal (1982) for democratic school reform and published more than fifty books, including Aristotle for Everybody and his intellectual autobiographies. He received the National Medal of Arts in 1991.

Philosophy

Adler defended objective truth and a realist, common-sense philosophy against relativism and modern scepticism, arguing that philosophy should address the perennial questions — the "great ideas" such as truth, goodness, beauty, liberty, and equality — that recur throughout the Western tradition. Late in life he described his religious journey in How to Think About God (1980) and was baptised into the Episcopal Church in 1984.
