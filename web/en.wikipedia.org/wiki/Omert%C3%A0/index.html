Omertà

Omertà

OriginSouthern Italy (Sicily, Campania, Calabria)
MeaningCode of silence about criminal activity
Associated withMafia organisations

Omertà (Italian pronunciation: [omerˈta]) is a Southern Italian code of silence and honour that places importance on silence in the face of questioning by authorities or outsiders; non-cooperation with authorities, the government, or outsiders; and willfully ignoring and generally avoiding interference with the illegal activities of others.

Origins

The word is thought to derive from the Sicilian omu ("man") or from Latin humilitas reshaped through Spanish influence. The code emerged in the 19th century in regions where state authority was weak or distrusted, and it became a defining feature of the Sicilian Mafia, the Camorra, and the 'Ndrangheta.

The code

In its classic formulation, omertà forbids a person from seeking redress from the law, requires wrongs to be avenged personally or through intermediaries, and treats informing — the role of the infame — as the gravest dishonour, punishable by death. Breaking omertà is what distinguishes a pentito (turncoat witness) in Italian anti-Mafia trials.

Decline

Since the Maxi Trials of the 1980s and the assassinations of judges Giovanni Falcone and Paolo Borsellino in 1992, which provoked public revulsion, thousands of mafiosi have broken the code to cooperate with the state, and public campaigns have sought to erode its hold on everyday life.
