Philippine adobo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A bowl of chicken and pork adobo glistening with its dark sauce]

Philippine adobo is the unofficial national dish of the Philippines: meat — chicken, pork, or both — braised in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, bay leaves and black peppercorns until tender and bronzed in its own sauce. The method is older than the name: pre-colonial Filipinos preserved meat in vinegar and salt in a tropical climate, Chinese traders added soy sauce, and Spanish colonisers, recognising a marinade, called it adobo (from adobar, to marinate) — though it shares little with Spain's own adobos or Mexico's chile-based ones beyond the word. Every province and family keeps a version: with coconut milk in the south (adobo sa gata), without soy sauce in "white" adobo, crisped into flakes, or dried down until the sauce all but vanishes and the meat fries in its own fat.

1. The standardisation quarrel

The standardisation quarrel

In 2021 the Philippines' Department of Trade and Industry announced a technical committee to develop a national standard recipe for adobo (and other classics) for international promotion — and the country erupted, mostly in delighted outrage: whose adobo? With potato? With sugar, as some regions insist? The government clarified the standard would be a reference, not a law, and the episode entered the dish's lore as proof of what adobo means: a technique (vinegar first, always) open to a thousand loyal interpretations. Its keeping quality is the ancestral point — adobo tastes better, and safer, on the second day, a refrigerator-free technology that colonial observers already remarked on.
