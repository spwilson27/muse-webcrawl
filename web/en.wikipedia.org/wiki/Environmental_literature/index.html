Environmental literature

Environmental literature is writing — including fiction, nonfiction, poetry and drama — that treats the relationship between human beings and the natural environment as a central concern. It encompasses the older tradition of nature writing, from Gilbert White and Henry David Thoreau to the conservation-minded works that helped spur the modern environmental movement, as well as contemporary "climate fiction" addressing anthropogenic climate change.

History

The genre has roots in pastoral poetry and in early natural history. In Britain, Gilbert White's The Natural History of Selborne (1789) modelled close observation of a single place. In the United States, Thoreau's Walden (1854) and the essays of John Muir shaped a tradition of reflective nature writing tied to preservation. In the 20th century, Aldo Leopold's A Sand County Almanac (1949) articulated a "land ethic," and Rachel Carson's Silent Spring (1962) — an exposé of pesticide damage — is widely credited with launching modern environmentalism.

Themes and study

Environmental literature commonly explores wilderness, sustainability, environmental justice, human impacts on ecosystems, and nonhuman perspectives. Its academic study is ecocriticism, which emerged in the late 20th century and reads texts in terms of their representations of nature and ecological ideas. Related movements include bioregional writing and the literature of place.

Climate fiction

Since the early 2000s, novels engaging directly with climate change — sometimes labelled "cli-fi" — have become prominent, alongside nonfiction on climate science and policy by writers such as Elizabeth Kolbert.
