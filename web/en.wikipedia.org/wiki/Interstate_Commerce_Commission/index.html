Interstate Commerce Commission


Formed: February 4, 1887
Dissolved: January 1, 1996
Successor: Surface Transportation Board

The Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) was a regulatory agency in the United States created by the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887. The agency's original purpose was to regulate railroads (and later trucking) to ensure fair rates, to eliminate rate discrimination, and to regulate other aspects of common carriers. It was the first independent federal regulatory agency and a model for later bodies.

History

The ICC was created in response to public demand, led by farmers organised in the Granger movement, that railroad operations be regulated. Its powers were strengthened by the Elkins Act (1903), the Hepburn Act (1906), which gave it authority to set maximum rates, and the Mann–Elkins Act (1910). Its jurisdiction expanded to telephone and telegraph (1910), interstate trucking and buses under the Motor Carrier Act of 1935, and domestic water carriers.

Deregulation and abolition

From the 1970s, deregulation sharply reduced the ICC's role: the Railroad Revitalization and Regulatory Reform Act (1976), the Motor Carrier Act of 1980, and the Staggers Rail Act (1980) freed most rates and routes. The ICC Termination Act of 1995 abolished the commission effective January 1, 1996, transferring its remaining functions to the new Surface Transportation Board.
