First Nations in Canada

First Nations are the predominant Indigenous peoples in Canada south of the Arctic Circle, one of three constitutionally recognised groups of Aboriginal peoples alongside Inuit and Métis. There are more than 630 First Nations communities, speaking over 50 distinct languages, including Cree, Ojibwe, Innu, Dene and Haida nations.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited what is now Canada for at least 12,000 years, developing diverse societies, trade networks and confederacies such as the Haudenosaunee. European contact brought trade, alliances, epidemics and colonisation. The Royal Proclamation of 1763 recognised Aboriginal title; the Numbered Treaties (1871–1921) ceded vast territories. The Indian Act (1876) imposed band governance, reserves and status rules, and the residential school system (to 1996) forcibly assimilated children — the subject of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2008–2015).

Contemporary issues

First Nations pursue self-government and land claims, modern treaties and litigation (e.g. Delgamuukw, Tsilhqot'in), while facing disparities in water, housing, health and education, and movements such as Idle No More. The federal government formally adopted UNDRIP legislation in 2021.

Terminology

"First Nations" came into common use in the 1970s–80s, replacing "Indian", which remains a legal term under the Indian Act. Individual nations prefer their own names.
