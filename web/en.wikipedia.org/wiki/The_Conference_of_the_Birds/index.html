The Conference of the Birds

The Conference of the Birds or Speech of the Birds (Persian: Manṭiq-uṭ-Ṭayr, 1177) is a celebrated Persian poem by the Sufi poet Farid ud-Din Attar of Nishapur. Running to some 4,500 lines, it is one of the masterpieces of Persian literature and of Sufi allegory.

Synopsis

The birds of the world gather to decide who shall be their sovereign. The hoopoe, the wisest among them, proposes that they seek the legendary Simurgh, a great bird of Persian mythology. Though many birds — the nightingale, the parrot, the peacock, the duck, and others — make excuses rooted in their worldly attachments, the hoopoe answers each objection with parables. A company of birds sets out across seven valleys: the Valleys of Quest, Love, Knowledge, Detachment, Unity of God, Wonderment, and finally Poverty and Annihilation. Most perish or turn back; only thirty birds reach the dwelling of the Simurgh, where they discover that the Simurgh is themselves — the Persian phrase si murgh means "thirty birds."

Themes and influence

The poem allegorises the soul's journey toward God, in which the traveller ultimately finds the divine within the annihilated self. It has inspired poets, illustrators and dramatists for centuries; Peter Brook and Jean-Claude Carrière's stage adaptation toured the world in the 1970s, and illustrated manuscripts of the poem are treasures of Islamic art.
