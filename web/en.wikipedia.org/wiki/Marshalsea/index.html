Marshalsea

The Marshalsea was a notorious prison in Southwark, just south of the River Thames in London, England. In the first half of the 19th century it was primarily a debtor's prison, housing mostly debtors owing small sums. It became internationally notorious through the writings of Charles Dickens, whose father John Dickens was imprisoned there in 1824, and who set much of his novel Little Dorrit (1857) in the prison.

History

The prison took its name from the Marshalsea Court, the court of the Royal Household's Marshal, which originally dealt with offences within the verge of the court. The first Marshalsea prison stood in the High Street, Borough; in 1811 it moved to a new site off Borough High Street, near St George's Fields. Conditions in the prison were harsh: inmates paid fees for lodging, food, and even for the removal of leg irons, and those unable to pay could starve. A parliamentary investigation in 1729 had already exposed extreme cruelty in the earlier prison, including torture and deaths.

The Dickens connection

John Dickens was imprisoned for a debt of £40 to a baker in February 1824. The twelve-year-old Charles was sent to work at Warren's blacking factory while his family lived in the prison. The experience scarred Dickens for life. In Little Dorrit, the Marshalsea is the birthplace and lifelong home of Amy Dorrit, and her father William is the "Father of the Marshalsea", its longest-serving inmate.

Closure and remains

The prison was closed in 1842 and its inmates transferred to the Queen's Prison. The buildings were largely demolished; a long brick wall survives in Angel Place, with a plaque commemorating Dickens and Little Dorrit.
