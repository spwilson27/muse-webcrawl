The House of Hanover (German: Haus Hannover) is a European royal house, a branch of the House of Welf, itself a younger branch of the Italian House of Este. The Hanoverians originated in the Duchy of Brunswick-Lüneburg in northern Germany. Members of the house ruled the Electorate (later Kingdom) of Hanover, and from 1714 until 1901 provided the monarchs of Great Britain and, after the Acts of Union 1800, of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland.

British succession

Under the Act of Settlement 1701, which excluded Roman Catholics from the throne, the Protestant Electress Sophia of Hanover — granddaughter of James I of England — and her heirs were placed in the line of succession. On the death of Queen Anne in 1714, Sophia's son succeeded as George I, the first Hanoverian king of Great Britain, despite speaking little English at his accession. He was followed by George II, George III, George IV, William IV, and finally Queen Victoria.

Because Hanoverian (Salic) law barred female succession in Hanover, the personal union between Britain and Hanover ended on Victoria's accession in 1837, when the Hanoverian crown passed to her uncle Ernest Augustus I. The British branch of the house ended with Victoria's death in 1901, when the throne passed to her son Edward VII of the House of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha.

Hanover

The Hanoverian line in Germany continued as Dukes of Brunswick-Lüneburg and Electors, then from 1814 as Kings of Hanover. The kingdom was annexed by Prussia after the Austro-Prussian War of 1866, and the last king, George V, went into exile. The deposed Hanoverian royals continued to style themselves heads of the house; the current head is Ernst August, Prince of Hanover.
