Numinous

Numinous (from Latin numen, "divine power" or "nod") is a term derived from the Latin numen that describes the sense of awe, mystery, and fascination experienced in the presence of the divine or the holy. It was made prominent in religious studies by the German theologian Rudolf Otto in his 1917 book The Idea of the Holy (Das Heilige).

Otto's account

Otto described the numinous experience as a mysterium tremendum et fascinans — a mystery that is at once terrifying (tremendum) and compelling or fascinating (fascinans). The experience includes a feeling of creature-dependence before an overwhelming "wholly other" reality, accompanied by awe, majesty, urgency, and wonder. Otto argued that this non-rational dimension of religion is its core, prior to ethical or doctrinal elaborations.

Influence

The concept strongly influenced 20th-century theology and the phenomenology of religion, and was taken up by C. S. Lewis, who discussed the numinous in The Problem of Pain, and by Carl Jung, who treated numinous experiences as encounters with archetypes of the unconscious and used the term extensively in his psychology. Mircea Eliade's related notion of hierophany also reflects this tradition.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether the numinous is a universal, irreducible category of experience or a construct shaped by particular theological commitments; anthropologists note wide cultural variation in how encounters with sacred power are described.
