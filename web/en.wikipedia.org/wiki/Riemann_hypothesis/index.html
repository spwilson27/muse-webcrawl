Riemann hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A plot of the zeta function in the complex plane, with the critical line marked]

The Riemann hypothesis is a conjecture about the distribution of the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, proposed by Bernhard Riemann in his 1859 paper 'On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude'. It states that all non-trivial zeros of the zeta function have real part 1/2.

It is generally considered the most important unsolved problem in pure mathematics. It is one of Hilbert's problems (the 8th, 1900) and one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems (2000), carrying a US$1 million prize.

Significance

The zeta function ζ(s), defined for complex s, encodes the distribution of the prime numbers through Euler's product formula connecting it to the primes. The hypothesis controls the error term in the prime number theorem: it implies primes are as regularly distributed as possible. Trillions of zeros have been computed numerically, all on the 'critical line' Re(s) = 1/2, but no proof exists; a disproof would be equally momentous.

Generalisations (the generalised Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions) underlie many conditional results in analytic number theory and algorithms (e.g. deterministic primality testing).

Attempts

Hardy proved infinitely many zeros lie on the critical line (1914); Selberg and others increased the proportion, and modern computations push verification ever higher. Approaches via operator theory and random matrix theory have yielded deep analogies but no proof.
