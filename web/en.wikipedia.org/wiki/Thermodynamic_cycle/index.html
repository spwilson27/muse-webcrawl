Thermodynamic cycle

A thermodynamic cycle consists of a linked series of thermodynamic processes involving the transfer of heat and work into and out of a system, while varying pressure, temperature and other state variables, that eventually returns the system to its initial state. In the process, the system may convert heat into work (a heat engine) or use work to move heat (a heat pump or refrigerator).

Heat engines and refrigeration

Because the system returns to its initial state, its net change in internal energy over a cycle is zero, so the net work done equals the net heat absorbed, in accordance with the first law of thermodynamics. On a pressure–volume diagram a clockwise cycle represents a heat engine, its enclosed area being the net work output; an anticlockwise cycle represents a refrigerator or heat pump.

Examples

Important model cycles include the Carnot cycle (the most efficient possible between two temperatures), the Rankine cycle of steam power plants, the Otto cycle of petrol engines, the Diesel cycle, the Brayton cycle of gas turbines and jet engines, and the Stirling cycle. Real machines approximate these idealised cycles.
