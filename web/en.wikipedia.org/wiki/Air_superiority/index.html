Air superiority

Air superiority
DomainAerial warfare

MeansFighter aircraft, suppression of enemy air defences, strikes on airfields

Air superiority is the degree of dominance of one side's air power over another in aerial warfare that permits the conduct of operations by the dominant side's land, sea and air forces at a given time and place without prohibitive interference by the opposing force. NATO and US doctrine distinguish it from air supremacy, the highest degree of control, in which the opposing air force is incapable of effective interference at all.

Levels of control

Classifications run from air supremacy, through air superiority and a favourable air situation (enemy air action possible but unable to seriously affect operations), to air parity (neither side controls the skies) and air denial (one side cannot fly without heavy losses but prevents the enemy from enjoying superiority either, for example through dense surface-to-air missile defences).

History

The concept emerged in the First World War, when fighters first fought for control of the air above the trenches. In the Second World War, the Battle of Britain turned on Germany's failure to win air superiority over southern England, while by 1944–45 the Allies had achieved near-supremacy over both Europe and the Pacific. In the Korean, Vietnam and Arab–Israeli wars, air superiority was contested by increasingly capable fighters and missiles. The 1991 Gulf War demonstrated coalition air supremacy, won through stealth aircraft, precision weapons and the systematic suppression of Iraqi air defences.

Methods

Air superiority is gained by defeating enemy aircraft in air combat, destroying them on the ground, suppressing or destroying air defence systems (SEAD/DEAD), and attacking supporting infrastructure such as radar, command posts and fuel. Dedicated air superiority fighters — such as the F-15 Eagle and F-22 Raptor — are designed primarily for this mission.
