Prince Gong Mansion, in the Shichahai area of Xicheng District, Beijing, is the largest and best-preserved princely mansion of the Qing dynasty. The residence was built in 1777 for Heshen, the powerful and notoriously corrupt favourite of the Qianlong Emperor. After Heshen's fall in 1799, the mansion was confiscated and granted to Prince Qing, and in 1851 the Xianfeng Emperor gave it to his brother Yixin, Prince Gong, from whom it takes its present name.

Layout

The complex covers roughly 60,000 square metres, divided between the residential palace in the south and gardens in the north. The residence follows the regulated layout of a princely establishment, with halls along a central axis flanked by courtyards. The rear garden (Hua Yuan) contains artificial hills, pavilions, ponds and a large rock carved with the character fu (福, "good fortune"), said to be in the calligraphy of the Kangxi Emperor and long rumoured — probably falsely — to be one of Beijing's treasures.

Notable features

The mansion includes a grand theatre house, one of the compound's "three treasures", where Prince Gong entertained guests with opera. Another treasure is the Western-style gate. The writer's great hall and the bat-shaped motifs throughout the garden — bats symbolising fortune — are popular with visitors.

Later history

In the 20th century the mansion housed Fu Jen Catholic University and government offices, and parts fell into disrepair. A major restoration, begun in the 1980s and completed for the 2008 Olympics, opened the entire complex as a museum and major tourist attraction.
