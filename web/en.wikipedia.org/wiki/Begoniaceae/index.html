Begoniaceae is a family of flowering plants in the order Cucurbitales, comprising two genera: Begonia, one of the largest genera of flowering plants with more than 2,000 described species, and Hillebrandia, a monotypic genus endemic to Hawaii. The family is pantropical in distribution, with the greatest diversity in the Americas and Asia.

Description

Members of Begoniaceae are mostly succulent herbs, sometimes climbing or shrubby. Their leaves are characteristically asymmetrical — the basis for the common description of begonias as having "lopsided" foliage — with palmate venation and fleshy stems that store water. Plants are monoecious: male and female flowers occur on the same plant, the males bearing prominent stamens and the females an inferior, often winged ovary that develops into a capsule containing numerous tiny seeds.

Taxonomy

The genus Begonia was named by Charles Plumier in honor of Michel Bégon, a French governor of Saint-Domingue and patron of botany, and the name was adopted by Linnaeus. New species continue to be described at a rapid rate, particularly from Southeast Asia and South America, making Begonia one of the fastest-growing plant genera taxonomically. Hillebrandia sandwicensis, the family's only other species, is the sole member of the family native to the Hawaiian Islands and is thought to be an ancient lineage sister to all of Begonia.

Cultivation

Begonias are among the most popular ornamental plants worldwide, bred in thousands of cultivars grouped horticulturally as wax (fibrous-rooted) begonias, tuberous begonias grown for their large showy flowers, and rhizomatous and rex begonias grown for patterned foliage. They are used as bedding plants, houseplants, and in hanging baskets.
