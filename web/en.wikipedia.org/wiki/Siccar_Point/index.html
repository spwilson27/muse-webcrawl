Siccar Point is a rocky headland on the North Sea coast of Berwickshire in the Scottish Borders, famous in the history of geology as the site of Hutton's Unconformity. Here, gently sloping Devonian Old Red Sandstone rests directly on top of steeply tilted Silurian greywacke strata — a junction that provided crucial evidence for the immense age of the Earth and for the uniformitarian theory of James Hutton.

At Siccar Point, near-vertical Silurian greywacke is capped by almost horizontal Devonian sandstone.

Hutton's visit

In June 1788, geologist James Hutton sailed along the coast with John Playfair and James Hall in search of an exposure he had predicted from his theory that rock strata are deposited, uplifted, eroded and buried in endlessly repeating cycles. Finding the unconformity at Siccar Point, Hutton recognised that the tilted greywacke must have been deposited as sediment, hardened, raised into mountains, and worn down to a plain before the red sandstone above it was laid down — a sequence requiring time on a scale far beyond the few thousand years then commonly accepted for the age of the Earth. Playfair later wrote of the experience that "the mind seemed to grow giddy by looking so far into the abyss of time."

Geology

The lower greywackes were deposited as deep-marine turbidite sediments around 435 million years ago during the Silurian period and were crumpled nearly vertical during the Caledonian orogeny. After an interval of roughly 65 million years — represented by the eroded junction itself — rivers of the Devonian period deposited the red breccias and sandstones of the Old Red Sandstone across the planed-off surface. Siccar Point remains a pilgrimage site for geologists and is protected as a Site of Special Scientific Interest.
