Apsis

An apsis (plural apsides) is the farthest or nearest point in the orbit of a planetary body about its primary body. The line of apsides is the line connecting the two extreme points. For orbits about the Sun, the points are called aphelion (farthest) and perihelion (nearest); for orbits about Earth, apogee and perigee; and generically, apoapsis and periapsis. The word comes from Ancient Greek ἁψίς (hapsís), "arch, vault, orbit". (Not to be confused with the architectural apse, though the words share this Greek root.)

Orbital mechanics

For an elliptical orbit with semi-major axis a and eccentricity e, the periapsis distance is a(1 − e) and the apoapsis distance is a(1 + e). By Kepler's second law and conservation of angular momentum, the orbiting body moves fastest at periapsis and slowest at apoapsis; its speeds obey the vis-viva relation. Earth passes perihelion (about 147.1 million km) in early January and aphelion (about 152.1 million km) in early July, so the seasons are governed by axial tilt rather than by this distance variation. Over long timescales the line of apsides rotates — apsidal precession — caused by perturbations from other bodies and, for Mercury, by general relativity; the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion (43 arcseconds per century beyond Newtonian predictions) was an early triumph of Einstein's theory.

Argument of periapsis

The orientation of the apsides within the orbital plane is specified by the argument of periapsis (ω), measured from the ascending node, and together with the longitude of periapsis it fixes the orbit's direction in space — quantities central to mission design, eclipse prediction, and the study of exoplanets, whose periastra feature in radial-velocity and transit analyses.
