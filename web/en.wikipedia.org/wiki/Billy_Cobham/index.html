William Emanuel Cobham Jr. (born May 16, 1944) is a Panamanian-American jazz drummer who came to prominence in the late 1960s and early 1970s with trumpeter Miles Davis and the Mahavishnu Orchestra. Renowned for his power, ambidexterity, and precision, he is widely regarded as one of the most influential drummers in jazz fusion, pioneering a style that combined jazz technique with the volume and drive of rock.

Early life and career

Cobham was born in Colón, Panama, and moved with his family to New York City at the age of three. Coming from a musical family, he began drumming as a child, studied at the High School of Music & Art, and served as a percussionist in a U.S. Army band from 1965 to 1968. After his discharge he joined Horace Silver's quintet and co-founded the early fusion band Dreams with Randy and Michael Brecker.

Miles Davis and Mahavishnu Orchestra

Cobham's session work brought him to Miles Davis's studio bands, appearing on albums including Bitches Brew (1970), A Tribute to Jack Johnson, and On the Corner. In 1971 guitarist John McLaughlin recruited him for the Mahavishnu Orchestra, whose albums The Inner Mounting Flame (1971) and Birds of Fire (1973) set new standards for virtuosity in fusion, with Cobham driving the band's odd meters and high-volume intensity.

Solo career

Cobham's solo debut, Spectrum (1973), recorded with guitarist Tommy Bolin and keyboardist Jan Hammer, became a fusion classic; its track "Stratus" has been widely sampled. He has since released dozens of albums, led bands including Spectrum revivals and international touring ensembles, worked extensively as a clinician and educator, and collaborated with artists from George Duke to the Grateful Dead's Phil Lesh. He was inducted into the Modern Drummer Hall of Fame. Known for playing matched grip with an open-handed approach on a large kit — unusual at a time when many drummers led with one hand — Cobham reshaped expectations of the drummer's role.
