New Keynesian economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

New Keynesian economics is a school of macroeconomics that strives to provide microeconomic foundations for Keynesian economics. It developed partly as a response to criticisms of Keynesian macroeconomics by the followers of new classical macroeconomics, particularly the Lucas critique. New Keynesians retain the assumption of rational expectations — borrowed from the new classical school — but relax the assumption that markets clear continuously, instead building models with imperfect competition and sticky (slowly adjusting) prices and wages.

Key ideas

Nominal rigidities

Because firms face menu costs and staggered price-setting (formalised in the Calvo model), prices do not adjust instantly to shocks. As a result, changes in the money supply and in aggregate demand have real effects on output and employment in the short run, restoring a role for stabilisation policy.

The New Keynesian Phillips curve

Inflation depends on expected future inflation and on real marginal cost (or the output gap). This forward-looking Phillips curve is a core building block of modern monetary models, alongside an IS curve derived from households' intertemporal optimisation and a policy rule for the central bank, such as the Taylor rule.

DSGE models

New Keynesian dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models became the standard workhorse at central banks for policy analysis and forecasting, combining rational expectations, optimising agents and nominal rigidities.

History and contributors

Important contributors include Stanley Fischer, John B. Taylor, Guillermo Calvo, N. Gregory Mankiw, David Romer, Olivier Blanchard, Jordi Galí, Michael Woodford and Lawrence Christiano. Woodford's Interest and Prices (2003) provided a comprehensive statement of the framework. The approach was the dominant paradigm in central banking before and during the 2008 financial crisis; the crisis prompted criticism that the standard models neglected financial frictions, leading to extensions incorporating banks and credit markets.

Policy implications

Because prices are sticky, monetary policy can stabilise output as well as inflation in the short run, and credible commitments about future policy — forward guidance — become a powerful tool, especially at the zero lower bound on interest rates.
