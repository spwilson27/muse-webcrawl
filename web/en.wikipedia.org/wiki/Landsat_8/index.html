Landsat 8

Landsat 8 is an American Earth observation satellite launched on February 11, 2013. It is the eighth satellite in the Landsat program and the seventh to reach orbit successfully (Landsat 6 was lost at launch). Originally called the Landsat Data Continuity Mission (LDCM), it is a collaboration between NASA, which built and launched the spacecraft, and the United States Geological Survey (USGS), which operates it and distributes its data free of charge. Together with Landsat 9 (launched 2021), it provides moderate-resolution imagery of the Earth's entire land surface every eight days.

Instruments

The Operational Land Imager (OLI), built by Ball Aerospace, collects data in nine spectral bands at 30 m resolution (15 m for the panchromatic band). Unlike the whisk-broom scanners of earlier Landsats, OLI is a push-broom sensor with a long focal-plane array, improving signal-to-noise and radiometric precision (12-bit data). OLI added a deep-blue "coastal/aerosol" band and a shortwave-infrared "cirrus" band for cloud detection. The Thermal Infrared Sensor (TIRS), built by NASA Goddard, images in two thermal bands at 100 m; a stray-light problem identified after launch is corrected in ground processing. Landsat 8 collects roughly 700 scenes per day, far more than Landsat 7.

Applications

Landsat 8 data are used for monitoring agriculture, forests and deforestation, water resources, urban growth, glaciers and ice sheets, wildfire damage, and natural disasters — including operational products such as global forest-change maps and drought monitoring. The free-and-open data policy of the Landsat archive (since 2008) is credited with generating economic benefits estimated in the billions of dollars per year and underpinning services such as Google Earth Engine analyses.
