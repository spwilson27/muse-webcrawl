Cinema of Russia

The Odessa Steps sequence from Battleship Potemkin (1925).

The cinema of Russia began in the Russian Empire, developed under the Soviet Union — when Russian film belonged to the wider cinema of the Soviet Union — and continues in the Russian Federation today. Russian and Soviet filmmakers pioneered film theory, in particular the montage editing of Lev Kuleshov, Sergei Eisenstein, and Dziga Vertov, whose work profoundly influenced world cinema.

Russian Empire

Films were first exhibited in Russia in 1896, and the first Russian dramatic film, Stenka Razin, appeared in 1908. By the First World War, directors such as Yakov Protazanov and Yevgeni Bauer had built a flourishing industry making melodramas and literary adaptations; production continued, amid shortages, through the war and revolution until the industry was nationalised in 1919.

Soviet cinema

In the 1920s, Soviet filmmakers, supported by state funding but constrained by film-stock scarcity, developed the theory and practice of montage. Eisenstein's Strike (1925) and Battleship Potemkin (1925), Vsevolod Pudovkin's Mother (1926), and Vertov's documentary Man with a Movie Camera (1929) are landmarks of world cinema. From the 1930s, socialist realism became the official style, though directors from Grigori Aleksandrov to, later, Andrei Tarkovsky produced celebrated work; Tarkovsky's Andrei Rublev (1966) and Solaris (1972) are among the most acclaimed films ever made. Sergei Bondarchuk's War and Peace (1965–67) won the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film, as did Vladimir Menshov's Moscow Does Not Believe in Tears (1980).

Post-Soviet cinema

After 1991, state funding collapsed and output plummeted, but a new generation emerged. Aleksei Balabanov's Brother (1997) became a cult hit; Andrey Zvyagintsev's The Return (2003) won the Golden Lion at Venice, and his Leviathan (2014) and Loveless (2017) received international acclaim and Oscar nominations. Commercial successes such as the Night Watch films (2004–2006) helped revive the domestic market, supported increasingly by state funding conditioned on patriotic themes.
