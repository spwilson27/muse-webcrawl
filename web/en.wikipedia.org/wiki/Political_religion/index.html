Political religion

Political religion is a concept in political science and sociology describing a system of belief, ritual, and symbolism that sacralises political power, giving a political ideology, regime, movement, or leader a religious character. The term is most often applied to totalitarian regimes such as Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, and the Soviet Union under Stalin, whose cults of personality, mass rituals, and doctrines of infallibility are said to have functioned like religions.

Mass rallies and leader cults are characteristic features attributed to political religions.

Definition

Scholars such as Eric Voegelin, who coined the German term politische Religionen in 1938, and Emilio Gentile, who developed the concept of the "sacralisation of politics," argue that certain political movements adopt the forms of religion: sacred texts, prophets and martyrs, liturgical calendars, processions, hymns, and an eschatology promising collective salvation. Unlike a civil religion, which coexists with traditional faiths and underpins a democratic order, a political religion is typically monopolistic and intolerant of rival loyalties.

Characteristics

Commonly cited features include a charismatic leader regarded as infallible or semi-divine; a founding myth and sacred history; martyrs celebrated in ritual; monumental architecture and mass spectacle; a doctrine dividing the world into the faithful and enemies; and the promise of a transformed future — a "new man" and a redeemed society.

Examples

The personality cults of Joseph Stalin, Adolf Hitler, Benito Mussolini, Mao Zedong, and North Korea's Kim dynasty are the most frequently analysed cases. The Kim cult, with its sacred sites, hagiographies, and doctrine of the leader's eternal presidency, is often described as the closest modern approximation of a state religion centred on a ruling family.

Criticism

Critics caution that the label can be stretched too far: not every fervent ideology is a religion, and the concept may obscure the material and coercive bases of totalitarian power. Others note that the boundary between political religion, civil religion, and ordinary nationalism is contested.
