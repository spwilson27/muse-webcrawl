Baja California Sur (Spanish pronunciation: [ˈbaxa kaliˈfoɾnja ˈsuɾ], "South Lower California") is the second-smallest Mexican state by population and the 31st admitted state of the 32 states which make up the Federal Entities of Mexico. It occupies the southern half of the Baja California Peninsula, south of the 28th parallel, and was a federal territory until it was granted statehood on October 8, 1974. Its capital and largest city is La Paz, and its best-known resort area is Los Cabos, at the peninsula's southern tip.

Geography

The state covers 73,909 square kilometres of the southern Baja California Peninsula, bordered by the Pacific Ocean to the west, the Gulf of California (Sea of Cortés) to the east, and the state of Baja California to the north. A mountainous spine — including the Sierra de la Giganta and the volcanic Sierra de la Laguna, a UNESCO biosphere reserve — runs down the peninsula. The climate is predominantly arid desert, with rainfall concentrated in late-summer tropical storms. The state includes numerous islands in both the Pacific and the Gulf, among them Isla Espíritu Santo, and it administers five municipalities: Comondú, La Paz, Loreto, Los Cabos and Mulegé.

The waters around the peninsula are exceptionally rich in marine life. Jacques Cousteau called the Gulf of California "the world's aquarium": its bays, including Bahía Magdalena and the protected waters of Loreto Bay National Park, host whales, sea lions, whale sharks and hundreds of fish species. Gray whales migrate to the lagoons of Ojo de Liebre and San Ignacio each winter to calve, and the Vizcaíno Desert and its biosphere reserve protect pronghorn, bighorn sheep and ancient cave paintings in the Sierra de San Francisco, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

The peninsula was inhabited for thousands of years by hunter-gatherer peoples including the Cochimí, Guaycura and Pericú, who left elaborate rock paintings in the central sierra. Spanish explorers reached the area in the 1530s; Hernán Cortés's expedition landed near present-day La Paz in 1535, and for two centuries "California" was believed to be an island. Jesuit missionaries under Juan María de Salvatierra founded Mission Loreto in 1697, the first permanent Spanish settlement in the Californias, followed by a chain of missions. After Mexican independence the region became the southern district of Baja California territory, administered separately after 1931, and achieved statehood in 1974 following a campaign by local residents.

Economy

Tourism dominates the modern economy, centred on Los Cabos (San José del Cabo and Cabo San Lucas) and La Paz, drawing visitors with sport fishing, whale watching, golf, beaches and the famous El Arco rock formation. Salt production at Guerrero Negro, one of the largest saltworks in the world, fishing and aquaculture, and agriculture in the Santo Domingo valley are also significant. The Transpeninsular Highway, completed in 1973, links the state to the rest of the peninsula.
