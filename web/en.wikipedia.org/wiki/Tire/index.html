Tire

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tire (North American English) or tyre (British English) is a ring-shaped component that surrounds a wheel's rim to transfer a vehicle's load from the axle through the wheel to the ground, and to provide traction on the surface over which the vehicle travels. Most modern tires are pneumatic — filled with compressed air — with a structure of rubber, fabric and steel cord.

Construction

A radial tire consists of a tread band that contacts the road, sidewalls, bead bundles that seal against the rim, and internal plies of fabric cord, usually reinforced with steel belts beneath the tread. The rubber compound combines natural and synthetic rubbers with carbon black, silica and other fillers, balancing grip, rolling resistance and wear. Sidewall markings encode the size, load index, speed rating, and week and year of manufacture.

History

Robert William Thomson patented the pneumatic tire in 1845, but it was John Boyd Dunlop's 1888 reinvention for bicycles, followed by Michelin's detachable car tires, that made the design practical. Charles Goodyear's vulcanisation process (1839/1844) made durable rubber possible. The radial-ply design, commercialised by Michelin after the Second World War, became the world standard for passenger cars.

Use and maintenance

Correct inflation pressure is essential for safety, even wear and fuel economy; modern vehicles use tire-pressure monitoring systems. Tread depth is legally regulated in most countries, and worn tires greatly increase stopping distances on wet roads. Tire wear particles are also a significant source of microplastic pollution, and recycling or retreading end-of-life tires is an active industry concern.
