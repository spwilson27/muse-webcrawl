Fountains in Rome

Fountains in Rome number over 2,000, more than any other city in the world. They range from the monumental baroque showpieces of the seventeenth century to the small drinking fountains known as nasoni that still supply the city with fresh water. Rome's fountains are intimately connected with its ancient aqueducts and with the patronage of popes, who restored the water supply after the fall of the Roman Empire and commissioned fountains as symbols of civic and papal glory.

The Trevi Fountain (1762), the most famous fountain in Rome

History

In antiquity, Rome was supplied by eleven major aqueducts, whose waters fed hundreds of public fountains, baths and private houses. When the aqueducts were cut during the Gothic Wars of the sixth century, Rome's fountains fell dry and the city shrank dramatically. Beginning in the Renaissance, successive popes restored the ancient aqueducts — among them the Acqua Vergine, which feeds the Trevi Fountain, and the Acqua Felice, completed under Pope Sixtus V in 1587 — and built new fountains at their termini.

The Baroque era produced Rome's most celebrated fountains. Gian Lorenzo Bernini designed the Fountain of the Four Rivers (1651) in Piazza Navona, the Triton Fountain in Piazza Barberini, and the Fountain of the Bees. Nicola Salvi's Trevi Fountain, completed in 1762, became the archetype of the Roman monumental fountain.

Notable fountains

  • Trevi Fountain — the largest Baroque fountain in the city, marking the terminus of the Acqua Vergine.
  • Fountain of the Four Rivers — Bernini's masterpiece in Piazza Navona, representing the Nile, Danube, Ganges and Río de la Plata.
  • Fontana dell'Acqua Paola — a monumental fountain on the Janiculum, modelled on a Roman triumphal arch.
  • Turtle Fountain (Fontana delle Tartarughe) — a late Renaissance fountain in Piazza Mattei.
  • Fountain of Neptune and Fountain of the Moor — the two flanking fountains of Piazza Navona.

Nasoni

The nasoni ("big noses") are small cast-iron drinking fountains with a curved metal spout, introduced in the 1870s. About 2,500 remain in service across the city, providing free drinking water continuously.
