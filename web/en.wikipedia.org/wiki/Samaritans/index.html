The Samaritans (Samaritan Hebrew: ࠔࠠࠌࠝࠓࠩࠉࠌ, Shamerim, "Keepers of the Torah") are an ethno-religious group originating from the ancient Israelites of the Near East. They regard themselves as the true descendants of the northern tribes of Israel who remained in the land, and their religion, Samaritanism, centres on the Samaritan Pentateuch and on Mount Gerizim near modern Nablus (ancient Shechem), which they hold — rather than Jerusalem — to be the chosen place of God.

History

Samaritan tradition traces the schism with the Jerusalem priesthood to the time of Eli, while Jewish sources in the Hebrew Bible and later writings describe the Samaritans as descendants of foreign settlers imported by Assyria after the fall of the northern kingdom of Israel in 722 BCE. Relations between Samaritans and Jews were often hostile in the Second Temple period; the Samaritans built their own temple on Mount Gerizim, which was destroyed by the Hasmonean king John Hyrcanus around 110 BCE. Under Roman and Byzantine rule the Samaritans were at times numerous but suffered crushing reprisals after a series of revolts in the fifth and sixth centuries CE, and their numbers declined further under Islamic rule through conversion and persecution.

Modern community

By the early twentieth century the community had fallen to barely more than a hundred people. It has since recovered modestly, numbering roughly 800–900 members today, split between Kiryat Luza on Mount Gerizim and the city of Holon in Israel. Samaritans hold both Israeli and Palestinian documentation depending on residence, serve in the Israel Defense Forces (with exemptions for those on Mount Gerizim), and have relaxed earlier bans on marriage outside the community to counter the effects of a narrow gene pool, which has left them with a high rate of genetic disorders.

Beliefs and practice

Samaritans accept only the first five books of the Bible, in their Samaritan version, and reject the later Jewish scriptures and oral law. They observe the Sabbath strictly, celebrate the biblical festivals, and uniquely continue the Passover sacrifice — the communal slaughter and roasting of lambs on Mount Gerizim each spring. Their high priest, resident on the mountain, leads the liturgy, which is sung in Samaritan Hebrew and Aramaic using the archaic Samaritan script.
