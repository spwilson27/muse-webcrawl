Mobbing

Mobbing, as a term in the social sciences, means bullying of an individual by a group, in any context, such as a family, peer group, school, workplace, neighbourhood, community or online. In the workplace, mobbing has been defined — notably by German-Swedish psychologist Heinz Leymann, who popularised the term in the 1980s — as hostile and unethical communication directed systematically by one or more individuals, mainly toward one individual, who is pushed into a helpless and defenceless position for a prolonged period.

Characteristics

Researchers distinguish mobbing from ordinary conflict and from one-on-one bullying by its collective, systematic character and duration. Leymann's typology grouped mobbing behaviours into five categories: attacks on the victim's ability to communicate; attacks on social relations; attacks on reputation; attacks on the quality of the occupational situation; and direct attacks on health. Studies have associated mobbing with severe stress, depression, post-traumatic symptoms and elevated suicide risk, and with substantial organisational costs through absence and turnover.

Legal and cultural aspects

Several countries — including Sweden (1993), France and Belgium — have enacted laws against psychological harassment ("harcèlement moral") at work. In English usage, "mobbing" for workplace behaviour is more common in European research than in North America, where "workplace bullying" predominates; school-based group bullying is widely studied under that broader term. Konrad Lorenz originally used "mobbing" in ethology for the behaviour of prey animals that group together to harass a predator, as small birds do to an owl — the sense still used in ornithology.
