Perfect (grammar)

Perfect
CategoryGrammatical aspect / tense
MeaningPast event with present relevance

The perfect is a grammatical construction used to describe an event that occurred before a reference time and has some present relevance. The term is used in several related senses in the grammar of different languages. In traditional English grammar, constructions such as "I have eaten" and "they had arrived" are called the present perfect and past perfect (pluperfect). The word "perfect" comes from Latin perfectum, "completed" — it should not be confused with the perfective aspect, which views an event as a completed whole but does not require present relevance.

Meaning

Analyses of the perfect commonly identify several uses:

  • Resultative — a past action whose result still holds: "I have lost my keys" (so I can't get in now).
  • Experiential — an event that happened at least once in a period up to the present: "She has visited Peru."
  • Recent past — "hot news": "The president has resigned."
  • Continuative — a situation that began in the past and continues: "We have lived here for years."

In English

The English perfect is formed with the auxiliary verb have (in the appropriate tense) followed by the past participle of the main verb. Combined with tense and the progressive aspect it yields a family of forms:

FormExample
Present perfectI have finished.
Past perfect (pluperfect)I had finished (before he arrived).
Future perfectI will have finished by six.
Perfect progressiveI have been working.

Unlike many languages, English generally disallows the present perfect with a definite past-time adverbial: "I have seen him yesterday" is ungrammatical in standard English.

In other languages

In Ancient Greek, the perfect tense — formed with reduplication of the verb stem — primarily denoted a state resulting from a completed action. Latin's perfect tense merged the older perfect and aorist. In modern German, Dutch, French and Italian, compound perfects formed with "have" or "be" plus a participle have largely taken over the function of narrating past events, while in Spanish and Portuguese the perfect retains a closer tie to the present.
