Long bone

Long bone
ExamplesFemur, tibia, fibula, humerus, radius, ulna, metacarpals, metatarsals, phalanges
SystemSkeletal system

The long bones are one of the five types of bones in the human body, characterised by being longer than they are wide. They consist of a long shaft (the diaphysis) with two expanded ends (the epiphyses). Long bones are found in the limbs and are essential for movement, support, and blood cell production. Despite their name, some long bones — such as the phalanges of the fingers — are quite small; it is their shape, not their absolute size, that defines the category.

Structure

A typical long bone has:

  • Diaphysis — the hollow tubular shaft, composed of dense compact bone surrounding a medullary cavity filled with yellow bone marrow in adults.
  • Epiphyses — the ends of the bone, filled with spongy (cancellous) bone containing red bone marrow, and covered with articular cartilage where they form joints.
  • Metaphysis — the region between shaft and end; in growing children it contains the epiphyseal (growth) plate of hyaline cartilage responsible for lengthening the bone.
  • Periosteum — a fibrous membrane covering the outer surface, important for growth, repair, and muscle attachment.

Development

Long bones develop by endochondral ossification: a cartilage model of the bone is gradually replaced by bone tissue, beginning at a primary ossification centre in the shaft before birth and later at secondary centres in the epiphyses. Growth in length continues at the epiphyseal plates until they fuse in late adolescence or early adulthood.

Function

Long bones act as levers moved by muscles, bear the weight of the body, and their marrow produces red and white blood cells and platelets. The femur is the longest and strongest long bone in the body.
