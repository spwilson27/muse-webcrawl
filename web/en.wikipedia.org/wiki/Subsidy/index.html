Subsidy

A subsidy or government incentive is a type of government expenditure targeted at individuals and households, as well as businesses or industries, that facilitates the production, purchase, or consumption of a good or service, or that supports specified behavior. Subsidies may take the form of direct cash payments, grants, tax breaks, low-interest loans, or the provision of goods and services below market cost.

Purpose and rationale

Governments use subsidies to encourage activities thought to produce positive externalities — such as research and development, renewable energy, public transport, and vaccination — to support infant industries, to keep strategic sectors viable, to lower consumer prices for essentials like food and fuel, and to pursue regional or distributional goals. Export subsidies are restricted under World Trade Organization rules because they can distort international trade.

Economic analysis

Economists' assessment of subsidies is mixed. A well-designed subsidy can correct a market failure by aligning private incentives with social benefits. However, subsidies can also create deadweight losses, encourage rent-seeking, be captured by producer interests, and persist long after their rationale has vanished. Fossil-fuel subsidies, for instance, are widely criticized for worsening emissions and disproportionately benefiting wealthier consumers who use more fuel. The cost of subsidies may be explicit in budgets, or "implicit" when goods are supplied below cost or external costs go unpriced.

Types

  • Production subsidies paid per unit of output, common in agriculture.
  • Consumer subsidies that lower purchase prices, e.g. food or housing subsidies.
  • Employment subsidies that reduce the cost of hiring.
  • Tax expenditures: reliefs and credits functioning as indirect subsidies.
  • Cross-subsidies, where one group of customers pays more so another pays less, common in utilities and transport.
