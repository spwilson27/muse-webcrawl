Chvostek sign

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Chvostek sign is a clinical sign of neuromuscular excitability associated with hypocalcaemia. It consists of twitching of the facial muscles in response to tapping over the facial nerve just in front of the ear. It is named after the Austrian physician František Chvostek (1835–1884), who described it in 1876.

Mechanism

Low levels of ionised calcium lower the threshold for nerve excitation, making peripheral nerves abnormally irritable. Mechanical stimulation of the facial nerve therefore triggers contraction of the ipsilateral facial muscles, ranging from a slight twitch of the upper lip to spasm of the whole side of the face.

Clinical use and limitations

The sign is traditionally sought, together with the Trousseau sign, in suspected hypocalcaemia and latent tetany, for example after thyroid or parathyroid surgery. However, it is neither fully sensitive nor specific: it may be present in a significant minority of healthy people (often quoted around 10–25 per cent) and absent in some patients with genuine hypocalcaemia, so it supplements rather than replaces serum calcium measurement.
