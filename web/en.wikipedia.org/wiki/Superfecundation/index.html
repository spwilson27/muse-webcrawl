Superfecundation

Superfecundation is the fertilisation of two or more ova from the same ovulatory cycle by sperm from separate acts of intercourse, which can lead to the birth of twins whose biological fathers may be different. When the twins have different fathers, the phenomenon is known as heteropaternal superfecundation.

Mechanism

During a single menstrual cycle in which two ova are released (hyperovulation), each ovum remains capable of fertilisation for roughly 12 to 48 hours, while sperm can survive in the female reproductive tract for up to about five days. If intercourse with two different males occurs within this fertile window, each ovum may be fertilised by sperm from a different partner. The resulting twins are dizygotic (fraternal), and DNA testing can demonstrate that they are half-siblings as well as twins.

Incidence

Superfecundation is well documented in many mammals, including cats and dogs, whose litters commonly have multiple sires. In humans it is considered rare, although estimates vary widely. One frequently cited study of paternity disputes involving twins suggested that heteropaternal superfecundation occurred in a small percentage of such disputed cases; the true rate in the general population is unknown because most cases are never genetically tested.

Distinction from superfetation

Superfecundation should not be confused with superfetation, in which a second conception occurs after a pregnancy is already established, in a later ovulatory cycle. Superfetation in humans is exceptionally rare and medically controversial, whereas superfecundation involves a single cycle and is an accepted biological phenomenon.

Documented cases

A number of cases have been confirmed through court-ordered paternity testing and reported in the medical literature and news media, typically arising during child-support or custody proceedings in which DNA results showed twins with different fathers.
