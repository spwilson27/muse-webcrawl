The ARM architecture family is a family of reduced instruction set computer (RISC) instruction set architectures for computer processors, developed by Arm Ltd. and licensed to hundreds of companies. ARM processors ship in the tens of billions annually and power the overwhelming majority of smartphones, as well as tablets, embedded systems, laptops (such as Apple's M-series Macs) and an increasing share of servers and supercomputers.

History

The original ARM (Acorn RISC Machine) was designed at Acorn Computers in Cambridge, England, by Sophie Wilson and Steve Furber; the ARM1 first worked in April 1985. ARM2 powered the Acorn Archimedes. In 1990 Acorn spun the design team into Advanced RISC Machines Ltd. (later Arm), a joint venture with Apple — which used the ARM610 in the Newton — and VLSI Technology. The ARM7TDMI core, licensed broadly and used in the Nokia 6110 and Game Boy Advance, established ARM's licensing model. Thumb (16-bit) instructions, the Cortex families, and the 64-bit ARMv8 architecture (2011) followed; ARMv9 (2021) added confidential computing and the SVE2 vector extensions.

Design

ARM is a load–store architecture with a large register file and (in 32-bit A32 code) conditional execution of most instructions. Profiles divide the architecture: A-profile (application processors), R-profile (real-time) and M-profile (microcontrollers). Arm Ltd. sells both licensable core designs (Cortex, Neoverse) and architecture licences that let firms such as Apple and Qualcomm design their own compatible cores (Apple silicon; Snapdragon). The original company motto-grabbing selling point — very low power consumption — underlies ARM's dominance in battery-powered devices.

Market position

Cumulative shipments of ARM-based chips passed 250 billion in the early 2020s. Arm was acquired by SoftBank in 2016; a proposed sale to Nvidia collapsed in 2022, and Arm returned to public markets with a Nasdaq IPO in September 2023, with SoftBank retaining majority ownership.
