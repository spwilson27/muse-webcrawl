Relational aesthetics

Relational aesthetics (French: esthétique relationnelle) is a term coined by the French curator and critic Nicolas Bourriaud in the 1990s, and set out in his book Relational Aesthetics (1998; English translation 2002), to describe art whose medium is the social encounter itself. A relational artwork creates a social environment in which people share an activity, meal or conversation; the artist is a catalyst rather than a maker of autonomous objects.

Concept

Bourriaud argued that 1990s artists such as Rirkrit Tiravanija — who cooked and served Thai curry to gallery visitors in works such as pad thai (1990) and Untitled (Free) (1992) — were producing "micro-utopias": temporary social interstices within which human relations could be experienced and modelled outside the logic of consumption. The artwork's form is the encounter; documentation, leftovers and structures are secondary traces.

Criticism

The concept was influential but sharply criticised. In her 2004 October essay "Antagonism and Relational Aesthetics", Claire Bishop argued that Bourriaud's model privileges harmonious conviviality, suppresses the antagonism that theorists such as Chantal Mouffe regard as essential to democracy, and ignores the quality and politics of the relations produced. Others questioned whether gallery sociability could be more than a leisure experience for an art-world in-group. Bourriaud later extended his thinking in Postproduction (2002) and the "altermodern" thesis.

Legacy

Relational aesthetics helped frame the turn toward participation that fed into social practice, participatory art and much subsequent curating, even as many artists and critics distance themselves from the term.
