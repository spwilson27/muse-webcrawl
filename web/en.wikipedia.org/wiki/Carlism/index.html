Carlism (Spanish: Carlismo; Basque: Karlismo) is a traditionalist and legitimist political movement in Spain that arose in 1833 in support of the claim of Infante Carlos, Count of Molina, to the Spanish throne, in opposition to the reign of his niece Isabella II. Over the following century and a half, Carlism became one of the most durable counter-revolutionary movements in Europe, fighting three civil wars and shaping Spanish politics well into the twentieth century.

Origins

The movement originated in a succession dispute. King Ferdinand VII abolished the Salic law restricting succession to males through the Pragmatic Sanction of 1830, allowing his infant daughter Isabella to inherit the throne. On Ferdinand's death in 1833, his brother Carlos refused to recognize Isabella and claimed the crown for himself. His supporters—drawn from the clergy, the rural peasantry, defenders of regional privileges (the fueros), and opponents of liberalism—rallied behind the slogan "Dios, Patria, Rey" (God, Fatherland, King).

The Carlist Wars

The Carlists fought three major wars against the liberal governments in Madrid:

  • First Carlist War (1833–1840) — fought mainly in the Basque Country, Navarre, and Catalonia under generals such as Tomás de Zumalacárregui; ended with the Convention of Vergara.
  • Second Carlist War (1846–1849) — a smaller conflict centered on Catalonia, also called the War of the Matiners.
  • Third Carlist War (1872–1876) — waged in the name of Carlos VII during the chaos of the First Spanish Republic and the Restoration.

Twentieth century

Carlism survived into the 20th century as a mass movement, strongest in Navarre and the Basque provinces. Its militia, the requetés—recognizable by their red berets—were among the first and most effective Nationalist forces in the Spanish Civil War of 1936–1939. In 1937 Franco forcibly merged the Carlist Traditionalist Communion with the Falange into a single state party, a union many Carlists resented. Thereafter the movement fragmented over the succession question and over its relationship to the Franco regime, with one faction under Prince Xavier of Bourbon-Parma expelled from Spain and another eventually evolving toward a left-wing, self-management socialism in the 1970s. Carlism persists today as a small political current in Spain.
