The occupation of Latvia refers to the military occupation and annexation of the Republic of Latvia by the Soviet Union in 1940, its occupation by Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1944–45, and its renewed occupation and incorporation into the USSR from 1944–45 until the restoration of independence in 1991. The United States and many other Western states never recognised the annexation as lawful, maintaining that Latvia's statehood continued de jure throughout the period.

Background

Latvia declared independence in 1918 and was recognised internationally after its war of independence. Under the secret protocols of the 1939 Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, Latvia was assigned to the Soviet sphere of influence; a forced mutual-assistance treaty admitted Red Army bases in autumn 1939.

First Soviet occupation, 1940–1941

Following an ultimatum in June 1940, Soviet forces occupied Latvia; a staged election produced a "people's parliament" that proclaimed a Latvian Soviet Socialist Republic and requested incorporation into the USSR, effected on 5 August 1940. The year of Soviet rule — remembered as the "Year of Terror" — brought nationalisation, purges, and the June 1941 deportation of more than 15,000 people to Siberia days before the German invasion.

German occupation, 1941–1945

Germany invaded in June 1941 and incorporated Latvia into the Reichskommissariat Ostland. Nazi rule brought the Holocaust in Latvia: most of Latvia's roughly 70,000 Jews were murdered, including in the Rumbula massacre of November–December 1941, and Roma and others were also persecuted. Latvians were conscripted into both German and Soviet forces, and the Latvian Legion (Waffen-SS) was formed in 1943.

Second Soviet occupation, 1944–1991

The Red Army retook most of Latvia in 1944 (the Courland Pocket held out until May 1945). Soviet rule resumed with renewed repression: the March 1949 deportation removed over 42,000 people to facilitate collectivisation, and the Forest Brothers partisan resistance continued into the 1950s. Large-scale immigration from other Soviet republics during industrialisation sharply reduced the ethnic Latvian share of the population, and Russification pressures intensified.

Restoration of independence

Perestroika allowed the rise of the Popular Front of Latvia and the "Singing Revolution". On 4 May 1990 the Supreme Council declared the restoration of independence; after the failed August 1991 coup in Moscow, the USSR recognised Latvia's independence on 6 September 1991. The last Russian troops withdrew in 1994.
