Assisted living

An assisted living community balances independence with daily support.

Assisted living is a housing arrangement for older adults or adults with disabilities who need help with activities of daily living — such as bathing, dressing, medication management and meals — but who do not require the intensive medical care provided by a nursing home. Residents typically live in private apartments within a community setting and receive personalised care services, housekeeping, meals and social activities.

Concept and history

The modern assisted living model emerged in the United States in the 1980s and 1990s, influenced by Scandinavian residential care concepts and by consumer demand for alternatives to institutional nursing homes that emphasised privacy, autonomy and a homelike environment. The sector grew rapidly as the population aged; in the U.S., regulation is primarily at state level, and licensing categories and names vary widely.

Services and levels of care

Facilities typically provide three meals a day in a communal dining room, assistance with medication, personal care, laundry, transportation and round-the-clock staff availability. Many communities offer specialised memory care units for residents with Alzheimer's disease and other dementias. Costs in the United States commonly run to several thousand dollars per month and are usually paid privately, as Medicare does not cover room and board in assisted living.

Comparison with other settings

Assisted living sits between independent living communities, which provide housing and amenities with little care, and skilled nursing facilities, which provide 24-hour medical care. Continuing care retirement communities combine all of these levels on one campus, allowing residents to move between them as their needs change.
