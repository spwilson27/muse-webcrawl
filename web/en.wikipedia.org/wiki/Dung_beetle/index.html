Dung beetle
OrderColeoptera
Main groupsScarabaeinae, Geotrupidae, some Aphodiinae
DietAnimal dung (coprophagy)

Dung beetles are beetles that feed on dung, wholly or partly. Most belong to the scarab subfamily Scarabaeinae, though the habit also occurs in the earth-boring dung beetles (Geotrupidae) and some Aphodiinae. Thousands of species are known, found on every continent except Antarctica, and they play a major ecological role in burying and recycling animal waste.

Ecology and behaviour

Dung beetles are often classified by how they use dung. Rollers shape a portion of dung into a ball, roll it away—travelling in a straight line, guided in part by the light of the Milky Way—and bury it as food or an egg chamber. Tunnellers dig burrows directly beneath a dung pat and stock them underground, while dwellers live inside the pat itself. By burying dung, the beetles improve soil fertility and structure, spread seeds, reduce breeding sites for parasitic flies and help control intestinal parasites of livestock.

Human significance

The sacred scarab of ancient Egypt (Scarabaeus sacer) was a powerful religious symbol associated with the god Khepri and the rolling of the sun across the sky. In modern agriculture, dung beetles have been deliberately introduced—for example, Australia's CSIRO imported dozens of species from the 1960s to deal with cattle dung that native beetles could not process, improving pastures and reducing bush fly numbers.
