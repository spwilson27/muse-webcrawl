Medical device

A medical device is any instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, appliance, implant, reagent for in vitro use, software, or similar article intended by the manufacturer to be used for the diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, treatment, or alleviation of disease or injury, or for the investigation or modification of anatomy or a physiological process, where the primary intended action is not achieved by pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic means.

Examples

Medical devices range from simple items such as tongue depressors, bandages, and thermometers, through diagnostic equipment such as blood glucose meters and MRI scanners, to life-sustaining implants such as pacemakers, artificial heart valves, and hip prostheses. Software used for diagnosis or treatment, including some mobile health apps, is also regulated as a medical device in many jurisdictions.

Classification and regulation

Regulators classify devices by risk. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) uses three classes:

ClassRiskExamplesTypical pathway
ILowBandages, examination glovesGeneral controls; often exempt from premarket notification
IIModerateInfusion pumps, surgical drapes510(k) premarket notification
IIIHighPacemakers, implantable defibrillatorsPremarket approval (PMA)

In the European Union, the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) similarly uses risk classes I, IIa, IIb, and III, with conformity assessment by notified bodies and CE marking. Other major regulators include Health Canada, Japan's PMDA, and China's NMPA.

History and issues

Device regulation developed after high-profile failures, including the Dalkon Shield intrauterine device in the 1970s and the Björk–Shiley heart valve. Ongoing issues include device recalls, cybersecurity of connected devices, the use of real-world evidence, and harmonization of standards across countries.
