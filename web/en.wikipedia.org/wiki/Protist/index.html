Protist

Diversity of protists.

A protist (from Greek prōtistos, "the very first") is any eukaryotic organism that is not an animal, plant, or fungus. Protists do not constitute a natural group or clade, but are a paraphyletic grouping historically used for convenience: they are eukaryotes that lack the specialised features defining the three great multicellular kingdoms.

Diversity

Protists are mostly microscopic and unicellular, though some, such as the giant kelps among the brown algae, grow to tens of metres. They include the ciliates, flagellates, amoebae, sporozoans, diatoms, dinoflagellates, euglenids, slime moulds, and the algae. Their nutrition spans photosynthesis, predation, parasitism and absorption of dissolved nutrients.

Ecology and importance

Marine protists are the base of most oceanic food webs: diatoms alone are estimated to produce roughly a fifth of Earth's oxygen through photosynthesis. Other protists are serious pathogens — Plasmodium causes malaria, Trypanosoma causes sleeping sickness and Chagas disease, and Giardia causes intestinal infection. The oomycete Phytophthora infestans, which caused the Irish potato famine, is a protist rather than a true fungus.

Classification

The term was popularised by Ernst Haeckel in 1866. Modern phylogenetics replaces the old kingdom Protista with supergroups such as SAR (stramenopiles, alveolates, rhizarians), Archaeplastida, Excavata, Amoebozoa and Opisthokonta, reflecting that "protists" are far more diverse genetically than all animals, plants and fungi combined.
