Diabetes mellitus type 1

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Type 1 diabetes is a chronic autoimmune condition in which the immune system destroys the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas, leaving the body unable to make Insulin. Without insulin treatment it is fatal; with it, people can live full lives but must manage blood glucose daily. It accounts for roughly 5–10% of diabetes and usually starts in children and young adults, though it can appear at any age.

Presentation and management

Loss of insulin causes high blood glucose with symptoms of thirst, frequent urination, weight loss and fatigue, and can progress to diabetic ketoacidosis — a dangerous acid build-up that is often the presenting emergency. Management is lifelong insulin by injections or pump, guided by blood-glucose testing or continuous glucose monitors, with carbohydrate counting and adjustment for exercise and illness. Closed-loop 'artificial pancreas' systems automate much of this.

Causes and outlook

Type 1 diabetes arises from genetic susceptibility (especially HLA genes) plus environmental triggers, possibly including viral infections; it is not caused by diet or lifestyle. Complications of long-standing diabetes include eye, kidney, nerve and cardiovascular disease, reduced by good glucose control. Immunotherapy (teplizumab) can delay onset in high-risk individuals — the first treatment to modify the disease's course.
