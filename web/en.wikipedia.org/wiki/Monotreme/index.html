Monotremes (/ˈmɒnətriːmz/) are mammals of the order Monotremata. They are the only mammals known to lay eggs, rather than giving birth, aside from giving milk to their young. The extant monotreme species consist of the platypus and four species of echidna, all found in Australia and New Guinea.

Characteristics

Monotremes share the defining mammalian traits of hair and milk production, but retain several reptile-like features: they lay leathery eggs, have a single opening (cloaca) for reproduction and excretion — the source of the name "monotreme", meaning "single hole" — and their shoulder girdles include bones absent in other living mammals. They have a lower body temperature than most mammals (about 32 °C in the platypus) and lack nipples; milk is secreted onto patches of skin from which the young lap it up. Adult monotremes lack teeth, and males of the platypus are venomous — one of the few venomous mammals. Monotremes also possess electroreception, sensing prey by electric fields generated by muscular contractions.

Species

  • Platypus (Ornithorhynchus anatinus) — eastern Australia and Tasmania.
  • Short-beaked echidna (Tachyglossus aculeatus) — Australia and New Guinea.
  • Western long-beaked echidna (Zaglossus bruijnii) — New Guinea.
  • Eastern long-beaked echidna (Zaglossus bartoni) — New Guinea.
  • Sir David's long-beaked echidna (Zaglossus attenboroughi) — New Guinea.

Evolution

Monotremes diverged from other mammals early in mammalian evolution; the oldest known fossil monotreme, Teinolophos, dates to about 123 million years ago. Fossil forms once ranged into South America, indicating a Gondwanan distribution.
