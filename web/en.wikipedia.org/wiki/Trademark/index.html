Trademark

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trademark (also trade mark) is a sign capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one enterprise from those of others — words, logos, shapes, colours, sounds and even smells — protected by intellectual-property law. Rights arise from registration (the usual route, through national offices and the international Madrid system administered by WIPO), and in common-law countries also from use in trade. The owner may prevent others from using identical or confusingly similar signs for related goods or services; famous marks additionally enjoy protection against dilution.

The modern statutes (UK Trade Marks Act 1994; US Lanham Act 1946) implement harmonised regimes under the Paris Convention (1883) and TRIPS Agreement (1994). Trademarks, unlike patents and copyright, can be renewed indefinitely in ten-year terms, making marks such as early twentieth-century logos durable property. Their functions are economic (reducing consumer search costs, signalling quality) and their limits doctrinal: generic terms lose protection (“genericide” — aspirin, escalator), descriptive and functional signs are refused, and free-speech and parody defences test the boundary against expression. Trade dress, geographical indications and certification marks occupy the penumbra.
