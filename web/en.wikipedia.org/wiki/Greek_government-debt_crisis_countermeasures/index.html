Greek government-debt crisis countermeasures

The Greek government-debt crisis countermeasures were the series of austerity packages, economic reforms, rescue loans, and debt restructurings adopted in Greece and by its eurozone partners between 2010 and 2018 in response to the Greek government-debt crisis. Following the revelation in late 2009 that Greece's budget deficit had been far larger than reported (15.4% of GDP for 2009), Greek borrowing costs soared and the state lost market access, prompting three international bailout programmes conditioned on fiscal consolidation and structural reform.

Bailout programmes

  • First programme (May 2010): €110 billion in loans from eurozone states and the IMF, tied to public-sector wage and pension cuts, tax rises, and labour-market reforms.
  • Second programme (2012): roughly €130 billion in new financing, combined with a restructuring of privately held Greek debt—the largest sovereign debt restructuring in history—in which bondholders accepted a nominal haircut of 53.5% (about 75% in present-value terms).
  • Third programme (August 2015): up to €86 billion from the European Stability Mechanism, agreed after Greece narrowly avoided leaving the euro, with further pension, tax, privatization, and banking reforms.

Measures and effects

Successive governments raised VAT and property taxes, cut public wages and pensions repeatedly, raised retirement ages, liberalized closed professions, created a privatization fund (HRADF/TAIPED), and recapitalized the banks. The economy contracted by roughly a quarter from its pre-crisis peak, unemployment peaked at about 27.5% in 2013 (youth unemployment near 60%), and public debt remained above 170% of GDP, mitigated by long maturities and low official interest rates. Greece exited the third programme in August 2018 and returned to regular market borrowing, while remaining under enhanced post-programme surveillance for a time.
