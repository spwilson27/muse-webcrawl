Melodrama

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A melodrama is a dramatic work in which the plot, typically sensational and centred on intense emotional appeals, is subordinated — although the term is now often used more loosely — to exaggerated characterisation and dialogue meant to appeal to the emotions. Characters are often flat or stock types: the virtuous heroine, the villain, the hero. The term derives from the Greek melos (song) and French drame, reflecting the original form's use of music to accompany dramatic action.

Origins

Melodrama arose in late 18th-century France, in works such as Jean-Jacques Rousseau's Pygmalion (1770), which combined spoken text with orchestral accompaniment. After the French Revolution, melodrama flourished on Parisian boulevard stages — playwrights such as Guilbert de Pixérécourt perfected a formula of persecuted innocence, spectacular stage effects and moral resolution — partly because licensing restrictions on dialogue in popular theatres encouraged spectacle and gesture.

Theatrical melodrama

In 19th-century Britain and America, melodrama became the dominant popular stage genre: adaptations of novels such as Uncle Tom's Cabin and East Lynne, temperance plays like The Drunkard, and sensation dramas with on-stage rescues, fires and railway perils. Stock villains in top hats tying heroines to railway tracks became an enduring cliché of the form.

Film and television

Melodrama transferred naturally to silent film and then to sound cinema: the family melodramas of directors such as Douglas Sirk (All That Heaven Allows, Imitation of Life) are now critically celebrated for their stylised emotion and social critique beneath a glossy surface. The mode persists in soap operas, telenovelas and much popular film and television drama worldwide, and "melodramatic" remains a common — usually pejorative — term for exaggerated emotional display.
