Amun

Amun
Egyptian: imn ("the Hidden One")
Major cult centre: Thebes (Karnak)
Symbol: Double-plumed crown, ram, goose, was-sceptre
Consort: Mut (and Amunet)
Offspring: Khonsu
Greek equivalent: Zeus

Amun (also Amon, Ammon) was a major ancient Egyptian deity, a member of the Hermopolitan Ogdoad representing hiddenness, who rose to become king of the gods as Amun-Ra during the New Kingdom, when Thebes became Egypt's capital.

Rise to prominence

Originally a local Theban god, Amun gained national importance in the Middle Kingdom and, after the expulsion of the Hyksos, merged with the sun god Ra as Amun-Ra. His temple complex at Karnak became the largest religious complex in Egypt, and his priesthood grew immensely wealthy and politically powerful.

Depiction and worship

Amun is usually shown as a man wearing a crown with two tall vertical plumes, holding the ankh and was-sceptre; he could also appear as a ram or ram-headed figure. The annual Opet Festival carried his statue by river from Karnak to Luxor Temple. Oracles of Amun, including at the Siwa Oasis, were consulted across the Mediterranean — Alexander the Great famously visited Siwa in 331 BC and was proclaimed son of Amun.

Later history

The reforming pharaoh Akhenaten suppressed Amun's cult in favour of the Aten in the 14th century BC, but the cult was restored under Tutankhamun. Amun remained central in Nubia (Napata and Meroë) long after his decline in Egypt, and the Greeks identified him with Zeus.
