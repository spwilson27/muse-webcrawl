P. G. Wodehouse

Sir Pelham Grenville Wodehouse (15 October 1881 – 14 February 1975) was an English writer and humorist, one of the most widely read comic novelists of the 20th century. In a career lasting more than seventy years he wrote over ninety books, some forty plays and around two hundred short stories, and collaborated on Broadway and Hollywood musicals. He is best remembered for his recurring fictional worlds: the idle young gentleman Bertie Wooster and his imperturbable valet Jeeves; the denizens of Blandings Castle, ruled over by the amiable, pig-obsessed Lord Emsworth; the school stories and the early Psmith novels; and the golf and Ukridge stories. His prose — elegantly constructed, rich in simile and classical and biblical allusion turned to absurd ends — is often cited as the finest comic writing in English.

Life

Wodehouse was born in Guildford, the son of a Hong Kong civil servant, and was educated at Dulwich College, whose grounds he adored and to which he returned in fiction throughout his life. Unable for financial reasons to follow his brother to Oxford, he worked for two years at the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank in London while writing in his spare time. From 1902 he wrote for The Globe and contributed school stories to boys' magazines; his first novel, The Pothunters, appeared in 1902. He found fame in the United States, where he lived for long periods: in New York he wrote lyrics and books for Broadway musicals with Guy Bolton and Jerome Kern — the "Princess Theatre shows" that helped invent the modern American musical — and later worked as a Hollywood screenwriter, making famous indiscreet remarks to the press about being paid $104,000 for doing little.

In 1914 he married Ethel Newton; their marriage lasted until his death. The first Jeeves story appeared in 1915, and in the 1920s and 1930s Wodehouse reached the height of his popularity on both sides of the Atlantic, publishing a stream of novels including Right Ho, Jeeves (1934) and The Code of the Woosters (1938).

The wartime broadcasts and aftermath

Living in France in 1940, Wodehouse was interned by the Germans after the fall of France and held at Tost in Upper Silesia. Released shortly before his sixtieth birthday, he was persuaded — naively, as he and most later judges concluded — to make five light-hearted broadcasts over German radio to America, describing his internment with self-deprecating humour. In Britain the broadcasts provoked fury: he was denounced in Parliament and in the press as a collaborator, and a broadcast by the journalist William Connor ("Cassandra") attacked him fiercely. MI5 investigated and concluded he had been foolish but not treacherous; no charges were brought. Nevertheless Wodehouse never returned to England, settling permanently in the United States, where he became a citizen in 1955.

Later years and reputation

Wodehouse continued writing into his nineties, producing new Jeeves and Blandings novels that maintained his comic level remarkably; his last, Aunts Aren't Gentlemen, appeared in 1974, and he left a final Blandings novel, Sunset at Blandings, unfinished. He was appointed a Knight Bachelor in the 1975 New Year Honours — widely seen as official British forgiveness — but was too frail to travel, and died in hospital on Long Island on St Valentine's Day 1975, aged 93. His reputation has continued to grow: he is admired by writers from Evelyn Waugh (who called him "the head of my profession") to Douglas Adams and Stephen Fry, and the Jeeves and Wooster stories have been repeatedly adapted for television, most famously with Fry and Hugh Laurie.
