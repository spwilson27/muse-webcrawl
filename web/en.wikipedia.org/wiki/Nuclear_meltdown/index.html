Nuclear meltdown

A nuclear meltdown, also commonly referred to as a partial core melt accident or core melt accident, is a severe nuclear reactor accident that results in core damage from overheating. The term is not officially defined by the International Atomic Energy Agency; it is generally understood to mean the melting of the fuel in a reactor core when the heat generated cannot be removed by the coolant.

Causes and progression

After a reactor shuts down, radioactive decay continues to generate heat — initially several percent of full power. If cooling is lost (a loss-of-coolant accident, or LOCA), fuel cladding can overheat, react with steam to produce hydrogen, rupture, and eventually melt at temperatures above about 2,800 °C. The molten fuel, called corium, can slump to the bottom of the reactor pressure vessel, breach it, and interact with concrete in the containment, generating gases and aerosols.

Major accidents

  • Three Mile Island (1979) — a partial meltdown in Unit 2 in Pennsylvania, United States; roughly half the core melted, but the containment largely held and releases were small.
  • Chernobyl (1986) — a steam explosion and graphite fire at the Soviet RBMK reactor destroyed the core and released large quantities of radioactive material; not a pure meltdown but the worst nuclear accident in history.
  • Fukushima Daiichi (2011) — after the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, Units 1–3 suffered core melts, hydrogen explosions damaged the reactor buildings, and corium breached the pressure vessels.

Prevention and mitigation

Modern designs employ passive safety systems, multiple independent cooling trains, hydrogen recombiners, filtered venting and core catchers intended to retain corium. The hypothetical extreme scenario in which molten fuel melts through the earth was popularised as the "China syndrome".
