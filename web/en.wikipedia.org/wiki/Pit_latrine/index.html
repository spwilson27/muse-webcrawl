Pit latrine

A simple pit latrine in a rural area, showing the slab and shelter structure.

A pit latrine, also known as a pit toilet, is a type of toilet that collects human feces in a hole in the ground. Urine and feces enter the pit through a drop hole in the floor, which might be connected to a toilet seat or squatting pan for user comfort. A pit latrine may not use any water or only use a very limited amount of water for cleaning (in contrast with a flush toilet which uses a significant amount of water). When the pit is full, it must be either emptied or the hole covered and the facility moved to a new location.

Pit latrines are used by an estimated 1.8 billion people around the world. They are the most common and least expensive form of improved sanitation available. However, pit latrines are also sometimes responsible for groundwater pollution, particularly if the pit is located close to a drinking water well or in soil conditions that allow pathogens to travel.

Design considerations

A pit latrine typically consists of three major parts: a hole in the ground, a concrete slab or floor with a small drop hole, and a shelter. The shelter is often known as an outhouse. The pit is typically at least three meters (10 feet) deep and one meter (3.2 feet) across. The World Health Organization recommends they be built a reasonable distance from the house, balancing easy access against the smell. The distance from water wells and surface water should be at least 10 metres (33 ft) to decrease the risk of groundwater pollution.

The slab should provide a smooth, cleanable surface with a hole about 250 mm wide. If feces are deposited on the floor of the latrine rather than through the hole, cleaning becomes difficult and smells increase. A lid for the drop hole can reduce odor and flies.

Ventilated improved pit latrine

The ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrine adds a vent pipe covered with a fly screen. Wind blowing across the top of the pipe creates suction that draws odors up and out of the pit, while the screen prevents flies from entering and breeding in the pit. The interior of the superstructure is kept somewhat dark so that flies inside the pit are attracted to the light at the top of the vent pipe, where they are trapped.

Emptying

When a pit fills to within about half a meter of the top, it must be emptied or abandoned. Manual emptying is hazardous and unpleasant work, often carried out by marginalised workers in developing countries. Mechanical emptying with vacuum trucks is common in urban areas. The sludge removed is known as fecal sludge and requires treatment before safe disposal or reuse.
