Red fox

Red fox
Scientific name: Vulpes vulpes
Family: Canidae
Conservation status: Least Concern (IUCN)
Distribution: Entire Northern Hemisphere; introduced to Australia

The red fox (Vulpes vulpes) is the largest of the true foxes and one of the most widely distributed members of the order Carnivora, occurring across the entire Northern Hemisphere from the Arctic Circle to North Africa, Central America, and Asia. It was introduced into Australia in the 19th century, where it is a damaging invasive species.

Description

Adults typically weigh 4–8 kg, with a body length of 45–90 cm plus a bushy tail ("brush") of 30–55 cm tipped with white. The coat is characteristically orange-red above and white below, with black-backed ears and black "stockings" on the legs; colour phases include silver and cross foxes, valued in the fur trade and the basis of fox farming.

Behaviour and diet

Red foxes are opportunistic omnivores feeding mainly on small rodents, rabbits, birds, invertebrates, fruit, and carrion, and they cache surplus food. Their hearing is acute enough to locate prey under snow, which they catch with a characteristic mousing pounce — a technique whose alignment may exploit the Earth's magnetic field. Foxes live singly or in family groups occupying a territory and rear litters of four or five cubs in an earth (den) in spring. Highly adaptable, they thrive in farmland, suburbs, and city centres, where urban populations scavenge extensively.

Relationship with humans

The fox is a stock figure of cunning in folklore from Aesop and Reynard the Fox to the Japanese kitsune. It has been hunted for fur and sport — fox hunting with hounds became a defining rural pursuit in Britain before its restriction in 2004 — and is a reservoir of rabies in parts of its range, controlled partly by oral vaccination campaigns in Europe.
