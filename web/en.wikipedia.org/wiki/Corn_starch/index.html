Corn starch

Corn starch, maize starch, or cornflour (in the United Kingdom) is the starch derived from corn (maize) grain. The starch is obtained from the endosperm of the kernel. Corn starch is a common food ingredient, often used to thicken sauces or soups, and to make corn syrup and other sugars. It is the most common starch in the world and a versatile industrial material used in adhesives, paper products, and as an anti-sticking agent.

Production

The corn is steeped for 30 to 48 hours in warm water containing a small amount of sulfur dioxide, which softens the kernel and begins fermentation. The germ is separated, and the remaining endosperm is ground and washed over screens and hydrocyclones to separate the starch from the gluten and fibre. The purified starch is dried to a fine white powder.

Uses

In cooking, corn starch gelatinizes when heated in liquid and is used as a thickener for sauces, gravies, puddings and pie fillings; it gives a glossy, translucent finish. Mixed with water it forms a non-Newtonian fluid (sometimes called oobleck) that behaves like a solid under sudden pressure. Industrially, corn starch is used in papermaking, corrugating adhesives, textiles, pharmaceuticals (as a binder and disintegrant), and as a feedstock for bioplastics and ethanol.
