Ancient DNA (aDNA) is DNA isolated from ancient remains — bones, teeth, hair, seeds, shells, sediments, and other specimens of organisms that died long ago, from decades to more than a million years in the past. Unlike modern DNA, ancient DNA is degraded into short fragments and chemically damaged (notably by cytosine deamination), and samples are easily overwhelmed by contaminating modern DNA, so work is conducted in dedicated clean laboratories with strict authentication criteria. The field has transformed archaeology, palaeontology, and human evolutionary studies.

History

The first ancient DNA sequence was reported in 1984 from a museum specimen of the extinct quagga. The polymerase chain reaction enabled a wave of studies in the 1990s, some early spectacular claims (such as DNA from amber-preserved insects and dinosaur bones) proving irreproducible. From the mid-2000s, high-throughput sequencing revolutionised the field: the Neanderthal genome was published in 2010, revealing interbreeding with modern humans, and a finger bone from Denisova Cave identified a previously unknown archaic group, the Denisovans. Svante Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work. Sediment DNA has since yielded genomes from extinct cave bears and, in Greenland permafrost, an ecosystem around two million years old; DNA from Siberian mammoth teeth exceeded one million years.

Applications

Ancient DNA has traced prehistoric migrations (such as the steppe expansions into Europe), reconstructed the domestication of horses, dogs, and crops, identified pathogens in historical epidemics including plague, and resolved the phylogenies of extinct species from moa to woolly mammoths. It also raises ethical questions about sampling museum specimens and the remains of Indigenous ancestors.
