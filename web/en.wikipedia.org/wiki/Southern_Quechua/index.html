Southern Quechua

Southern Quechua (Quechua: Urin Qichwa) is the most widely spoken variety of the Quechuan language family, with several million speakers in southern Peru, Bolivia and north-western Argentina. It descends from the form of Quechua spread as the administrative lingua franca of the Inca Empire and later of Spanish colonial administration.

Varieties

The main regional varieties include Cusco Quechua (Qusqu-Qullaw), Ayacucho Quechua (Chanka) and Bolivian Quechua. They are largely mutually intelligible and share the family's characteristic three-vowel system, in which /i/ and /u/ lower to [ɪ] and [ʊ] near the uvular consonant /q/. Southern Quechua varieties developed aspirated and glottalised stop consonants, a feature attributed to contact with Aymara.

Standardisation

Debate continues over a unified written standard. Cusco's Academia Mayor de la Lengua Quechua advocates a five-vowel alphabet preserving distinctions of the Inca-era language, while linguists and Peru's Ministry of Education support a three-vowel phonemic alphabet. Southern Quechua is a co-official language in Bolivia and has official status regionally in Peru.
