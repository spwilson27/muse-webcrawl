Tunnel rat

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The tunnel rats were American, Australian, New Zealand and South Vietnamese soldiers who performed underground search and destroy missions during the Vietnam War.

Later, similar teams performed similar missions in the War in Afghanistan and the Iraq War. Tunnel rats also appeared prominently in other theatres of war, including the tunnels beneath the battlefields of the First World War and the tunnels of Cu Chi in Vietnam.

Vietnam War

During the Vietnam War, "tunnel rat" became an unofficial specialty for volunteer combat engineers and infantrymen from the United States, Australia and New Zealand who cleared and destroyed enemy tunnel complexes. Their motto was the tongue-in-cheek Latin phrase Non Gratus Anus Rodentum ("not worth a rat's ass").

The Viet Cong had built extensive underground tunnel systems, most famously the tunnels of Cu Chi, from which they could launch attacks and then vanish. When a tunnel entrance was discovered, tunnel rats were sent in to kill any hiding enemy soldiers and to plant explosives to destroy the tunnels.

Equipment and dangers

Tunnel rats were generally of small stature — the tunnels were often barely large enough to crawl through — and were generally armed only with a standard issue .45 calibre M1911 pistol, a bayonet, a flashlight and explosives. They faced numerous dangers inside the tunnels: enemy soldiers, booby traps, snakes, scorpions, bats and other creatures, flooding, and pockets of toxic gas. Revolvers with silencers and other specialised weapons were sometimes issued, as the sound of a pistol fired in a confined tunnel could deafen the firer.

Later conflicts

During the Soviet–Afghan War, Soviet forces encountered mujahideen operating from the ancient karez irrigation tunnels, and developed specialist tunnel-clearing teams of their own. Tunnel warfare reappeared in the Gaza Strip, where smuggling and military tunnels became a major feature of the conflict, and in the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan.
