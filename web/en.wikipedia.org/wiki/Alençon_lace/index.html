Alençon lace (French: point d'Alençon, [pwɛ̃ dalɑ̃sɔ̃]) is a needle lace originating in the town of Alençon in Normandy, France. Known as the "queen of lace", it is worked entirely by hand on a parchment pattern with fine linen thread and is characterised by floral motifs on a hexagonal mesh ground outlined with a raised cordonnet.

History

Lacemaking in Alençon dates to the 16th century, but the industry was transformed in 1665 when Jean-Baptiste Colbert established a royal workshop under Louis XIV to rival Venetian needle lace. Alençon lace became the height of luxury at the French court and remained prestigious through the 18th century. The French Revolution devastated the industry; a 19th-century revival under makers such as the Augustine sisters of Alençon restored its fame, though machine lace and changing fashion eroded demand.

Technique

The craft involves ten stages, from design and pricking the pattern to the final assembly of separately worked sections; a square centimetre of ground mesh can take hours of work. In 2010 the craftsmanship of Alençon needle lace-making was inscribed by UNESCO on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Today the technique is preserved by lacemakers at the Musée des Beaux-Arts et de la Dentelle in Alençon.
