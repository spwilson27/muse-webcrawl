Arthur Cecil Pigou (18 November 1877 – 7 March 1959) was an English economist, a leading figure of the Cambridge school founded by Alfred Marshall. As the successor to Marshall's chair of political economy at Cambridge (1908–1943), he systematised welfare economics and developed the analysis of externalities for which he is best remembered: the Pigouvian tax, a levy on activities that impose negative external costs (such as pollution) equal to the marginal social damage, designed to align private and social cost.

Life and work

Pigou was educated at Harrow and King's College, Cambridge, where he read history before turning to economics under Marshall's influence. Elected a fellow of King's in 1901, he succeeded Marshall as professor in 1908 at the age of thirty, holding the chair until 1943. A shy, aloof bachelor don, he was also a keen mountaineer and, despite his reserve, an influential teacher whose students included many of the next generation of economists.

Major works

His magnum opus, The Economics of Welfare (1920, expanded from Wealth and Welfare, 1912), founded modern welfare economics, distinguishing economic from total welfare and analysing divergences between marginal private and social net product — the analytical root of externality theory. He also wrote The Theory of Unemployment (1933), whose claim that real-wage cuts could cure unemployment made him the principal target of Keynes's General Theory (1936), which cast Pigou as the representative "classical" economist. Pigou responded generously, later conceding much of Keynes's critique. The Pigou effect — the stimulus to consumption from rising real money balances as prices fall — preserves his name in macroeconomic debates as well.

Legacy

Pigouvian taxes underpin modern carbon pricing and congestion charges, and Pigou is regarded as the father of welfare economics. He was elected to the British Academy and, notably, declined official honours.
