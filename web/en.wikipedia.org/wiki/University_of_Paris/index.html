University of Paris

The University of Paris (French: Université de Paris), known metonymically as the Sorbonne, was the leading university in Paris, France, from around 1150 until its dissolution in 1970 (with a successor of the same name re-established in later reorganisations). Emerging from the cathedral schools of Notre-Dame, it became one of the oldest universities in the world and the archetype of the medieval university, famed especially for theology and philosophy.

History

Medieval origins

Masters and scholars gathered around the cathedral school of Notre-Dame and the schools of the Left Bank by the mid-12th century. In 1200 King Philip II Augustus granted the scholars royal protection; Pope Innocent III's legate Robert de Courçon issued statutes in 1215, and in 1231 the bull Parens scientiarum of Gregory IX — sometimes called the university's Magna Carta — confirmed its autonomy. The university was organised into four faculties: Arts (the undergraduate faculty), Theology, Law (canon law), and Medicine. Students were grouped into four "nations" by origin.

The Dominican and Franciscan orders founded colleges in Paris, and theologians such as Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, and Bonaventure made the university the intellectual capital of Latin Christendom. The College of Sorbonne, founded in 1257 by Robert de Sorbon, chaplain to Louis IX, became its most famous college and lent the whole institution its nickname.

Early modern and revolutionary suppression

The university remained a powerful corporate body — and a frequent political actor — through the Reformation and the Wars of Religion. It was suppressed in 1793 during the French Revolution and its faculties were abolished, replaced by specialised schools. Napoleon refounded a University of France in 1806–1808 as a centralised teaching corps, within which faculties in Paris were re-established.

Modern university and the 1970 split

The loi Faure reforms following the student revolt of May 1968 led to the breakup of the University of Paris into thirteen autonomous universities in 1970 (Paris I–XIII), which inherited its buildings, libraries and traditions. Several of these (notably Sorbonne University, formed in 2018) rank among France's leading research universities today.

Organisation

Medieval Paris was governed by a rector elected by the Faculty of Arts, which comprised the four nations (French, Norman, Picard, and English-German). Degrees followed the sequence of bachelor, licentiate and master/doctor, with the arts course based on the trivium and quadrivium and higher study in theology lasting well over a decade.

Notable alumni and masters

Associated figures include Peter Abelard, Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus, William of Ockham, Erasmus, John Calvin, Ignatius of Loyola, Voltaire, and, in the modern era, Marie Curie and Pierre Curie, who taught at the Paris faculties.
