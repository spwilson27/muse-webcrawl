Treaty Clause

Treaty Clause
Location: U.S. Constitution, Article II, Section 2, Clause 2

The Treaty Clause of the United States Constitution (Article II, Section 2, Clause 2) grants the President power, "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate, to make Treaties, provided two thirds of the Senators present concur."

History and purpose

The Framers divided treaty-making between executive and Senate to combine secrecy and dispatch with deliberation and a supermajority safeguard. Early practice under George Washington shifted from oral consultation to written Senate advice and consent after ratification negotiations.

Scope

Treaties are part of the "supreme Law of the Land" under the Supremacy Clause. Supreme Court cases including Missouri v. Holland (1920) and Reid v. Covert (1957) addressed treaty power's reach: treaties may not violate the Constitution. Self-executing treaties take domestic effect without implementing legislation (Foster v. Neilson).

Executive agreements

Presidents increasingly use executive agreements — congressional-executive agreements and sole executive agreements — which do not require two-thirds Senate consent and now greatly outnumber Article II treaties, raising ongoing debate about circumvention.
