The K-ration was an individual daily combat food ration introduced by the United States Army during the Second World War. It was designed by the physiologist Ancel Keys for lightly equipped troops — originally paratroopers — who needed a light, compact, non-perishable ration that could be carried in a pocket. Three separate boxed meal units, designated breakfast, dinner, and supper, provided one day's food. [Image: The three meal units of a K-ration: breakfast, dinner, and supper]

Development

In 1941 the Army asked Keys, at the University of Minnesota, to design a ration that was small, light, shelf-stable, and reasonably nourishing. Keys famously bought candidate items — hard biscuits, dry sausage, hard candy, and chocolate — at a local grocery, and field-tested the prototypes with soldiers. The first orders were placed with the Cracker Jack Company in 1942; the "K" designation honoured Keys himself. Intended only for short missions of a few days, the ration ended up being used by troops in the field for much longer periods.

Each unit came in a waxed cardboard carton printed with morale-boosting graphics. The three units together weighed about 2.5 pounds (1.1 kg) and supplied roughly 2,830–3,000 kilocalories — adequate for short periods but a deficit for active soldiers over weeks.

Reception and replacement

Troops found the K-ration monotonous and insufficient for sustained operations; studies in Italy and elsewhere documented significant weight loss when it was consumed for extended periods, and soldiers famously traded or discarded its least-liked items. Nevertheless it fed millions of men through campaigns from North Africa to the Pacific. Production ended with the war and the K-ration was formally superseded by the C-ration as the standard individual combat ration, with Keys's experience informing his later landmark Minnesota Starvation Experiment.
