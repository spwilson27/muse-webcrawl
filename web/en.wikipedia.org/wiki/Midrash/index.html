Midrash

Midrash (Hebrew: מִדְרָשׁ; pl. midrashim) is an ancient Jewish mode of biblical interpretation, as well as the body of literature produced by that interpretation. The term derives from the Hebrew root darash (דרש), meaning "to seek", "to inquire", or "to expound". Midrash seeks to go beyond the plain sense (peshat) of the biblical text, drawing out legal rulings, moral teachings, and narrative expansions.

Types of Midrash

Midrash is conventionally divided into two broad categories:

  • Halakhic Midrash — legal exegesis, deriving Jewish law (halakha) from the Torah. Major examples include the Mekhilta on Exodus, the Sifra on Leviticus, and the Sifrei on Numbers and Deuteronomy.
  • Aggadic Midrash — non-legal material: stories, parables, theology, ethics, and folklore that expand upon biblical narratives. Major collections include Genesis Rabbah, Leviticus Rabbah, and the Midrash on the Five Megillot.

History

Midrashic interpretation has its roots in the biblical period itself — the Books of Chronicles retell earlier histories with expansions and harmonizations — but it flourished in the rabbinic era of late antiquity, roughly from the 2nd to the 10th centuries CE. The classic collections were compiled in Roman and Byzantine Palestine and in Babylonia. Midrash continued to be produced and compiled throughout the medieval period, culminating in great anthologies such as the Yalkut Shimoni.

Method

Midrash characteristically works verse by verse, word by word, and sometimes letter by letter. It fills gaps in the biblical narrative, explains apparent contradictions, connects distant verses through shared vocabulary (gezerah shavah), and reads contemporary concerns back into the ancient text. Famous narrative expansions include the stories of Abraham smashing his father's idols and the detailed accounts of the childhood of Moses — stories not found in the Bible but deeply embedded in Jewish tradition.

Major collections

  • Midrash Rabbah — a series of commentaries on the Five Books of Moses and the Five Scrolls.
  • Tanhuma — homiletic midrash on the Torah, organized around the weekly readings.
  • Pesikta de-Rav Kahana and Pesikta Rabbati — homilies for festivals and special Sabbaths.
  • Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer — a narrative retelling from Creation to the death of Miriam.
