Ovid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A medieval manuscript illumination of Ovid composing the Metamorphoses, quill in hand at a slanted writing desk]

Publius Ovidius Naso (20 March 43 BC – AD 17/18), known in English as Ovid, was a Roman poet of the Augustan age, contemporary with Virgil and Horace and the most immediately influential of the three on later European literature. His works — love elegies in youth, the great mythological epic Metamorphoses in middle life, and the poems of exile — trace the full arc of a brilliant career broken by disgrace.

The Metamorphoses (AD 8), fifteen books of myth from Chaos to Julius Caesar's deification woven by transformation, became the Middle Ages' and Renaissance's handbook of classical story — the source from which Chaucer, Shakespeare, Titian and Bernini drew Apollo and Daphne, Narcissus, Pygmalion, Orpheus and a hundred more. His Ars Amatoria (the art of love) taught seduction with a wit that scandalised Augustus's moral programme.

Exile

In AD 8 Augustus banished Ovid to Tomis on the Black Sea (modern Constanța, Romania) for a 'poem and a mistake' (carmen et error) never fully explained — the Ars Amatoria's offence is the usual guess for the poem; the mistake remains one of classical scholarship's favourite mysteries. His Tristia and Epistulae ex Ponto, begging recall, went unanswered by Augustus and by Tiberius; he died in exile, Rome's most entertaining poet marooned among the Getae.

Influence

Ovid is the classical poet the European tradition actually read most: Dante, Petrarch, Chaucer, Spenser, Shakespeare (whose works are steeped in Golding's 1567 translation), Milton and Keats all write in his debt, and his witty, sceptical, metamorphic imagination is the ancient world's most modern-seeming.
