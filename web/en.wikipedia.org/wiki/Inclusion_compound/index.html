Inclusion compound

In host–guest chemistry, an inclusion compound is a complex in which one chemical species (the "host") forms a cavity or crystal lattice containing another species (the "guest"), held by non-covalent forces rather than by ordinary chemical bonds. The guest is selected by the size and shape of the host's cavity.

Examples

Classic examples include clathrates, in which guests are trapped in cage-like lattices (as in gas hydrates of water), and the cyclodextrins — ring-shaped sugar molecules whose hydrophobic cavities bind small organic molecules in water. Crown ethers, calixarenes, cucurbiturils and porous materials such as zeolites and metal–organic frameworks also form inclusion compounds.

Applications

Inclusion compounds are used to stabilise reactive or volatile substances, to separate mixtures (for example isomers or enantiomers), in drug formulation to improve solubility — cyclodextrin complexes are widely used in pharmaceuticals and food flavourings — and in sensing and catalysis.
