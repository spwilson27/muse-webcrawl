The Solomonic dynasty was the ruling imperial house of Ethiopia, traditionally tracing its descent from King Solomon of Israel and Makeda, the Queen of Sheba. Founded in 1270 by Yekuno Amlak, who overthrew the Zagwe dynasty, it provided Ethiopia's emperors—with interruptions—until the deposition of Haile Selassie I in 1974, making it one of the longest-ruling dynasties in world history.

Legend and origins

According to the 14th-century national epic Kebra Nagast ("Glory of Kings"), the Queen of Sheba visited Solomon in Jerusalem and bore him a son, Menelik I, who later returned to Ethiopia carrying the Ark of the Covenant. The dynasty's emperors claimed descent from Menelik, a claim central to Ethiopian royal ideology and later enshrined in the 1955 constitution, which declared Haile Selassie's line to descend "without interruption from the dynasty of Menelik I."

History

Yekuno Amlak seized power in 1270 claiming restoration of the ancient Solomonic line. Under rulers such as Amda Seyon I and Zara Yaqob the empire expanded and consolidated Orthodox Christianity. The dynasty survived the devastating wars of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi in the 16th century, the Zemene Mesafint ("Era of the Princes", 1769–1855), during which emperors became puppets of regional warlords, and was restored to real power by Tewodros II and his successors. Menelik II expanded the empire and defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896. The last emperor, Haile Selassie I, was deposed by the Derg military government in 1974, ending over 700 years of Solomonic rule.

Legacy

The dynasty shaped Ethiopia's identity as an ancient Christian empire. Its Solomonic claim remains culturally potent, and members of the imperial family continue to hold a ceremonial role among monarchists and in the Ethiopian diaspora.
