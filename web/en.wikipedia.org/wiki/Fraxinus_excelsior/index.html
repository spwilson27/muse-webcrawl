Fraxinus excelsior

Fraxinus excelsior, known as the ash, or European ash or common ash to distinguish it from other types of ash, is a flowering plant species in the olive family Oleaceae. It is native throughout mainland Europe east to the Caucasus and Alborz mountains, and to the British Isles, which lie at the western edge of its range.

Description

It is a large deciduous tree growing to 12–18 m (39–59 ft) tall, exceptionally to 35 m, with a trunk up to 2 m in diameter and a tall, narrow crown. The bark is smooth and pale grey on young trees, becoming thick and vertically fissured with age. The winter buds are distinctive jet-black. The leaves are pinnate, 20–35 cm long, with 7–13 leaflets. The flowers open before the leaves in spring; trees are usually dioecious. The fruit is a single-winged samara 2.5–4.5 cm long, hanging in clusters known as "ash keys".

Ecology and distribution

Common ash grows best on moist, base-rich soils and is widespread in woodland, hedgerows and upland ashwoods across Europe. It is an important tree for wildlife, supporting hundreds of species of insects, lichens and fungi.

Ash dieback

Since the 1990s European ash has been devastated by ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus (syn. Chalara fraxinea), first described in Poland and since spread across most of the continent, including Britain, where it was confirmed in 2012. The disease causes crown dieback and death, particularly of young trees; a small proportion of trees show genetic resistance, which is the focus of breeding and conservation programmes.

Uses

Ash timber is tough, flexible and shock-resistant, traditionally used for tool handles, oars, hurley sticks in hurling, furniture, flooring and sports equipment. In Norse mythology the world tree Yggdrasil is generally identified as an ash.
