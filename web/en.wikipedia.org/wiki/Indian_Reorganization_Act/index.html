Indian Reorganization Act

The Indian Reorganization Act (IRA) of June 18, 1934, also known as the Wheeler–Howard Act, was major federal legislation in the United States that secured certain rights to Native Americans. It is considered the centrepiece of the "Indian New Deal" promoted by Commissioner of Indian Affairs John Collier under President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Provisions

The act reversed the assimilationist allotment policy of the Dawes Act of 1887, under which tribal landholdings had shrunk by tens of millions of acres. It ended further allotment of tribal lands, extended trust periods on existing allotments indefinitely, authorised the restoration of some surplus lands to tribes, and appropriated funds for purchasing new land. It also encouraged tribes to adopt written constitutions and corporate charters for self-government, established a revolving credit fund for tribal economic development, and gave employment preference to Native Americans in the Bureau of Indian Affairs.

Adoption and opposition

Tribes voted in referendums whether to accept the act; a majority of voting tribes accepted it, but some prominent nations, including the Navajo — alienated by Collier's livestock reduction programme — rejected it. Critics within Congress substantially weakened Collier's original, more ambitious bill. The constitution-drafting process also imposed uniform governmental forms that did not always match traditional tribal structures.

Legacy

The IRA halted the catastrophic loss of the tribal land base and provided the legal framework under which most federally recognised tribal governments still operate. Although a later federal policy of termination in the 1950s moved in the opposite direction, the IRA's framework was reaffirmed by the self-determination policies that followed.
