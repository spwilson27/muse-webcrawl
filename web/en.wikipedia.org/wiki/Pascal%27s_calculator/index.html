Pascal's calculator
Inventor: Blaise Pascal
Invented: 1642
Purpose: Addition and subtraction
Also called: Pascaline, arithmetical machine

Pascal's calculator (also known as the arithmetic machine, Pascaline, or Pascaline) is a mechanical calculator invented by the French mathematician and philosopher Blaise Pascal in 1642. It was one of the earliest mechanical calculators and could add and subtract two numbers directly, and multiply and divide by repetition.

History

Pascal began work on the machine at the age of nineteen, motivated by the laborious tax calculations of his father, who was a tax commissioner in Rouen, France. After years of development and some fifty prototypes, Pascal presented a finished version in 1645 and received a royal privilege (patent) for the device in 1649. About fifty machines were built, in several variants for decimal currency, accounting, and surveying units.

Design

The Pascaline was a brass rectangular box fitted with a row of numbered input wheels, one for each digit. Each wheel (for units, tens, hundreds, and so on) displayed its result in a window above. The machine's key innovation was an automatic carry mechanism: when one wheel passed from 9 to 0, a weighted lever, the sautoir, caused the next wheel to advance by one. Subtraction was performed using nine's complement arithmetic, displayed through a sliding bar that revealed a second row of digits. Because the wheels could only turn in one direction, true division required repeated subtraction.

Legacy

Although commercially unsuccessful — its price was high and it required skilled operators — the Pascaline demonstrated that a machine could perform arithmetic reliably, inspiring later inventors such as Leibniz, whose stepped reckoner built on similar principles. Several original Pascalines survive in museums, including the Musée des Arts et Métiers in Paris.
