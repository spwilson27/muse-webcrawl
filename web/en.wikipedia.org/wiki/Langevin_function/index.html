Langevin function

The Langevin function is a mathematical function introduced by Paul Langevin in 1905 in his classical theory of paramagnetism. It describes the magnetization of an ensemble of freely rotating magnetic moments—or, equivalently, the average orientation of electric dipoles in a dielectric—as a function of an applied field and temperature.

L(x) = coth(x) − 1/x

where coth is the hyperbolic cotangent. The variable x is typically the ratio of the interaction energy of a moment with the field to the thermal energy, for example x = μB/(kBT) for a magnetic moment μ in a magnetic field B at absolute temperature T.

Properties

The Langevin function is an odd function of x with the limiting behaviours:

  • For small x, L(x) ≈ x/3 − x³/45, giving a linear response that underlies Curie's law of paramagnetic susceptibility.
  • For large x, L(x) → 1 − 1/x, corresponding to saturation as all moments align with the field.

More precisely, the function has the series expansion L(x) = (1/3)x − (1/45)x³ + (2/945)x⁵ − …, involving Bernoulli numbers, and a continued-fraction representation useful for computation. The inverse Langevin function, which has no closed form, is needed in models of rubber elasticity and polymer chain statistics, and various rational approximations to it are used in practice.

Applications

The Langevin function appears throughout statistical physics. In magnetism it gives the classical magnetization curve of a paramagnet and is the classical limit of the more general Brillouin function, which accounts for quantized angular momentum. In dielectric theory, the same function describes the orientation polarization of polar molecules in an electric field (the Debye–Langevin theory). In polymer physics, the force required to stretch a freely jointed chain follows the inverse Langevin function, forming the basis of non-Gaussian chain models of rubber elasticity.
