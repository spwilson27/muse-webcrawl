Lagerstätte

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Lagerstätte (plural Lagerstätten; from German Lager, "storage/deposit", and Stätte, "place" — also written Lagerstaette or Lagerstatte) is a sedimentary deposit that exhibits extraordinary fossils with exceptional preservation, sometimes including preserved soft tissues. The term was introduced by the palaeontologist Adolf Seilacher in 1970.

Types

  • Konservat-Lagerstätten ("conservation deposits") – noted for exceptional preservation of soft-bodied organisms, usually thanks to anoxia, rapid burial or hypersalinity that excluded scavengers and decay bacteria. Examples: the Cambrian Burgess Shale (Canada) and Chengjiang biota (China), the Jurassic Solnhofen Limestone (Germany) that preserved Archaeopteryx, and the Eocene Messel Pit (Germany).
  • Konzentrat-Lagerstätten ("concentration deposits") – noted for enormous accumulations of hard parts, such as bone beds, shell beds and fossil fish layers.

Significance

Lagerstätten are rare and unevenly distributed in geological time, but they provide a large fraction of what is known about soft-bodied life and whole ancient ecosystems. Because exceptional preservation requires unusual conditions, the fossil record between Lagerstätten is biased toward animals with shells, bones and teeth; palaeontologists therefore treat these deposits as precious "windows" into past biodiversity.

Notable examples

Burgess Shale (Canada) · Chengjiang (China) · Hunsrück Slate (Germany) · Solnhofen (Germany) · Mazon Creek (USA) · Messel Pit (Germany) · La Brea Tar Pits (USA, a Konzentrat-Lagerstätte) · Baltic amber deposits.
