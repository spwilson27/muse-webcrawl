Taste

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Taste is the chemical sense by which dissolved substances in the mouth are detected by taste buds, mostly on the tongue. It identifies nutrients and hazards before swallowing: the basic tastes are sweet, sour, salty, bitter and umami (savoury). Together with Smell, texture and temperature, taste creates flavour.

Mechanism and basic tastes

Taste buds are clusters of receptor cells in papillae on the tongue, palate and throat; each bud responds to several tastes, though individual cells are tuned. Sweet, umami and bitter are detected by G protein-coupled receptors; salt and sour largely by ion channels sensing sodium and hydrogen ions. The once-popular 'tongue map' assigning each taste to one region is a myth — all regions detect all tastes, with modest sensitivity differences.

Biological role

Sweetness signals energy-rich carbohydrate, umami signals protein, salt signals electrolytes, while sour and intense bitterness warn of spoilage and toxins — one reason medicines taste bitter. Taste changes with age, illness, smoking and some drugs, and genetic variation in bitter receptors (such as PTC tasting) is a classic classroom genetics example.
