United States Fish and Wildlife Service

The United States Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS or FWS) is an agency of the US Department of the Interior dedicated to the management of fish, wildlife and natural habitats. Its mission is "working with others to conserve, protect, and enhance fish, wildlife, plants, and their habitats for the continuing benefit of the American people." The Service employs some 9,000 people and is headquartered in Washington, D.C.

History

The agency descends from the US Commission of Fish and Fisheries (1871) and the Bureau of Biological Survey. The modern Service was formed in 1940 by consolidating the Bureau of Biological Survey and the Bureau of Fisheries within the Interior Department, and took its present name in 1956. It administers landmark conservation laws including the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, the Lacey Act, the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (shared with the National Marine Fisheries Service) and the Pittman–Robertson and Dingell–Johnson wildlife and sport-fish restoration programmes funded by excise taxes.

Programmes

The Service manages the National Wildlife Refuge System, the world's largest network of lands dedicated to wildlife conservation, comprising more than 560 national wildlife refuges and 38 wetland management districts covering over 95 million acres, founded with Pelican Island, Florida, in 1903. It operates 70 national fish hatcheries, lists and recovers threatened and endangered species, enforces wildlife trade law (including CITES) through inspectors at ports, runs the Federal Duck Stamp programme — whose revenues have protected millions of acres — and funds migratory bird conservation and international wildlife work.

Its law-enforcement office investigates wildlife crime, while its ecological services programme reviews federal projects for impacts on listed species and habitats. The Service also leads efforts on invasive species, pollinator decline and climate adaptation for wildlife.
