Louis VII of France

Louis VII (1120 – 18 September 1180), called the Younger or the Young (French: le Jeune), was King of the Franks from 1137 until his death in 1180. He was the son and successor of King Louis VI. His reign saw the beginning of the long rivalry between the Capetian kings of France and the Angevin (Plantagenet) kings of England, and was marked by his marriage to and divorce from Eleanor of Aquitaine and his participation in the Second Crusade.

Early reign and marriage to Eleanor

Louis, the second son of Louis VI, was educated for a career in the Church until the death of his elder brother Philip in 1131 made him heir. He was crowned co-king at Reims in the same year and succeeded his father in 1137. That year he married Eleanor, heiress to the Duchy of Aquitaine, which vastly enlarged the territory under Capetian control, at least nominally. The young king, pious and mild-tempered, was guided early on by the abbot Suger of Saint-Denis, his father's great minister.

The Second Crusade

After the fall of Edessa in 1144, Louis took up the cross, urged on by Bernard of Clairvaux. He and Eleanor travelled overland to the Holy Land with the Second Crusade (1147–1149). The expedition was a failure: the French forces were mauled in Anatolia, and the crusaders' attack on Damascus in 1148 collapsed within days. Louis visited Jerusalem and returned to France by sea in 1149. The crusade also deepened the rift between king and queen.

Divorce and the Angevin threat

In March 1152 the marriage to Eleanor was annulled on grounds of consanguinity. Eleanor promptly married Henry Plantagenet, Count of Anjou and Duke of Normandy, who became King Henry II of England in 1154. The Angevin accumulation of England, Normandy, Anjou and Aquitaine created an empire that dwarfed the French royal domain, and Louis spent much of the rest of his reign in intermittent warfare and diplomacy against Henry, often supporting Henry's rebellious sons. He married twice more, and his third wife, Adela of Champagne, finally bore him a son, Philip, in 1165 — the future Philip II Augustus.

Later reign and legacy

At home Louis strengthened royal authority by protecting the Church, granting charters to communes and fostering the growth of Paris, where the construction of Notre-Dame Cathedral began in 1163 during his reign. He suffered a stroke in 1179, crowned Philip co-king at Reims that November, and died in Paris on 18 September 1180. He was buried at Barbeau Abbey, which he had founded; his remains were later transferred to Saint-Denis.
