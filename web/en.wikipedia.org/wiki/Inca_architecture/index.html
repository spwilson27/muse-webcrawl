Inca architecture

Inca architecture is the most significant pre-Columbian architecture in South America, developed by the Inca Empire in the Andes during the fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. It is renowned for monumental stonework fitted together without mortar, for buildings that harmonise with mountain landscapes, and for an empire-wide network of roads, terraces, temples, and storehouses. Famous examples include Machu Picchu, Sacsayhuamán above Cusco, and Ollantaytambo.

Ashlar and polygonal masonry at Machu Picchu, fitted without mortar.

Characteristics

The hallmark of fine Inca masonry is precisely cut stone blocks joined so tightly that a blade cannot pass between them. Two main styles are recognised: polygonal masonry, in which multi-sided blocks interlock like a jigsaw, and ashlar masonry, of regular rectangular blocks laid in courses. Walls typically lean slightly inward, and doorways, windows, and niches are trapezoidal — narrower at the top — features that give the buildings great resistance to earthquakes, which are frequent in the Andes. Ordinary buildings used fieldstone set in mud mortar and roofed with ichu grass thatch on wooden beams.

Forms and planning

Characteristic buildings include the kallanka, a great rectangular hall; the ushnu, a ceremonial platform; circular or rectangular storehouses (qollqa); and agricultural terraces (andenes) that stair down mountainsides. The finest temples, such as the Coricancha in Cusco, combined perfect masonry with gold sheathing and astronomical alignments. Inca cities were planned around plazas, and the empire's road system, the Qhapaq Ñan, linked settlements across thousands of kilometres with way stations called tambos.

Construction

Without wheeled transport, draft animals capable of haulage, or iron tools, Inca labourers — mobilised through the mit'a tax system — quarried andesite and limestone with hammerstones, dragged blocks on rollers and ramps, and finished them by grinding with sand. The scale of labour available to the state allowed projects of extraordinary precision and size.
