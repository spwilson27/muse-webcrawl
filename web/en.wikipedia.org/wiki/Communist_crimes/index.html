Communist crimes

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Communist crimes (Polish: zbrodnie komunistyczne) is a legal and historical term used, particularly in Polish law and in several post-communist states, for crimes committed by functionaries of communist regimes — including murder, political repression, deportation, and persecution — during the 20th century. The concept underlies the work of institutions such as Poland's Institute of National Remembrance (IPN), which investigates and prosecutes offences committed against Polish citizens by communist state organs between 1917 and 1990.

Scope

Historians discuss under this heading events including the Red Terror and Great Purge in the Soviet Union, the Holodomor famine in Ukraine, mass deportations of national groups, the Gulag camp system, the Katyn massacre, the Cultural Revolution and Great Leap Forward famine in China, the Khmer Rouge killing fields in Cambodia, and the suppression of uprisings in Hungary (1956) and Czechoslovakia (1968). Estimates of total excess deaths under communist governments vary enormously — The Black Book of Communism (1997) proposed a figure approaching 100 million, which has been both influential and contested by historians who dispute its methods and some of its component estimates.

Legal treatment

After 1989, several Central and Eastern European states adopted lustration laws, opened secret-police archives, and created remembrance institutes. The Council of Europe's 2006 resolution on the "need for international condemnation of crimes of totalitarian communist regimes" and the European Parliament's 2009 resolution on European conscience and totalitarianism called for recognition of victims; the Prague Declaration of 2008 proposed a common day of remembrance on 23 August, the anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact.

Debate

Scholarly debate concerns how to compare regimes, the role of ideology versus circumstance, and whether famine deaths should be classed as intentional crimes — questions on which historians such as Timothy Snyder, Anne Applebaum, and Stephen Kotkin have taken differing positions on specific cases.
