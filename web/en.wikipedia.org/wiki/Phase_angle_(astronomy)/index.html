Phase angle (astronomy)

In observational astronomy, the phase angle is the angle between the light incident onto an observed object and the light reflected from the object. For an object in the Solar System it is specifically the Sun–object–observer angle: the angle, at the object, between the direction to the Sun and the direction to the observer. Phase angle ranges from 0° for a fully illuminated object (at opposition or superior conjunction, "full phase") to 180° for an object seen entirely in shadow (at inferior conjunction, "new phase").

Explanation

The phase angle determines the illuminated fraction of a body's visible disc: for a sphere, the fraction lit is (1 + cos α)/2. The Moon's phase angle changes through the month — near 0° at full moon, about 90° at the quarter phases, and near 180° at new moon. Venus and Mercury, orbiting inside Earth's orbit, can be observed through the full range of phase angles, exhibiting the phases Galileo famously observed for Venus in 1610, evidence for the heliocentric model. Outer planets, seen from Earth, never exceed modest phase angles (Mars reaches about 47°).[1]

Phase curves

A body's brightness as a function of phase angle is its phase curve, which encodes surface roughness and scattering properties. Many airless bodies show an opposition surge — a sharp brightening within a few degrees of zero phase — attributed to shadow hiding and coherent backscatter. Phase curves are essential for interpreting observations of asteroids and for predicting the detectability of exoplanets, whose reflected-light phase curves are measured as they orbit their stars.
