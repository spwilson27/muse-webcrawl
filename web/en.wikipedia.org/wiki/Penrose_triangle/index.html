Penrose triangle


Type: Impossible object / optical illusion
Described by: Oscar Reutersvärd (1934); Roger Penrose and L. S. Penrose (1954)

The Penrose triangle, also known as the Penrose tribar or impossible triangle, is an optical illusion depicting a triangular object that appears physically solid in a drawing but cannot exist as a normal solid object in three-dimensional Euclidean space. Each of its three beams seems to rest on the next in a continuous loop that defies consistent depth.

History

The figure was first drawn by the Swedish artist Oscar Reutersvärd in 1934. It was independently devised and popularised in the 1950s by the physicist Roger Penrose and his father, the scientist and artist Lionel Penrose, who described it as "impossibility in its purest form" in a 1954 paper. The Dutch artist M. C. Escher, inspired by the Penroses' work, incorporated impossible constructions into lithographs such as Waterfall (1961).

Interpretation

The illusion arises because the visual system interprets each corner of the drawing as locally consistent while the global structure is contradictory. Physical sculptures that mimic the triangle can be built from disconnected or warped parts that align only from one special viewpoint; large examples stand in Perth, Australia, and in Gotschuchen, Austria.

In culture

The tribar is a widely used emblem of impossibility, appearing in logos, mathematics texts, and art exploring perceptual ambiguity.
