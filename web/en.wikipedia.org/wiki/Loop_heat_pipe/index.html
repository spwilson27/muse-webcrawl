Loop heat pipe

A loop heat pipe (LHP) is a two-phase heat transfer device that uses capillary action to remove heat from a source and passively move it to a condenser or radiator. It is a more capable relative of the conventional heat pipe: by separating the vapour and liquid flows into distinct smooth-walled lines and confining the capillary wick to the evaporator, an LHP can transport heat over metres rather than centimetres and against gravity over appreciable distances.

Construction and operation

The system consists of an evaporator with a fine-pored wick, an attached compensation chamber (reservoir) that holds excess liquid and sets the operating temperature, a vapour transport line, a condenser, and a liquid return line. Heat applied to the evaporator vaporises the working fluid (commonly ammonia, propylene or water); capillary pressure developed in the wick drives the vapour to the condenser, where it gives up its latent heat and the condensate returns to the evaporator, completing the loop. The technology was developed in the Soviet Union in the 1970s–80s (notably by Yury Maidanik) and patented as the "thermal regulator" before being commercialised in the West in the 1990s.

Applications

Loop heat pipes are widely used in spacecraft thermal control — aboard satellites and the International Space Station — and increasingly for cooling electronics, avionics and computer processors where passive, pump-free cooling and flexible routing are required.
