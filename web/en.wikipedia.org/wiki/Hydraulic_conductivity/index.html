Hydraulic conductivity

Hydraulic conductivity, symbolically represented as K, is a property of porous materials, soils and rocks that describes the ease with which a fluid (usually water) can move through pore spaces or fractures. It depends on the intrinsic permeability of the material, the degree of saturation, and the density and viscosity of the fluid. Hydraulic conductivity has dimensions of velocity (e.g. metres per day) and is a key parameter in hydrogeology and soil science.

Darcy's law

Hydraulic conductivity appears in Darcy's law, which states that the specific discharge through a porous medium is proportional to the hydraulic gradient: q = −K (dh/dl). It is related to the intrinsic permeability k of the medium by K = kρg/μ, where ρ is fluid density, g gravitational acceleration and μ dynamic viscosity.

Typical values

Values span many orders of magnitude: clean gravels may have conductivities of 10−1 m/s or more, sands around 10−5–10−3 m/s, silts much less, and unfractured clays as low as 10−11 m/s. Because of this range, conductivity is often reported on a logarithmic scale, and field-scale values may be dominated by fractures or layering (anisotropy).

Measurement

Hydraulic conductivity is measured in the laboratory with constant-head or falling-head permeameters, and in the field with slug tests, pumping tests and tracer tests. Empirical estimates such as the Hazen equation relate it to grain-size distribution for sands.
