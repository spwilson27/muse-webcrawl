Turner Prize

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A Turner Prize exhibition gallery: a pickled animal vitrine, an unmade bed and a light going on and off in adjacent rooms of the Tate]

The Turner Prize, named after the painter J. M. W. Turner, is an annual prize presented to a British visual artist. Organised by Tate and established in 1984, it is the United Kingdom's most famous (and most argued-about) award for contemporary art, presented for an outstanding exhibition or presentation of work in the preceding year.

Until 2016 restricted to artists under 50, the prize is judged on a nominated exhibition rather than a single work; the shortlist show (at Tate Britain in London years, partner venues elsewhere) and December announcement became, in the 1990s, British culture's annual argument about what art is.

The controversial decades

The Young British Artists made the prize their arena: Damien Hirst (won 1995, the shark and the sliced animals), Tracey Emin's My Bed (shortlisted 1999 — the exhibit that made the nation argue), Chris Ofili (1998, dung and resin), Grayson Perry (2003, accepting in a dress as his alter ego Claire) and Martin Creed (2001, the lights going on and off) defined an era when the tabloids covered art as sport. Sponsors and channels changed; the rows were the point.

Later years

The prize matured into video, performance and collective practice: Lubaina Himid (2017, the first woman of colour and, at 63 after the age rule's abolition, among its oldest winners), the all-shortlist collective win of 2019 (the four nominees asked to share it), and 2021's shortlist of collectives only. The prize money stands at £25,000 to the winner (of a £55,000 fund) by recent schemes.
