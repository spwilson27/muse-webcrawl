Flag

Flags are used as symbols of nations, organisations and signals.

A flag is a piece of fabric (most often rectangular or quadrilateral) with a distinctive design and colours. It is used as a symbol, a signalling device, or for decoration. The term can also be used to refer to the design used, or an abstract term for the artefact as a whole. The study of flags is known as vexillology, from the Latin word vexillum, meaning flag or banner.

Flags were originally used in warfare, and to some extent they have remained a standard ofheraldry, but they have also been used for signalling, decoration, advertising and other purposes. Since the late 18th century national flags have represented nations and other polities. Flags also represent other entities, such as companies, organisations, individuals and even fictional characters.

History

The earliest known flags were used by ancient civilisations. The Roman vexillum was a cloth hung from a horizontal crossbar. Chinese armies used banners of silk at least as early as the Zhou dynasty. In medieval Europe, flags became associated with heraldry, and standards, banners and pennons identified nobles and armies in battle. Maritime flags developed in the Age of Sail, culminating in systems of signal flags used by navies to communicate over distance.

The modern national flag emerged during the 17th century, with the Netherlands' Prince's Flag and the Danish Dannebrog among the earliest. The revolutionary era of the late 18th and 19th centuries produced the French tricolour, which influenced flag design worldwide. During the 20th century, decolonisation led dozens of new states to adopt flags, frequently drawing on pan-national colour schemes such as the pan-African and pan-Arab colours.

Design and symbolism

Flag designs commonly employ stripes (horizontal, vertical or diagonal), crosses, cantons, fields of solid colour, and charges such as stars, suns, crescents, animals and coats of arms. Colours frequently carry symbolic meaning: red may signify valour or revolution, blue loyalty or the sea, green land or Islam, and so on, though such meanings vary by culture and are sometimes assigned retrospectively. The five most common elements on national flags are stars, crosses, stripes, crescents and coats of arms.

Most national flags have a width-to-length ratio between 3:5 and 2:3. The flags of Switzerland and Vatican City are square (1:1), while Nepal's flag is uniquely non-rectangular, consisting of two stacked triangles.

Use

Flags are flown on government buildings, ships, schools and private homes. Protocols govern their display, including rules on hoisting, half-masting as a sign of mourning, and folding. On ships, an ensign indicates nationality; courtesy flags and house flags serve additional functions. International signal flags assign a flag to each letter of the alphabet and are used for maritime communication.

Flags are also powerful rallying symbols: they are waved at sporting events, burned in protest, and planted to claim territory — most famously on the Moon by Apollo astronauts.
