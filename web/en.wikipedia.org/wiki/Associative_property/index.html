Associative property

Associativity of addition: (2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4)

In mathematics, the associative property is a property of some binary operations, which states that rearranging the parentheses in an expression does not change the result. Formally, an operation ∗ is associative if (x ∗ y) ∗ z = x ∗ (y ∗ z) for all x, y, z. Addition and multiplication of real numbers are associative: (2 + 3) + 4 = 2 + (3 + 4) = 9, and likewise for products.

Examples

Associative operations include addition and multiplication of integers, rational, real and complex numbers; string concatenation; function composition; and the operations of groups, where associativity is one of the defining axioms. Matrix multiplication is associative but not commutative, illustrating that the two properties are independent.

Non-associative operations

Subtraction and division are not associative: (10 − 5) − 2 = 3 but 10 − (5 − 2) = 7. Exponentiation is also non-associative, and by convention is read right-associatively. The cross product of vectors and the operations of octonions are other standard non-associative examples; in such settings, parentheses are essential to meaning.

Importance

Where an operation is associative, chains of operations can be written without parentheses, evaluated in any grouping, and reorganised freely — a fact exploited in algebra, in the design of parallel algorithms, and (with care about floating-point rounding, which breaks exact associativity) in computing.
