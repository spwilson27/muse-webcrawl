Conodont

Temporal rangeCambrian – Triassic (~515–200 Ma)
GroupConodonta
Known fromTooth-like elements; rare body fossils
Conodont elements — microscopic phosphatic tooth-like structures — are among the most useful index fossils of the Palaeozoic.

Conodonts are an extinct group of jawless, eel-like vertebrates that lived worldwide in marine environments from the Cambrian to the end of the Triassic. For more than a century they were known only from their microscopic (0.25–2 mm) tooth-like conodont elements, composed of the mineral apatite, which are extraordinarily abundant in marine limestones. The identity of the "conodont animal" was a famous palaeontological mystery until soft-bodied specimens were described in 1983 from the Lower Carboniferous Granton Shrimp Bed of Scotland.

The animal

Body fossils reveal an elongated animal a few centimetres long with large eyes, a notochord, V-shaped muscle blocks (myomeres), and a caudal fin — a suite of features placing conodonts among the vertebrates, close to (or within) the jawless fishes. The elements formed an apparatus in the mouth and throat, functioning in grasping and processing food; wear patterns suggest some species were active predators, and the elements may be the earliest mineralised vertebrate "teeth".

Importance in geology

Conodonts evolved rapidly and are premier index fossils: their zones subdivide Cambrian through Triassic marine strata, and the first appearance of particular species defines several international stage boundaries, including the base of the Triassic (marked by Hindeodus parvus). Because apatite changes colour predictably with heating, the conodont alteration index (CAI) is a standard tool for estimating the thermal maturity of source rocks in petroleum exploration. They also provide oxygen-isotope records used to reconstruct ancient sea temperatures.

Extinction

Conodonts survived the Ordovician and Devonian mass extinctions but dwindled in the late Palaeozoic and disappeared in the end-Triassic mass extinction, about 200 million years ago.
