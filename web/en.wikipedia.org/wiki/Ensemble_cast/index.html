Ensemble cast

An ensemble cast is a cast in which the principal performers are assigned roughly equal amounts of importance in a film, television programme or play — as opposed to a production built around a single protagonist supported by secondary characters. Ensemble casts are usually built around multiple intersecting storylines, and the format has become a hallmark of both prestige television and large-scale film drama and comedy.

Structure

In an ensemble production, no single character dominates; screen time and narrative weight are distributed among several leads whose plotlines may run in parallel and interact. This structure suits stories about institutions and communities — hospitals (ER, Grey's Anatomy), police precincts, workplaces (The Office), families and groups of friends (Friends) — and allows long-running series to absorb cast changes more readily than star vehicles. In film, directors such as Robert Altman (Nashville, Short Cuts, Gosford Park) and Paul Thomas Anderson (Boogie Nights, Magnolia) became identified with the multi-strand ensemble form, and star-studded ensemble casts are also a marketing device, as in the Ocean's films.

History

Grand-scale ensemble storytelling descends from nineteenth-century multi-plot novels and was carried into cinema by films such as Grand Hotel (1932) and Dinner at Eight (1933). On television, serials and soap operas used large rotating casts from the start, and prime-time drama adopted the format widely from Hill Street Blues (1981) onward. Awards bodies have created specific honours for ensembles, notably the Screen Actors Guild Awards for ensemble performance in film and television, which recognise the cast collectively rather than individual performances.
