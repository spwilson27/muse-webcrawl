Polycrystal

Type: Crystalline solid

Building blocks: Crystallites (grains)

A polycrystal is a solid material composed of many microscopic crystals, called crystallites or grains, joined at interfaces known as grain boundaries. Most common metals, ceramics, rocks and ice are polycrystalline, in contrast to single crystals, in which the crystal lattice extends unbroken through the whole specimen.

Formation

Polycrystals typically form when a liquid solidifies: crystals nucleate at many points and grow until they impinge on one another, each grain having a different crystallographic orientation. The resulting grain size depends on the cooling rate and on subsequent processing; rapid cooling and many nucleation sites give fine grains, while annealing at high temperature allows grains to grow.

Properties

Grain boundaries obstruct the motion of dislocations, so — by the Hall–Petch relationship — a fine-grained polycrystal is generally stronger and harder than a coarse-grained one of the same material. At high temperatures, however, grain boundaries can slide and act as paths for diffusion and creep, so single-crystal components (such as turbine blades) are preferred in the most demanding hot environments. If the grains are randomly oriented the material is roughly isotropic; processing such as rolling can align them into a preferred orientation, called texture.
