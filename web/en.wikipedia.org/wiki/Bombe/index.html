The bombe was an electro-mechanical device used by British cryptologists at Bletchley Park during the Second World War to help decipher German Enigma-machine-encrypted secret messages. It was designed by Alan Turing in 1939–1940, building on the pre-war Polish bomba kryptologiczna, and substantially improved by Gordon Welchman's "diagonal board".


A rebuilt bombe at Bletchley Park

Principle of operation

The German Enigma machine enciphered messages using rotors whose daily settings — rotor order, ring settings, and plugboard connections — produced an astronomically large number of possible keys. The bombe attacked the problem using a guessed fragment of plaintext, a "crib". By wiring together the equivalent of many Enigma machines, the bombe could test all 17,576 rotor positions for a given rotor order in about twenty minutes, eliminating settings that led to logical contradictions. Cryptanalysts then checked the surviving "stops" by hand on a replica Enigma.

Production and operation

The first bombe, named "Victory", was delivered to Bletchley Park in March 1940. Production machines were built by the British Tabulating Machine Company at Letchworth. By the end of the war some 210 bombes were in operation at outstations including Eastcote and Stanmore, staffed largely by members of the Women's Royal Naval Service ("Wrens"), running around the clock. The United States Navy also built over 120 high-speed bombes for attacking the four-rotor naval Enigma.

Legacy

The bombe was one of the foundations of the Allied signals-intelligence effort known as Ultra, which historians credit with significantly shortening the war. A working reconstruction can be seen at The National Museum of Computing at Bletchley Park.
