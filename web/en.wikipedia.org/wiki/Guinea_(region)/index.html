Guinea (region)

Guinea is a traditional name for the region of West Africa that lies along the Gulf of Guinea, encompassing the coastal lands from Guinea-Bissau in the northwest through Nigeria and Cameroon and, in some usages, as far south as Gabon and Congo. The name is thought to derive from a Berber word for the lands of black peoples south of the Sahara.

European traders divided the coast by its chief exports — the Grain (Pepper) Coast, Ivory Coast, Gold Coast and Slave Coast — and the region became a major theatre of the Atlantic slave trade from the 16th to the 19th century. Inland states such as the Ashanti Empire and the Kingdom of Dahomey were major powers in the region.

The name survives in three modern states: Guinea, Guinea-Bissau and Equatorial Guinea (the latter in Central Africa), while Papua New Guinea in the Pacific was named for the perceived resemblance of its people to West Africans. The guinea coin and the guinea pig also take their names from the region's trading associations.
