Xhosa (isiXhosa), also isiXhosa as an endonym, is a Bantu language of the Nguni branch, spoken principally in South Africa by the Xhosa people. It is one of South Africa's official languages and the second most common first language in the country after Zulu, with roughly 8 million native speakers concentrated in the Eastern Cape and Western Cape, and millions more who speak it as an additional language. Xhosa is famous among world languages for its use of click consonants, sounds it shares with its close relative Zulu and, ultimately, acquired through contact with Khoisan languages.

Click consonants

Xhosa has three basic click types: dental clicks (written c), alveolar/postalveolar clicks (written q) and lateral clicks (written x), each occurring in several series (plain, aspirated, breathy-voiced and nasalised), yielding a set of fifteen click consonants. Clicks appear in everyday words — the name Xhosa itself begins with a lateral click — and are typically the feature by which outsiders recognise the language. Nelson Mandela, whose first name Rolihlahla contains a click, often demonstrated their pronunciation.

Grammar and phonology

Like other Bantu languages, Xhosa is agglutinative and tonal: words are built from roots with chains of prefixes and suffixes, nouns fall into a system of classes marked by prefixes (e.g. umu-/aba- for persons) which control agreement across the sentence, and verbs mark subject, object, tense, aspect and mood. The language has two contrastive tones, high and low. Its vowel system has five vowels (a, e, i, o, u), and the Latin-based orthography is largely phonemic and disjunctive in its word division.

History and literature

Written Xhosa developed in the 19th century through missionary presses in the Eastern Cape; an early landmark was John Bennie's orthographic work and the printing of religious texts. The poet S. E. K. Mqhayi (1875–1945), often called the father of Xhosa literature, established modern Xhosa poetry and prose. Under apartheid, Xhosa was promoted in the Transkei and Ciskei "homelands"; since 1994 it has been one of South Africa's official languages, used in broadcasting (e.g. by the SABC), education and parliament, though English dominates many formal domains.
