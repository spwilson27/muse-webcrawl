Gamut

In color reproduction, including computer graphics and photography, the gamut, or color gamut, is a certain complete subset of colors. The most common usage refers to the subset of colors that can be accurately represented in a given circumstance, such as within a given color space or by a certain output device.

Another sense, less frequently used but still correct, refers to the complete set of colors found within an image at a given time. In this context, digitizing a photograph, converting a digitized image to a different color space, or outputting it to a given medium using a certain output device generally alters its gamut, in the sense that some of the colors in the original are lost in the process.

Introduction

The term gamut was adopted from the field of music, where in middle Latin "gamma ut" meant the lowest note of the Guidonian hexachord, and in time came to imply the entirety of the hexachord. Shakespeare's use of the term in The Taming of the Shrew is sometimes attributed as the invention of the term by the author. In the 1850s, the term was applied to a range of colors or hues, and in the 20th century was first used in the context of color reproduction by, among others, the color scientist David MacAdam.

Gamut representation

Gamuts are commonly represented as areas in the CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram, with the curved edge representing the spectrum of monochromatic (single-wavelength) colors and the straight edge representing purple, a mixture of red and violet light. Devices that reproduce color, such as displays and printers, can only reproduce a limited gamut, usually shown as a triangle inside the full visible gamut.

Limitations of color representation

No physical device can reproduce the entire visible gamut. Additive devices such as RGB displays are limited by the spectral properties of their primaries, while subtractive devices such as CMYK printers are limited by the absorption properties of their inks. Colors outside a device's gamut must be approximated or remapped, a process known as gamut mapping.
