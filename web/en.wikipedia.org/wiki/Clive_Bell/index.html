Arthur Clive Heward Bell (16 September 1881 – 18 September 1964) was an English art critic, associated with formalism and the Bloomsbury Group. He developed the theory of "significant form" in his book Art (1914), one of the most influential works of early modernist aesthetics in English.

Life

Bell was educated at Marlborough College and Trinity College, Cambridge, where he entered the circle of the Cambridge Apostles that formed the nucleus of the Bloomsbury Group. In 1907 he married Vanessa Stephen, sister of Virginia Woolf; the marriage, though unconventional, was lifelong. The Bells' home, Charleston in Sussex, became a hub of Bloomsbury artistic life.

Significant form

In Art (1914), Bell argued that what distinguishes art from other objects is "significant form" — a particular combination of lines and colours that provokes a distinctive "aesthetic emotion" in the viewer. Representation, he claimed, is irrelevant or even harmful to aesthetic value. The theory, developed alongside Roger Fry's advocacy of Post-Impressionism, which Bell helped introduce to Britain through the 1910 and 1912 exhibitions, championed Cézanne and the modern movement.

Criticism and later work

Critics charged that significant form is circular — defining art by the emotion and the emotion by art — and Bell himself moderated the theory in later works such as Since Cézanne (1922) and Civilization (1928). He remained a prolific essayist and broadcaster on art and literature for the rest of his career.
