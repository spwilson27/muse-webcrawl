pH is a logarithmic scale used to specify the acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution: pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Solutions with pH below 7 are acidic, above 7 basic (alkaline), and pure water at 25 °C is neutral at pH 7.

Because the scale is logarithmic, each whole pH unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion concentration: lemon juice (about pH 2) is roughly 100,000 times more acidic than pure water. The concept was introduced by Danish chemist Søren Peter Lauritz Sørensen in 1909 at the Carlsberg Laboratory.

[Image: A universal indicator pH colour scale from red to purple with sample solutions]

Measurement

pH is measured approximately with indicator papers or universal indicator, which changes colour across the spectrum from red (acidic) through green (neutral) to purple (strongly basic), and precisely with a glass-electrode pH meter.

Typical values

Stomach acid ~1.5–2; vinegar ~2.5; coffee ~5; blood 7.35–7.45 (tightly controlled); seawater ~8; soapy water ~9–10; oven cleaner ~13–14. Many biological systems are sensitive to small pH shifts.
