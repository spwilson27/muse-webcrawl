Gantt chart

A Gantt chart plots tasks as bars against a calendar timescale.

A Gantt chart is a bar chart that illustrates a project schedule. It was designed and popularized by Henry Gantt around the years 1910–1915. Modern Gantt charts also show the dependency relationships between activities, progress relative to plan (shading of bars), milestones (usually diamonds) and often the critical path. They are a standard feature of project management software such as Microsoft Project, Primavera and many web tools.

Structure

Time runs along the horizontal axis and tasks are listed on the vertical axis. Each task is a bar whose position and length represent its start date, duration and finish date. Arrows or links indicate dependencies (e.g. finish-to-start). A vertical line marks the current date. Resource allocations and percentage-complete can be overlaid. Gantt charts communicate, at a glance, what is to be done, when, and in what order — but become cluttered on very large projects and do not by themselves show workload or the consequences of delays as clearly as network methods such as PERT and the critical path method.

History

An early precursor was the harmonogram of Polish engineer Karol Adamiecki (1896). Henry Gantt, an associate of Frederick Winslow Taylor, developed his chart in the 1910s for production scheduling in factories; it was used extensively in the First World War for munitions planning and in interwar megaprojects such as the Hoover Dam. Wallace Clark's 1952 book popularised it for management audiences. With personal computers in the 1980s, Gantt charts became the default visual language of project planning.
