Buddhism in Myanmar is predominantly of the Theravada tradition, practised by roughly 88–90 percent of the country's population — one of the highest proportions of Buddhist adherents in any nation. Burmese Buddhism is interwoven with nat (spirit) worship and astrology, and the monastic order (sangha) of some 500,000 monks and novices occupies a central place in national life, sustained by lay merit-making through almsgiving (dāna).

History

Tradition traces Burmese Buddhism to the time of the Buddha himself and to missions of Ashoka, while historically Theravada took firm root under King Anawrahta of Pagan in the 11th century, who unified the country and built thousands of temples at Bagan. In the 19th century King Mindon convened the Fifth Buddhist Council (1871) and had the Pali Canon inscribed on 729 marble slabs at Kuthodaw Pagoda in Mandalay. Under British colonial rule the sangha became a focus of nationalist resistance, led by monks such as U Ottama, and Buddhism retained a powerful political role after independence — including the monks' protests of the 2007 "Saffron Revolution" and, controversially, Buddhist nationalist movements in the 2010s.

Practice

Most Burmese boys undergo a temporary ordination ceremony (shinbyu) as novices. Meditation practice (vipassanā) in the traditions of teachers such as Mahasi Sayadaw and S. N. Goenka spread from Myanmar to the world, strongly shaping the modern global mindfulness movement.
