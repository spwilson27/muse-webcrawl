Mortgage law


A historic mortgage deed.

Mortgage law is a body of law governing the creation, transfer and enforcement of mortgages — security interests in real property granted by a borrower (the mortgagor) to a lender (the mortgagee) to secure a loan. Mortgage law is a branch of property law and differs substantially between jurisdictions.

History

The mortgage developed in English common law from the "dead pledge" (French mort gage): land was conveyed to the lender on condition that it would revert if the debt was paid by a fixed date. Equity courts later developed the borrower's equity of redemption, protecting borrowers from forfeiture after the date, and the corresponding remedy of foreclosure.

Key concepts

  • Title theory vs lien theory: in title-theory jurisdictions the mortgage conveys legal title to the lender; in lien-theory jurisdictions it creates only a lien while the borrower keeps title.
  • Deed of trust: in some US states a three-party instrument involving a trustee is used instead of a mortgage.
  • Priority: mortgages generally rank by order of registration; recording statutes determine whose interest prevails against later purchasers.
  • Foreclosure: judicial or non-judicial sale of the property on default, depending on the jurisdiction.
