Philippine–American War

The Philippine–American War, known in the United States as the Philippine Insurrection, was an armed conflict between the First Philippine Republic and the United States that lasted from February 4, 1899, to July 2, 1902. In the US, it was considered an insurrection and not a war because the US regarded the Philippines as American territory. Emerging from the Spanish–American War, the conflict arose when Filipino nationalists under Emilio Aguinaldo, who had proclaimed independence from Spain, refused to accept the cession of the Philippines to the United States under the Treaty of Paris (1898).

Background

Filipino revolutionaries had fought Spain since 1896. After Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay (May 1, 1898), Aguinaldo returned from exile and declared Philippine independence on June 12, 1898. The United States nevertheless annexed the archipelago for $20 million under the Treaty of Paris. Filipino forces, barred from entering Manila after its capture by American troops, surrounded the capital as tensions mounted.

Course of the war

Fighting began on February 4, 1899, when an American private fired on Filipino soldiers near Manila. Conventional operations in 1899—battles for Manila, Malolos, and the northward advance into central Luzon—destroyed the Philippine Republic's field army by November 1899, and Aguinaldo shifted to guerrilla warfare. American forces responded with increasingly harsh counterinsurgency measures, including reconcentration of civilians in some provinces.

Aguinaldo was captured at Palanan, Isabela, on March 23, 1901, by forces under Frederick Funston, and swore allegiance to the United States. Resistance continued under generals such as Miguel Malvar in Batangas. After guerrillas ambushed an American company at Balangiga, Samar (September 28, 1901), the U.S. retaliation on Samar under General Jacob H. Smith became a scandal in the United States, as did reports of torture such as the "water cure," prompting Senate hearings. President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war over on July 2, 1902, although fighting by Moro groups in the south continued for years.

Casualties and aftermath

About 4,200 American soldiers died, most from disease. Filipino combatant deaths are estimated at 16,000–20,000, and civilian deaths at 200,000 or more from fighting, famine, and disease, with some estimates considerably higher. The war established the Philippines as an American colony under civil government (from 1901) and deeply shaped both countries' politics; the Philippines gained independence on July 4, 1946.
