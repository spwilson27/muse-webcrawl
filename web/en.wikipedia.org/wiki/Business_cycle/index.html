Business cycle

[Image: a graph-style city skyline silhouette rising and falling in waves, symbolising booms and slumps]

The business cycle is the pattern of fluctuation in economic activity around its long-run growth trend: expansions of output, employment and income give way to peaks, contract into recessions, reach troughs, and recover. Cycles vary in length from about two to ten years and are a central object of Macroeconomics.

Features and theories

Many variables move together (procyclical output, employment, investment and profits; countercyclical unemployment), and turning points are dated officially in the US by the NBER. Theories attribute cycles to demand shocks and rigidities (Keynesian and New Keynesian models), monetary mismanagement (monetarist), over-investment and credit booms (Minsky; Austrian theory), or real shocks to technology and supply (real business cycle theory). Named rhythms — inventory (Kitchin), fixed-investment (Juglar) and long-wave (Kondratiev) cycles — remain heuristic. Stabilisation policy aims to damp the cycle, not abolish it; the 'Great Moderation' of the 1980s–2000s was declared prematurely before 2008.
