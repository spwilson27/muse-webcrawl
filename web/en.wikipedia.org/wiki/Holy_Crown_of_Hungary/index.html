The Holy Crown of Hungary (Hungarian: Szent Korona), also known as the Crown of Saint Stephen, is the coronation crown used by the Kingdom of Hungary for most of its history. More than regalia, it became the supreme symbol of Hungarian statehood: in Hungarian constitutional doctrine, sovereignty resided in the Holy Crown itself, and no king was legitimate without being crowned with it.

The Holy Crown, notable for its enamel plaques of Christ and the saints and its crooked cross.

Description and origins

The crown consists of two main parts. The lower band, the corona graeca, is a Byzantine work of the 1070s, sent by Emperor Michael VII Doukas to King Géza I; its enamel plaques depict Christ Pantocrator, the emperor, and saints, with Greek inscriptions. The upper part, the corona latina, bears Latin inscriptions and images of apostles and is of later, debated origin — possibly 12th century. Tradition connects the crown with Stephen I, Hungary's first king, crowned in 1000/1001 and canonised in 1083, and legend holds it was sent by Pope Sylvester II. The cross on top is bent, damage traditionally dated to the 17th century.

The Doctrine of the Holy Crown

From the later Middle Ages a distinctive legal doctrine developed — articulated in Werbőczy's Tripartitum (1514) — treating the Holy Crown as a legal person and the embodiment of the state, its "members" being the king and the nobility. Lands of the Crown, lawful coronation with the Crown at Székesfehérvár by the Archbishop of Esztergom, and oaths to the Crown underpinned constitutional thought into the 20th century.

Later history

The crown was repeatedly hidden, seized and carried abroad during wars. At the end of the Second World War it was taken west and eventually held by the United States at Fort Knox; President Jimmy Carter returned it to Hungary in 1978, a major symbolic event. Since 2000 it has been displayed in the central hall of the Hungarian Parliament Building, guarded by the Crown Guard, and appears on Hungary's coat of arms.
