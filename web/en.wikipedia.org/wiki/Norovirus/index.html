Norovirus, sometimes referred to as the winter vomiting disease, is the most common cause of acute gastroenteritis globally. It is a genus of highly contagious, non-enveloped RNA viruses in the family Caliciviridae, named after Norwalk, Ohio, where an outbreak in 1968 led to the virus's identification (published in 1972 by Albert Kapikian and colleagues).

Disease

Infection is characterised by non-bloody diarrhoea, vomiting, stomach cramps, nausea, low-grade fever and malaise, typically beginning 12–48 hours after exposure and lasting 1–3 days. Most people recover fully, but the very young, elderly and immunocompromised can suffer severe dehydration. The infectious dose is extremely low — as few as a few dozen viral particles — and the virus spreads by the faecal–oral route, contaminated food and water, fomites, and aerosolised vomit. Oysters and other shellfish filter-feeding in contaminated water are a classic vehicle.

Epidemiology

Norovirus causes an estimated 685 million cases of acute gastroenteritis worldwide each year, including about 200 million in children under five, and around 200,000 deaths, mostly in developing countries. Outbreaks are notorious in closed settings such as cruise ships, hospitals, schools, care homes and military camps. Genogroup II, genotype 4 (GII.4) strains dominate human infections and periodically generate pandemic variants.

Prevention and treatment

There is no specific antiviral treatment or licensed vaccine (several candidates are in trials); management is supportive, chiefly oral rehydration. Prevention relies on handwashing with soap (alcohol gels are less effective against the non-enveloped virus), surface disinfection with chlorine bleach, and excluding ill food handlers. Immunity after infection is partial and short-lived, and susceptibility is influenced by histo-blood group antigens — people with non-secretor status are naturally resistant to many strains.
