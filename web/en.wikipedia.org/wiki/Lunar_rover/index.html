A lunar rover (or Moon rover) is a space exploration vehicle designed to move across the surface of the Moon. Rovers may be robotic, teleoperated or autonomous, or crewed vehicles carrying astronauts. They extend the range of surface exploration far beyond a landing site, carrying cameras, spectrometers, drills and sample-collection equipment.

History

The first rover on the Moon was the Soviet Union's Lunokhod 1, landed by Luna 17 in November 1970; driven by operators on Earth, it travelled about 10 kilometres over eleven lunar days. Its twin, Lunokhod 2, covered roughly 39–42 km in 1973. The only crewed lunar rover is NASA's Lunar Roving Vehicle (LRV), a battery-powered four-wheeled vehicle built by Boeing that carried two astronauts on each of the final three Apollo missions (Apollo 15, 16 and 17, 1971–1972), greatly expanding their traverse range and sample return.

Later missions

China's Chang'e 3 landed the Yutu rover in 2013, and Chang'e 4's Yutu-2, deployed in January 2019, became the first rover to operate on the far side of the Moon, surviving for years. India's Chandrayaan-3 landed the small Pragyan rover near the lunar south pole in August 2023. Japan's SLIM mission carried two small hopping rovers in 2024. Future crewed programmes, including NASA's Artemis campaign, plan a Lunar Terrain Vehicle for astronaut traverses.

Design challenges

Lunar rovers must withstand vacuum, temperature extremes between lunar day and night, abrasive regolith dust, cosmic radiation and one-sixth Earth gravity, while communicating with Earth across a delay of about 1.3 seconds each way.
