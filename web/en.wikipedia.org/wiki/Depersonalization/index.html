Depersonalization involves a persistent feeling of detachment from one's self.

Depersonalization is a dissociative phenomenon characterised by a subjective experience of detachment, as though one is an outside observer of one's mental processes or body. The feeling often includes a sense of detachment from one's own physicality; an inability to relate to the feeling of self; and a sensation that one's surroundings or environment may also be unreal — the latter being known as derealization.

Occasional, brief moments of mild depersonalization are common in the general population; chronic depersonalization is referred to as depersonalization-derealization disorder. Depersonalization is classed as a dissociative disorder in the diagnostic manuals. Common descriptions include feeling disconnected from one's body, as if watching oneself in a movie, emotional numbness, and a sense that one's memories lack emotional colour.

Signs and symptoms

Individuals who experience depersonalization feel divorced from their own personal self by sensing their body sensations, feelings, emotions, behaviors, etc. as not belonging to the same person or identity. Often a person who has experienced depersonalization claims things seem unreal or hazy. Also, a familiarity with self breaks down, which was described by a theory collaborator using the acronym AHIS (alienation from self). Sufferers may report an inability to picture themselves, a feeling of robotic or automatic functioning, and distorted perceptions of time.

Causes

Depersonalization can be a symptom of anxiety disorders, depression, schizophrenia, epilepsy, migraine, and obsessive-compulsive disorder, among others. It can also arise from psychological trauma, sleep deprivation, and the use of certain recreational drugs, particularly cannabis and hallucinogens. Transient depersonalization is a common response to severe stress, dangerous situations, and extreme fatigue.

Mechanism

Neurobiological models propose that depersonalization involves abnormal activity in prefrontal regions that inhibit limbic structures such as the amygdala, dampening emotional responses while heightening self-monitoring. Neuroimaging studies have found altered responses in regions involved in emotional processing and body awareness, consistent with the combination of emotional numbing and hypervigilant detachment reported by patients.

Diagnosis and treatment

Persistent depersonalization with intact reality testing, in the absence of another explanatory condition, supports a diagnosis of depersonalization-derealization disorder. No medication is specifically approved for the disorder; treatment usually targets comorbid conditions such as anxiety and depression, and psychotherapeutic approaches, including cognitive behavioral therapy adapted for dissociation, may help. Many people recover fully, particularly when symptoms follow a transient period of stress.
