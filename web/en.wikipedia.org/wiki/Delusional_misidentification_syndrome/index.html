Delusional misidentification syndrome

Faces are processed in the fusiform gyrus; misidentification syndromes are linked to disrupted connection between recognition and emotional familiarity

Delusional misidentification syndrome is an umbrella term in neuropsychiatry for a group of delusional beliefs that a person, place or object has been replaced, duplicated, transformed or changed in identity. The syndromes are relatively rare and are associated with schizophrenia, dementia and right-hemisphere brain injury.

Types

The principal types are Capgras delusion, in which the patient believes a familiar person has been replaced by an impostor; Fregoli delusion, the belief that different people are actually a single person in disguise; intermetamorphosis, in which people are believed to swap identities; and reduplicative paramnesia, in which a place is believed to exist in duplicate or to have been relocated.

Proposed mechanisms

A leading explanation holds that face recognition occurs normally but the normal sense of emotional familiarity is disconnected, prompting confabulated explanations for the discrepancy. Lesion studies implicate the right frontal lobe and connected structures, and damaged belief-evaluation processes are thought to explain why the implausible beliefs persist.
