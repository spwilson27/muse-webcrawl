Sir John Monash (27 June 1865 – 8 October 1931) was an Australian civil engineer and military commander of the First World War. He commanded the Australian Corps in France in 1918 and is widely regarded as one of the most effective Allied generals of the war.

Early life and engineering career

Monash was born in Melbourne to German-Jewish immigrant parents. He studied at Scotch College and the University of Melbourne, graduating in engineering, arts and law. Working as a civil engineer, he became a leader in the introduction of reinforced concrete construction in Australia and designed bridges and railway works across Victoria.

Military career

Monash joined the militia as a young man and rose steadily through the citizen forces. At the outbreak of the First World War he commanded the 4th Infantry Brigade, which he led at Gallipoli in 1915. Promoted to command the newly formed 3rd Division in 1916, he trained it in England before taking it to the Western Front, where it fought at Messines and Passchendaele in 1917.

In May 1918 Monash succeeded William Birdwood as commander of the Australian Corps, the largest corps on the Western Front. On 4 July 1918 his meticulously planned attack at the Battle of Hamel combined infantry, artillery, tanks, aircraft and supplies dropped by parachute into a model all-arms operation completed in 93 minutes. He then directed the Australian spearhead in the Battle of Amiens on 8 August 1918, described by German general Erich Ludendorff as "the black day of the German Army".

Later life

After the war Monash oversaw the repatriation of Australian troops and then became general manager of Victoria's State Electricity Commission, developing the brown coal resources of the Latrobe Valley. Monash University in Melbourne is named in his honour, as are numerous suburbs, roads and the Monash Freeway. He is commemorated on the Australian one-hundred-dollar note.
