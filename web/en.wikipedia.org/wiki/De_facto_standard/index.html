A de facto standard is a custom or convention that has achieved a dominant position by public acceptance or market forces, even though it has not been formally ratified by a standards organization. The term de facto is Latin for "in fact" (as opposed to de jure, "in law"). De facto standards arise through broad adoption, historical accident, or the market power of a single vendor, and competitors often must conform to them to remain compatible.

Examples

Classic examples include the QWERTY keyboard layout, which persists despite alternatives designed to supersede it; the IBM PC architecture and Microsoft Windows in personal computing; the PDF file format before its formal standardization as ISO 32000; and VHS's victory over Betamax in the videotape format war. In software, many file formats and APIs become de facto standards because one implementation dominates its field.

Relationship to formal standards

A de facto standard may later be codified by a standards body, at which point it becomes a de jure standard as well. Formalizing a dominant practice can improve interoperability and reduce vendor lock-in, but it can also freeze in suboptimal choices. Governments and large purchasers sometimes prefer open, formal standards in procurement to avoid dependence on a single supplier.

Format wars

Competing would-be de facto standards can produce "format wars," in which incompatible products fight for the installed base and network effects that make one of them dominant. Outcomes often depend less on technical merit than on licensing terms, alliances, and timing.
