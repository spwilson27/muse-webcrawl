The Family Computer, commonly abbreviated as Famicom, is an 8-bit home video game console developed and manufactured by Nintendo. Released in Japan on July 15, 1983, at a price of ¥14,800, it was Nintendo's first cartridge-based home system and the platform on which franchises such as Super Mario Bros., The Legend of Zelda, Metroid, and Final Fantasy began. A redesigned version, the Nintendo Entertainment System (NES), was released in North America in 1985 and in Europe from 1986, reviving the home console market after the video game crash of 1983.

Development and release

Designed by Masayuki Uemura and his team at Nintendo R&D2, the Famicom was built around a Ricoh CPU based on the MOS Technology 6502 — a low-cost component obtained after Nintendo negotiated for inexpensive chips — paired with a custom Picture Processing Unit capable of colourful sprites and scrolling backgrounds. Its red-and-white casing was reportedly chosen because president Hiroshi Yamauchi wanted cheap, distinctive plastic; the hardwired controllers included a microphone in the second pad, a feature used by a handful of games. An early production fault caused some games to crash and forced one of Japan's first major console recalls, but the system thereafter sold steadily, outselling Sega's competing SG-1000, which launched the same day.

Hardware and peripherals

Games were distributed on ROM cartridges; Nintendo's control over cartridge manufacturing and licensing shaped the industry's business model. Accessories included the Famicom Disk System (1986), a floppy-disk peripheral with rewritable disks used for titles such as the original The Legend of Zelda; the Family BASIC keyboard; the Famicom 3D System; and numerous controllers and light guns. In 1993 Nintendo released the AV Famicom (New Famicom), a redesigned model with detachable controllers and composite video output. Production of the original Famicom continued in Japan until 2003, giving it a twenty-year manufacturing run; Nintendo offered repair support until 2007.

Legacy

Combined with the NES, the platform sold more than 61 million units worldwide and is credited with establishing the modern console licensing model, standardising the directional-pad controller, and launching many of gaming's most enduring franchises. In 2016 Nintendo released the NES Classic Edition/Famicom Mini, a miniature replica with 30 built-in games, which sold out rapidly. The Famicom remains an icon of Japanese popular culture; its colour scheme and controller design recur throughout Nintendo's later products.
