Babylonian astrology

Babylonian astrology is the earliest recorded organized system of astrology, arising in Mesopotamia during the second millennium BCE. Developed by the priests and scholars of Babylon and earlier Sumerian and Akkadian cultures, it treated the movements of the Sun, Moon, and planets as signs — omens — sent by the gods concerning the fate of the king, the state, and the harvest.

A cuneiform tablet of celestial omens in the tradition of the Enūma Anu Enlil series.

Origins and development

The earliest roots of Babylonian sky-watching lie in Sumerian star lore of the third millennium BCE, but the systematic compilation of celestial omens began in the Old Babylonian period (c. 2000–1600 BCE). The great omen series Enūma Anu Enlil, assembled by about 1100 BCE, gathered some 7,000 omens concerning the Moon, Sun, planets, and weather on seventy tablets. Observations were recorded nightly by temple scribes, and the resulting diaries — the Babylonian astronomical diaries — form the longest continuous observational record of the ancient world.

The zodiac and mathematical astrology

By the fifth century BCE the Babylonians had divided the ecliptic into twelve signs of thirty degrees each — the zodiac still used in Western astrology. In the same period they developed arithmetic schemes for predicting lunar and planetary phenomena, and the earliest known personal horoscopes appear: birth charts cast for private individuals rather than for the king alone. Babylonian methods passed through Persia into the Hellenistic world after the conquests of Alexander the Great, where they merged with Greek geometry and Egyptian traditions to produce the horoscopic astrology of the Greco-Roman world.

Planets and gods

Each visible planet was identified with a major deity: Jupiter with Marduk, Venus with Ishtar, Saturn with Ninurta, Mercury with Nabu, and Mars with Nergal. The Moon (Sin) and Sun (Shamash) held central places in the omen literature. Eclipses were regarded as especially dangerous omens for the king, and rituals such as the appointment of a substitute king were devised to deflect the threatened evil.

Legacy

Babylonian astrology supplied the conceptual and mathematical foundations — the zodiac, planetary periods, and the horoscope itself — on which Hellenistic, Indian, and later medieval Islamic and European astrology were built.
