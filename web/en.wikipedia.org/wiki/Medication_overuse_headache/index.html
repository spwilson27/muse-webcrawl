Medication overuse headache

Medication overuse headache (MOH), also known as rebound headache, is a chronic headache disorder caused by the excessive use of acute headache medication by people with a pre-existing primary headache disorder, most often migraine or tension-type headache.

Diagnosis

Diagnostic criteria include headache occurring on 15 or more days per month, in a person with a pre-existing headache disorder, who has regularly overused one or more acute headache medications for more than three months. Overuse thresholds depend on the drug:

  • Simple analgesics (e.g., paracetamol/acetaminophen, NSAIDs): 15 or more days per month.
  • Triptans, ergotamines, opioids, and combination analgesics: 10 or more days per month.

Mechanism

The exact mechanism is not fully understood. Repeated exposure to acute medication is thought to produce central sensitization and changes in pain-processing pathways, lowering the threshold for further headaches and creating a cycle in which medication relieves a headache briefly while driving the next one.

Treatment

The cornerstone of treatment is withdrawal of the overused medication — ideally complete cessation, though gradual tapering may be used for opioids and barbiturates. Headaches commonly worsen for one to two weeks after withdrawal before improving. Preventive therapy for the underlying headache disorder (such as topiramate, onabotulinumtoxinA, or a CGRP-targeting treatment for migraine) may be started at or before withdrawal. Education about medication limits and headache diaries are important, as relapse rates are significant.

Epidemiology

MOH affects roughly 1–2% of the general population and a much larger share of patients seen in headache clinics. It is more common in women and in people with frequent migraine.
