Horsemanship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A rider practising collected work

Horsemanship is the art and practice of handling, training, and riding horses. It encompasses the rider's skill in the saddle — position, balance, and the use of the aids (seat, legs, hands, and voice) — as well as ground skills such as grooming, handling, longeing, and stable management. The term is also used for competitive classes in which the rider, rather than the horse, is judged.

Traditions

Two broad traditions dominate in the English-speaking world. The English tradition, rooted in continental cavalry schools and classical dressage descending from Xenophon's treatise On Horsemanship (c. 350 BC) through the riding academies of Renaissance Italy and the Spanish Riding School of Vienna, emphasises collected gaits and precise arena work. The Western tradition developed from Spanish colonial and American ranch practice, emphasising a free-moving horse ridden one-handed on a loose rein for cattle work. Other major traditions include the vaquero and charro schools of Mexico, Mongolian horse culture, and the gaucho tradition of South America.

Competition

In North American horse shows, "horsemanship" is also a specific judged class: Western horsemanship classes evaluate the rider's form and control through a pattern of manoeuvres, while English "equitation" classes do the same over flat work and fences. Equestrian sport more broadly, governed internationally by the Fédération Équestre Internationale, includes dressage, show jumping, eventing, driving, endurance, and reining.
