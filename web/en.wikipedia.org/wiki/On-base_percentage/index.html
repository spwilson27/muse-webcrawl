On-base percentage

In baseball statistics, on-base percentage (OBP; sometimes called on-base average, OBA) measures how frequently a batter reaches base. It is the ratio of a batter's times-on-base (hits, walks, and times hit by pitch) to their number of plate appearances, calculated as (H + BB + HBP) / (AB + BB + HBP + SF). Unlike batting average, OBP credits walks and hit-by-pitches.

History and significance

OBP became an official Major League Baseball statistic in 1984. Sabermetric research, popularized by Bill James and the book and film Moneyball, showed that OBP correlates more strongly with scoring runs than batting average, since avoiding an out is the batter's most valuable contribution. A league-average OBP is around .320; .370 is considered excellent and .400 exceptional. Ted Williams holds the career record at .482, and Barry Bonds set the single-season record of .609 in 2004. Combined with slugging percentage, OBP forms on-base plus slugging (OPS).
