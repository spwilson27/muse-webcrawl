Critical theory is a social theory oriented toward critiquing and changing society as a whole, in contrast to traditional theory oriented solely toward understanding or explaining it. Rooted in the work of the Frankfurt School at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt, critical theory draws on Marxism, psychoanalysis, and other traditions to examine power, domination, and ideology in modern societies.

History

The term was coined by Max Horkheimer in his 1937 essay "Traditional and Critical Theory". Horkheimer defined critical theory as a social theory aiming "to liberate human beings from the circumstances that enslave them", distinguishing it from "traditional theory", which merely describes society without seeking to transform it.

After the Nazi rise to power, the Institute moved to New York, affiliated with Columbia University, and returned to Frankfurt after the Second World War. Major works of the first generation include Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno's Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947), which argued that the Enlightenment's project of rational liberation had inverted into new forms of domination through instrumental reason and the "culture industry", and Herbert Marcuse's Eros and Civilization (1955) and One-Dimensional Man (1964).

Key concepts

  • Instrumental rationality — reason reduced to a tool for efficiency and control.
  • Culture industry — mass-produced culture that pacifies consumers and standardises consciousness.
  • Reification — treating social relations as if they were relations between things.
  • Communicative rationality — Habermas's alternative grounded in undistorted communication and the public sphere.
  • Recognition — Honneth's account of social conflict as a struggle for recognition.

Second and third generations

Jürgen Habermas, the leading figure of the second generation, developed a theory of communicative action (1981) and discourse ethics, shifting critical theory toward language, law, and democracy. Axel Honneth, director of the Institute from 2001, centred the theory on recognition. Contemporary critical theorists such as Nancy Fraser have extended the framework to globalisation, feminism, and the crisis of care.

Influence

Critical theory has profoundly influenced sociology, literary studies, cultural studies, education, and political theory, and in a broader sense the term is now applied to many emancipatory approaches in the humanities and social sciences, including feminist theory, postcolonial theory, and critical race theory.
