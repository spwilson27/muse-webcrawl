Genetic fallacy

The genetic fallacy is a fallacy of irrelevance in which a claim or concept is judged solely on the basis of its origin or history, rather than on its present meaning or merits. It can take a positive or negative form: dismissing an idea because of where it came from, or endorsing one because of an admired source.

Description

The fallacy occurs when an argument's conclusion depends on the genesis of a belief rather than the evidence for it. For example, rejecting a scientific theory because its originator held disreputable views, or accepting a proposal merely because it comes from a prestigious institution, both commit the genetic fallacy. It is related to the ad hominem fallacy and to guilt by association.

When origins are relevant

Considering a source is not always fallacious: the reliability of testimony, conflicts of interest, and the provenance of evidence can legitimately affect how much weight a claim deserves. The fallacy lies in treating origin as decisive in place of evaluating the claim itself.

History of the term

The concept was discussed by Morris Cohen and Ernest Nagel in their 1934 work on logic and scientific method. The term "genetic" here refers to genesis — origin — and should not be confused with genetics in the biological sense.
