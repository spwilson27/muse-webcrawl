In silico

In silico is an expression meaning "performed on computer or via computer simulation", coined by analogy with the Latin phrases in vivo, in vitro, and in situ. The phrase refers to experiments carried out in a virtual environment — on silicon computer chips — rather than in living organisms (in vivo) or in laboratory glassware (in vitro).

Origin

The term is generally credited to Pedro Miramontes, who used "in silico" in 1989 in the title of a paper on cellular automata presented at a workshop in Los Alamos. It gained wide currency through the 1990s as computational biology, bioinformatics, and systems biology expanded, and it is now standard in pharmacology, toxicology, and genomics.

Applications

In silico methods include molecular docking and virtual screening in drug discovery, computational modelling of cells and organs, genome-scale metabolic models, and simulations used to predict toxicity or pharmacokinetics before laboratory testing. Whole-cell computational models, such as the 2012 model of Mycoplasma genitalium, aim to simulate an entire organism in silico.

Advantages and limitations

Computational experiments are fast, cheap, and raise no animal-welfare concerns, and regulators increasingly accept in silico evidence alongside laboratory data. However, models are only as good as their assumptions and the data used to parameterise them, so in silico results typically require experimental confirmation.
