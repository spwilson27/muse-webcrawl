Carbon fibers

Image placeholder: Woven carbon fiber cloth.

Carbon fibers or carbon fibres (alternatively CF, graphite fiber, or graphite fibre) are fibers about 5 to 10 micrometres in diameter composed mostly of carbon atoms. They have several advantages over steel and other structural materials, including high stiffness, high tensile strength, high strength-to-weight ratio, chemical resistance, temperature tolerance, and low thermal expansion.

Production

Most commercial carbon fiber is made from polyacrylonitrile (PAN) precursor fiber, which is stabilised by heating in air, carbonised in an inert atmosphere at temperatures up to about 1,500 °C (higher for graphitised grades), surface-treated, and sized. A smaller share is produced from petroleum pitch. The resulting fibers are bundled into tows, woven into fabrics, or chopped, and are usually combined with a polymer resin matrix to form carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) composites.

Applications

Carbon fibers are used extensively in aerospace (aircraft wings and fuselages, satellite structures), wind turbine blades, motorsport and high-end automobiles, sporting goods such as bicycles, rackets, and fishing rods, and civil engineering reinforcement. High cost — driven by precursor and energy-intensive processing — remains the principal limit to wider use, especially in mass-market cars.

History

Carbon filaments were used in early incandescent lamps by Joseph Swan and Thomas Edison in the late 19th century; the modern high-strength material was developed in the late 1950s and 1960s at facilities including the Royal Aircraft Establishment in Britain and Union Carbide in the United States.
