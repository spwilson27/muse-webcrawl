Myxogastria

DomainEukaryota
PhylumAmoebozoa
ClassMyxogastria (Myxomycetes)

Myxogastria, also called Myxomycetes or true slime molds, is a class of slime molds within the Amoebozoa containing roughly 900 described species. They are characterised by a life cycle that includes a mobile, multinucleate feeding stage called a plasmodium, which can grow to many centimetres across and move by rhythmic cytoplasmic streaming.

The plasmodium of Fuligo septica, commonly called dog vomit slime mold.

Life cycle

Haploid spores germinate to release amoeboid or flagellated cells (myxamoebae and swarm cells), which feed on bacteria and can fuse as gametes to form a diploid zygote. The zygote's nucleus divides repeatedly without cell division, producing the plasmodium — a single, often brightly coloured, fan-shaped mass of protoplasm enclosed in a slime sheath. Upon starvation or environmental change, the plasmodium transforms into fruiting bodies (sporangia, aethalia, or plasmodiocarps) that produce new spores by meiosis.

Ecology

Myxogastrians are common in moist terrestrial habitats worldwide, feeding on bacteria, yeasts, and decaying organic matter on rotting wood, leaf litter, bark, and dung. Familiar species include Physarum polycephalum, a model organism famous for experiments on maze-solving and network optimisation; Lycogala epidendrum ("wolf's milk"); and Stemonitis species forming brown, hair-like clusters.

Classification

The group is usually divided into orders including the Echinosteliales, Liceales, Physarales, Stemonitales, and Trichiida (Trichiales). Though historically studied by mycologists alongside fungi, molecular evidence places them firmly among the Amoebozoa.
