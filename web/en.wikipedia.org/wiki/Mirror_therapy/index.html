Mirror therapy

Mirror therapy (or mirror visual feedback) is a therapeutic technique in which a mirror is placed between a patient's limbs so that the reflection of the intact limb is superimposed over the position of the affected or amputated limb, creating the visual illusion that the missing or impaired limb is present and moving normally. It was pioneered in the 1990s by neurologist V. S. Ramachandran for the treatment of phantom limb pain.

Uses

The best-established application is relief of phantom limb pain after amputation: watching the reflected limb "move" can unclench a painfully cramped phantom and ease cramping pain. Mirror therapy has also been investigated for rehabilitation after stroke, particularly for upper-limb motor recovery and neglect, and for complex regional pain syndrome, often as part of graded motor imagery programmes.

Mechanism and evidence

The proposed mechanism involves resolving a mismatch between motor commands and visual/proprioceptive feedback, engaging cortical mirror-neuron and sensorimotor networks. Evidence is strongest for phantom limb pain; results for stroke rehabilitation are promising but heterogeneous.
