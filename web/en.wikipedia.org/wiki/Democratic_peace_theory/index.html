Democratic peace theory

Democratic peace theory
Key proponents: Michael Doyle, Bruce Russett, John Owen
Intellectual root: Kant, Perpetual Peace (1795)

Democratic peace theory proposes that democratic states rarely, if ever, go to war with one another. It is one of the strongest empirical regularities in international relations, first theorised by Immanuel Kant and revived by Michael W. Doyle in 1983.

Explanations

Normative explanations hold that democracies externalise norms of compromise; institutional explanations cite checks and balances, public accountability and the difficulty democratically elected leaders face mobilising for war. Some scholars argue the peace is capitalist, territorial, or alliance-based rather than democratic.

Evidence and debate

Statistical studies by Rudolph Rummel, Bruce Russett and others find dyads of mature democracies have essentially no wars. Critics dispute definitions of democracy and war, cite near-miss cases (War of 1812, Spanish–American War, Kargil), covert interventions against elected governments, and note that democratising states may be more war-prone (Mansfield and Snyder).

Policy

The theory underpinned democracy-promotion policies from Woodrow Wilson to the Clinton and George W. Bush administrations.
