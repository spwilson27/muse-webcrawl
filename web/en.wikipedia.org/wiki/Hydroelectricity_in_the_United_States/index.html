Hydroelectricity in the United States

Hydroelectricity in the United States is the second largest source of renewable energy in the country by generated electricity, after wind power. Hydroelectric power stations in the United States are currently the third largest source of electrical energy in the country overall, after natural gas and coal. At the end of 2023, U.S. hydropower had an installed capacity of roughly 103 GW, including about 22 GW of pumped-storage hydroelectricity, and conventional hydropower accounted for approximately 6 percent of total U.S. utility-scale electricity generation.

History

Hydroelectric power was among the earliest forms of electricity generation in the United States. In 1882, a hydroelectric plant began operation in Appleton, Wisconsin, on the Fox River, supplying power to paper mills and homes. The late nineteenth century saw rapid growth of small hydroelectric stations near industrial centers, and the great dam-building era of the 1930s — driven by New Deal public works — produced landmark projects such as Hoover Dam (completed 1936) and Grand Coulee Dam, operated by the Tennessee Valley Authority, the Bureau of Reclamation, and the Army Corps of Engineers.

Major facilities

The largest hydroelectric facility in the United States by capacity is Grand Coulee Dam in Washington, with a nameplate capacity of about 6,809 MW. Other major plants include the Chief Joseph Dam and John Day Dam on the Columbia River, the Robert Moses Niagara Power Plant, and Hoover Dam on the Colorado River. The Pacific Northwest, particularly the Columbia and Snake River systems, produces the largest share of U.S. hydropower, followed by the Southeast and New York state.

Pumped storage

Pumped-storage hydroelectricity accounts for the great majority of grid-scale energy storage in the United States. Facilities such as the Bath County Pumped Storage Station in Virginia (about 3,003 MW) pump water to an upper reservoir during periods of low demand and release it through turbines at peak times.

Environmental and regulatory issues

Hydropower development has raised persistent concerns about fish migration, especially Pacific salmon runs on the Columbia and Snake rivers, river ecosystems, and sedimentation. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) licenses most non-federal hydroelectric projects, and relicensing proceedings frequently become forums for debates over dam removal, fish passage, and tribal rights. Several smaller dams in the United States have been removed in recent decades, most notably the Elwha and Glines Canyon dams in Washington and the Klamath River dams in Oregon and California.
