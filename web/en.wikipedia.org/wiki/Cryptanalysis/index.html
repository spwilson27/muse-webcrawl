Cryptanalysis

Cryptanalysis (from the Greek kryptós, "hidden", and analýein, "to analyse") is the study of analysing information systems in order to understand their hidden aspects — above all, the art and science of breaking ciphers: recovering plaintext from ciphertext without the key, or recovering the key itself. Together with cryptography (the design of ciphers) it forms the field of cryptology. In modern practice cryptanalysis also evaluates the security of protocols, implementations and random number generators, and its attacks drive the continual strengthening of cryptographic standards.

History

The earliest known systematic treatise is the ninth-century Manuscript on Deciphering Cryptographic Messages by the Arab polymath Al-Kindi, which introduced frequency analysis: in any language some letters are far more common than others, so a simple substitution cipher betrays itself through its letter statistics. Frequency analysis defeated classical ciphers for centuries, prompting polyalphabetic systems such as the Vigenère cipher, which in turn fell to the Kasiski examination and index-of-coincidence methods in the nineteenth century. The twentieth century mechanised both sides of the contest: Polish mathematicians led by Marian Rejewski first broke the German Enigma machine in the 1930s, and at Bletchley Park Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman and others — using electromechanical Bombes and the Colossus computers against the Lorenz cipher — turned cryptanalysis into an industrial-scale, mathematically sophisticated enterprise. Claude Shannon's 1949 theory of secrecy systems founded its modern mathematical basis.

Types of attack

Attacks are classified by what the analyst is assumed to have: ciphertext only; known plaintext–ciphertext pairs; chosen plaintexts (or chosen ciphertexts) submitted to an encryption oracle; or related keys. Against modern block ciphers the principal generic techniques are differential cryptanalysis (Biham and Shamir, c. 1990), which tracks how input differences propagate through rounds, and linear cryptanalysis (Matsui, 1993), which exploits approximate linear relations among plaintext, ciphertext and key bits; both influenced the design of DES's successors and of AES. Real-world breaks more often exploit implementation flaws: side channels such as timing, power consumption and acoustic emissions, faulty random number generation, protocol misuse and padding-oracle errors. Hash functions are attacked by seeking collisions, as in the practical SHA-1 collision of 2017.

Quantum and future threats

Shor's algorithm (1994) showed that a sufficiently large quantum computer could factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time, breaking RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography; this prospect has driven the standardisation of post-quantum cryptographic algorithms by NIST in the 2020s. Symmetric ciphers are less affected — Grover's algorithm merely halves their effective key length — so doubling key sizes largely compensates.

Practice

Cryptanalysis is conducted by signals-intelligence agencies such as the NSA and GCHQ, by academic researchers who publish attacks on standardised algorithms, and in industry security evaluation. Kerckhoffs's principle — that a system should remain secure even if everything about it except the key is public — frames cryptanalysis as the expected adversary any sound design must withstand.
