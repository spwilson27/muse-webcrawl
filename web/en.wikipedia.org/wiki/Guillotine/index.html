Guillotine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A guillotine is an apparatus designed for efficiently carrying out executions by beheading. The device consists of a tall, upright frame with a weighted and angled blade suspended at the top. The condemned is secured at the bottom by a hinged board, the neck positioned directly below the blade, which is released to fall swiftly and sever the head from the body with a single pass.

Origins

Similar devices, such as the Scottish Maiden and the Halifax Gibbet, existed earlier, but the French guillotine was proposed in 1789 by Dr. Joseph-Ignace Guillotin, who argued in the National Assembly for a quick, painless and egalitarian method of execution. A prototype was built by Tobias Schmidt to designs refined by Dr. Antoine Louis, and the first execution — of highwayman Nicolas-Jacques Pelletier — took place on 25 April 1792.

Revolution and after

The guillotine became the emblem of the Reign of Terror (1793–94), during which some 17,000 people were officially executed, including Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette. It remained France's standard means of capital punishment for nearly two centuries: public executions continued until 1939, and the last use was the execution of Hamida Djandoubi in Marseille in 1977. France abolished the death penalty in 1981.

Use elsewhere

The device was also used in Belgium, Sweden, Italy and extensively in Nazi Germany, where an estimated 16,500 people were executed by the Fallbeil between 1933 and 1945, including members of the White Rose resistance group.
