The Carlton Club meeting, held on 19 October 1922, was a meeting of Conservative Party Members of Parliament at the Carlton Club in London at which the party voted to end its coalition with David Lloyd George's Liberals and to fight the next general election as an independent party. It is one of the most famous meetings in British political history and gave rise to the name of the 1922 Committee of Conservative backbenchers.

Background

Since 1918 Britain had been governed by a coalition led by the Liberal David Lloyd George, in which Conservatives formed the larger partner. By 1922 discontent among Conservatives had grown over the Chanak Crisis, the honours scandal and Lloyd George's style of government. Austen Chamberlain, the party leader, supported continuing the coalition; Stanley Baldwin, then President of the Board of Trade, led the opposition, memorably describing Lloyd George as "a dynamic force" that was "a very terrible thing" for a party.

The meeting and vote

At the meeting Chamberlain argued for the coalition, but was opposed by Baldwin and by former leader Bonar Law, whose presence signalled he was willing to lead again. The MPs voted by 187 to 87 to leave the coalition. Lloyd George resigned as Prime Minister the same day, and Bonar Law formed a purely Conservative government, winning the general election that followed in November 1922.

Legacy

The backbenchers who organised the revolt formed the committee of Conservative private members which became known as the 1922 Committee, still the forum of Conservative backbench MPs and the body that oversees leadership elections and votes of confidence.
