Cross section (geometry)

In geometry and science, a cross section is the non-empty intersection of a solid body in three-dimensional space with a plane, or the analog in higher-dimensional spaces. Cutting an object into slices creates many parallel cross-sections. The boundary of a cross-section in three-dimensional space that is parallel to two of the axes — that is, parallel to the plane determined by two coordinate axes — is sometimes called a contour line; for example, if a plane cuts through mountains on a raised-relief map parallel to the ground, the resulting boundary is a contour line in two-dimensional space showing points of equal elevation.

Examples

The cross-sections of a right circular cone parallel to its base are circles, and planes at other angles produce the conic sections — ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola. A plane slicing a sphere always yields a circle; slicing a cube can produce a triangle, quadrilateral, pentagon, or regular hexagon. Cavalieri's principle states that solids with corresponding equal cross-sectional areas have equal volumes, an idea that anticipated integral calculus and underlies the computation of volumes by integrating cross-sectional area along an axis.

Applications

Cross-sections are used throughout technical drawing and engineering to reveal internal structure, in geology to depict subsurface strata along a profile line, in medicine and biology in histological sections and tomographic imaging (CT and MRI produce cross-sectional slices of the body), and in physics, where "cross section" also denotes a measure of the probability of a scattering interaction.
