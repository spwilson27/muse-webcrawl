Music education

Music education is a field of practice in which educators are trained for careers as elementary or secondary music teachers, school or music conservatory ensemble directors. Music education is also a research area in which scholars do original research on ways of teaching and learning music. Music education scholars publish their findings in peer-reviewed journals, and teach undergraduate and graduate education students at university education or music schools, who are training to become music teachers.

A school music class with students learning instruments together.

Music is an art form and cultural activity whose medium is sound. It is an integral part of every known culture, and music training has been shown to help children develop fine motor skills, literacy, self-confidence, and social skills. In primary and secondary schools, music education may take the form of general music classes, choral ensembles, orchestras, concert bands, marching bands, and jazz bands.

History

Music instruction dates back to antiquity. In ancient Greece, music was a core part of education, taught alongside mathematics, gymnastics, and philosophy; Plato considered music essential to the training of the soul. In medieval Europe, singing schools attached to cathedrals trained choristers in plainchant, and the conservatory tradition developed in Naples in the 16th century, where orphanages (conservatori) taught music to children.

In the United States, the Boston Academy of Music, founded in 1833 by Lowell Mason, helped introduce music into public school curricula. The 20th century saw the development of influential pedagogical approaches including the Orff Schulwerk of Carl Orff, the Kodály method of Zoltán Kodály, the Dalcroze method of eurhythmics, and the Suzuki method of Shinichi Suzuki, which teaches young children music in the manner of a mother-tongue language.

Approaches and methods

Orff Schulwerk

Developed by Carl Orff and Gunild Keetman, the Orff approach combines music, movement, drama, and speech into lessons that draw on a child's innate playfulness. Students use specially designed percussion instruments, including xylophones and glockenspiels with removable bars.

Kodály method

The Kodály method, developed in Hungary, uses folk songs, movable-do solfège with hand signs, and rhythmic syllables to build musicianship from an early age, progressing from the simple to the complex.

Suzuki method

Shinichi Suzuki's "talent education" holds that every child can learn music if immersed in it early, as with language. Children begin as young as three or four, learn by ear before reading notation, and parents attend lessons and supervise practice.

Music education in schools

In many countries, music is part of the national curriculum. In the United States, the National Association for Music Education (NAfME) advocates for school music programs and publishes national standards. Ensemble participation — band, choir, and orchestra — remains the dominant model in American secondary schools, while general music classes predominate at the elementary level.
