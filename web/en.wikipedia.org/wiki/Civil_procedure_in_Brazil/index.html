Civil procedure in Brazil

Civil procedure in Brazil is governed principally by the Code of Civil Procedure (Código de Processo Civil, CPC), currently Law No. 13,105 of 16 March 2015, which entered into force in March 2016. The code regulates litigation in civil, commercial, and other non-criminal matters before Brazilian courts, and applies subsidiarily in labour, electoral, and administrative proceedings. Brazilian civil procedure belongs to the civil-law tradition and is strongly shaped by the Constitution of 1988, which guarantees due process of law (devido processo legal), adversarial proceedings (contraditório), and full defence.

History

The first unified national code was the Code of Civil Procedure of 1939. It was replaced by the Code of 1973 (Law No. 5,869), drafted under the leadership of Alfredo Buzaid, a former Minister of Justice and professor at the University of São Paulo, and influenced by Italian procedural scholarship, particularly the work of Giuseppe Chiovenda, Enrico Tullio Liebman, and Piero Calamandrei. The 1973 code was amended many times over four decades, notably by reforms introducing anticipatory relief (tutela antecipada) and strengthening enforcement proceedings.

Work on a new code began with a commission of jurists appointed by the Senate in 2009, chaired by Luiz Fux, then a minister of the Superior Court of Justice. The resulting 2015 code emphasised cooperation between judge and parties, precedent, conciliation and mediation, and the reduction of excessive formalism and delay — perennial concerns in a system burdened by tens of millions of pending cases.

Structure of proceedings

An ordinary civil action (procedimento comum) begins with a statement of claim (petição inicial) filed by the plaintiff. Before or after service, the judge may schedule a conciliation or mediation hearing, encouraged by the 2015 code and by the Mediation Law of the same year. The defendant is summoned (citação) to appear and to present a defence (contestação), after which the parties may reply and rejoinder. The judge then sanitises the case (saneamento), fixing the points in dispute and ordering the production of evidence — documents, witness testimony, expert examination (perícia), and judicial inspection. A hearing of instruction and judgment (audiência de instrução e julgamento) may follow before judgment (sentença) is given.

Appeals and precedent

Decisions may be challenged by a range of appeals (recursos), including the appeal proper (apelação) to the state or federal court of appeals, interlocutory appeals (agravo de instrumento), and, on questions of federal law, the special appeal (recurso especial) to the Superior Court of Justice (STJ) and the extraordinary appeal (recurso extraordinário) on constitutional questions to the Supreme Federal Court (STF). The 2015 code systematised a regime of binding and persuasive precedents, including repetitive appeals (recursos repetitivos) and the incident for resolution of repetitive demands (IRDR), intended to promote uniformity and predictability.

Enforcement

Judgment enforcement (cumprimento de sentença) takes place, as a rule, in the same proceedings as the action itself — a feature introduced by reform in 2005 — with attachment (penhora) of the debtor's assets, including electronic attachment of bank balances through the judiciary's SisbaJud system. Separate enforcement proceedings based on extrajudicial titles, such as cheques and notarised instruments, also exist.
