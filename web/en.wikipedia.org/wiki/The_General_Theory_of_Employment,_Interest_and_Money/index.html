The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money

The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money is a book by the English economist John Maynard Keynes, published in February 1936. It is widely regarded as the foundation of modern macroeconomics and the core text of Keynesian economics. Written against the background of the Great Depression, the book argued that aggregate demand determines the overall level of economic activity, and that inadequate demand leads to prolonged involuntary unemployment.

Contents

Keynes introduced concepts that remain central to economics: the consumption function and the multiplier, the marginal efficiency of capital, liquidity preference as the theory of the interest rate, and the role of "animal spirits" in investment decisions. He rejected Say's law and the classical view that markets automatically clear at full employment, arguing instead that economies can settle into underemployment equilibrium.

Impact

The book transformed economic policy: governments in the post-war decades used fiscal policy — public spending and taxation — to manage demand, and the Bretton Woods system bore Keynes's imprint. Its influence waned with the stagflation of the 1970s and the rise of monetarism and new classical macroeconomics, but revived after the 2008 financial crisis. Keynes himself famously wrote in the preface that "the ideas of economists and political philosophers, both when they are right and when they are wrong, are more powerful than is commonly understood."
