Stratified sampling

In statistics, stratified sampling is a method of sampling from a population which can be partitioned into subpopulations, called strata. In stratified sampling, a random sample is drawn independently from each stratum; the sample sizes within strata may be proportional to the size of the stratum in the population, or allocated by another rule such as Neyman allocation.

A population partitioned into strata, with a sample drawn from each.

Advantages

Stratification can improve the representativeness of the sample and reduce sampling error, because within-stratum variance is typically smaller than overall variance. It guarantees that each subgroup of interest is represented, ensures adequate sample sizes for estimating parameters of small subpopulations, and can lower cost when different strata are sampled with different methods or intensities.

Allocation

  • Proportional allocation — the sampling fraction is the same in all strata.
  • Optimal (Neyman) allocation — sample size in a stratum is proportional to the stratum's population size multiplied by its standard deviation, minimising variance for a given total sample size.
  • Disproportionate allocation — oversampling small or rare strata, with results weighted back in analysis.

Comparison with cluster sampling

Stratified sampling contrasts with cluster sampling: in stratified sampling the strata should be internally homogeneous and all strata are sampled; in cluster sampling the clusters should be internally heterogeneous (mini-populations) and only some clusters are sampled.
