Gaelic type

Gaelic type (Irish: Cló Gaelach) is a family of insular typefaces devised for printing Classical Gaelic and the Irish language. It was in use from 1571 through the mid-20th century, and remains in decorative and ceremonial use today. Gaelic typefaces are characterised by letterforms derived from the medieval insular script, including distinctive shapes for d, f, g, r, s, and t, and the use of the Tironian et (⁊) for "and".

History

The first Gaelic type was cut for the first book printed in Irish, John Kearney's Aibidil Gaoidheilge & Caiticiosma (1571), printed in Dublin under the patronage of Elizabeth I — the typeface later known as the "Queen Elizabeth type". In Scotland, Gaelic printing began with the Foirm na n-Urrnuidheadh (1567), using roman type initially, and dedicated Scottish Gaelic types followed. Major Irish founts included the Newman (Dublin), Moxon (for the Congregation of Propaganda Fide in Rome, 1676), Parker, Watts, and the "Petrie" type of the 19th century.

Characteristics

Gaelic type uses the dot above (ponc séimhithe) over a consonant to indicate lenition, instead of the following "h" used in roman type; thus ḃ, ċ, ḋ, ḟ, ġ, ṁ, ṗ, ṡ, and ṫ in Gaelic type correspond to bh, ch, dh, fh, gh, mh, ph, sh, and th in roman orthography. The alphabet traditionally lacks j, k, q, v, w, x, y, and z.

Decline and revival

From the 1930s Irish government printing moved toward roman type for economy and interoperability, and the 1948 spelling reforms and later standardisation completed the transition in official use; the last Irish-published books in Gaelic type largely ceased by the 1960s. Gaelic type persists on signage, pub fronts, monuments, book covers, Gaelic League publications, and in digital fonts, as an emblem of Irish identity.
