Transposition or transposing in music means shifting an entire collection of pitches — a melody, a chord progression, or a whole piece — up or down in pitch by a constant interval, so that the internal relationships between the notes remain unchanged while the passage sounds in a different key.

Reasons for transposing

Music is transposed for many practical reasons. A singer may need a song moved into a comfortable vocal range; an accompanist may transpose at sight to suit a soloist. Many instruments are transposing instruments — for example, a B♭ clarinet sounds a major second lower than written — so parts are written transposed so the player reads in a convenient key. Transposition is also used pedagogically and analytically to compare passages in different keys, and by performers on instruments such as the guitar, where a capo effectively transposes open-string patterns.

Chromatic and diatonic transposition

In chromatic transposition, every note is shifted by exactly the same number of semitones, preserving the exact interval qualities of the original. In diatonic (or scalar) transposition, notes are shifted by a fixed number of scale degrees within a key, so interval sizes may change slightly (a major third may become a minor third, and so on). Chromatic transposition is the norm for changing key; diatonic transposition is common in folk music and in sequenced patterns within a single key.

Transposition and pitch-class sets

In musical set theory, transposition is one of the basic operations on pitch-class sets, along with inversion. Transposing a set by n semitones adds n (mod 12) to every pitch class. Sets related by transposition are considered members of the same set class and share the same interval content.
