Sanskritisation

Sanskritisation (or Sanskritization) is a term in sociology and anthropology, coined by the Indian sociologist M. N. Srinivas, for the process by which "low" caste or tribal groups in India seek upward social mobility by emulating the rituals, practices, ideology and way of life of higher, traditionally "twice-born" (dvija) castes — especially Brahmins.

Srinivas introduced the concept in his study of the Coorgs of Karnataka, Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India (1952), and developed it in later works. He initially used the term "Brahminisation" but broadened it to Sanskritisation because the model imitated might be a Kshatriya, Vaishya or other locally dominant caste rather than Brahmins specifically.

Process

A group undergoing Sanskritisation typically adopts, over a generation or two, practices associated with higher status: vegetarianism and teetotalism, wearing the sacred thread, employing Brahmin priests for life-cycle rituals, building temples, changing its name and claiming a higher varna origin — for example, descent from a Kshatriya lineage or a mythological hero. Customs considered "polluting" or low, such as widow remarriage bans being lifted or animal sacrifice and alcohol use, are correspondingly abandoned.

Srinivas stressed that Sanskritisation usually accompanies an improvement in a group's economic or political position: the claim to higher ritual status tends to follow, rather than precede, the acquisition of land, wealth or political power. It is also a positional change within the caste system rather than a structural change of the system itself — a group rises relative to its neighbours while the hierarchy remains.

Examples and related concepts

Historians have applied the idea widely: Rajputisation describes tribal chiefs claiming Kshatriya (Rajput) status; similar processes have been traced in medieval South India, Nepal and among tribal communities of central India. Srinivas also developed the related idea of the dominant caste — a caste that wields decisive economic and political power in a locality regardless of its ritual rank — which often serves as the local reference model for Sanskritisation.

Criticism

Critics note that the concept can understate conflict and alternative movements: many groups have instead asserted pride in non-Sanskritic identities, through bhakti traditions, conversion to Buddhism, Islam or Christianity, or anti-caste movements such as those associated with B. R. Ambedkar, who rejected Sanskritisation in favour of political organisation and religious conversion. Scholars such as J. F. Staal questioned the coherence of the category, and others point out that Sanskritisation does not capture material changes brought by markets and the state. Nonetheless the term remains a standard tool in the study of Indian society.
