The electrical conduction system of the heart is a network of specialised cardiac muscle cells that generates and transmits the electrical impulses responsible for the coordinated contraction of the heart muscle. Unlike ordinary cardiac muscle, cells of the conduction system can depolarise spontaneously, giving the heart its intrinsic rhythm.

Pathway of an impulse

The heartbeat begins in the sinoatrial (SA) node, a cluster of pacemaker cells in the wall of the right atrium near the opening of the superior vena cava. The impulse spreads across both atria, causing them to contract, and reaches the atrioventricular (AV) node in the interatrial septum. There it is delayed by about a tenth of a second, allowing the ventricles to fill. The signal then travels rapidly down the bundle of His, divides into right and left bundle branches along the interventricular septum, and reaches the Purkinje fibres, which distribute it throughout the ventricular myocardium so the ventricles contract from the apex upward, ejecting blood efficiently.

Pacemaker hierarchy

If the SA node fails, subsidiary pacemakers take over at slower intrinsic rates: the AV junction fires at roughly 40–60 per minute and ventricular tissue at 20–40 per minute. The autonomic nervous system modulates the SA node's rate — sympathetic activity speeds it, the vagus nerve slows it — while hormones such as adrenaline also raise heart rate.

Disorders and treatment

Conduction abnormalities include heart block (delayed or interrupted AV conduction), bundle branch blocks, and rhythm disorders such as atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia arising from abnormal impulse formation or re-entry circuits. The electrocardiogram records the system's activity as the P wave (atrial depolarisation), QRS complex (ventricular depolarisation) and T wave (ventricular repolarisation). Artificial pacemakers and implantable defibrillators substitute for or correct a failing conduction system.
