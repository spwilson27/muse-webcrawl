The Diocletianic or Great Persecution was the last and most severe persecution of Christians in the Roman Empire. Beginning on 23 February 303 AD, the emperors Diocletian, Maximian, Galerius and Constantius issued a series of edicts rescinding Christians' legal rights, ordering the destruction of churches and scriptures, and eventually requiring all inhabitants of the empire to sacrifice to the traditional gods on pain of imprisonment or death. Enforcement varied greatly by region and lasted until 311 in the West and 313 in parts of the East.

Background

For most of the third century, Christianity had grown under conditions of de facto toleration after the brief persecutions of Decius (250) and Valerian (257–258); Gallienus had restored church property in 260. Diocletian's reforms aimed at restoring imperial unity and traditional Roman religion. Ancient sources, especially Lactantius and Eusebius, attribute the turn to persecution to the Caesar Galerius, who pressed a reluctant Diocletian after incidents such as Christian soldiers disrupting sacrifices and a fire in the imperial palace at Nicomedia blamed on Christians.

The edicts

The first edict (February 303) ordered churches razed, scriptures burned, property confiscated, and Christians dismissed from imperial service and stripped of legal protections. A second edict ordered the arrest of clergy; a third offered imprisoned clergy release if they sacrificed. A fourth edict, issued in 304 under Galerius and Maximian, extended the requirement to sacrifice to the entire population. Those who refused — the martyrs commemorated in later Christian tradition — faced torture, exile to mines, or execution. Among the most famous martyrs associated with the persecution are Saint George, Saint Sebastian, Saint Agnes and Saint Lucy, though their acts were recorded much later.

Regional variation and end

Enforcement was most rigorous in the East under Galerius and later Maximinus Daia, milder in Gaul and Britain under Constantius Chlorus, who largely confined himself to demolishing churches. After Diocletian's abdication in 305, the persecution continued in the East even as it waned in the West. On his deathbed, Galerius issued the Edict of Serdica (311), granting Christians toleration. The Edict of Milan (313), agreed by Constantine and Licinius, legalised Christianity throughout the empire, and Constantine's later patronage ended the era of persecution permanently. Modern historians debate the number of victims; contemporary accounts are rhetorically inflated, but thousands likely died.
