Vacuous truth

In mathematics and logic, a vacuous truth is a conditional or universal statement (a universal statement that can be converted to a conditional statement) that is true because the antecedent cannot be satisfied. It is sometimes said that a statement is vacuously true because it does not really say anything.

Examples

The statement "all cell phones in the room are turned off" is vacuously true if there are no cell phones in the room. In material implication, a conditional with a false antecedent is always true: "if I am in France, then the Moon is made of cheese" is true for anyone not in France. Similarly, in set theory, every element of the empty set has every property.

Scope

Vacuous truths arise in the formal treatment of quantifiers and conditionals, and are important in mathematical proofs, where a claim about all members of an empty set may hold the base case of an induction or simplify definitions.
