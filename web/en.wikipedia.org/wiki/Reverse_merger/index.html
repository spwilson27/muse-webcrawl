Reverse merger

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A reverse merger or reverse takeover (RTO) is the acquisition of a public company by a private company, usually to bypass the lengthy and complex process of going public. The transaction typically requires reorganization of capitalization of the acquiring company. In the most common form, the private company merges into a publicly traded shell corporation with no assets or liabilities; the shareholders of the private company end up controlling the combined entity, which then operates the private company's business under public listing.

Process

In a typical reverse merger, the shell company issues a large majority of its shares to the owners of the private company in exchange for their shares; the private company's managers and board take control, the company is usually renamed, and its operations continue as a public company. On completion the shell and the private company are generally considered a single company. The process can be completed in weeks rather than the many months required for a conventional initial public offering (IPO), and does not depend on favourable market windows.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include speed, lower cost, and the absence of the underwriting process and roadshow; a private company may also gain liquidity for its shareholders and access to public capital markets for later financing. However, a reverse merger by itself raises no capital (unlike an IPO), the company inherits whatever liabilities and shareholder base the shell carries, and its shares often trade thinly on over-the-counter markets until it qualifies for a national exchange. Public-company reporting, audit, and governance costs apply from the outset.

A related structure, the special-purpose acquisition company (SPAC) merger, similarly takes a private company public via a listed shell, but the SPAC raises its capital through its own IPO beforehand.

Regulation and criticism

Regulators have scrutinized reverse mergers, particularly backdoor listings of foreign companies — notably a wave of Chinese companies that listed in the United States via reverse mergers around 2007–2011, some of which were later accused of accounting fraud. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission has issued investor warnings about reverse-merger companies, and exchanges adopted rules requiring merged companies to trade for a "seasoning" period and meet additional requirements before listing on Nasdaq or the NYSE.

Examples

Well-known companies that became public through reverse mergers include Berkshire Hathaway (formed when Warren Buffett's partnership took control of a listed textile firm), Burger King, Blockbuster, Waste Management, and the New York Stock Exchange itself, which merged with the electronic trading platform Archipelago in 2006.
