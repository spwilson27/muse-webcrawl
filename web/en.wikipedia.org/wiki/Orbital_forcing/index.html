Orbital forcing

Orbital forcing is the effect on climate of slow changes in the tilt of the Earth's axis and in the shape of the Earth's orbit around the Sun. These orbital changes alter the total amount and the seasonal and latitudinal distribution of sunlight (insolation) reaching the Earth's surface, and thereby drive long-term climate patterns such as the timing of the ice ages.

Mechanism

Three main variations, collectively called Milankovitch cycles, are involved. Eccentricity changes the orbit's shape from nearly circular to slightly elliptical over cycles of about 100,000 and 405,000 years. Obliquity, the tilt of the axis relative to the orbital plane, varies between about 22.1° and 24.5° over about 41,000 years, changing the contrast between seasons. Axial precession, the wobble of the axis, together with the rotation of the orbit itself, determines at which season the Earth is closest to the Sun, with periods around 19,000–23,000 years. Although the change in global annual insolation is small, the redistribution — particularly summer insolation at high northern latitudes, where the great ice sheets formed — can be substantial.

Evidence

The theory was developed by Serbian geophysicist Milutin Milanković in the early 20th century and was confirmed in the 1970s when deep-sea sediment cores (notably the 1976 analysis by Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton) showed climate cycles matching the orbital periods. Spectral peaks at 100,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years dominate the glacial record of the later Pleistocene; the dominance of the 100,000-year cycle, despite eccentricity's weak direct effect, is known as the "100,000-year problem". Orbital forcing also paces monsoon strength, recorded in cave speleothems and lake sediments, and it underlies the astronomical tuning of geological timescales.
