EcoRI (pronounced "eco R one") is a restriction endonuclease enzyme isolated from the bacterium Escherichia coli strain RY13. It is one of the best-known and historically most important restriction enzymes: named for its source — E. coli, R (strain) I (first identified) — it became a foundational tool of recombinant DNA technology and molecular cloning in the 1970s.

Recognition and cleavage

EcoRI recognises the palindromic DNA sequence 5′-GAATTC-3′ and cuts both strands between the G and the A, producing fragments with single-stranded four-base "sticky ends":

5′---G    AATTC---3′
3′---CTTAA    G---5′

Because every EcoRI fragment carries the same AATT overhang, fragments from different DNA molecules can anneal to one another and be joined by DNA ligase — the basis of gene cloning. The enzyme functions as a homodimer and requires Mg2+ for cleavage. Under non-optimal conditions (such as high glycerol, low salt, or excess enzyme) it can exhibit relaxed "star activity," cutting at similar but non-canonical sites.

Role in the host and in research

In the bacterium, EcoRI forms part of a restriction–modification system with the methylase MEcoRI, which methylates the host's own GAATTC sites to protect them while unmethylated foreign DNA — for example from bacteriophages — is cleaved. EcoRI was among the first restriction enzymes to have its structure solved bound to DNA, making it a model for understanding protein–DNA recognition.
