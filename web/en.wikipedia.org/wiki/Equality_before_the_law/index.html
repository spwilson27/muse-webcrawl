Equality before the law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Equality before the law, also known as equality under the law or legal egalitarianism, is the principle that all people must be equally protected by and accountable to the law, regardless of birth, wealth, office or identity. It is one of A. V. Dicey's three meanings of the Rule of law (1885): every person, official or private, is subject to the ordinary law administered by the ordinary courts.

The principle is proclaimed in Article 7 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and Article 26 of the ICCPR, and constitutionalised in equal-protection clauses — most famously the Fourteenth Amendment's guarantee (1868), the vehicle for American jurisprudence from Brown v. Board (1954) onward. It is a formal principle: equal application of the law, compatible in itself with unequal laws' content and unequal resources to use the law — Anatole France's irony about the law forbidding rich and poor alike to sleep under bridges. Substantive equality doctrines (anti-discrimination law, affirmative action, disparate-impact liability) attempt to close the gap between formal equality and unequal outcomes, and their proper extent is a standing political argument.
