Aquatic plants, also called hydrophytes, are vascular plants that have adapted to living in aquatic environments, in either fresh water or salt water. They grow in or near water and may be emergent, submergent, or floating. Aquatic plants are distributed across many families and are thought to have evolved from terrestrial ancestors on numerous independent occasions; fully marine flowering plants, the seagrasses, are comparatively few in number.

Types

Botanists commonly group aquatic plants by growth habit. Emergent plants, such as cattails (Typha) and bulrushes, root in submerged sediment but extend most of their stems and leaves above the surface. Floating-leaved plants, such as water lilies (Nymphaea), root in the sediment and bear leaves that float on the surface. Free-floating plants, including duckweeds (Lemna), water hyacinth, and water lettuce, drift unattached, absorbing nutrients directly from the water. Submerged plants, such as pondweeds (Potamogeton) and eelgrass, grow entirely beneath the surface, though their flowers may reach the air for pollination.

Adaptations

Aquatic plants display characteristic adaptations to their environment. Many have aerenchyma, spongy tissue with large air spaces that supplies oxygen to submerged roots and provides buoyancy. Leaves of submerged species are often thin and finely divided, reducing drag and increasing surface area for absorbing dissolved gases and minerals directly from the water, while stomata may be absent or non-functional on submerged surfaces. Floating leaves, by contrast, bear stomata on their upper surface only and have waxy coatings that shed water.

Ecology

Aquatic plants are foundational to freshwater and coastal ecosystems. They oxygenate the water, stabilise sediments, shelter fish and invertebrates, and provide food for waterfowl. Some species, removed from their native ranges, become aggressive invaders: water hyacinth, for example, can blanket lakes and channels, blocking navigation and depleting oxygen. Conversely, aquatic plants are used in constructed wetlands and phytoremediation to strip excess nutrients and pollutants from wastewater.

Human uses

People cultivate aquatic plants for food — rice, though usually grown in flooded paddies rather than truly aquatic conditions, as well as water chestnut, lotus, and taro — and extensively as ornamentals in aquaria and garden ponds. Algae and seaweeds, though often called aquatic plants in everyday speech, are not true vascular plants and are treated separately.
