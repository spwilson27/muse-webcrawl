Statistical hypothesis testing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A distribution curve with a shaded rejection region in the tail]

Statistical hypothesis testing is a method of statistical inference by which a hypothesis about a population is assessed using sample data. The analyst states a null hypothesis (H₀) and an alternative, computes a test statistic, and asks how surprising the data would be if H₀ were true.

The framework combines ideas of R. A. Fisher (significance testing and the p-value, 1920s) and of Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson (alternative hypotheses, errors, and power, 1930s) — a hybrid that remains standard practice, and a focus of modern criticism.

Errors and significance

A Type I error rejects a true null (probability α, the significance level, often 0.05); a Type II error fails to reject a false null (probability β); power is 1 − β. The p-value is the probability, under H₀, of data at least as extreme as observed — not the probability that H₀ is true.

Common tests include the t-test, chi-squared test, ANOVA, and nonparametric alternatives such as the Mann–Whitney U test.

Criticism and reform

Hypothesis testing has been criticised for binary thinking, p-hacking, and publication bias contributing to the replication crisis; the American Statistical Association issued a statement on p-values in 2016. Alternatives emphasise effect sizes, confidence intervals, and Bayesian methods.
