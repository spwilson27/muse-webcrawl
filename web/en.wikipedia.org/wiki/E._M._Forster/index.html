Edward Morgan Forster (1 January 1879 – 7 June 1970) was an English novelist, short story writer, essayist and librettist. Many of his novels examine class difference and hypocrisy, including A Room with a View (1908), Howards End (1910) and A Passage to India (1924), the last generally regarded as his greatest success. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature in 22 separate years.

Early life

Forster was born in Marylebone, London, into a middle-class family of Welsh and Irish ancestry. His father, an architect, died of tuberculosis before Forster's second birthday, and he was raised by his mother and aunts. He inherited a substantial sum from his great-aunt Marianne Thornton, which made his education and travels possible. He attended Tonbridge School and then King's College, Cambridge, where he was elected to the Cambridge Apostles society and formed friendships that shaped his intellectual life.

Career

After Cambridge, Forster travelled in Italy and Greece, experiences that inform his early "Italian" novels, Where Angels Fear to Tread (1905) and A Room with a View (1908). The Longest Journey (1907) and Howards End (1910) followed; the latter, with its famous epigraph "Only connect," explores class relations in Edwardian England and established his reputation.

Forster visited India in 1912–13 and again in 1921, serving as private secretary to the Maharaja of Dewas. From these experiences came A Passage to India (1924), a searching portrayal of relations between Britons and Indians under the Raj, which won the James Tait Black Memorial Prize and is often ranked among the greatest English-language novels of the twentieth century.

After 1924 Forster published no further novels in his lifetime, turning to essays, criticism, broadcasting and the libretto for Benjamin Britten's opera Billy Budd. His novel Maurice, a story of homosexual love written in 1913–14, was published posthumously in 1971.

Death and legacy

Forster spent his later years at King's College, Cambridge, where he was an honorary fellow. He died in Coventry in 1970. His novels were adapted into acclaimed films by Merchant Ivory Productions and others, bringing his work to a wide new audience.
