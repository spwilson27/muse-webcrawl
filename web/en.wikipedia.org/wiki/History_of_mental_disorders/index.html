History of mental disorders

The history of mental disorders traces how societies have understood and treated psychological disturbance, from supernatural and humoral explanations in the ancient world, through the rise of asylums and moral treatment, the development of psychiatry and psychoanalysis, to the modern era of diagnostic manuals, psychopharmacology and community care.

Bethlem Royal Hospital ("Bedlam") in London, founded in the thirteenth century, became notorious in popular culture.

Ancient and medieval views

Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts attributed madness to gods or demons, while Hippocratic writers explained melancholia and mania by imbalances of the four humours — black bile, yellow bile, phlegm and blood. Galen systematised humoral psychiatry for over a millennium. In the medieval period, explanations mixed the natural and the demonic; some sufferers were cared for in monasteries or by families, and a few institutions, such as the bimaristans of the Islamic world, provided hospital care.

The asylum era and moral treatment

From the seventeenth century, European states confined the mad in workhouses and asylums, often in appalling conditions. Reformers such as Philippe Pinel in Paris and William Tuke at the York Retreat (founded 1796) pioneered "moral treatment" — humane, structured care without chains. The nineteenth century saw a vast expansion of county asylums in Britain and state hospitals in America, alongside degeneration theory and early classification efforts.

Psychiatry, psychoanalysis and classification

Emil Kraepelin's distinction between dementia praecox (later schizophrenia) and manic-depressive illness shaped twentieth-century nosology. Sigmund Freud's psychoanalysis dominated much psychotherapy from the 1900s to the 1960s. The American Psychiatric Association published the first Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM) in 1952; DSM-III (1980) introduced operational criteria and a multiaxial system.

Deinstitutionalisation and medication

The introduction of chlorpromazine in the 1950s, followed by antidepressants and lithium, combined with civil-liberties critiques (such as Erving Goffman's Asylums, 1961) to drive deinstitutionalisation: large hospitals closed and care shifted, unevenly, to community services. Contemporary psychiatry integrates biological, psychological and social models, while debates continue over diagnosis, stigma and the medicalisation of distress.
