History of Washington, D.C.

The history of Washington, D.C. begins with the Residence Act of 1790, which created a federal district on the Potomac River, and follows the growth of the United States capital from a planned city to a majority-Black metropolis that still lacks voting representation in Congress.

Founding

The Constitution empowered Congress to govern a federal district "not exceeding ten miles square". The Residence Act of 1790, part of the Compromise of 1790, sited it on the Potomac between Maryland and Virginia. President George Washington chose the location and appointed Pierre Charles L'Enfant, whose 1791 plan laid out grand avenues radiating from the Capitol and the President's House. Benjamin Banneker assisted the survey. The city was named for Washington, the district for Columbia.

Early years and the War of 1812

The federal government moved from Philadelphia in 1800. In August 1814 British troops burned the Capitol, the White House and other public buildings during the War of 1812 — the only time a foreign power has occupied the US capital. In 1846 the portion south of the Potomac (Alexandria County) was retroceded to Virginia.

Civil War and growth

The Civil War transformed Washington into an armed camp and administrative hub; Lincoln's Compensated Emancipation Act freed the district's enslaved people in 1862, nine months before the Emancipation Proclamation. Under Boss Shepherd's Board of Public Works in the 1870s the city was paved, sewered and planted with trees. The McMillan Plan of 1901 restored L'Enfant's vision, creating the National Mall as it is known today.

20th century and home rule

Washington grew with both World Wars and the New Deal. It became a centre of African-American culture and, by 1957, the first major US city with a Black majority. The 1968 riots after Martin Luther King Jr.'s assassination scarred commercial corridors for decades. Congress granted limited home rule in 1973; Marion Barry's long mayoralty mixed political achievement with scandal. Residents gained a non-voting House delegate and (since 1964) presidential electors, but the D.C. statehood movement continues to seek full representation.
