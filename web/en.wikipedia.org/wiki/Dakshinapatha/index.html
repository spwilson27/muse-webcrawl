Dakshinapatha (Sanskrit: दक्षिणापथ, Dakṣiṇāpatha, "the Southern Road") is a historical term used in ancient Indian texts both for a major trade and pilgrimage route leading from northern India into the Deccan and the peninsula, and for the southern region itself — roughly corresponding to the Deccan and South India. It is the counterpart of the Uttarapatha, the great northern route.

The route

The Dakshinapatha began in the middle Ganges valley — with Rajagriha and later Pataliputra as key eastern points — passed through Prayaga and Kaushambi, crossed the Vindhya range near Ujjain in Malwa, and continued south across the Deccan plateau to Pratishthana (Paithan) on the Godavari, and onward to the Tamil country and ports of the far south. Along it moved goods such as gold, gems, muslins and spices, as well as armies, monks and ideas.

Historical significance

The route is mentioned in Buddhist texts, the Ramayana and Mahabharata, and in Kautilya's Arthashastra, which judged the southern route especially profitable for trade in precious commodities. Traditions credit the sage Agastya with first carrying Vedic culture south across the Vindhyas. In later centuries the term "Dakshinapatha" came to denote the Deccan region generally, and rulers such as the Satavahanas styled themselves "Lord of the Dakshinapatha" (Dakshinapathapati).
