The Emirate of Córdoba (Arabic: Imārat Qurṭubah) was an independent Islamic emirate in the Iberian Peninsula (al-Andalus) ruled by the Umayyad dynasty from 756 to 929, with its capital at Córdoba. It was founded by Abd al-Rahman I, a young Umayyad prince who escaped the Abbasid overthrow of his family in Damascus and seized power in Córdoba, making al-Andalus politically independent of the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad.

History

Abd al-Rahman I (r. 756–788) consolidated the emirate against internal revolts, Berber unrest and the Christian kingdoms of the north, and began the Great Mosque of Córdoba in 785–786 on the site of a Visigothic church. His successors — including Hisham I, al-Hakam I and Abd al-Rahman II — organised a centralised court and administration modelled partly on Damascus, while campaigning against Asturias and the Franks and facing down internal opposition such as the revolt of the Córdoba suburb (818) and the long rebellion of Umar ibn Hafsun. Abd al-Rahman III (r. 912–961) restored the emirate's authority after decades of fragmentation, and in 929 proclaimed himself caliph, transforming the emirate into the Caliphate of Córdoba.

Society and culture

The emirate ruled a religiously plural society of Muslims, Christians (Mozarabs) and Jews, with Arabic spreading as the language of administration and high culture. Córdoba grew into one of the largest and most splendid cities of medieval Europe, noted for its paved and lit streets, libraries and scholars. Irrigation agriculture, new crops from the east and flourishing crafts underpinned its prosperity.
