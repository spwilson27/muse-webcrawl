Woodturning

Woodturning is the craft of using a wood lathe with hand-held tools to cut a shape that is symmetrical around the axis of rotation. Like the potter's wheel, the wood lathe is a mechanism that can generate a variety of forms. The operator, known as a turner, is usually trained in the use of a range of hand tools — gouges, chisels, parting tools and scrapers — presented to the rotating workpiece to shape it. In modern practice, woodturning is pursued both as an industrial process (for products such as balusters, furniture legs, and baseball bats) and as a studio craft and hobby.

Most suitable lathe work is either spindle turning, in which the grain of the wood runs parallel to the lathe axis — as in table legs and newel posts — or faceplate turning (also called face work), in which the grain runs perpendicular to the axis — as in bowls, platters, and hollow vessels. Much contemporary craft turning blurs this distinction, and ornamental turning lathes add rose-engine patterning to the repertoire.

History

Turning is an ancient technology. The earliest evidence is often taken to be turned wooden objects from Etruscan tombs of the 8th century BC, and the pole lathe — in which a treadle and a springy pole rotate the work back and forth — was known in ancient Egypt and remained in use by rural bodgers in England into the 20th century. The introduction of continuous rotation, first by crank and flywheel and later by water, steam, and electric power, greatly increased the craft's productivity. The lathe was the first machine tool to be adapted to early industrial manufacture, and turned components became standard elements of furniture and architecture.

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, turning was largely an industrial trade and a school workshop subject. The later 20th century saw a revival of studio woodturning, particularly in Britain, North America, Australia, and New Zealand, with turners such as David Ellsworth, Richard Raffan, and Rude Osolnik developing new forms — notably thin-walled vessels and natural-edged bowls — and organisations such as the American Association of Woodturners (founded 1986) supporting a large amateur community.

Equipment

The lathe

A wood lathe consists of a bed, a headstock containing the powered spindle, a tailstock that supports the other end of long work, and a tool rest that supports the tool close to the work. Work is held by a chuck, a faceplate, a drive centre, or between centres. Modern lathes commonly have variable-speed motors; mini- and midi-lathes serve small work, while large floor-standing machines can turn bowls over 500 mm in diameter.

Tools

  • Roughing gouge — a deep-fluted gouge for reducing square stock to a cylinder.
  • Spindle gouge — a shallower gouge for coves and details in spindle work.
  • Bowl gouge — a deep-fluted gouge, often with a swept-back ("fingernail" or Irish) grind, for bowls and vessels.
  • Skew chisel — for planing cuts, beads, and V-grooves on spindles.
  • Parting tool — a narrow chisel for cutting grooves and separating finished work.
  • Scrapers — used with a burr edge, mainly for refining surfaces and interiors.

Tools are traditionally of carbon steel or high-speed steel, kept sharp on a bench grinder or honing system; sharpness is essential both to cut quality and to safety.

Techniques

In spindle turning, basic forms — tapers, beads, coves, and fillets — are combined into designs such as chair legs and stair balusters, often copied from a pattern or story stick. In faceplate work, a bowl blank is mounted on a faceplate or in a chuck, shaped first on the outside and then hollowed; turners talk of the "three cuts" of bowl making and of riding the bevel of the gouge to obtain a clean, controlled cut. Sanding is done on the lathe at successive grits, followed by finishes such as oils, waxes, lacquers, or friction polishes.

Safety considerations include eye and respiratory protection, securing loose clothing and hair, checking the work for cracks or defects, and standing out of the line of fire when starting the lathe, since a workpiece that leaves the lathe is the craft's principal hazard.

Related crafts

Woodturning is related to wood carving and woodworking generally, though it is unusual in that the tool moves against a rotating workpiece rather than the work being held still. Ornamental turning, practised on rose-engine lathes such as the Holtzapffel lathe, produces complex geometric patterns historically applied to ivory, precious woods, and later plastics.
