Goryeo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Goryeo celadon vase with inlaid crane and cloud design]

Goryeo (918–1392) was a Korean kingdom founded by Wang Geon (King Taejo), who reunified the peninsula after the Later Three Kingdoms period and gave Korea its modern name (from Goguryeo, whose successor Goryeo claimed to be). From its capital Gaegyeong (modern Kaesong) it ruled for nearly five centuries: a Buddhist state of celebrated culture — celadon pottery, the Tripitaka Koreana woodblocks, the world's first metal movable type (predating Gutenberg) — that endured Khitan and Jurchen invasions, four decades of Mongol domination, and military rule, before Yi Seong-gye founded Joseon in 1392.

Buddhist kingdom

Wang Geon unified Silla and the Later Baekje by 936, ruling through marriage alliances and magnanimity to former enemies. Goryeo society fused aristocratic clans, a Chinese-style bureaucracy (with civil examinations from 958) and fervent Buddhism: the state carved the entire Buddhist canon onto 81,258 woodblocks (the Tripitaka Koreana, still at Haeinsa temple) as an act of national protection, twice — the first set was burned by the Mongols. Goryeo celadon, inlaid (sanggam) with cranes and clouds, was prized as the finest pottery in East Asia; the metal type used for the Jikji (1377) is the oldest extant book printed with movable metal type.

Mongol century and fall

Khitan Liao invasions (993–1019) were repelled by diplomacy and by General Gang Gam-chan's victory at Kuju. The military coup of 1170 made generals the real rulers for a century; the Mongol invasions (1231–1259) drove the court to Ganghwa Island and ended with Goryeo as the Yuan dynasty's 'son-in-law state', its kings marrying Mongol princesses. As Yuan power waned, King Gongmin's reforms failed amid faction and Japanese pirate (wakō) raids; the general Yi Seong-gye, ordered to invade Ming China in 1388, instead turned his army back at Wihwa Island, seized power, and in 1392 founded Joseon — ending Goryeo after 474 years.
