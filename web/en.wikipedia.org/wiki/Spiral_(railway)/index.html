Spiral (railway)

A spiral (sometimes called a spiral loop) is a technique employed by railways to ascend steep hills, in which the track runs in a continuous loop, passing over itself as it gains height, so that a train can climb a gradient it could not manage on a straight alignment within the available horizontal distance.

Description

The spiral allows a railway to gain elevation while keeping the ruling gradient within the limits of adhesion locomotives. A train travelling around the loop covers a much greater distance than the direct horizontal distance, and so climbs at a gentler effective slope. Spirals are often combined with tunnels, in which case the loop may be partly or wholly enclosed within the mountain.

Examples

Famous examples include the spiral tunnels of the Canadian Pacific Railway at Kicking Horse Pass in British Columbia, the Tehachapi Loop in California, and the spirals on the Darjeeling Himalayan Railway in India. The Brusio spiral viaduct on the Bernina line in Switzerland carries the track in an open circular viaduct. Mountain railways in the Alps, such as the Gotthard railway, make extensive use of spiral tunnels.

Related techniques

Where terrain does not permit a spiral, railways may use a series of switchbacks (zig zags), in which trains reverse direction repeatedly to gain height, or a rack railway, in which a toothed rail provides traction on steep grades. Spirals avoid the delays of reversing but require more elaborate civil engineering.
