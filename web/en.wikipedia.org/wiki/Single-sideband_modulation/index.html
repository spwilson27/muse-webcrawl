Single-sideband modulation

Single-sideband modulation (SSB or SSB-AM) is a type of modulation used to transmit information, such as an audio signal, by radio waves. A refinement of amplitude modulation, it uses transmitter power and bandwidth more efficiently. Amplitude modulation produces an output signal whose bandwidth is twice the maximum frequency of the original baseband signal. Single-sideband modulation avoids this bandwidth increase, and the power wasted on a carrier, at the cost of increased device complexity and more difficult tuning at the receiver.

History

Single-sideband was first patented in the United States on 1 December 1915 by John Renshaw Carson of AT&T, though the patent was not granted until 1923. The first publicized transmission used long-wave transatlantic radiotelephone service between New York and London in 1927. Amateur radio operators began serious experimentation with SSB after World War II, and it was adopted as a military standard by the United States Air Force Strategic Air Command in the 1950s. By the 1960s it had become the dominant voice mode for long-distance high-frequency (HF) communications because it offered roughly twice the effective range of conventional AM at the same transmitter power.

Concept

An ordinary amplitude-modulated signal consists of a carrier wave and two identical mirror-image sidebands, each carrying the same information. The carrier itself conveys no information but may account for two-thirds of the total transmitted power. Single-sideband modulation suppresses the carrier (usually to a very low level) and eliminates one of the two sidebands, transmitting only the upper sideband (USB) or the lower sideband (LSB). The signal therefore occupies only about half the bandwidth of a double-sideband signal and no power is wasted on the redundant components.

Generation

SSB can be generated by the filter method, in which a balanced modulator produces a double-sideband suppressed-carrier signal and a sharp band-pass filter (often a crystal or mechanical filter) removes the unwanted sideband; or by the phasing method, based on the Hartley modulator, in which the baseband signal and its Hilbert transform modulate two carriers in quadrature. In practice, precise filtering at an intermediate frequency became the standard approach in commercial equipment. Related techniques include independent sideband (ISB), in which the two sidebands carry different information, and vestigial sideband (VSB), used in analogue television broadcasting.

Demodulation

To recover the signal, the receiver must reinsert a locally generated carrier at exactly the original frequency, using a beat-frequency oscillator (BFO) or product detector. Even small frequency errors cause the pitch of recovered speech to shift, which is why SSB receivers require fine tuning, though speech remains intelligible with errors of a few tens of hertz.

Uses

SSB is widely used on the HF bands by amateur radio operators, in maritime and aeronautical mobile services, and by military and diplomatic services. By convention, USB is used on frequencies above 10 MHz and LSB below, in the amateur service. Because of its efficiency in both bandwidth and power, SSB long dominated long-distance voice radiotelephony.
