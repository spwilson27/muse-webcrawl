The Ohio Gang was a nickname for the group of politicians and industry leaders who came to wield great influence within the administration of U.S. President Warren G. Harding (1921–1923). Many of them were Harding's friends and political allies from his home state of Ohio, and several became embroiled in the scandals — above all Teapot Dome — that permanently damaged the administration's reputation.

Members and scandals

Central figures included Attorney General Harry M. Daugherty, Harding's campaign manager; Secretary of the Interior Albert B. Fall, who secretly leased naval petroleum reserves at Teapot Dome, Wyoming, and Elk Hills, California, to oil companies in return for bribes, becoming the first U.S. cabinet officer convicted of a felony committed in office; Charles R. Forbes, director of the Veterans' Bureau, convicted of massive fraud in hospital construction and supply contracts; and Jess Smith, an unofficial fixer in the Justice Department who died by suicide in 1923 as investigations closed in. Newspaper publisher Edward B. McLean and others moved in the group's social circle, which was associated with poker games and bootleg liquor at the "Little Green House on K Street" in Washington.

Legacy

Harding died in office in August 1923, before the worst revelations emerged. Successive congressional investigations and trials through the 1920s made "Ohio Gang" a byword for cronyism and graft in American politics, overshadowing Harding's policy record and shaping his low standing in historians' rankings of presidents.
