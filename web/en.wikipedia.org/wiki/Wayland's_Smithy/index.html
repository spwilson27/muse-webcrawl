Wayland's Smithy is an early Neolithic chambered long barrow located near the village of Ashbury in the southwestern part of Oxfordshire, England, close to the Ridgeway long-distance path and about 1.2 miles west of the Uffington White Horse. The monument consists of an earthen mound some 56 metres long containing a stone burial chamber, and it is one of the best-known prehistoric sites in southern England.

Archaeology

Excavations in 1962–63 revealed that the site was built in two phases. The first, around 3600 BC, was a small oval barrow with a wooden mortuary chamber containing the remains of at least 14 individuals. This was covered around 100 years later by a much larger trapezoidal long barrow with a sarsen stone chamber and a monumental entrance facade of large upright stones. The second chamber held the disarticulated remains of at least eight people.

Folklore

The name associates the barrow with Wayland the Smith, the legendary master craftsman of Germanic mythology. Local folklore recorded as early as the 18th century held that a traveller whose horse had lost a shoe could leave the animal and a silver coin at the smithy and return later to find the horse shod and the coin gone. The site appears in literature and legend as a place of supernatural smithcraft, and the name "Wayland's Smithy" is attested in a charter of 955 AD — thousands of years after the barrow was built, showing how the monument was reinterpreted by Anglo-Saxon settlers.
