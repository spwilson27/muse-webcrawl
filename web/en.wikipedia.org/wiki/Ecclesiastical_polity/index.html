Ecclesiastical polity

Ecclesiastical polity
Principal formsEpiscopal, presbyterian, congregational
Concerned withChurch governance, ministry, authority
Related fieldEcclesiology

Ecclesiastical polity (or church polity) is the system of government and organisational structure of a Christian church — its ministry, offices, and the way authority is distributed and exercised. Different traditions ground their polity in differing readings of Scripture and church history, and polity has been a major source of division and debate among denominations since the Reformation.

Principal forms

Episcopal polity

In episcopal polity, authority is exercised by bishops (from Greek episkopos, "overseer") in a hierarchical structure. Bishops ordain and govern clergy within dioceses, and churches such as the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and many Lutheran and Methodist bodies trace episcopal authority through apostolic succession. Variations range from the papal monarchy of Roman Catholicism to the conciliar episcopacy of Orthodoxy.

Presbyterian polity

Presbyterian polity is government by assemblies of elders (presbyters). Local congregations are governed by a session of elders, which sends representatives to regional presbyteries and to a general assembly. It is characteristic of Reformed and Presbyterian churches, which argue that the New Testament uses "bishop" and "elder" for the same office.

Congregational polity

In congregational polity, each local congregation is autonomous and self-governing under Christ, typically making decisions democratically and calling its own ministers. Baptists, Congregationalists, Disciples of Christ, and many independent and non-denominational churches follow this pattern, often while cooperating in voluntary associations.

Other arrangements

Some bodies mix forms — Methodism's "connexional" system combines bishops with conferences — while others, such as the Quakers, have historically dispensed with ordained clergy altogether. Polity also covers matters such as the ownership of church property, the discipline of members and clergy, and the relation of church to state.
