The Battle of Pavia, fought on the morning of 24 February 1525 — the feast of St Matthias and the birthday of the Emperor Charles V — was the decisive battle of the Italian War of 1521–1526. A Spanish-Imperial army relieved the besieged city of Pavia and annihilated the army of King Francis I, who was captured on the field: "All is lost save honour," he wrote to his mother. The battle is a landmark in military history — the first great victory won primarily by handheld firearms — and it established Spanish Habsburg dominance in Italy for a generation.

Background

Francis I crossed the Alps in October 1524, retook Milan and laid siege to Pavia, defended by Antonio de Leyva. Through the winter the French siege faltered with disease and desertion, while an Imperial relief army under the Viceroy of Naples, Charles de Lannoy, gathered at Lodi with German landsknechts under Georg von Frundsberg, financed by the Fugger bankers. On the night of 23–24 February the Imperials breached the wall of the great Visconti hunting park north of Pavia and entered the French camp area to force a battle.

The battle

At dawn, in fog, the Imperial vanguard seized the castle of Mirabello. Francis, eager for a chivalric triumph, charged with his heavy cavalry — masking his own artillery in the process — and initially scattered the Imperial horse. But Spanish arquebusiers under the Marquis of Pescara, shooting from cover in squads, shot down the French gendarmes and their horses; Imperial landsknechts destroyed the Swiss pike squares in French service; and the Pavia garrison sallied into the French rear. The French army disintegrated. Francis, unhorsed and wounded, was captured by Imperial soldiers — disputing for the honour among themselves — and handed over to Lannoy. The Black Band and much of the French high nobility (the seigneurs de La Palice, Bonnivet, and the exiled Richard de la Pole among them) were killed.

Aftermath

Francis was taken to Madrid and released only after signing the Treaty of Madrid (1526), renouncing his Italian claims and surrendering his sons as hostages — terms he repudiated on his release, sparking renewed war. The famed series of Pavia tapestries, woven in Brussels from designs by Bernard van Orley, commemorates the victory.
