Functional magnetic resonance imaging

Functional magnetic resonance imaging
PurposeMeasuring brain activity by detecting changes in blood flow
Common contrastBOLD (blood-oxygen-level-dependent)

Functional magnetic resonance imaging or functional MRI (fMRI) measures brain activity by detecting changes associated with blood flow. This technique relies on the fact that cerebral blood flow and neuronal activation are coupled: when an area of the brain is in use, blood flow to that region also increases. fMRI is a form of neuroimaging developed in the early 1990s, principally by Seiji Ogawa and colleagues, building on the phenomenon of blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) contrast.

BOLD signal

The primary form of fMRI uses the BOLD contrast. Oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin have different magnetic properties: deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic and distorts the local magnetic field, reducing the MRI signal. When neurons become active, local blood flow increases more than oxygen consumption, so the proportion of deoxyhaemoglobin falls and the signal rises — typically peaking about 4–6 seconds after the neural event, a lag captured by the haemodynamic response function.

Uses

fMRI is used both in research — to map the brain systems involved in perception, memory, language and emotion, and to study functional connectivity, including in the resting state — and clinically, for presurgical mapping of eloquent cortex and evaluation of some neurological disorders. It offers spatial resolution on the order of millimetres and whole-brain coverage without ionising radiation, but its temporal resolution is limited by the slowness of the haemodynamic response, and its signal is an indirect proxy for neural activity, which has prompted ongoing methodological debate.
