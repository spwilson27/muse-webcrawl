Jean Rouch

Jean Rouch (31 May 1917 – 18 February 2004) was a French filmmaker and anthropologist, a pioneer of visual anthropology, ethnofiction and cinéma vérité. His decades of work in West Africa, including Les maîtres fous (1955), Moi, un noir (1958) and Chronique d'un été (1961, with Edgar Morin), shaped documentary cinema and influenced the French New Wave.

Jean Rouch
Born: 31 May 1917, Paris, France
Died: 18 February 2004, Niger
Occupation: Filmmaker, anthropologist
Known for: Cinéma vérité, ethnofiction

Career

Trained as a civil engineer, Rouch went to Niger in 1941 to supervise construction, witnessed a Songhay possession ritual, and turned to anthropology under Marcel Griaule. After the war he travelled the Niger River by canoe, filming migrations and rituals, and made ethnographic films for the Musée de l'Homme, later founding the Comité du film ethnographique.

Films and method

Les maîtres fous showed Hauka spirit-possession mimicking colonial officials in Accra, provoking debate about colonialism and representation. In Moi, un noir, Nigerian migrants in Abidjan improvised their own stories — Rouch's "ethnofiction," blurring documentary and fiction. Chronique d'un été (1961), shot with lightweight synchronous-sound equipment, coined cinéma vérité (translating Dziga Vertov's kino-pravda) and won at Cannes. Other works include Jaguar, La pyramide humaine, Petit à petit and hundreds of short ethnographic films made with African collaborators such as Damouré Zika.

Legacy

Rouch championed "shared anthropology" — screening footage back to subjects and collaborating with them — and helped found the Bilan du Cinéma Ethnographique (now Jean Rouch International Film Festival). He died in a car accident in Niger, where he had worked for over sixty years. See French New Wave.
