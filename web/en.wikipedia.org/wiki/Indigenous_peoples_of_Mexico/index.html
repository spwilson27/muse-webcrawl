Indigenous peoples of Mexico

The Indigenous peoples of Mexico (Spanish: pueblos indígenas de México) are the communities that descend from the pre-Columbian peoples of Mesoamerica and northern Mexico. Depending on the criterion used — self-identification or language — they number between roughly 17 and 25 million people, around 15–20% of Mexico's population, the largest Indigenous population in the Americas in absolute terms.

Peoples and languages

The National Institute of Indigenous Languages recognises 68 national Indigenous languages in 11 families. The largest groups include the Nahua (descendants of the Aztecs/Mexica and others), Yucatec Maya, Zapotec, Mixtec, Otomí, Totonac, Tzotzil, Tzeltal, Mazahua, Huastec and Purepecha. Indigenous people form majorities or large shares of the population in states such as Oaxaca, Chiapas, Yucatán, Guerrero and Puebla.

History and rights

After the Spanish conquest (1519–1521), Indigenous communities survived under colonial rule and later within the republic, often defending communal lands (ejidos). The Mexican Revolution and later indigenismo policies reshaped state policy; constitutional reforms (2001) recognised Mexico as a pluricultural nation, though implementation is contested. The 1994 Zapatista uprising in Chiapas brought Indigenous rights to national attention, and advocacy continues over land, autonomy, language education and political representation.
