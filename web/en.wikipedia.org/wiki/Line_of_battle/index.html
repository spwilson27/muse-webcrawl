In naval warfare, the line of battle is a tactic in which a naval fleet of ships forms a single file line end to end. First used systematically during the Anglo-Dutch Wars of the mid-17th century, the line allowed every ship to fire its broadside at the enemy without masking the guns of its fellows, while presenting the enemy with no friendly ship in the line of fire. Ships powerful enough to stand in the line were called ships of the line — the origin of the term "line-of-battle ship" and, later, "battleship".

Development

Ad hoc single-file formations appeared in Portuguese, Spanish and Dutch service around 1500–1650, but the formal adoption is usually credited to the English Commonwealth navy in the First Anglo-Dutch War: the Fighting Instructions of 1653 issued by Blake, Deane and Monck prescribed the line ahead. Both the English and Dutch codified the tactic, and for the next 150 years fleet actions in the Channel, Mediterranean and Caribbean typically consisted of two roughly parallel lines exchanging fire, sometimes for hours, often indecisively.

Tactics

The commander sought the weather gage (upwind position) to control the engagement. The ideal was to "double" part of the enemy line or to break through it, concentrating superior force on a portion of the enemy fleet. Rigid adherence to the line caused frustration in Britain during the mid-18th century — contributing to Admiral Byng's execution after Minorca (1756) — and later commanders such as Rodney (the Saintes, 1782), Howe and Nelson sought decisive results by breaking the line deliberately: at Trafalgar (1805) Nelson attacked in two columns perpendicular to the Franco-Spanish line, shattering it at the cost of his own life.

Steam era and end

Steam propulsion, shell guns and iron armour changed everything: after Lissa (1866) ramming and melee briefly came back into fashion, but by the 1880s fleets again fought in line ahead, now of steel battleships with turret guns. The Russo-Japanese War's Battle of Tsushima (1905) and the First World War's Battle of Jutland (1916) — where the Grand Fleet twice "crossed the T" of the High Seas Fleet — were the supreme and essentially last great line-of-battle engagements: aircraft carriers, submarines and air power made the massed gun line obsolete in the Second World War.
