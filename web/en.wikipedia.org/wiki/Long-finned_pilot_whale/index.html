Long-finned pilot whale

The long-finned pilot whale (Globicephala melas) is a large species of oceanic dolphin. It shares the genus Globicephala with the short-finned pilot whale. Despite their name, pilot whales are dolphins, characterised by a bulbous melon forehead, dark body, and — in this species — exceptionally long, sickle-shaped pectoral flippers, which can reach a fifth of the body length.

Description and behaviour

Adults are black or dark grey with a lighter anchor-shaped patch behind the dorsal fin and on the throat. They are highly social, travelling in pods typically of 10–30 individuals organised matrilineally, and feed mainly on squid, octopus, and schooling fish, diving to several hundred metres at night. Their name is thought to derive from the belief that each pod followed a single leader or "pilot".

Strandings

The species is notorious for mass strandings, in which whole pods beach themselves; the strong social bonds that hold pods together may cause healthy animals to follow distressed ones ashore. Large strandings have been recorded in New Zealand, Tasmania, and the North Atlantic coasts.

Human interaction

Long-finned pilot whales have long been hunted in drive fisheries, most famously the grindadráp of the Faroe Islands, where several hundred may be taken in a year, as well as historically in Newfoundland and Cape Cod. Threats also include bycatch, noise pollution, and contaminants, though overall population numbers appear large.
