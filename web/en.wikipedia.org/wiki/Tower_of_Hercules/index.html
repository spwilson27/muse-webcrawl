Tower of Hercules

The Tower of Hercules (Galician: Torre de Hércules) is an ancient Roman lighthouse on a peninsula about 2.4 kilometres from the centre of A Coruña, in Galicia, north-western Spain. Standing 55 metres tall, it is the oldest known extant lighthouse in the world and has been in continuous use since it was built in the 1st or 2nd century AD. In 2009 it was inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List.

The tower was constructed on a rocky headland to guide shipping along the dangerous Galician coast, part of the route between the Mediterranean and northern Europe. It was extensively restored in the late 18th century, when its Roman core was encased in a neoclassical exterior.

History

The lighthouse is thought to have been built under the Roman emperor Trajan, possibly on the site of an earlier Phoenician structure. A carved inscription at its base names the architect Gaius Sevius Lupus, from Aeminia (modern Coimbra) in Lusitania. The tower originally had an external ramp, traces of which survive, by which fuel for the beacon could be carried to the top.

After falling into disuse in the Middle Ages, the tower was restored between 1788 and 1791 under the direction of naval engineer Eustaquio Giannini, who added the present neoclassical facade, a new lantern and the diagonal banding that distinguishes its appearance today.

Legend

According to medieval legend, the hero Hercules fought the giant tyrant Geryon for three days and nights, defeated him, and buried his head and weapons beneath a tower built on the spot; the skull and bones in the city's coat of arms recall the story. Another tradition links the tower to Breogán, mythical king of Galicia in Irish mythology, whose sons spotted Ireland from its top.
