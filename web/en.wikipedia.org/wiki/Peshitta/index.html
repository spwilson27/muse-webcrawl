The Peshitta (Syriac: ܦܫܺܝܛܬܳܐ, pšīṭtā, "the simple version") is the standard version of the Bible for churches in the Syriac tradition. It comprises a translation of the Old Testament from Hebrew (with some books from Greek or Aramaic sources) and a New Testament translated from Greek, and remains the liturgical Bible of the Church of the East, the Syriac Orthodox Church, and other Eastern churches.

Development

The Old Testament Peshitta was translated, probably in Edessa and Adiabene, during the first and second centuries AD, in part by Jewish or Jewish-Christian scholars; the Pentateuch and Psalms show affinities with the Targums. The New Testament canon of the Peshitta — 22 books, lacking 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude, and Revelation — was standardised by the early fifth century, superseding the Diatessaron gospel harmony and the separate Old Syriac gospel versions. Tradition attributes the revision to Bishop Rabbula of Edessa (d. 435).

Significance

Because Syriac is an Aramaic dialect close to the language of Jesus, the Peshitta has attracted claims — strongly contested by most scholars — that it preserves an Aramaic original of the New Testament ("Aramaic primacy"). The Peshitta is a major witness in textual criticism of both Testaments, and its many manuscripts, some from the fifth and sixth centuries, are among the oldest surviving biblical codices. Critical editions have been produced by the Peshitta Institute in Leiden.
