The Temple of Vesta (Latin: Aedes Vestae) is an ancient temple in the Roman Forum in Rome, located near the Regia and the House of the Vestal Virgins. It housed the sacred fire of Vesta, goddess of the hearth, whose flame was tended day and night by the Vestal Virgins and was regarded as essential to the survival of Rome itself. The temple's most prominent surviving feature is its circular plan.

History

Tradition attributed the original temple to Numa Pompilius, the second king of Rome. In its earliest form it was probably a simple round hut of wicker and reed with a thatched roof, recalling the earliest Roman dwellings; even after successive rebuildings in stone and marble, the temple retained a circular plan of about 14.8 metres in diameter, preserving the memory of the primitive hut. The temple burned and was restored many times; the structure whose ruins stand today dates largely to a rebuilding under Julia Domna, wife of the emperor Septimius Severus, around AD 205.

The sacred fire was finally extinguished and the college of Vestals dissolved in AD 394, when Christian emperors suppressed the old state cults. The temple was partially reconstructed in the 1930s from surviving fragments.

Architecture and contents

The temple stood on a high circular podium, ringed by twenty slender Corinthian columns supporting an entablature and a conical roof with an opening for smoke. Inside, the temple held not a cult statue but the undying flame itself, together with sacred objects kept hidden from public view — most famously the Palladium, a small wooden image of Pallas Athena said to have been brought from Troy by Aeneas, whose safety was bound up with Rome's destiny.

The Vestal Virgins

The flame was guarded by the Vestals, priestesses chosen as girls who served for thirty years under a vow of chastity. If the fire went out through their negligence, it was an ill omen punishable by scourging; a Vestal who broke her vow was buried alive. Beside the temple stood their residence, the House of the Vestals, a large complex of rooms around a courtyard of statues honouring chief Vestals.
