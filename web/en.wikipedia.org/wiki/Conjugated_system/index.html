Conjugated system

In organic chemistry, a conjugated system is a system of connected p-orbitals with delocalized electrons in a molecule, generally lowering the overall energy of the molecule and increasing its stability. Conventionally, conjugated systems are represented as alternating single and multiple (usually double) bonds: –C=C–C=C–. The simplest conjugated hydrocarbon is 1,3-butadiene. Lone pairs, radicals, carbenium ions, and atoms with empty p-orbitals can also participate in a conjugated system, as in the allyl cation or conjugated carbonyl compounds (α,β-unsaturated carbonyls).

Delocalization and bonding

In a conjugated chain, adjacent p-orbitals overlap along the whole system, so the π electrons are not confined between pairs of atoms but are delocalized over the entire framework; this is commonly described by resonance structures or molecular orbital theory, in which the difference between alternating "single" and "double" bond lengths largely disappears — in butadiene the central C–C bond is noticeably shorter than a typical C–C single bond. Conjugation gives molecules characteristic chemical and physical properties: extra thermodynamic stability (reflected in lower heats of hydrogenation), distinctive reactivity such as 1,4-addition and the Diels–Alder reaction, and lower-energy electronic transitions.

Colour and applications

Extending conjugation shifts absorption of light to longer wavelengths; sufficiently long conjugated systems absorb visible light and appear coloured. This is the basis of most organic dyes and pigments: azo dyes, the carotenoids (such as β-carotene and lycopene), chlorophyll, and the visual pigment retinal. Aromatic compounds are cyclic conjugated systems with special stability. Conjugated polymers such as polyacetylene, polypyrrole, and PPV are used in organic electronics, including OLEDs, organic photovoltaics, and conductive plastics.
