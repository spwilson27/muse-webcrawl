A public house, more usually known as a pub, is a drinking establishment licensed to serve alcoholic drinks for consumption on the premises. The term "public house" first appeared in the late 17th century, and was used to differentiate private houses from those which were, quite literally, open to the public as "alehouses", "taverns" and "inns". Pubs are a fundamental part of British, Irish, and Commonwealth social culture, traditionally serving draught beer and cider and often providing food, games such as darts, and a focus for the local community.

History

Drinking establishments have existed in Britain since the Roman occupation (when taverns, tabernae, served wine to soldiers and travellers) and alehouses were ubiquitous by the medieval period. The number of pubs and the hours they could open were progressively regulated: the Beerhouse Act 1830 liberalised beer selling, while nineteenth- and twentieth-century licensing laws restricted hours — notably the wartime Defence of the Realm Act restrictions — until liberalisation in 1988 and the Licensing Act 2003.

Types

Pubs may be "tied" to a brewery or pub company, obliged to stock its products, or "free houses" owned independently. Many pubs are divided historically into a public bar (plainer, cheaper) and a lounge or saloon bar. Specialist forms include the gastropub (emphasising food), the micropub (small, cask-ale-focused), and the Irish pub, a style exported worldwide.

Culture

The pub is traditionally the centre of community life in villages and neighbourhoods, each with its own "regulars" and a publican or landlord. Distinctive features include pictorial inn signs (necessary when most customers were illiterate), pub games, quiz nights, and in Britain the institution of "last orders" and closing time. In recent decades pub numbers in the UK have declined steadily under pressure from changing drinking habits, taxation, and property values.
