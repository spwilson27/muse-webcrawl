The County Palatine of Durham was a county palatine in northern England governed for centuries by the Bishop of Durham, who exercised quasi-royal powers within the palatinate. Its origins lie in the liberty of the bishops of Durham, custodians of the shrine of St Cuthbert; because Durham stood on the dangerous border with Scotland, its bishops — later styled "Prince Bishops" — were granted extensive rights to raise armies, levy taxes, mint coins, hold their own courts and parliament, and pardon offences, in return for defending the northern frontier.

The palatinate had its own judicial system, including a Court of Chancery of the County Palatine of Durham and a palatine exchequer, and the bishops appointed the county's sheriff and other officers. Successive monarchs trimmed these powers: Henry VIII curtailed the bishops' rights in the 1530s, and the palatinate's separate representation arrangements persisted until the county was given MPs in 1673.

Abolition

The Durham (County Palatine) Act 1836 transferred the palatine jurisdiction from the bishop to the Crown, though the bishop retained his estates for a time and Durham retained some distinctive institutions. The palatine courts were gradually merged into the general court system: the Court of Chancery of the County Palatine of Durham survived until the Courts Act 1971. Durham remains a ceremonial county, and the historic palatinate identity endures in local institutions such as Durham University, founded in 1832 with the bishop's involvement.
