Maria Fitzherbert

Maria Fitzherbert (1756–1837), portrait by Reynolds

Maria Anne Fitzherbert (née Smythe, previously Weld; 26 July 1756 – 27 March 1837) was a longtime companion — and, in the eyes of the Catholic Church, the wife — of the future King George IV of the United Kingdom. Their secret marriage in 1785 was invalid under English law because it had not received the consent of King George III, and was never publicly acknowledged during the Prince's lifetime.

Early life

Born at Brambridge, Hampshire, into a Catholic gentry family, Maria Smythe was educated at a convent in Paris. She married Edward Weld in 1775, who died months later, and then Thomas Fitzherbert in 1778, who died in 1781, leaving her a wealthy, socially established widow in London society.

Secret marriage

The Prince of Wales fell in love with her in 1784 and pursued her relentlessly, reportedly threatening suicide. A devout Catholic, she refused to become his mistress and fled to the Continent; to resolve the impasse they were married on 15 December 1785 at her house in Park Street, Mayfair, by an Anglican clergyman. The marriage breached the Royal Marriages Act 1772 (no royal consent) and, had it been valid, the Act of Settlement would have barred the prince from the throne for marrying a Catholic. Whig leader Charles James Fox publicly denied the marriage in Parliament at the prince's prompting, deeply wounding her.

Later life

Despite the prince's marriage to Caroline of Brunswick in 1795, George repeatedly returned to Fitzherbert, and they were reconciled until a final separation around 1811; she received a substantial pension. She lived at Steine House in Brighton, was honoured by William IV, who asked her to wear the royal arms, and died in 1837. Papers sealed at her request were published in the 20th century, confirming the marriage ceremony.
