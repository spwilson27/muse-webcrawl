Slow motion

Slow motion (commonly abbreviated as slo-mo or slow-mo) is an effect in film-making whereby time appears to be slowed down. It was invented by the Austrian priest August Musger in the early 20th century. This effect can be achieved through the use of high-speed cameras and then playing the footage produced at a normal speed, or by adding frames or slowing the playback rate in post-production.

Technique

Recording at a higher frame rate than playback — "overcranking", from the era of hand-cranked cameras — is the primary method. Footage shot at 120 frames per second and played at 24 fps appears five times slower. True high-speed photography reaches thousands or millions of frames per second for scientific imaging of impacts, ballistics and fluid behaviour. Modern consumer cameras and phones offer 120–960 fps modes. Where no extra frames exist, software interpolation (optical-flow retiming) can synthesise in-between frames, sometimes with visible artefacts.

Uses

Slow motion is a staple of action cinema (the gunfights of Sam Peckinpah and John Woo, The Matrix's bullet time variant), of sports broadcasting for analysing plays and finishes, and of nature documentary, revealing behaviours too fast for the eye — a hummingbird's wingbeats or a mantis shrimp strike. Psychological research suggests slow-motion replay can bias viewers toward judging actions as more intentional.
