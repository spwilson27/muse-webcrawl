Breastfeeding

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Breastfeeding is the feeding of babies and young children with milk from a woman's breast. Health authorities including the WHO recommend exclusive breastfeeding for about the first six months of life, and continued breastfeeding alongside solid foods for two years or beyond. Human milk changes composition over time — from antibody-rich colostrum in the first days to mature milk — matching the baby's needs.

Benefits and practice

Breast milk provides nutrition, antibodies and other immune factors, and is associated with lower rates of infection, and probably obesity and some chronic diseases, in children; for mothers it reduces risks of breast and ovarian cancer and type 2 diabetes. Successful breastfeeding depends on technique (latch), frequency and support; difficulties such as pain, perceived low supply and returning to work lead many to stop earlier than intended. Where breastfeeding is impossible or chosen against, infant formula is a safe alternative, and expressed or donor milk may be used.

Policy

Global breastfeeding rates fall short of targets (exclusive breastfeeding under six months is around 44–48% worldwide). The International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes (1981) restricts formula advertising, and 'baby-friendly hospital' practices, maternity leave and workplace facilities support breastfeeding.
