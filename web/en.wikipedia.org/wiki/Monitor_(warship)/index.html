Monitor (warship)

Monitor
TypeArmoured warship
OriginUnited States, 1862
CharacteristicsLow freeboard, revolving gun turret, shallow draught
Era1862 – mid-20th century

A monitor is a relatively small warship that is neither fast nor strongly armoured, but carries disproportionately large guns. The type originated with USS Monitor, designed by the Swedish-American engineer John Ericsson and built for the Union Navy during the American Civil War. Monitors were defined by a very low freeboard hull and one or two revolving armoured turrets.

History

On 9 March 1862, USS Monitor fought the Confederate ironclad CSS Virginia at the Battle of Hampton Roads — the first battle between ironclad warships. Although tactically inconclusive, the duel made wooden warships obsolete and made the "monitor" famous worldwide.

The United States built dozens of monitors during the Civil War for coastal and riverine service, including twin-turreted and river monitors. The type's low freeboard made early monitors poor seagoing vessels: USS Monitor herself foundered in a storm off Cape Hatteras on 31 December 1862.

Later development

European navies adopted the monitor for coastal defence, and larger "breastwork monitors" improved seaworthiness. During the First World War, the Royal Navy used monitors extensively for shore bombardment, mounting guns of up to 18 inches. Monitors served again in the Second World War, including HMS Roberts and HMS Erebus, which supported the Normandy landings. The US Navy used armoured riverine monitors ("Zippos" and command craft) during the Vietnam War.

Design characteristics

Typical monitors combined shallow draught for inshore work, heavy guns in rotating turrets, strong armour on turret and waterline, and modest speed and range. They were primarily bombardment and coastal defence vessels rather than fleet units.
