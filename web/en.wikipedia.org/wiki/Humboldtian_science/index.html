Humboldtian science refers to the methodology of precise, quantitative measurement of natural phenomena over large geographical areas, as practised and promoted by the Prussian naturalist Alexander von Humboldt (1769–1859) in the first half of the nineteenth century. The term was coined by historian of science Susan Faye Cannon. Humboldtian science combined accurate instrumentation, global networks of observers, and visual representation of data — such as isotherm maps — with a holistic view of nature as an interconnected whole.

Characteristics

Cannon identified several defining features: a concern with accurate measurement of specific phenomena using the most advanced instruments; a rejection of Baconian fact-gathering in favour of measurement organised by sophisticated concepts; and a search for general laws applicable on a planetary scale. Typical Humboldtian enterprises included worldwide surveys of terrestrial magnetism, the mapping of isothermal lines pioneered in Humboldt's 1817 memoir, meteorological observation networks, and the study of the geographical distribution of plants.

Humboldt's American expedition

Between 1799 and 1804 Humboldt and Aimé Bonpland travelled through Spanish America, collecting tens of thousands of plant specimens and making systematic measurements of altitude, temperature, humidity and magnetic intensity. Humboldt's ascent of Chimborazo in 1802 and his famous cross-section diagram (the Naturgemälde) illustrated how vegetation zones varied with elevation — a foundational image for biogeography and ecology.

Influence

Humboldt's call for coordinated global observation inspired the "Magnetic Crusade" of the 1830s and 1840s, in which observatories were established across the British Empire and beyond, as well as Russian and American measuring networks. His approach influenced figures including Charles Darwin, who carried Humboldt's Personal Narrative on HMS Beagle, and helped lay the institutional groundwork for the modern earth and environmental sciences. His late work Kosmos attempted a unified physical description of the universe.
