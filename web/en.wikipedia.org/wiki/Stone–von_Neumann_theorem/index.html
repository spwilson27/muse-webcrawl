Stone–von Neumann theorem

In mathematics, the Stone–von Neumann theorem, proved by Marshall H. Stone and John von Neumann in the early 1930s, gives a uniqueness result for representations of the canonical commutation relations of quantum mechanics.

Roughly, it states that any two irreducible, strongly continuous unitary representations of the Weyl relations — the exponentiated form of the position and momentum commutation relations — for a system with finitely many degrees of freedom are unitarily equivalent. In other words, the Schrödinger representation, in which position acts by multiplication and momentum acts by differentiation on square-integrable functions, is essentially the unique representation of the canonical commutation relations.

The theorem was of foundational importance for quantum mechanics because it justified the equivalence of the matrix mechanics and wave mechanics formulations. Its uniqueness conclusion fails for systems with infinitely many degrees of freedom, such as quantum fields, where inequivalent representations exist — a fact underlying phenomena such as Haag's theorem.
