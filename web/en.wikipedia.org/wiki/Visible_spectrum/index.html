Visible spectrum

The visible spectrum is the band of the electromagnetic spectrum that the human eye can detect, typically wavelengths from about 380 to 750 nanometres (frequencies of roughly 400–790 terahertz). Electromagnetic radiation in this range is simply called light. The spectrum does not contain all the colours the eye and brain can distinguish — purples and magentas, for instance, are combinations of wavelengths rather than single spectral colours.

Spectral colours

Approximate divisions: violet 380–450 nm, blue 450–495 nm, green 495–570 nm, yellow 570–590 nm, orange 590–620 nm, red 620–750 nm. Isaac Newton, whose prism experiments (published in Opticks, 1704) founded the science of colour, divided the spectrum into seven colours — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet — by analogy with the musical scale.

History

Colour spectra were observed in the 13th century by Roger Bacon and Theodoric of Freiberg, who explained rainbows. Newton showed that white light is composed of spectral colours and that a prism merely separates them. In 1800 William Herschel discovered infrared radiation beyond red; a year later Johann Ritter found ultraviolet beyond violet.

Vision in other species

Many insects and birds see into the ultraviolet; some animals, such as certain snakes, detect infrared. The human visible range corresponds roughly to the wavelengths that pass through the "optical window" of Earth's atmosphere and water, and to the peak output of the Sun.
