Road sign

A road sign or traffic sign is a sign placed at the side of or above a road to give instructions or provide information to road users. Signs regulate, warn, and guide traffic, and their designs have been progressively standardized so that drivers can recognize them across regions and languages.

History

The earliest road signs were milestones erected by the Romans to mark distances along their roads. Direction signs (fingerposts) appeared in Britain and elsewhere in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The arrival of the bicycle in the late nineteenth century prompted cycling organizations to erect warning signs at dangerous hills and corners, and the motor car soon made systematic signing essential. Britain's Motor Car Act of 1903 introduced the first national motor signs, and the League of Nations and later the United Nations sponsored international conventions to harmonize designs.

Standardization

Two broad families of signs dominate worldwide. The Vienna Convention on Road Signs and Signals (1968), followed by most of Europe and many other countries, uses predominantly pictorial signs: triangular warning signs with red borders, circular prohibitory and mandatory signs, and rectangular informational signs. The United States follows its own Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD), which uses more text, distinctive shapes for special meanings (such as the octagonal stop sign and the inverted-triangle yield sign), and diamond-shaped warning signs. The stop sign—a red octagon bearing the word STOP—is the most nearly universal sign in the world.

Categories

  • Regulatory signs state rules that must be obeyed: speed limits, prohibitions on turns or parking, one-way streets.
  • Warning signs alert drivers to hazards ahead: curves, crossings, pedestrians, animals.
  • Guide and information signs give directions, distances, route markers, and locations of services.
  • Temporary signs govern road works and detours, often in orange or yellow.

Design

Modern signs use retroreflective sheeting so they are legible in headlights at night. Pictograms are favored over text to cross language barriers, and typefaces such as Transport, Highway Gothic, and Clearview are engineered for legibility at speed and distance. Variable-message signs add electronic displays for changing conditions.
