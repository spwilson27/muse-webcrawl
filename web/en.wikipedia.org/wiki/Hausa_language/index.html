Hausa language

Hausa (Harshen Hausa) Native speakers: ~50 million (L1), plus tens of millions as L2
Region: Nigeria, Niger, West Africa
Family: Afroasiatic → Chadic
Writing: Latin (Boko), Arabic (Ajami)

Hausa (Harshen Hausa) is a Chadic language spoken by the Hausa people, primarily in northern Nigeria and southern Niger, and as a lingua franca across much of West Africa. It is the most widely spoken language in the Chadic branch of the Afroasiatic family, with roughly 50 million native speakers and many tens of millions more second-language speakers, making it one of the most spoken languages in Africa.

Hausa is written in both the Latin Boko script and the Arabic-based Ajami.

Features

Hausa is a tonal language with two basic tones (high and low, plus a falling contour), and it distinguishes sounds unusual among world languages, including implosive and ejective consonants such as ɓ, ɗ, ƙ, and ts. Nouns belong to masculine or feminine gender, with plurals formed by many different patterns. Standard Hausa is based on the dialect of Kano.

Writing systems and media

Hausa has two long-standing orthographies: Boko, a Latin-based alphabet introduced under British colonial administration and now official, and Ajami, an adapted Arabic script used for centuries, especially for Islamic poetry and correspondence. Hausa has a rich oral literature as well as a modern novel tradition dating to the 1930s. It is broadcast internationally by services including the BBC, Voice of America, and Deutsche Welle, and is one of Nigeria's three major national languages.
