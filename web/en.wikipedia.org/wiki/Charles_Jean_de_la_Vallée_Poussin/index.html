Charles-Jean Étienne Gustave Nicolas, baron de la Vallée Poussin (14 August 1866 – 2 March 1962) was a Belgian mathematician, best known for proving the prime number theorem independently in 1896, at the same time as Jacques Hadamard.

Life

De la Vallée Poussin studied at the Catholic University of Leuven, where his uncle Louis-Philippe Gilbert was a professor, and took up a chair of mathematical analysis there in 1892, holding it for an exceptionally long tenure. During the First World War he also lectured at Harvard and at the Sorbonne. He founded the International Mathematical Union's precursor activities in Belgium and served as its first president in 1920. King Albert I granted him the title of baron in 1928.

Work

His 1896 proof of the prime number theorem used complex analysis of the Riemann zeta function, refining estimates of its zero-free region; improved remainder terms in the theorem still bear his name. His multi-volume Cours d'analyse infinitésimale (1903–06) was a standard text for generations of students and included important treatments of Lebesgue integration and harmonic functions. The de la Vallée Poussin kernel in approximation theory and the Poussin graph in graph theory are also named for him (the latter for a problem he posed in 1879 concerning what is now related to Hamiltonian paths).
