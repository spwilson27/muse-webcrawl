Ruthenians

Ruthenians (Latin: Rutheni) is an exonym historically applied to the East Slavic inhabitants of the lands of Kievan Rus and its successor states — the ancestors principally of modern Ukrainians and Belarusians, and in a narrower modern usage of the Rusyns. The name derives from Ruthenia, the Latin form of Rus.

Historical usage

In medieval and early modern Latin documents, Rutheni denoted the Orthodox East Slavs of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, distinguishing them from the (Catholic) Poles and from the Muscovites of the Tsardom of Russia. Their written language was Ruthenian (also called Chancery Slavonic), an ancestor of modern Ukrainian and Belarusian, and their church life was reshaped by the Union of Brest (1596), which created the Ruthenian Uniate (Greek Catholic) Church.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

Under the Habsburg monarchy, "Ruthenians" was the official designation for the Ukrainians of Galicia and Bukovina and the Rusyns of Transcarpathia, who were recognised as a nationality in Austrian censuses. In the Russian Empire the same populations were officially classed as "Little Russians". As modern national movements developed, the term Ukrainians progressively displaced Ruthenians in the early 20th century.

Modern usage

Today Ruthenians most often refers specifically to the Rusyns, an East Slavic group of the Carpathian region (in Slovakia, Poland, Ukraine, Serbia, and Croatia) — in Rusyn, Rusnaky or Rusyny. Rusyn is recognised as a distinct language in several countries, though Ukrainian official usage has generally treated Rusyns as a Ukrainian ethnographic group. The Ruthenian Catholic Church in the United States (the Byzantine Catholic Metropolitan Church) preserves the older name.
