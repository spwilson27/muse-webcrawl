Transduction (machine learning)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, statistical inference and machine learning, transduction is reasoning from observed, specific (training) cases to specific (test) cases. In contrast, induction is reasoning from observed training cases to general rules, which are then applied to the test cases. The distinction is most interesting in cases where the predictions of the transductive model are not achievable by any inductive model, because transduction can exploit information in the unlabeled test examples themselves.

History and motivation

Transductive inference was introduced by Vladimir Vapnik in the 1990s, motivated by his observation that in many practical problems one is interested only in predictions at specific points of interest (the given test set), not in estimating a general rule valid everywhere. Solving this more specific problem directly can be easier and can require fewer labeled examples, since unlabeled test data carry information about the structure of the problem, such as clusters and low-density regions separating classes.

Transductive support vector machines

The best-known transductive algorithm is the transductive support vector machine (TSVM), an extension of the standard support vector machine that simultaneously finds a labeling of the unlabeled test points and a maximum-margin hyperplane separating labeled and inferred points. TSVM optimization is non-convex and is typically solved with heuristic label-switching procedures.

Relation to other paradigms

Transduction is closely related to semi-supervised learning, in which a model learns from both labeled and unlabeled data; the difference is that semi-supervised learning usually still aims at a general inductive rule, whereas a purely transductive learner produces predictions only for the given unlabeled points. Transduction also has an analogue in philosophy of science, in reasoning by analogy and case-based reasoning.
