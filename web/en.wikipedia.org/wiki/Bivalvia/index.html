Bivalvia is a class of marine and freshwater molluscs that have laterally compressed bodies enclosed by a shell consisting of two hinged parts. They have no head and lack some organs found in other molluscs, such as the radula and odontophore. The class includes clams, oysters, cockles, mussels, scallops, and numerous other families that live in sediments or attach to hard surfaces. Most bivalves are filter feeders, using their gills (ctenidia) both for feeding and respiration.

Anatomy

The two shell valves are joined by an elastic ligament along the dorsal hinge and are opened and closed by adductor muscles. The mantle cavity contains large, sheet-like gills; water enters and leaves via siphons in burrowing species. The muscular foot, used for burrowing (and locomotion in scallops by clapping the valves), is a prominent organ. Bivalves have an open circulatory system and a simple nervous system without a centralised brain.

Ecology and diversity

Bivalves appeared in the Cambrian period and radiated extensively; their fossil record makes them important index fossils and palaeoenvironmental indicators. Living species occupy habitats from deep-sea hydrothermal vents and wood falls (shipworms, actually highly modified clams) to freshwater rivers and lakes. Filter-feeding bivalves clarify water and link plankton to benthic food webs; oyster reefs provide habitat for many species.

Human use

Many bivalves are important food animals — oysters, mussels, clams, and scallops support major fisheries and aquaculture industries. Pearls and mother-of-pearl are produced by some species. Bivalves can accumulate toxins and pathogens, causing shellfish poisoning syndromes, and are used as bioindicators of water quality. Some, like the zebra mussel, are damaging invasive species.
