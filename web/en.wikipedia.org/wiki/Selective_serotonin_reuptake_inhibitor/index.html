Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are a class of drugs that are typically used as antidepressants in the treatment of major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, and other psychological conditions. SSRIs increase the extracellular level of the neurotransmitter serotonin by limiting its reabsorption (reuptake) into the presynaptic cell. They have varying degrees of selectivity for the other monoamine transporters, with pure SSRIs having strong affinity for the serotonin transporter and only weak affinity for the norepinephrine and dopamine transporters.

Medical uses

SSRIs are first-line treatment for major depressive disorder and for generalised anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder; several are also approved for bulimia nervosa and premenstrual dysphoric disorder. Commonly prescribed agents include fluoxetine (Prozac), sertraline (Zoloft), citalopram (Celexa), escitalopram (Lexapro), paroxetine (Paxil) and fluvoxamine (Luvox). Fluoxetine was approved in the United States in 1987 and became a cultural phenomenon in the 1990s.

Adverse effects

SSRIs are generally better tolerated than older tricyclic antidepressants and monoamine oxidase inhibitors, and are far safer in overdose. Common side effects include nausea, insomnia or somnolence, headache and sexual dysfunction. Abrupt discontinuation can produce a discontinuation syndrome, especially with short-half-life agents such as paroxetine. Rare but serious risks include serotonin syndrome (chiefly when combined with other serotonergic drugs, such as MAOIs) and increased bleeding risk, particularly with anticoagulants. Regulatory agencies carry boxed warnings about increased suicidal thoughts in children, adolescents and young adults at the start of treatment.

Mechanism of action

SSRIs block the serotonin transporter (SERT) on presynaptic neurons, acutely raising synaptic serotonin; the delayed therapeutic effect is thought to involve downstream adaptations such as desensitisation of inhibitory autoreceptors and changes in neuroplasticity. Despite the class's popularity, the simple "low serotonin" account of depression is regarded by researchers as an incomplete explanation of how these drugs work.
