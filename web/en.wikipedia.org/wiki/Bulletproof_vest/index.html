Bulletproof vest

A bulletproof vest, also known as a ballistic vest or bullet-resistant vest, is an item of body armour that helps absorb the impact and reduce or stop penetration of the torso by firearm-fired projectiles and fragments from explosions. No vest is entirely "bulletproof"; the industry and standards bodies prefer the term bullet-resistant, because protection is rated against specified threats. Soft vests of woven ballistic fibres are worn by police and security personnel against handgun rounds, while military and tactical armour adds hard plates of ceramic, metal, or polyethylene to stop rifle fire.

How it works

Soft armour is made of many layers of woven or laminated high-strength fibres such as Kevlar (an aramid) or ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene (e.g., Dyneema, Spectra). When a bullet strikes, the fibres stretch and engage across a wide area, catching the projectile and dispersing its energy like a net, deforming the bullet and spreading force so that it does not penetrate. The wearer may still suffer blunt trauma — bruising or, in severe cases, internal injury — behind the armour. Hard armour plates work by shattering or deforming the projectile on a hard surface backed by fibre layers that catch the fragments.

History

Silk vests were used around the turn of the 20th century, and the priest-inventor Casimir Zeglen demonstrated silk armour capable of stopping pistol bullets. Flak jackets of nylon and fibreglass plates protected Second World War aircrew against shell fragments. The modern era began with DuPont's Kevlar in the 1970s, which made lightweight, concealable vests practical for routine police wear; the US National Institute of Justice (NIJ) standard 0101.06 and its successors classify armour in levels from IIA to IV according to the rounds defeated.

Use and limitations

Vests are standard issue for police officers in many countries and are credited with saving thousands of officers' lives. Protection degrades with heat, moisture, ultraviolet exposure, and age, and armour does not protect against knives and spikes unless specifically rated for stab resistance. Rifle-rated plates cover only part of the torso, and areas such as the head, neck, sides, and limbs remain exposed.
