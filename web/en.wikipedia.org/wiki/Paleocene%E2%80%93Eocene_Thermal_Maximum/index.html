Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum

Dateabout 56 million years ago
Warming5–8 °C global average
Durationabout 200,000 years

The Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM) was a period of rapid global warming about 56 million years ago, marking the boundary between the Paleocene and Eocene epochs. Global temperatures rose by roughly 5–8 °C over a few thousand years, driven by a massive release of carbon into the ocean and atmosphere, recorded worldwide as a sharp negative carbon-isotope excursion.

Cause

Proposed triggers include volcanic emissions from the North Atlantic Igneous Province, melting of methane clathrates on the seafloor, and thawing permafrost; the relative contributions remain debated. The carbon release appears to have been comparable in total mass to — though slower than — projected human fossil-fuel emissions, making the PETM a key natural analogue for modern climate change.

Effects

The oceans acidified and deep-sea benthic foraminifera suffered a major extinction, while on land mammals underwent rapid evolution and dispersal, including the first appearance of early primates and modern hoofed mammals in the fossil record of the northern continents.
