Johnson Sea Link

Johnson Sea Link
Type: Deep-sea submersible
Operator: Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute
Launched: 1971 (JSL I); 1975 (JSL II)
Depth rating: about 900 m (3,000 ft)

The Johnson Sea Link (JSL) is a class of deep-sea research submersibles designed by engineer and diving pioneer Edwin Link and operated for many years by the Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute in Fort Pierce, Florida. Two vehicles were built, Johnson Sea Link I (1971) and Johnson Sea Link II (1975), and between them they completed thousands of scientific dives.

Design

The Johnson Sea Link's most distinctive feature is its spherical acrylic (transparent plastic) personnel sphere, about 1.45 metres in diameter, giving the pilot and an observer an almost unobstructed panoramic view. A separate aft compartment can carry additional crew or a diver, and the original design included lock-out diving capability. The submersibles are equipped with manipulator arms, sampling baskets, cameras, and lights, and are rated to depths of roughly 900 metres (3,000 feet).

History

In June 1973, Johnson Sea Link I became trapped on the wreck of the destroyer USS Fred T. Berry off Key West, Florida, during a dive. Two of the four men aboard died before the vehicle was freed, in one of the most serious accidents in civilian submersible history; the incident led to safety changes in submersible operations.

Despite the accident, the two vehicles went on to long scientific careers supporting research on deep-sea ecology, fisheries, geology, and biomedical prospecting, including work connected with the discovery of deep-water Oculina coral reefs off Florida. They also assisted in the recovery of debris from the Space Shuttle Challenger disaster in 1986, helping locate the crew compartment. The submersibles were eventually retired from Harbor Branch service as the institute reorganised its operations.
