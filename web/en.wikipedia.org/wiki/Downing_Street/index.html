Downing Street is a street in the City of Westminster, London, close to the Houses of Parliament and Whitehall. Built in the 1680s by Sir George Downing, it has housed the official residences of two of the most senior British ministers for over two centuries: the Prime Minister at Number 10 and the Chancellor of the Exchequer at Number 11. "Downing Street" is commonly used as a metonym for the British government.

History

The street stands on the site of a medieval mansion and, later, Hampden House. George Downing — a soldier, diplomat and sometime spy for both Cromwell and Charles II — acquired the lease in the 1660s and built a row of terraced houses in the 1680s. The houses were cheaply constructed on shallow foundations over reclaimed marsh, a defect that has troubled their fabric ever since; Winston Churchill described Number 10 as "shaky and lightly built by the profiteering contractor whose name it bears".

Notable buildings

  • Number 10 — official residence and office of the Prime Minister since 1735, when George II offered the house to Robert Walpole, generally regarded as Britain's first prime minister.
  • Number 11 — residence of the Chancellor of the Exchequer since 1828.
  • Number 12 — formerly the Whips' Office.
  • Number 9 — used by government departments, including for a time the Brexit department.

The street is closed to the public by security gates installed in 1989 during the era of IRA terrorism; earlier in the century it had been an ordinary public thoroughfare where tourists could walk up to the famous black door of Number 10.
