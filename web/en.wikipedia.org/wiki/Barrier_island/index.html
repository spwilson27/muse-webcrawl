Barrier island


Barrier island and lagoon, aerial view

Barrier islands are long, narrow, low-lying offshore deposits of sand or sediment that lie parallel to the mainland coast, separated from it by a lagoon, bay or tidal marsh. They occur mainly on gently sloping, trailing-edge coasts with abundant sediment and moderate tidal range, and protect the mainland shore from storm waves and erosion.

Formation

Several mechanisms can form barrier islands: the upward growth of offshore sand bars, the submergence of coastal ridges and dunes as sea level rises, and the segmentation of spits by tidal inlets. Storms continually reshape them: overwash carries sand from the beach into the lagoon, causing islands to migrate landwards over time, while longshore drift extends or reshapes their ends.

Distribution and examples

About 15 per cent of the world's coastlines have barrier island systems. The Atlantic and Gulf coasts of the United States host extensive chains, including the Outer Banks of North Carolina, Galveston Island in Texas and Padre Island — the world's longest barrier island at about 182 km (113 mi). Others occur along the Frisian coast of the North Sea and in Australia and Mexico.

Development and hazards

Many barrier islands are heavily developed with resorts and second homes, exposing buildings to hurricanes, storm surge and chronic erosion. Management responses include beach nourishment, dune restoration, inlet dredging and, increasingly, managed retreat as sea level rise accelerates island migration.
