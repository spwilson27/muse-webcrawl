Psychogeography is the study of the effects of the geographical environment—above all the city—on the emotions and behaviour of individuals, and the set of playful, subversive practices developed to explore and expose those effects. Guy Debord, who defined the term in 1955 in his "Introduction to a Critique of Urban Geography", described psychogeography as "the study of the precise laws and specific effects of the geographical environment, consciously organized or not, on the emotions and behavior of individuals". Developed within the Letterist International and then the Situationist International, psychogeography was part of a revolutionary critique of capitalist urbanism: its central technique, the dérive ("drift")—an unplanned wandering through the city guided by the attractions of the terrain—was meant to reveal the "unities of ambience" and currents of desire that official planning and the spectacle conceal.

Concepts and practice

In the dérive, participants abandon their usual routes and motives for movement and let themselves be drawn by the psychogeographical contours of the city—its "constant currents, fixed points and vortexes" that encourage entry into or exit from certain zones. Debord recommended small groups, durations of about a day, and careful note-taking; the results were distilled into psychogeographic maps such as The Naked City (1957, with Asger Jorn), which cuts a map of Paris into fragments reconnected by red arrows marking the spontaneous turns of inclination between districts. Related ideas include détournement (the rerouting of existing cultural material), "unitary urbanism" (the construction of environments favourable to free life), and the figure of the wanderer inherited from Baudelaire's and Benjamin's flâneur.

History

The term had scattered earlier uses, including in discussions of geographic influences on mental life, but its operative meaning comes from the Paris avant-garde of the 1950s: Debord and his comrades (Ivan Chtcheglov, whose 1953 "Formulary for a New Urbanism" imagined a city of shifting quarters of feeling; Michèle Bernstein; and others) treated playful exploration as political research against the functionalist planning then rebuilding Paris. After the Situationist International dissolved in 1972, psychogeography persisted in fragments—in the Italian Luther Blissett project, in London groups such as the London Psychogeographical Association (associated with the writer Stewart Home), and in zines and walking collectives.

Contemporary revival

From the 1990s psychogeography flourished in British writing: Iain Sinclair's walks around the M25 (London Orbital) and through the city's occulted histories, Will Self's "Psychogeography" columns, and work by Merlin Coverley (whose Psychogeography (2006) is a standard introduction) recast the dérive as a literary and artistic method for reading London's layered pasts. Artists and academics have taken it into urban studies, locative media and augmented-reality walking apps, while critics complain that the term, now used loosely for almost any reflective walking or "sense of place" writing, has been drained of its original politics—Debord's drift was research for revolution, not a lifestyle pastime. The tension between these two psychogeographies—situationist critique and literary flânerie—defines the field today.
