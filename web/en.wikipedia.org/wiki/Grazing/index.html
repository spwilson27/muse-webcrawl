Grazing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In agriculture, grazing is a method of animal husbandry in which domestic herbivores are allowed to roam outdoors and consume wild vegetation in order to convert grass and other forage into meat, milk, wool, and other animal products. In ecology, the term describes the feeding behaviour of any herbivore that crops grasses and low vegetation rather than browsing leaves from shrubs and trees.

Grazing systems

  • Continuous grazing — animals have unrestricted access to a pasture for an entire season or year.
  • Rotational grazing — land is divided into paddocks and stock are moved between them, allowing grazed plants a recovery period; intensive variants include mob grazing and management-intensive grazing.
  • Zero grazing (cut-and-carry) — forage is harvested and brought to housed animals, common in dairy systems with limited land.
  • Transhumance and nomadic grazing — seasonal movement of herds between pastures, historically widespread in mountain and arid regions.

Ecology

Grazing maintains grassland ecosystems by suppressing woody encroachment and recycling nutrients through dung and urine. Moderate grazing can increase plant diversity by preventing competitive dominance, and many grassland species evolved under grazing pressure from wild herbivores. Conversely, overgrazing — stocking beyond the land's carrying capacity — removes protective vegetation, compacts and erodes soil, and is a major driver of desertification. Grazing animals also produce methane through enteric fermentation, making livestock management relevant to climate policy; well-managed grazing can conversely build soil carbon in some settings.

History

Pastoralism arose with the domestication of sheep, goats, and cattle in the Neolithic, roughly 10,000 years ago. Grazing rights over common land shaped rural law in Europe for centuries, and conflicts over enclosure and open-range grazing were central episodes in the histories of Britain and the American West. Today grazing occupies more land area than any other human land use, covering roughly a quarter of Earth's ice-free land surface.
