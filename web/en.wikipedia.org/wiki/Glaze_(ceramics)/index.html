Ceramic glaze

Ceramic glaze
MaterialFused silicate glass coating
Main ingredientsSilica, fluxes (e.g. feldspar, lead oxide, soda), alumina, colourants
PurposeWaterproofing, decoration, hygiene

Ceramic glaze is a layer or coating of a glassy substance fused to a ceramic body through firing. A glaze serves to colour, decorate or waterproof an item, rendering porous earthenware impermeable to liquids and easier to clean, and giving stoneware and porcelain a smooth, durable surface.

Composition

Glazes consist principally of silica, which forms glass when melted, combined with fluxes such as feldspar, whiting, soda ash, borax or (historically) lead oxide, which lower the melting temperature; alumina, which stiffens the melt and prevents it running off the ware; and metal-oxide colourants such as cobalt (blue), copper (green and turquoise), iron (browns and celadon greens) and manganese (purple-brown). Glazes are classified by firing temperature as low-fire (earthenware), mid-fire or high-fire (stoneware and porcelain) glazes.

Application and firing

Glaze is usually applied to bisque-fired ware by dipping, pouring, brushing or spraying, then fired in a kiln to temperatures from about 900 °C to 1,400 °C depending on the type. Important glazing traditions include ash glazes (made with wood ash, originating in China), salt glaze, lead glaze, tin-glaze (used for maiolica, faience and delftware), celadon, raku and crystalline glazes.

History

Glazed ceramics appeared in the ancient Near East and Egypt — Egyptian faience dates to the fourth millennium BC — and developed through Greek and Roman lead glazes, Tang-dynasty sancai ware, Islamic lustreware and tin-glazed pottery, Chinese celadon and blue-and-white porcelain, and European stoneware and porcelain of the early modern period onward.
