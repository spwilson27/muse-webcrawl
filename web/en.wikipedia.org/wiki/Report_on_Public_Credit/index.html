First Report on the Public Credit

The First Report on the Public Credit was the first of four major reports on economic policy issued by Alexander Hamilton, the first United States Secretary of the Treasury, presented to the House of Representatives on January 9, 1790.

Contents

Hamilton proposed that the federal government fund the national debt at par — paying foreign and domestic creditors in full — and assume the remaining war debts of the states, so as to establish public credit, unite the interests of creditors with the new government, and stimulate the economy. He recommended funding through new bonds backed by tariff and excise revenues.

Controversy and passage

The plan sparked fierce debate. James Madison objected to paying full value to speculators who had bought certificates cheaply from original holders such as soldiers, proposing "discrimination" between them; his amendment failed. Assumption of state debts was initially defeated, then passed as part of the Compromise of 1790, in which southern states accepted assumption in exchange for locating the permanent capital on the Potomac (the future Washington, D.C.). The Funding Act of 1790 enacted the program.
