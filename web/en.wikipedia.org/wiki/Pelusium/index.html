Pelusium (Greek: Πηλούσιον, Pēlousion; Egyptian: Peremoun; Arabic: Tell el-Farama) was an ancient fortified city at the easternmost mouth of the Nile Delta in Egypt, about 30 km southeast of the modern Port Said. Standing on the Pelusiac branch of the Nile at the gateway between Egypt and Asia, it was Egypt's eastern frontier fortress and a major port, repeatedly fought over by invading armies.

History

Pelusium guarded the coastal road from Egypt to Syria, the "Ways of Horus". The Assyrians under Sennacherib were said to have been struck by plague before its walls in 701 BC. In 525 BC the decisive Battle of Pelusium between Pharaoh Psamtik III and Cambyses II opened Egypt to Persian conquest. The city was fought over again in the wars of the Diadochi: Antigonus I besieged it in 306 BC, and Ptolemy IV won a major victory over Antiochus III nearby at Raphia in 217 BC.

Under Rome, Pelusium was a prosperous commercial city, exporting linen and receiving trade from Arabia and India. Pompey was murdered on the shore near Pelusium in 48 BC after fleeing the Battle of Pharsalus. The Persians captured it in 619 AD, and the Arabs in 640 AD.

Decline

The Pelusiac branch of the Nile gradually silted up (it had largely failed by the 1st century AD, accelerating in later centuries), cutting Pelusium off from both river and sea. The city shrank through the medieval period — Crusader Baldwin I of Jerusalem took the diminished town in 1118 — and its ruins at Tell el-Farama were eventually abandoned. Excavations have revealed a Roman theatre, baths, churches, and a fortress.
