The CNO cycle (for carbon–nitrogen–oxygen) is one of the two known sets of fusion reactions by which stars convert hydrogen to helium, the other being the proton–proton chain. Unlike the proton–proton chain, the CNO cycle is catalytic: carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen nuclei act as catalysts that are regenerated at the end of the cycle, with the net effect 4 1H → 4He + 2 e+ + 2 νe + energy.

The CNO-I cycle, the main branch of the CNO cycle.

The cycle

In the main CNO-I branch, a 12C nucleus captures successive protons, passing through isotopes of nitrogen and oxygen with beta-plus decays, until an alpha particle is emitted from 15N and the original 12C is restored. Side branches (CNO-II through CNO-IV) involve fluorine and heavier CNO isotopes and become significant at higher temperatures.

Role in stars

The CNO cycle's energy generation rate depends extremely steeply on temperature (roughly T17), compared with T4 for the proton–proton chain. As a result, the CNO cycle dominates energy production in stars more massive than about 1.3 solar masses, whose cores exceed roughly 1.7×107 K. This steep dependence also drives convection in the cores of such stars. The cycle was proposed independently by Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker in 1938 and Hans Bethe in 1939.

CNO-processed material is a source of the nitrogen enrichment observed in some stellar populations, and neutrinos emitted by the cycle in the Sun's core were directly detected by the Borexino experiment in 2020.
