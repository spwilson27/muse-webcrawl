Lochnagar Crater

The Lochnagar Crater, near the village of La Boisselle in the Somme department of France, is a large crater formed by the detonation of an underground mine at 7:28 a.m. on 1 July 1916, the first day of the Battle of the Somme in the First World War. It was the largest mine crater of the war and remains one of the largest man-made craters in the world.

The mine

The mine was placed by the Royal Engineers' tunnelling companies beneath a German field fortification known as Schwaben Höhe. Two chambers were packed with 27,000 kg (60,000 lb) of ammonal explosive at a depth of about 16 metres, reached by a gallery some 310 metres long dug from the British lines. The mine took its name from the trench from which tunnelling began, Lochnagar Street.

Detonation

The Lochnagar mine was sprung two minutes before zero hour, at 7:28 a.m. Debris was thrown an estimated 1,200 metres into the air, and the explosion destroyed the German position above it. Along with the nearby Y Sap mine, it signalled the start of the infantry assault. Despite the mines, the attack at La Boisselle on the first day largely failed, at heavy cost.

Preservation

The crater was purchased in 1978 by Englishman Richard Dunning to preserve it from infilling and development. It is maintained as a memorial, with an annual ceremony held at 7:28 a.m. on 1 July. A wooden cross and memorial plaques at the crater's rim commemorate the men of the Tyneside Scottish and other units who fought there.
