Victor Emmanuel II (Italian: Vittorio Emanuele II; 14 March 1820 – 9 January 1878) was King of Sardinia (also known as Piedmont-Sardinia) from 1849 until 1861, when he assumed the title of King of Italy and became the first king of a united Italy since the 6th century — a title he held until his death in 1878. Remembered as one of the "fathers of the fatherland" (Padre della Patria), he presided, with his prime minister Camillo Benso, Count of Cavour, over the Risorgimento that unified the Italian peninsula.

Early life and accession

Born in Turin, the eldest son of Charles Albert, Prince of Carignano (later King of Sardinia), Victor Emmanuel received a largely military education and fought in the First Italian War of Independence (1848–49). When his father abdicated after the defeat at Novara, he became king on 23 March 1849. He negotiated an armistice with Austria, resisted pressure to revoke the liberal Statuto Albertino constitution — earning the nickname il Re Galantuomo ("the Gentleman King") — and in 1852 appointed Cavour as prime minister, beginning a decisive partnership.

Unification of Italy

Under Cavour's diplomacy, Piedmont joined the Crimean War, secured French alliance at Plombières (1858), and defeated Austria in the Franco-Piedmontese war of 1859, gaining Lombardy. In 1860 the central Italian states voted for annexation, and Giuseppe Garibaldi's Expedition of the Thousand conquered Sicily and Naples in the king's name. Victor Emmanuel met Garibaldi at Teano on 26 October 1860 and was handed the south. On 17 March 1861 the parliament in Turin proclaimed him King of Italy — retaining his regnal number "II" to stress continuity with the Sardinian crown. Venetia was annexed in 1866, and Rome was taken in 1870 and made the capital.

Later reign and death

As constitutional monarch of Italy he contended with the Roman Question (his excommunication-era conflict with Pope Pius IX, who rejected the loss of the Papal States), brigandage in the south, and fragile parliamentary politics. He died in Rome on 9 January 1878 and was buried in the Pantheon. The colossal Vittoriano monument in Rome (also called the Altare della Patria) was built in his honour. He was succeeded by his son Umberto I.
