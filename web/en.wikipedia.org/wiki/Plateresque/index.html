Plateresque, from the Spanish word plateresco, is an artistic style in Spanish architecture and decoration that emerged in the late 15th century and flourished through the 16th century. The name compares its fine, highly detailed surface ornament — carved in low relief with the precision of a silversmith's (platero's) work — to the decoration of silver plate.

Characteristics

Plateresque ornament typically covers façades, cloisters, and choir screens with a dense filigree of carved motifs: classical medallions and busts, coats of arms, candelieri, grotesques, garlands, cherubs, and heraldic beasts, mingled with late Gothic pinnacles and Mudéjar interlace patterns. The underlying structures often remain essentially Gothic or early Renaissance in plan; it is the lavish surface carving that defines the style. Scholars conventionally distinguish an early, more Gothic phase (sometimes called "Isabelline Plateresque", under the Catholic Monarchs) from a later High Plateresque fully infused with Italian Renaissance classicism.

Notable examples

The most celebrated Plateresque work is the façade of the University of Salamanca (c. 1525–1533), a tapestry-like wall of carved stone centred on medallions of the Catholic Monarchs — and hiding, tradition says, a tiny carved frog atop a skull that students must spot for luck. Other masterpieces include the façade of the University of Alcalá de Henares (Rodrigo Gil de Hontañón), the Hospital de la Santa Cruz in Toledo, the Hospital de los Reyes Católicos in Santiago de Compostela, and the Convent of San Marcos in León. In Spanish America the style travelled to Mexico, where it appears in Augustinian mission churches such as Acolman and Yuriria, blended with indigenous carving.

Legacy

As the austere Herrerian style took over at the Spanish court in the later 16th century, Plateresque gave way, but it was revived in the 19th and 20th centuries in Spanish Revival and Monterrey architecture, and remains emblematic of Spain's Renaissance golden age.
