Inertial measurement unit

An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is an electronic device that measures and reports a body's specific force, angular rate, and sometimes orientation, using a combination of accelerometers, gyroscopes and sometimes magnetometers. IMUs are typically used to manoeuvre aircraft, spacecraft, missiles, ships and drones, and are embedded in most smartphones and game controllers.

MEMS IMUs are now tiny, cheap solid-state devices.

Operation

A typical IMU contains three accelerometers and three gyroscopes arranged on orthogonal axes, measuring linear acceleration and angular velocity in three dimensions. Integrating these measurements over time allows a navigation computer to track position, velocity and attitude — the basis of an inertial navigation system. Because integration accumulates small sensor errors, unaided inertial navigation drifts over time, so IMU data is usually fused with external references such as GPS using techniques like the Kalman filter.

History and types

Early IMUs for rockets and the Apollo spacecraft used precision gimballed platforms and spinning-mass gyroscopes. Modern strapdown designs — including ring-laser, fibre-optic and MEMS sensors — fix the sensors directly to the vehicle body and compute orientation mathematically. MEMS IMUs in particular have made inertial sensing ubiquitous in consumer electronics, vehicles and wearable devices.
