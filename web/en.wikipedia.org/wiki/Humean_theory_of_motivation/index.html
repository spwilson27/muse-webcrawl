Humean theory of motivation

The Humean theory of motivation is the view in the philosophy of action, named after the Scottish philosopher David Hume, that beliefs alone cannot motivate action: motivation requires a desire, or some other conative state, distinct from belief. On this view, beliefs inform us about the world, while desires direct us toward ends.

Background

Hume argued in his Treatise of Human Nature (1739–40) that "reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions", holding that reason by itself is inert and can influence action only by informing desire. Modern Humeanism, articulated by philosophers such as Michael Smith, combines this with the Humean theory of reasons, on which normative reasons derive from an agent's desires.

Criticism

Anti-Humeans argue that moral judgements or beliefs about reasons can motivate by themselves, pointing to the phenomenon of the virtuous agent who acts from duty. The debate connects to wider disputes about internalism and externalism about moral motivation.
