An alkane is a saturated acyclic hydrocarbon: carbon atoms joined only by single bonds in chains, with the general formula CₔH₂ₔ₊₂. The first members are methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), propane (C₃H₈) and butane (C₄H₁₀).

Alkanes are the main components of natural gas and petroleum. They are relatively unreactive — hence their old name 'paraffins' ('little affinity') — burning cleanly in oxygen and undergoing free-radical substitution with halogens. Longer chains form liquids (petrol, diesel) and waxes.

Trends

With increasing chain length, boiling points rise steadily (intermolecular forces grow), from methane at −161 °C to waxes melting above room temperature. Branching lowers boiling point — an effect exploited in petrol formulation (iso-octane is the 100 point of the octane scale).

Isomers

From butane upward, alkanes have structural isomers: the same formula arranged differently (butane and isobutane). Isomer counts explode with size — C₂₀H₄₂ has over 366,000 possible structures.
