Koliada (Ukrainian: Коляда; also Kolyada, Koleda, or Kolada) is a Slavic winter festival and the cycle of ritual songs associated with it, celebrated around the winter solstice and later absorbed into the Christmas and New Year season. The term also refers to the caroling custom in which groups of singers—often children or young people—go from house to house performing songs (koliadky) in exchange for food, sweets, or small gifts.

Origins

The festival is generally believed to have pre-Christian roots in the celebration of the winter solstice and the rebirth of the sun, associated in some interpretations with a deity or personification called Kolyada. With Christianization, the koliada season merged with Christmas, and carols increasingly celebrated the Nativity, frequently blending biblical themes with older agrarian wishes for fertility, health, and a good harvest.

Customs

Carolers traditionally carry a large decorated star on a pole, symbolizing the Star of Bethlehem, and sometimes wear costumes or carry a nativity scene (vertep). In Ukraine, the koliada season begins on Christmas Eve with the holy supper (Sviata Vechera) of twelve meatless dishes, and caroling continues through the New Year (when schedrivky, "generous songs," are sung on Malanka, the eve of the Old New Year) until Epiphany. A ritual sheaf of grain called a didukh is placed in the home as a symbol of ancestral spirits and abundance.

Songs

Koliadky typically open with wishes of health and prosperity for the household, address each family member in turn, and end with a request for a reward. Many later carols, such as the internationally known "Carol of the Bells" (derived from the Ukrainian New Year song "Shchedryk" by Mykola Leontovych), originate in this seasonal song tradition.
