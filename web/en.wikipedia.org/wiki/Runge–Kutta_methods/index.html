Runge–Kutta methods

In numerical analysis, the Runge–Kutta methods (English: /ˈrʊŋəˈkʊtɑː/) are a family of implicit and explicit iterative methods used in the numerical solution of ordinary differential equations. They were developed around 1900 by the German mathematicians Carl Runge and Wilhelm Kutta.

The classical fourth-order method

The best-known member of the family, usually just called "the Runge–Kutta method" or RK4, advances the solution of y′ = f(t, y) by combining four weighted slopes: k₁ at the start of the step, k₂ and k₃ at the midpoint, and k₄ at the end. The step is yn+1 = yn + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄). The local truncation error is of order h⁵ and the accumulated error of order h⁴, making the method fourth-order accurate.

General formulation

General Runge–Kutta methods are specified by a Butcher tableau (after John C. Butcher), which lists the coefficients c, b, and a of an s-stage method. Explicit methods have a strictly lower-triangular coefficient matrix; implicit methods, such as the Gauss–Legendre and Radau methods, can achieve higher order and better stability for stiff equations, at the cost of solving nonlinear systems at each step.

Adaptive methods

Practical implementations use embedded pairs of methods of different orders to estimate the error and adapt the step size. The Runge–Kutta–Fehlberg method (RKF45) and the Dormand–Prince method (used as ode45 in MATLAB and similar software) are standard examples.

Applications

Runge–Kutta methods are workhorses of scientific computing: orbit propagation, weather and climate models, chemical kinetics, control systems, and physics engines in games all rely on them where closed-form solutions do not exist.
