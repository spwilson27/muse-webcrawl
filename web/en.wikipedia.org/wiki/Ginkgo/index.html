Ginkgo

Ginkgo biloba, commonly known as ginkgo or the maidenhair tree, is a species of gymnosperm tree native to East Asia. It is the last living species in the order Ginkgoales, which first appeared over 290 million years ago, and is often called a "living fossil": fossils very similar to the living species extend back to the Middle Jurassic. The tree has distinctive fan-shaped leaves that turn brilliant yellow in autumn, and it is widely planted as an ornamental and street tree for its hardiness and resistance to pollution and disease. Its seeds are used in East Asian cuisine and its leaf extracts in herbal medicine.

Description and reproduction

Ginkgos are large, long-lived deciduous trees, typically 20–35 m tall (some Chinese specimens exceed 50 m), with an angular crown and erratic branching. The leaves are unique among seed plants: fan-shaped, with veins radiating from the stalk and often a central split (hence biloba). Ginkgo is dioecious — male and female reproductive structures grow on separate trees. Unusually for a gymnosperm, fertilisation involves motile sperm, discovered in 1896. Female trees produce plum-like seeds whose fleshy outer layer (sarcotesta) smells strongly of rancid butter owing to butyric acid, which is why male clones are preferred for street planting. Individual trees may live over a thousand years; some Chinese temple trees are reputed to be far older.

History and distribution

The ginkgo lineage was once worldwide, but contracted after the Cretaceous and vanished from North America and Europe during the ice ages. It survived in China, where trees persisted in mountain refuges and around temples — whether any truly wild populations remain is debated. The German naturalist Engelbert Kaempfer saw the tree in Japan in 1690 and introduced its seeds to Europe in the early 18th century; Linnaeus named it in 1771. Six ginkgos near the hypocentre of the Hiroshima bombing survived and still grow, making the tree a symbol of endurance in Japan.

Uses

The kernels of the seeds (ginkgo nuts, baiguo) are eaten in Chinese, Japanese and Korean cooking, though they contain toxins and should be eaten in moderation. Standardised leaf extracts (EGb 761) are among the most widely sold herbal supplements, marketed for memory and circulation; large clinical trials, including the US GEM study (2008), found no benefit in preventing dementia or cognitive decline. The timber is light and used for furniture and tea ceremony utensils in Japan.
