Mono no aware

Mono no aware (物の哀れ), literally "the pathos of things", and also translated as "an empathy toward things" or "a sensitivity to ephemera", is a Japanese idiom for the awareness of impermanence (無常, mujō), or the transience of things, and a transient gentle sadness (or wistfulness) at their passing as well as a longer, deeper gentle sadness about this state being the reality of life.

Origins

The term comes from Japanese literature of the Heian period. The word aware, in Heian texts, expressed a profound, often melancholic emotion upon witnessing something moving — originally an exclamation akin to "ah!" or "oh!". The concept was central to the writing of Murasaki Shikibu, whose 11th-century novel The Tale of Genji is pervaded by an appreciation of the beauty and brevity of courtly life, cherry blossoms, and the changing seasons.

Motoori Norinaga

The 18th-century Edo-period scholar Motoori Norinaga, a leading figure of the Kokugaku (national learning) movement, elevated mono no aware into a key aesthetic and literary-critical concept in his commentary on The Tale of Genji. For Norinaga, to "know mono no aware" was to be moved by, and to understand, the emotional truth of things and of human hearts — an essentially Japanese sensibility he contrasted with what he saw as the rigid moralism of imported Confucian and Buddhist doctrine.

In contemporary culture

The concept continues to inform Japanese aesthetics, from the appreciation of cherry-blossom viewing (hanami) to modern film, literature, and animation. Critics have applied it to the films of Yasujirō Ozu and to contemporary works that dwell on transience and gentle melancholy.
