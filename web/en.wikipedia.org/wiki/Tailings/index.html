Tailings

In mining, tailings are the materials left over after the process of separating the valuable fraction from the uneconomic fraction (gangue) of an ore. Tailings are different from overburden, which is the waste rock or other material that overlies an ore body and is displaced during mining without being processed.

The extraction of minerals from ore can be done in two ways: placer mining, which uses water and gravity to concentrate the valuable minerals, or hard rock mining, which pulverises the rock containing the ore and then relies on chemical reactions to concentrate the sought-after material. In placer mining, the extraction is complete once the valuable minerals have been removed; in hard rock mining, the extraction process yields a much larger volume of waste — the tailings — which may consist of finely ground rock, water and process chemicals in the form of a slurry.

Composition

Tailings are composed of ground rock, water, and small amounts of process chemicals such as cyanide, acids, or other reagents used in the beneficiation process. Depending on the ore, tailings can contain residual metals and sulfides that may generate acid mine drainage when exposed to air and water. The proportion of tailings to ore is very high for most hard rock minerals; for example, producing a single gold ring's worth of metal can generate many tonnes of tailings.

Storage

Tailings are most commonly stored on the surface in impoundments held back by dams, or discharged into tailings ponds where the solids settle and the water is recycled or treated. Tailings dams are among the largest engineered structures on earth. Alternative methods include dry stacking (in which tailings are dewatered and stacked), backfilling underground workings, and, controversially, submarine or riverine disposal. Failures of tailings dams have caused some of the worst mining disasters on record, including the Stava dam collapse in Italy in 1985, the Mount Polley failure in Canada in 2014, and the Brumadinho dam disaster in Brazil in 2019, which killed 270 people.

Reprocessing and reuse

Old tailings may contain minerals that were uneconomic when first processed but have since become valuable, or that can be extracted with newer technology; reprocessing can both recover value and reduce environmental liability. Tailings have also been investigated as sources of construction material and for the recovery of rare-earth elements and other critical minerals.

Environmental concerns

Improperly managed tailings can contaminate soil and water with heavy metals, generate acid rock drainage over centuries, and pose dust hazards when dry ponds are exposed to wind. Modern regulation increasingly requires engineered storage, monitoring, financial assurance for closure, and in some jurisdictions a prohibition on upstream-construction tailings dams after catastrophic failures.
