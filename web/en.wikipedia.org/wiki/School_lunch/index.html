School lunch

School meals are a major public nutrition program in many countries

A school lunch (also known as a school dinner or school meal) is a meal provided to students and sometimes teachers at a school, typically in the middle of the school day. School meal programs, subsidised to varying degrees, exist in most developed countries and many developing ones, serving goals of nutrition, food security, attendance, and learning.

United States

The National School Lunch Program (NSLP), established by the Richard B. Russell National School Lunch Act of 1946, provides low-cost or free lunches to children in public and nonprofit private schools. It serves roughly 30 million children daily. Children from households at or below 130% of the federal poverty line receive free meals; those up to 185% receive reduced-price meals. The Healthy, Hunger-Free Kids Act of 2010 raised nutrition standards — more whole grains, fruits and vegetables, and limits on sodium and trans fats — and created the Community Eligibility Provision, letting high-poverty schools serve free meals to all students. Debates continue over "lunch shaming", unpaid meal debt, and proposals for universal free school meals, adopted permanently by some states such as California and Maine.

Around the world

  • Japan — kyūshoku: nutritionally planned lunches, often served by students themselves as part of food education (shokuiku)
  • France — multi-course cantine meals emphasising structured courses and fresh ingredients
  • Finland — universal free school meals since 1948, the first such national program
  • India — the Mid-Day Meal Scheme (PM-POSHAN), the world's largest school feeding program, serving over 100 million children, credited with improving enrolment and nutrition
  • United Kingdom — standards-based school dinners; free school meals for eligible pupils; universal infant free school meals in England since 2014
  • Brazil — the National School Feeding Programme (PNAE), which sources a share of food from family farms

Nutrition and policy issues

School meals sit at the intersection of child health, agriculture policy, and welfare: they are a market for farm produce, a defence against childhood hunger, and a venue for shaping dietary habits. Researchers associate participation with improved food security and, in several studies, better academic outcomes for disadvantaged children, while menu quality, cultural appropriateness, and funding remain recurring policy questions.
