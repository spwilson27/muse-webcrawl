In the United States, a Social Security number (SSN) is a nine-digit number issued to U.S. citizens, permanent residents, and temporary (working) residents by the Social Security Administration, an independent agency of the United States government. Its primary purpose is to track individuals for Social Security benefits, but it has become a de facto national identification number for taxation and other purposes.

History

Social Security numbers were first issued in November 1936 as part of the New Deal Social Security programme, to track workers' earnings for benefit calculations. The first SSNs were distributed through local post offices; the often-cited "first" card was issued via a New York new-account process in 1936. Over time, federal agencies and private companies adopted the SSN as a general-purpose identifier, a practice expanded by a 1943 executive order and later statutes such as the Tax Reform Act of 1986, which required SSNs for dependent children claimed on tax returns.

Structure

An SSN is printed as three groups: an area number (three digits), a group number (two digits), and a serial number (four digits). Historically the area number reflected the mailing address on the application, and group numbers followed a specific odd/even issuance pattern. Since 25 June 2011, the SSA has used "randomization", assigning numbers without geographic or group significance, to extend the pool and reduce fraud. Certain combinations, such as area 666 or 900–999, are never issued.

Uses and misuse

The SSN is required for employment, federal and state taxation, opening many financial accounts, and obtaining credit. Because it functions simultaneously as identifier and authenticator, its theft is central to identity fraud in the United States; the SSA advises that cards and numbers be guarded, and replacement numbers are issued only in narrow circumstances. Most U.S. residents now receive a number at birth through the Enumeration at Birth programme, and more than 450 million numbers have been issued to date.
