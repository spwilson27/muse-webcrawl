Protestantism is a branch of Christianity that originated with the Reformation, a 16th-century movement against what its followers perceived as errors in the Catholic Church. It began conventionally in 1517, when Martin Luther published his Ninety-five Theses criticising the sale of indulgences. Protestants are united less by a single institution than by shared emphases: the authority of Scripture alone (sola scriptura), justification by faith alone (sola fide), and the priesthood of all believers.

History and branches

The Reformation spread rapidly with the printing press: Lutheran churches in Germany and Scandinavia; Reformed churches shaped by Zwingli in Zurich and Calvin in Geneva; the Church of England, whose break with Rome under Henry VIII produced Anglicanism; and the Radical Reformation of Anabaptists and others. The Catholic Counter-Reformation and wars of religion hardened the divisions. Later movements — Pietism, Methodism under the Wesleys, evangelical revivals, and 20th-century Pentecostalism, now among the fastest-growing forms of Christianity worldwide — repeatedly reshaped the Protestant world, which today comprises hundreds of denominations, from historic "mainline" churches to independent and non-denominational congregations.

Beliefs and practice

Most Protestants recognise two sacraments (Baptism and the Lord's Supper, variously understood) against the Catholic seven, reject papal authority and purgatory, and centre worship on preaching and congregational participation, historically in the vernacular. Traditions differ widely — on church government (episcopal, presbyterian, congregational), baptism (including believers' baptism), and the Eucharist (from memorialism to Lutheran sacramental union). Ecumenical dialogues since the 20th century have narrowed some historic disputes, including the 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church.
