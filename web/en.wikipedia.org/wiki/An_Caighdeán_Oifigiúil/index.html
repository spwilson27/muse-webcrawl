An Caighdeán Oifigiúil ([ənˠ ˈkəidʲaːnˠ əˈfʲɪɟuːlʲ], "The Official Standard") is the official standard for the spelling and grammar of the Irish language. It was developed by civil servants of the Irish state, principally through the Translation Section of the Oireachtas (the Irish parliament), and first published in 1958 as Gramadach na Gaeilge agus Litriú na Gaeilge: An Caighdeán Oifigiúil. A spelling standard, Litriú na Gaeilge, had earlier appeared in 1947.

Development

The standard was a compromise among the three major living dialects of Irish — Ulster, Connacht and Munster — intended to provide a single written norm for official documents, education and publishing. It simplified the classical spelling system, which had preserved many silent letters, while retaining features such as the initial mutations. In some respects, particularly its treatment of certain verb forms and the dative case, it leaned toward Munster and Connacht usage, and it was criticised by some Ulster writers.

Revisions

An advisory process led to a revised edition published in 2012, which regularised some inconsistencies — for example in the use of the vocative and genitive cases and in the spelling of certain borrowed words — followed by consolidated updated editions. The Caighdeán governs the Irish used in official publications and state examinations, though dialect literature continues to be written in regional norms.
