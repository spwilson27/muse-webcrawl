Kevlar is a strong, heat-resistant synthetic fibre, a para-aramid related to other aramids such as Nomex and Technora. It was developed by chemist Stephanie Kwolek at DuPont in 1965 and first used commercially in the early 1970s as a replacement for steel in racing tyres. Kevlar is best known for its use in ballistic and stab-resistant body armour, and it is commonly said to be five times stronger than steel on an equal-weight basis.

Production and structure

Kevlar is spun from a liquid-crystalline solution of poly-paraphenylene terephthalamide. Its molecules are held in highly aligned sheets by strong hydrogen bonds between polymer chains, giving the fibre exceptional tensile strength and stiffness. The material is supplied as yarn, fabric, pulp and staple fibre in grades such as Kevlar 29, 49 and 149, optimised for ballistic protection, structural composites and ropes respectively.

Properties

Kevlar does not melt; it begins to decompose around 450 °C and retains much of its strength at cryogenic temperatures. It is resistant to many solvents but degrades under prolonged ultraviolet exposure and is weakened by moisture uptake and certain acids and alkalis. Its characteristic colour is golden yellow.

Applications

  • Personal armour: bulletproof vests, helmets and vehicle armour.
  • Aerospace and marine composites, aircraft cargo liners and boat hulls.
  • Ropes, cables and mooring lines; fibre-optic cable strength members.
  • Tyres, brake pads, gaskets and friction products.
  • Sporting goods: canoes, skis, bicycle tyres, drumheads and protective gloves.

History

Kwolek discovered the stiff, cloudy polymer solution while searching for a lightweight fibre for tyres amid an anticipated gasoline shortage. DuPont invested heavily in scale-up, and Kevlar's adoption in soft body armour in the 1970s is credited with saving the lives of thousands of police officers.
