Chandragupta Maurya

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The hill of Chandragiri at Shravanabelagola, traditional site of Chandragupta's death]

Chandragupta Maurya (reigned c. 321–297 BCE) was the founder of the Maurya Empire, the first great empire of ancient India. Rising — in accounts embroidered by legend — from obscure origins with the aid of the Brahmin strategist Kautilya (Chanakya), he overthrew the unpopular Nanda dynasty at Pataliputra, unified northern India, then confronted Alexander the Great's successors in the east: by treaty with Seleucus I (c. 305 BCE) he gained the trans-Indus provinces of Arachosia, Gedrosia and the Paropamisadae in exchange for 500 war elephants. Jain tradition holds that he abdicated, became a monk under Bhadrabahu, and fasted to death (sallekhana) at Shravanabelagola in the south.

Founder of empire

The sources — Greek (Megasthenes' lost Indica, surviving in quotations), Hindu (the Puranas, Vishakhadatta's much later play Mudrarakshasa), Buddhist and Jain — agree on the outline and little else. Chandragupta's empire, administered from Pataliputra with a vast standing army and the bureaucratic machinery described in the Arthashastra tradition, spanned from the Hindu Kush to Bengal and from the Himalayas to the Vindhyas. Megasthenes, Seleucus' envoy, described the capital's wooden palisade, its municipal boards and the king's heavily guarded routine to a Greek audience that found India half-fabulous.

Abdication and legacy

The Jain account of his end — migration south with Bhadrabahu during a famine, death by ritual fasting on the hill at Shravanabelagola that still bears his name (Chandragiri) — is first recorded centuries later but deeply rooted. His son Bindusara held the empire; his grandson Ashoka carried it to its greatest extent and fame. Chandragupta's unification set the template of Indian empire: a Gangetic core, a professional army, and an ideology of universal kingship (chakravartin) pursued by Guptas, Mughals and the modern republic alike.
