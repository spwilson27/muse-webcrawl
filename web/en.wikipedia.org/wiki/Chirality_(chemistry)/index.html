In chemistry, a molecule or ion is called chiral if it cannot be superposed on its mirror image by any combination of rotations, translations, and conformational changes — it has no improper rotation symmetry. The property is called chirality, from the Greek χείρ (kheir), "hand," since hands are the familiar everyday example: left and right hands are mirror images but cannot be superimposed. The two mirror forms of a chiral molecule are called enantiomers.

Stereocenters

Chirality most commonly arises from a carbon atom bonded to four different substituents — a stereogenic (stereocenter) carbon — giving a tetrahedral arrangement with two mirror-image configurations. Configurations are labelled R (rectus) or S (sinister) using the Cahn–Ingold–Prelog priority rules. Chirality can also arise without a stereocenter, as in allenes, hindered biaryls (atropisomerism), helicenes, and many coordination complexes; conversely, a molecule with several stereocenters may be achiral if it has an internal mirror plane (a meso compound).

Properties of enantiomers

Enantiomers have identical physical properties in an achiral environment — the same melting point, boiling point, and spectra — except that they rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions (optical activity) and interact differently with other chiral substances. This matters in biology: enzymes and receptors are chiral, so the two enantiomers of a drug can have very different effects. The sedative thalidomide is the notorious example — one enantiomer is therapeutic, the other teratogenic — while the two enantiomers of carvone smell of spearmint and caraway respectively. An equal mixture of enantiomers is a racemic mixture (racemate).

History

Optical activity was observed by Jean-Baptiste Biot in 1815. In 1848 Louis Pasteur separated crystals of sodium ammonium tartrate by hand into two mirror-image forms that rotated light oppositely, founding stereochemistry. The tetrahedral carbon model was proposed independently by van 't Hoff and Le Bel in 1874. The word "chiral" was introduced by Lord Kelvin in 1894.

Homochirality of life

Life on Earth uses almost exclusively L-amino acids and D-sugars, a puzzle known as the problem of homochirality. The origin of this asymmetry — whether from chance, circularly polarised light in space, or weak-force parity violation — remains an open question, and the synthesis of single enantiomers (asymmetric synthesis) is a major field recognised by the 2001 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
