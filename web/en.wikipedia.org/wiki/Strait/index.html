Strait

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A strait is a narrow passage of water connecting two larger bodies of water and lying between two landmasses. Straits are natural chokepoints: trade, navies and migrating wildlife funnel through them, and control of a strait has strategic value out of all proportion to its width.

The great straits include Gibraltar (Europe–Africa, Atlantic–Mediterranean, 14 km), the Bosphorus and Dardanelles (the Turkish Straits), Malacca (the busiest, between Malaysia and Sumatra — see Singapore), Hormuz (see Strait of Hormuz), Bab el-Mandeb, the Taiwan Strait, Bering (Asia–America, 82 km, with the Diomedes mid-way), Magellan and the Danish straits. Under the law of the sea, straits used for international navigation enjoy the regime of transit passage.

Many straits were dry land in the ice ages — Beringia joined Siberia to Alaska, Doggerland joined Britain to Europe — and their flooding as seas rose is written into the myths and the biogeography of the peoples on both shores.
