Black Sox Scandal

The Black Sox Scandal was a game-fixing scandal in Major League Baseball in which eight members of the Chicago White Sox were accused of conspiring with gamblers to intentionally lose the 1919 World Series against the Cincinnati Reds. In exchange, the players were promised money by the gamblers, an arrangement organised through figures including former player Abe Attell and allegedly financed by New York gambler Arnold Rothstein.

Background

The White Sox, owned by Charles Comiskey, were one of the strongest teams in baseball but also one of the most poorly paid; resentment over low salaries and Comiskey's frugality is often cited as a motive. First baseman Chick Gandil is generally identified as the ringleader who approached gamblers before the Series.

The fix and the Series

The Reds won the best-of-nine Series five games to three. Suspicion fell on the Series almost immediately because of unusual betting patterns and erratic play, notably by pitchers Eddie Cicotte and Lefty Williams and by the team's hitting stars.

Investigation and trial

In September 1920 a Cook County grand jury indicted eight players: Cicotte, Williams, Gandil, outfielder "Shoeless" Joe Jackson, shortstop Swede Risberg, third baseman Buck Weaver, outfielder Happy Felsch and utility player Fred McMullin. Signed confessions by Cicotte, Williams and Jackson later disappeared from the prosecutor's files. At trial in 1921 all defendants were acquitted.

Aftermath

Despite the acquittal, the club owners appointed federal judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis as the first Commissioner of Baseball, and on 3 August 1921 he banned all eight men from professional baseball for life. Weaver, who claimed he attended meetings but refused the money and played well, repeatedly appealed for reinstatement without success. The scandal remains the most famous fixing episode in American sports and permanently changed baseball's governance and its relationship with gambling.
