Director of National Intelligence

The director of national intelligence (DNI) is a senior cabinet-level United States government official, required by the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004 to serve as executive head of the United States Intelligence Community (IC) and to direct and oversee the National Intelligence Program (NIP). The DNI also serves as the principal advisor to the president of the United States, the National Security Council, and the Homeland Security Council on all intelligence matters. The DNI is appointed by the president and confirmed by the United States Senate.

History

The position was created in the wake of the September 11 attacks and the findings of the 9/11 Commission, which recommended a single official to coordinate the work of the nation's many intelligence agencies. The Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004 established the office, superseding the director of central intelligence as head of the intelligence community. John Negroponte became the first DNI in April 2005. The office is supported by the Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI), headquartered in McLean, Virginia.

Duties and powers

Subject to the direction and control of the president, the DNI's responsibilities include ensuring that national intelligence is provided to the president, department and agency heads, senior military commanders, and Congress; developing and determining the annual budget for the National Intelligence Program; and managing and directing the tasking of intelligence collection and analysis. The DNI coordinates relationships with foreign intelligence services and administers the National Counterterrorism Center, the National Counterintelligence and Security Center, the National Counterproliferation and Biosecurity Center, and the Cyber Threat Intelligence Integration Center.

Position within the intelligence community

The intelligence community comprises 18 organizations, including the Central Intelligence Agency, the National Security Agency, the Defense Intelligence Agency, and the Federal Bureau of Investigation's intelligence branch. While the DNI leads the community, most agencies remain part of executive departments such as the Department of Defense and the Department of Justice, and the DNI's budgetary and personnel authorities over them are limited compared with those of the department heads.
