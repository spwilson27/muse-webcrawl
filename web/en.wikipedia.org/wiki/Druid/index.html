A druid was a member of the learned and priestly class in the ancient Celtic cultures of Gaul, Britain, and Ireland during the Iron Age. Druids acted as priests, judges, teachers, healers, and royal advisors; they presided over sacrifices and religious rites, memorized vast bodies of verse and law, and were exempt from taxes and military service. Almost everything known about them comes from external Greco-Roman writers and from much later Irish and Welsh literature, since the druids themselves transmitted their teaching orally and left no writings of their own.

Greco-Roman accounts

Julius Caesar, in Book 6 of De Bello Gallico, gives the fullest account: the druids of Gaul held an annual assembly in the territory of the Carnutes, elected a chief druid, taught that souls are immortal and pass to another body, and trained students for up to twenty years in memorized verse—deliberately avoiding writing their doctrines down. Caesar and other authors (Diodorus Siculus, Strabo) also attribute human sacrifice to them, a claim modern scholars treat cautiously as possibly Roman propaganda, though archaeological finds such as bog bodies like Lindow Man are sometimes cited in support. Pliny the Elder famously described a rite of cutting mistletoe from an oak with a golden sickle, from which the popular image of druids derives; Tacitus describes the druids of Anglesey (Mona) confronting Suetonius Paulinus's army in AD 60 with curses and torches.

Ireland and Wales

In early Irish literature, druids (druí) appear as advisors and prophets at royal courts—figures such as Cathbad at the court of Conchobar mac Nessa—and as opponents of St Patrick in hagiography. With Christianization their priestly role vanished, though related learned classes (the filid, poets) persisted. Welsh tradition preserves figures such as Taliesin, though direct links to druidry are late and romanticized.

Etymology

The word derives from Latin druides and Greek dryidai, probably from a Celtic compound of deru- ("oak" or "firm") and weid- ("to know, see")—hence "oak-knower" or "one with firm knowledge."

Modern reception

From the seventeenth century, antiquarians such as John Aubrey and William Stukeley romantically linked druids to Stonehenge and Avebury—monuments actually thousands of years older. The eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Druid revival produced fraternal orders like the Ancient Order of Druids (1781) and Welsh cultural institutions such as the Gorsedd of Bards (1792). Modern pagan neo-druidry (e.g., the Order of Bards, Ovates and Druids, founded 1964) draws inspiration from the ancient druids but is a contemporary spirituality rather than an unbroken survival.
