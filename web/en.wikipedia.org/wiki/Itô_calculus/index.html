Itô calculus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Itô calculus, named after the Japanese mathematician Kiyosi Itô, extends the methods of calculus to stochastic processes such as Brownian motion (the Wiener process). It has important applications in mathematical finance and in stochastic differential equations. Its central concept is the Itô stochastic integral, and its central result is Itô's lemma, a stochastic generalisation of the chain rule.

The Itô integral

Because Brownian paths have unbounded variation, the integral of a process against Brownian motion cannot be defined as an ordinary pathwise (Stieltjes) integral. Itô defined the integral as a limit of Riemann sums in which the integrand is evaluated at the left endpoint of each subinterval; the resulting integral has mean zero and satisfies the Itô isometry. The left-endpoint convention makes the integral a martingale and gives it a natural interpretation for applications: in finance, the integrand is the holding decided before the price change over each interval.

Itô's lemma

For a twice-differentiable function f(t, x) of a stochastic process following dX = μdt + σdW, Itô's lemma states that f itself satisfies a stochastic differential equation containing the extra second-order term ½σ²fxxdt — reflecting the fact that Brownian motion has nonzero quadratic variation, (dW)² = dt. The lemma is the workhorse of stochastic analysis and is used, for example, in deriving the Black–Scholes equation for option pricing.

Stochastic differential equations

An Itô process driven by Brownian motion can be written as the sum of a drift integral and a diffusion (Itô) integral. Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) built on this calculus are used to model systems influenced by random noise in physics, biology, engineering, and economics. Solutions are often studied through the associated Fokker–Planck (Kolmogorov forward) equation for their probability densities.

Comparison with Stratonovich calculus

The alternative Stratonovich integral evaluates the integrand at interval midpoints, obeys the ordinary chain rule, and arises as the limit of smooth noise approximations (the Wong–Zakai theorem). The two integrals differ by a correction term involving the quadratic covariation. Itô calculus is dominant in finance and filtering theory; Stratonovich calculus is common in physics and geometry.
