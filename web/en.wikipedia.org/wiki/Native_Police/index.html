Native Police

The Native Police were paramilitary forces of Aboriginal troopers commanded by European officers that operated in several Australian colonies during the nineteenth century, most notoriously in Queensland. Similar corps existed in New South Wales, Victoria, South Australia and Western Australia, as well as in other British colonies such as Natal in southern Africa.

Queensland Native Police

The Queensland force, formally the Native Mounted Police, was formed in 1849 under Commandant Frederick Walker. Recruits were drawn from Aboriginal groups distant from the districts in which they served, so that they had no local ties to the people they policed. Detachments of troopers under white officers patrolled the pastoral frontier, "dispersing" Aboriginal groups who resisted European settlement — a term that historians have documented as a frequent euphemism for punitive expeditions and mass killings. The force was a central instrument of the Australian frontier wars and was implicated in many massacres across Queensland over more than half a century.

Other colonies

In Victoria, the Native Police Corps established in 1842 at Narre Warren, associated with figures such as Henry Dana, performed similar duties on a smaller scale. New South Wales maintained native police units from the 1830s, and the Port Phillip and Border Police also used Aboriginal troopers. Historians such as Henry Reynolds and Jonathan Richards have reconstructed the forces' role from archival records, and the Queensland force in particular remains a focus of public and scholarly debate about frontier violence.

Disbandment

The Queensland Native Police was progressively reduced and was finally disbanded around 1904, as the frontier closed and protection-era policies replaced violent dispersal with reserve-based control under the Aboriginals Protection Act 1897.
