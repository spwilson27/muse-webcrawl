Sinusoid (blood vessel)

A sinusoid is a small blood vessel similar to a capillary but with a discontinuous endothelium. Sinusoids are found in the liver, lymphoid tissue, endocrine organs, and hematopoietic organs such as the bone marrow and the spleen. Their wide, fenestrated walls with large gaps between endothelial cells allow proteins, blood cells, and other large molecules to pass between the blood and surrounding tissue — a function essential to the organs in which they occur.

Structure

Sinusoids have a larger diameter than ordinary capillaries (typically 30–40 μm wide) and follow tortuous paths. Their endothelial lining contains fenestrations grouped in sieve plates and, in the liver, lacks a continuous basal lamina. The spaces between endothelial cells allow the passage of cells and macromolecules. In the liver, the perisinusoidal space (space of Disse) lies between the endothelium and hepatocytes and contains hepatic stellate cells.

Locations and function

Liver

Hepatic sinusoids receive mixed blood from the portal vein and hepatic artery and drain into central veins. Their fenestrated endothelium and resident macrophages (Kupffer cells) allow hepatocytes to exchange lipoproteins, glucose, and other substances with the blood while old red blood cells and pathogens are filtered out.

Spleen

In the red pulp of the spleen, blood passes through splenic sinusoids lined by elongated endothelial ("stave") cells separated by slits. Red blood cells must deform to squeeze through these slits; aged or rigid cells are trapped and destroyed by macrophages, a quality-control function for circulating blood.

Bone marrow and endocrine organs

Bone-marrow sinusoids permit newly formed blood cells to enter the circulation. Sinusoidal capillaries in endocrine glands such as the adrenal cortex and anterior pituitary allow hormones to pass readily into the bloodstream.
