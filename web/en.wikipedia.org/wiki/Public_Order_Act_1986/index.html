Public Order Act 1986

The Public Order Act 1986 (c. 64) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that codified the public order offences in England and Wales, replacing older common law offences such as riot and affray with a graded series of statutory offences, and laying down controls on processions, assemblies and racial hatred.

Background

The Act followed riots in Brixton and elsewhere in 1981 and the recommendations of the Scarman Report, as well as a Law Commission review of public order law. It received Royal Assent on 7 November 1986 and came into force on 1 April 1987, replacing the Public Order Act 1936 and repealing the common law offences of riot, rout, unlawful assembly and affray.

Offences

Part I created a hierarchy of offences based on the number of participants and the level of violence: riot (section 1, twelve or more persons using or threatening unlawful violence for a common purpose), violent disorder (section 2, three or more persons), affray (section 3, an individual offence), fear or provocation of violence (section 4), and threatening, abusive or insulting words or behaviour likely to cause harassment, alarm or distress (sections 4A and 5). Section 4A was added by the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994; section 5 was amended in 2013 to remove "insulting" from the offence involving behaviour within the hearing or sight of a person likely to be caused harassment, alarm or distress.

Processions and assemblies

Part II requires organisers of public processions intended to demonstrate support for or opposition to a cause to give six clear days' notice to the police, and gives senior police officers power to impose conditions on processions and static assemblies, and, in limited circumstances, to apply for processions to be banned. The Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022 later widened police powers, including conditions triggered by serious noise disruption.

Racial hatred

Part III replaced earlier race-relations provisions with offences of using threatening, abusive or insulting words or material intended or likely to stir up racial hatred. The Racial and Religious Hatred Act 2006 added a separate Part 3A covering religious hatred, framed more narrowly. In R v Keenan and later cases the courts stressed the need to balance the offences against freedom of expression under Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights.
