Social norm

Social norms are the shared standards of acceptable behaviour in a group or society: informal understandings that govern what members do, reinforced by approval, disapproval and other social sanctions. Norms range from everyday etiquette — queuing, greeting, dress codes — to fundamental rules against harm and dishonesty. Talcott Parsons treated norms as central to social order, while later social psychologists, notably Robert Cialdini, distinguished descriptive norms (what people typically do) from injunctive norms (what people typically approve of).

Types

William Graham Sumner's classic classification graded norms by severity: folkways are conventions whose violation draws mild disapproval; mores are norms tied to moral values, whose violation draws stronger sanctions; taboos are prohibitions so strong that violation provokes disgust; and laws are norms formally codified and enforced by the state. Norms may be explicit or tacit, and groups differ in "tightness" — how many norms exist and how strictly they are enforced.

Formation and change

Norms emerge from repeated interaction, are transmitted through socialisation, and can shift rapidly when expectations about others' behaviour change — for example cascades in which people abandon an old norm once they believe others have. Experiments from Sherif's autokinetic studies to Asch's conformity experiments demonstrated the power of perceived group norms over individual judgement. Dual-process accounts note that people follow norms both to be accurate ("what is right here?") and to gain approval.
