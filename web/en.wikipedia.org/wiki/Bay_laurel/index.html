Bay laurel (Laurus nobilis), also known simply as laurel or sweet bay, is an aromatic evergreen tree or large shrub native to the Mediterranean region. Its leaves are the culinary herb known as bay leaves, and the plant has deep cultural significance from classical antiquity, when wreaths of laurel crowned victors and poets.

Description

Bay laurel grows 10–18 metres tall, with smooth bark and glossy, dark-green, lanceolate leaves 6–12 cm long that are strongly aromatic when crushed. The plant is dioecious: male and female flowers appear on separate trees. The small yellow-green flowers are followed on female plants by black, egg-shaped berries about 1 cm long, each containing a single seed.

Cultivation and uses

The tree is widely grown in Mediterranean climates and in containers elsewhere, and tolerates clipping into hedges and topiary. Dried bay leaves flavour soups, stews, sauces and pickling mixtures throughout European and Middle Eastern cuisines; the leaves are usually removed before serving. Laurel oil and the berries have traditional medicinal uses. Several other plants are sold as "bay leaf" substitutes, including Indian bay leaf (Cinnamomum tamala) and California bay laurel (Umbellularia californica).

Symbolism

In Greek myth the laurel was sacred to Apollo, into whose tree the nymph Daphne was transformed. At Rome, laurel wreaths symbolised victory and imperial authority; the association survives in the words "laureate" and "baccalaureate", and in the expression "resting on one's laurels".
