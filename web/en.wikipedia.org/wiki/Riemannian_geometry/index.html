Riemannian geometry

Riemannian geometry is the branch of differential geometry that studies Riemannian manifolds: smooth manifolds equipped with a Riemannian metric, i.e. an inner product on each tangent space that varies smoothly from point to point. This allows one to define angles, lengths of curves, volumes, and curvature.

The field originates with Bernhard Riemann's 1854 habilitation lecture "Über die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen" (On the hypotheses which underlie geometry), published posthumously in 1867. Riemann generalized the intrinsic geometry of surfaces developed by Gauss to spaces of arbitrary dimension.

Basic notions

Key concepts include the metric tensor, the Levi-Civita connection (a torsion-free connection compatible with the metric), geodesic curves (locally length-minimizing curves), and the Riemann curvature tensor, from which sectional, Ricci, and scalar curvatures are derived. The Gauss–Bonnet theorem links curvature to topology in two dimensions; the Hopf–Rinow theorem connects metric completeness with geodesic completeness.

Applications

Riemannian geometry underlies Einstein's general theory of relativity (via its pseudo-Riemannian generalization), and is used in computer graphics, robotics, medical imaging, and machine learning on manifolds.
