Hadron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Chart of the light baryons and mesons arranged in Eightfold Way multiplets]
The hadron spectrum organised by the Eightfold Way.

In particle physics, a hadron is a composite subatomic particle made of two or more quarks held together by the strong interaction. Hadrons are categorised into baryons (three quarks), mesons (quark–antiquark) and exotic hadrons (tetraquarks, pentaquarks and glueball candidates). Protons and neutrons — the constituents of atomic nuclei — are the most familiar hadrons, and the proton is the only one that appears stable in isolation.

The name, from the Greek hadros ('stout, thick'), was introduced by Lev Okun in 1962, when accelerators were discovering hundreds of strongly interacting particles — the 'particle zoo'. The quark model (Murray Gell-Mann and Zweig, 1964) tamed the zoo by showing the spectrum follows from a handful of building blocks bound by Quantum chromodynamics; the Large Hadron Collider is named for the hadrons (protons) it collides.

Mass and structure

Almost all of a hadron's mass is QCD binding energy: the proton's three valence quarks contribute only about 1% of its 938 MeV, the rest arising from the energy of the gluon field and sea-quark pairs. Lattice QCD computes the light-hadron spectrum from first principles to percent accuracy — one of the strongest confirmations that QCD is the correct theory of the strong interaction.
