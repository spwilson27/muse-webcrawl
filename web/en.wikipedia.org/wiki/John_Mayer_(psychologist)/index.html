John Daniel Mayer (born December 6, 1953) is an American psychologist at the University of New Hampshire who specializes in emotional intelligence and personality psychology. Together with Peter Salovey and David R. Caruso, he developed the influential "ability model" of emotional intelligence and co-created the Mayer–Salovey–Caruso Emotional Intelligence Test (MSCEIT). He is also known for his theory of personal intelligence.

Education and career

Mayer received his B.A. in psychology and English from the University of Michigan and his Ph.D. in clinical psychology from Case Western Reserve University. He completed postdoctoral work at Stanford University before joining the faculty of the University of New Hampshire, where he has spent most of his career as a professor of psychology.

Emotional intelligence

In 1990, Mayer and Peter Salovey published the first formal theory and definition of emotional intelligence in the scientific literature: the ability to perceive, use, understand, and manage emotions. Their four-branch ability model treats emotional intelligence as a genuine cognitive ability to be measured with performance tests — like an IQ test — rather than with self-report questionnaires. This led to the MSCEIT, published in 2002, which scores respondents on tasks such as identifying emotions in faces and solving emotional problems. Mayer has been a prominent critic of popular "mixed model" accounts of emotional intelligence that fold in broad personality traits and competencies, arguing that the term should be reserved for the mental ability itself.

Personal intelligence

From the 2010s onward, Mayer has developed the theory of personal intelligence — the capacity to reason about personality in oneself and others — described in his book Personal Intelligence: The Power of Personality and How It Shapes Our Lives (2014). With colleagues, he developed the Test of Personal Intelligence (TOPI) to measure the ability.

Selected publications

Mayer's works include the foundational 1990 paper "Emotional Intelligence" (with Salovey), Emotional Intelligence: Key Readings (2004), and numerous journal articles on personality structure, mood, and intelligence. He has also written on how personality psychology can be integrated across its levels, from traits to life stories.
