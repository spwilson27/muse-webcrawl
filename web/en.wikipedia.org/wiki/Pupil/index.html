Pupil

Pupil
Part ofEye; opening in the centre of the iris
FunctionRegulates the amount of light reaching the retina
DiameterAbout 2–4 mm in bright light; up to 8 mm in darkness (young adults)

The pupil is the black circular opening in the centre of the iris of the eye that allows light to strike the retina. It appears black because most of the light entering it is absorbed by tissues inside the eye. The pupil's size is continuously adjusted by the muscles of the iris: it constricts in bright light and during near focus (miosis) and dilates in dim light and arousal (mydriasis), thereby regulating how much light reaches the retina in much the same way as the aperture of a camera.

Structure and control

The pupil is not a structure but an aperture whose boundary is the pupillary margin of the iris. Two smooth muscles govern its diameter: the sphincter pupillae, arranged in a ring and supplied by parasympathetic fibres of the oculomotor nerve, constricts the pupil; the dilator pupillae, arranged radially and supplied by the sympathetic system, widens it. Pupil size also varies with age (smaller in the elderly), emotional state, and drugs — opioids cause pinpoint pupils, while atropine-like drugs and many stimulants cause dilation.

Pupillary reflexes

The pupillary light reflex is a key part of the neurological examination: shining light in one eye normally constricts both pupils (direct and consensual responses), via a pathway from the retina to the pretectal area and the Edinger–Westphal nuclei. The near reflex — convergence, lens accommodation and pupillary constriction when focussing on a near object — involves a separate cortical pathway. Abnormalities such as a relative afferent pupillary defect (Marcus Gunn pupil), the light-near dissociation of the Argyll Robertson pupil, or a fixed dilated pupil in third-nerve palsy help localise lesions.

In animals

Pupil shape varies widely across species. Many grazing animals have horizontal slit pupils, cats have vertical slits that can close almost completely, geckos have pupils that contract into a series of pinholes, and cuttlefish have W-shaped pupils. These shapes are adaptations to different visual needs, such as panoramic surveillance for prey animals or depth estimation and glare control for ambush predators.
