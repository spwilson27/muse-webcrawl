The Siku Quanshu (四庫全書, "Complete Library of the Four Treasuries") is the largest collection of books in Chinese history, compiled under the Qianlong Emperor of the Qing dynasty between 1772 and 1782. Some 36,381 volumes, containing about 79,000 chapters and roughly 800 million characters, copied by hand by thousands of scribes, gathered more than 3,400 complete works into four divisions named for the imperial library's treasuries: classics (jing), histories (shi), masters or philosophy (zi), and literary collections (ji).

Compilation

Announced in 1772, the project drew books from the imperial collection and from private libraries across the empire, whose owners submitted some 13,000 titles for copying or cataloguing. The editorial bureau, headed by scholars including Ji Yun and Lu Xixiong, produced the annotated catalogue Siku Quanshu Zongmu Tiyao, summarising nearly 10,000 works and long the standard bibliography of traditional Chinese literature. Seven manuscript copies of the collection were made for imperial libraries, including the Forbidden City (Wenyuan Pavilion), the Old Summer Palace, Chengde, and libraries in Shenyang, Hangzhou and Zhenjiang.

Censorship and destruction

The compilation doubled as a literary inquisition. Works deemed hostile to the Manchu Qing — especially Ming-loyalist writings and texts using derogatory terms for northern peoples — were altered, expurgated or destroyed: roughly 2,855 works were banned and burned, and many others survived only in censored form, so that the project preserved and mutilated Chinese literature simultaneously.

Survival

Three of the seven copies were destroyed (two in the Taiping Rebellion, one when the Old Summer Palace was burned in 1860); four survive, in Beijing, Taipei, Lanzhou and Hangzhou. The work has been digitised and published in modern facsimile, and scholars continue to mine it — as they did the Yongle Encyclopedia before it — for texts lost elsewhere.
