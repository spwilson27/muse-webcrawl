Small-world experiment

Small-world experiment
FieldSocial psychology, network science
Principal investigatorStanley Milgram
Date1967
Key resultAverage chain length of about six intermediaries ("six degrees of separation")

The small-world experiment was a series of studies conducted by social psychologist Stanley Milgram and other researchers in the 1960s examining the average path length for social networks of people in the United States. The results suggested that any two people in the country could be connected through a short chain of acquaintances, with a median of roughly five or six intermediaries — the origin of the popular phrase "six degrees of separation."

Milgram's 1967 study

In the best-known version of the experiment, Milgram recruited participants in the American Midwest — including residents of Wichita, Kansas, and Omaha, Nebraska — and asked them to forward a letter or folder to a target person, a stockbroker living in Sharon, Massachusetts, near Boston. Participants were given only the target's name, occupation, and general location. They were instructed to send the package only to someone they knew personally, whom they judged more likely than themselves to know the target, and each forwarder added their name to a roster so the chain could be traced.

Of the chains that reached the target, the average length was about five to six intermediaries. Milgram published the results in a 1967 article in Psychology Today and a 1969 paper co-authored with Jeffrey Travers in Sociometry.

Criticism and later work

The experiment has been criticised on several grounds: a large fraction of the packages never reached the target (in the most famous study, fewer than a third of the chains were completed), so the completed chains may not be representative; and the starting populations were not randomly sampled from the whole United States. Judith Kleinfeld's archival research also questioned how strongly the published results supported the sweeping conclusions drawn from them.

Later large-scale replications using e-mail and online social networks — notably a 2003 Columbia University study by Duncan Watts and colleagues, and analyses of platforms such as Facebook — have generally confirmed that short paths exist between most pairs of people, typically finding average separations in the range of four to seven steps.

Legacy

The small-world experiment helped launch the modern science of networks. The related concept of a small-world network — a graph in which most nodes are not neighbours but can be reached in a few steps — was formalised by Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz in 1998. The idea has influenced sociology, epidemiology, computer science, and popular culture.
