False flag

False flag operation
Origin of termNaval warfare — ships flying false colours
PurposeDeception; disguising the true source of an action
UsersStates, militaries, intelligence agencies

A false flag operation is an act committed with the intent of disguising the actual source of responsibility and pinning blame on another party. The term originates in naval warfare, where a vessel would fly the flag of a neutral or enemy country — "false colours" — to deceive others; flying a false flag was considered acceptable as a ruse before combat, provided the true colours were raised before firing.

History

Famous confirmed examples include the 1931 Mukden incident, in which Japanese officers staged an explosion on a railway in Manchuria as a pretext for invading northeastern China; the 1939 Gleiwitz incident, in which Nazi SS personnel in Polish uniforms attacked a German radio station to justify the invasion of Poland; and the 1954 Lavon Affair, in which Israeli agents planted bombs in Egypt with the intent of blaming Egyptian groups. The proposed but never executed Operation Northwoods (1962), in which US Joint Chiefs suggested staging attacks to justify war with Cuba, was rejected by President Kennedy and declassified decades later.

In law and politics

Under the laws of armed conflict, using false flags on land or in the air can constitute perfidy, while naval ruses remain partly permitted. In modern political discourse "false flag" is also frequently used in conspiracy theories about terrorist attacks and mass shootings, often without evidence; analysts caution that genuine historical false flags do not validate unverified claims.
