Cosmic ray spallation

Cosmic ray spallation is a form of naturally occurring nuclear spallation in which high-energy cosmic rays collide with atomic nuclei, knocking out many individual nucleons (protons and neutrons) and leaving a lighter nucleus behind. It occurs both in the interstellar medium during cosmic ray propagation and in Earth's atmosphere and surface rocks.

Role in cosmic abundances

Spallation is the dominant source of the rare light elements lithium, beryllium and boron, which are largely destroyed (rather than made) in stellar interiors and therefore cannot be explained by stellar nucleosynthesis. As cosmic rays (mostly protons and alpha particles, with some heavier nuclei such as carbon and oxygen) travel through the galaxy, collisions break heavier nuclei down, producing the observed cosmic-ray abundances of Li, Be and B and transforming the composition of the cosmic ray flux itself.

Cosmogenic nuclides

In Earth's atmosphere, cosmic ray spallation of nitrogen and oxygen produces cosmogenic nuclides such as carbon-14 (used in radiocarbon dating), beryllium-10 and aluminium-26. In exposed rocks these nuclides accumulate at predictable rates and are used in surface exposure dating of landforms.
