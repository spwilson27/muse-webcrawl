Satyros of Paros (Greek: Σάτυρος) was a Greek architect and sculptor of the 4th century BC, from the marble-rich island of Paros. Together with Pythius of Priene, he is named by the Roman authors Vitruvius and Pliny the Elder as the architect of the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, the tomb of King Mausolus of Caria and one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.

The Mausoleum

Commissioned by Mausolus and completed under his widow Artemisia II around 350 BC, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum) rose about 45 metres in total: a massive rectangular podium carried a peristyle of 36 Ionic columns, above which a stepped pyramid of 24 steps supported a marble quadriga bearing colossal statues of the royal couple. Vitruvius records that Satyros and Pythius also wrote a treatise (commentary) describing their work on the monument, in the tradition of Greek architects publishing accounts of their buildings — a text now lost.

Style and context

The design attributed to Satyros and Pythius fused Greek temple architecture with the high podium tombs of Lycia and the stepped pyramids of the Near East, expressing the Hecatomnid dynasty's position between the Greek and Persian worlds. The sculptural programme was executed by the four leading sculptors of the day — Scopas, Bryaxis, Leochares and Timotheus — each responsible for one face of the building.

Assessment

Almost nothing else is securely known of Satyros's life or independent works; like many Greek architects, he is known only through his great commission and a handful of literary references. His partnership with Pythius on the Mausoleum nevertheless made both men bywords for monumental design in the Roman world, and the building's fame ensured that "mausoleum" became the generic term for a grand tomb.
