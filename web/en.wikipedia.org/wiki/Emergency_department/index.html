Emergency department

An emergency department (ED), also known as an accident and emergency department (A&E), emergency room (ER), emergency ward (EW) or casualty department, is a medical treatment facility specializing in emergency medicine, the acute care of patients who present without prior appointment, either by their own means or by that of an ambulance. The emergency department is usually found in a hospital or other primary care center.

Due to the unplanned nature of patient attendance, the department must provide initial treatment for a broad spectrum of illnesses and injuries, some of which may be life-threatening and require immediate attention. In some countries, emergency departments have become important entry points for those without other means of access to medical care.

Operation

The emergency departments of most hospitals operate 24 hours a day, although staffing levels may be varied in an attempt to reflect patient volume. Because a patient may arrive at any time and with any complaint, a key part of the operation of an emergency department is the prioritization of cases in order of medical urgency. This is called triage.

Triage typically involves a brief assessment by a nurse or other health professional when a patient first arrives, assigning a priority rating based on the severity of the presenting condition. Common systems include the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) used in the United States, the Manchester Triage System used in the United Kingdom, and the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS).

Services

Emergency departments are staffed by emergency physicians, nurses, and support staff trained in resuscitation, trauma care, and the management of acute illness. Facilities vary widely: a Level I trauma center in the United States provides the highest level of surgical care to trauma patients, while small rural departments may stabilize patients for transfer to larger centers.

Typical services include treatment of heart attack and stroke, management of injuries from accidents, treatment of severe infections, and psychiatric emergency care. Many departments include dedicated areas for resuscitation, minor injuries, and observation.

History

Accident services had already been provided in workmen's compensation plans and by railway companies in the late 19th century, but the first specialized trauma care center in the world was opened in 1911 in the United States at the University of Louisville Hospital in Louisville, Kentucky. Modern emergency medicine developed as a specialty in the 1960s and 1970s, driven by advances in ambulance services, cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and the experience of military medicine in the Korean and Vietnam wars.
