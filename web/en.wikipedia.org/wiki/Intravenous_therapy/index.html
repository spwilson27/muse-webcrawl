Intravenous therapy (abbreviated as IV therapy) is a medical technique that administers fluids, medications, and nutrients directly into a person's vein. The intravenous route of administration is commonly used for rehydration or to provide nutrients for those who cannot, or will not — due to impaired mental state or otherwise — consume food or water by mouth. It may also be used to administer medications or other medical therapy such as blood products or electrolytes to correct electrolyte imbalances.

Because substances are delivered directly into the systemic circulation, intravenous administration is the fastest route for distributing fluids and drugs throughout the body, with complete bioavailability.

Uses

  • Fluid replacement — correction of dehydration, shock, and electrolyte or acid–base disturbances with crystalloids (normal saline, lactated Ringer's solution) or colloids.
  • Medication administration — antibiotics, chemotherapy, analgesia, and drugs with poor oral absorption or a need for controlled infusion rates.
  • Blood transfusion — whole blood and components such as packed red cells, platelets, and plasma.
  • Parenteral nutrition — long-term intravenous feeding for patients with non-functioning gastrointestinal tracts.

Access

The most common form of access is a peripheral intravenous cannula placed in a vein of the hand or forearm. Where long-term or central delivery is needed, clinicians use central venous catheters, peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), tunneled lines such as Hickman catheters, or implanted ports. Intraosseous access is used in emergencies when venous access cannot be obtained quickly.

Complications

Complications include infection (local cellulitis or catheter-related bloodstream infection), phlebitis, infiltration and extravasation of fluid into surrounding tissues, air embolism, hematoma, and fluid overload. Central lines carry additional risks during insertion, including pneumothorax and arterial puncture.

History

Early experiments date to the 17th century — Christopher Wren injected substances into dogs' veins in 1656 — but safe human therapy awaited sterile fluids, the hollow needle and syringe (1853), and Thomas Latta's saline infusions for cholera in 1832. Modern plastic cannulas and blood banking transformed IV therapy during the mid-20th century.
