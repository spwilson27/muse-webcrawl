Boris Fyodorovich Godunov (c. 1551 – 23 April [O.S. 13 April] 1605) was a Russian statesman and de facto regent under Tsar Feodor I, who reigned as Tsar of all Russia from 1598 to 1605. The first non-Rurikid tsar, he rose from the boyar class through his sister Irina's marriage to Feodor, and his troubled reign ended in the descent into the Time of Troubles.

Rise to power

Godunov began his career in the court of Ivan the Terrible, becoming a boyar in 1580. As brother-in-law and chief minister of the childless Feodor I, he effectively governed Russia from the mid-1580s: he secured the establishment of the Moscow Patriarchate in 1589, conducted successful wars against Sweden, fortified the southern frontier, and founded numerous towns including Samara, Saratov, Voronezh and Belgorod.

Reign

On Feodor's death in 1598 the Zemsky Sobor elected Godunov tsar. His reign began with famine in 1601–1603, in which perhaps two million Russians died; his relief measures and price controls proved inadequate and unrest spread. He was also shadowed by suspicion that he had ordered the death of Feodor's younger brother, Dmitry of Uglich, in 1591. In 1604 a pretender claiming to be Dmitry — the first False Dmitry — invaded from Poland-Lithuania with magnate and Jesuit backing. Godunov died suddenly in April 1605; his son Feodor II was murdered within weeks, and the pretender took Moscow.

Legacy

Godunov is the subject of Pushkin's verse drama Boris Godunov (1825) and Mussorgsky's opera of the same name, which have fixed his image as a guilt-haunted usurper — a judgement modern historians regard as heavily influenced by the propaganda of his successors.
