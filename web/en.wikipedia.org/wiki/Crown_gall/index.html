Crown gall is a plant disease caused by the soil bacterium Rhizobium radiobacter (better known by its former name Agrobacterium tumefaciens). The bacterium infects plants through wounds — especially at the crown (the junction of root and stem), graft unions and roots — and causes tumour-like growths called galls. Crown gall affects a very wide range of dicotyledonous plants and is an economically important disease of grapevines, stone fruits, apples, roses, walnuts and many nursery crops.

A crown gall tumour at the base of a young fruit tree.

Mechanism

Crown gall is a natural example of horizontal gene transfer between kingdoms. Virulent bacteria carry a large tumour-inducing (Ti) plasmid. When the bacterium senses phenolic compounds such as acetosyringone released from a wounded plant, its vir genes are activated; a defined segment of the plasmid, the T-DNA, is transferred into a plant cell and integrated into the plant's chromosomes. The T-DNA genes force the plant cell to divide uncontrollably (by synthesising the plant hormones auxin and cytokinin) and to produce unusual compounds called opines, which only the bacterium can use as food. Once transformed, plant cells continue to form the tumour even if the bacteria are removed.

Symptoms and impact

Young galls are soft, round and white or green; they darken and become woody and rough with age. Large or numerous galls girdle stems, disrupt water and nutrient flow, stunt young plants and can kill nursery stock. The disease is worst in nurseries and young orchards and vineyards, and on grapes a related species (Rhizobium vitis) causes serious losses in cold climates after winter injury wounds the vines.

Control

There is no cure for infected plants. Management relies on prevention: certified disease-free planting material, good sanitation and wound avoidance, resistant rootstocks, and, uniquely, biological control with the non-pathogenic strain R. radiobacter K84 (and its derivative K1026), one of the first commercially successful biocontrol agents, used as a dip for seeds, cuttings and roots at planting.

Scientific importance

Because Agrobacterium naturally transfers DNA into plant cells, scientists (notably Mary-Dell Chilton and colleagues, whose work in 1977 demonstrated T-DNA integration) adapted it into the standard tool for producing genetically modified plants: the disarmed Ti plasmid is used to deliver chosen genes, making crown gall research foundational to plant biotechnology.
