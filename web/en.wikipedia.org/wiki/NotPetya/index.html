NotPetya is a family of destructive malware that struck on 27 June 2017, initially targeting organisations in Ukraine before spreading worldwide. Although it presented itself as ransomware — a variant of the earlier Petya malware — researchers soon concluded it was in fact a wiper: it irreversibly destroyed data, and payment could not restore encrypted files. The attack caused an estimated US$10 billion in damages, making it one of the most destructive cyberattacks in history.

Attack

The initial infection vector was the software update mechanism of M.E.Doc, a tax-accounting program widely used in Ukraine; the attackers had compromised the vendor's update server. From there NotPetya spread rapidly across networks using the EternalBlue and EternalRomance SMB exploits (also used by WannaCry a month earlier), together with stolen credentials harvested by a Mimikatz-like tool and legitimate administration utilities (PsExec and WMIC), allowing it to infect even fully patched machines.

On infection, the malware encrypted the master file table and overwrote the master boot record, then forced a reboot and displayed a ransom demand of $300 in Bitcoin. Because the displayed "personal installation key" was randomly generated and unrelated to any actual decryption key, recovery by payment was effectively impossible — evidence that destruction, not extortion, was the goal.

Impact

Ukrainian government ministries, banks, the postal service, Kyiv's metro and the Chernobyl monitoring systems were hit. The malware then spread globally via multinational firms with Ukrainian offices. Major victims included shipping giant Maersk (losses of roughly $300 million; its container terminals stalled worldwide), pharmaceutical company Merck (~$870 million), FedEx subsidiary TNT Express (~$400 million), Mondelez, Reckitt Benckiser and the law firm DLA Piper. The White House later put total damages above $10 billion.

Attribution and response

In February 2018 the United Kingdom and the United States publicly attributed NotPetya to the Russian military — specifically the Sandworm group of the GRU — describing it as an attack on Ukraine that recklessly spilled over. Russia denied responsibility. In October 2020 the US Department of Justice indicted six GRU Unit 74455 officers in connection with NotPetya and other operations. The attack also triggered landmark insurance litigation (notably Merck's claim against insurers who invoked a "war exclusion"), in which courts ruled the exclusion did not apply.
