Self-similar solution

In the study of partial differential equations, a self-similar solution is a solution in which the shape of the profile does not change in time, except for a rescaling of the spatial and dependent variables. Self-similar solutions arise naturally in problems without a natural length or time scale, and they reduce the partial differential equation to an ordinary differential equation in a similarity variable.

Examples

The classic example is the Sedov–Taylor solution for a strong spherical blast wave: the shock radius grows as R ∝ (Et²/ρ)1/5, where E is the released energy and ρ the ambient density, and the pressure, density and velocity profiles are functions of the single variable r/R(t). Other examples include the diffusion of a localised pulse under the heat equation, boundary-layer flow, and gravitational collapse solutions in astrophysics.

Self-similarity of the first and second kind

G. I. Barenblatt distinguished self-similarity of the first kind, where scaling exponents follow from dimensional analysis, from self-similarity of the second kind, where the exponents are eigenvalues determined by solving a nonlinear problem, often associated with incomplete similarity and intermediate asymptotics.

Applications

Self-similar solutions describe the long-time, intermediate behaviour of many physical systems — turbulent jets, porous-medium flow, and structure formation in cosmology — even when exact solutions of the full problem are unavailable.
