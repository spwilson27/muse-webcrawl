Lapse rate

The lapse rate is the rate at which an atmospheric variable, normally the temperature in Earth's atmosphere, falls with altitude. It corresponds to the vertical component of the spatial temperature gradient. Although the concept applies to any planetary atmosphere, it is most often used for the troposphere, where temperature generally decreases with height.

Types

  • Environmental lapse rate — the actual observed rate at a given time and place, averaging globally about 6.5 °C per kilometre in the standard atmosphere.
  • Dry adiabatic lapse rate — the rate at which a rising parcel of unsaturated air cools by expansion, about 9.8 °C per kilometre; it follows directly from the first law of thermodynamics and hydrostatic balance.
  • Saturated (moist) adiabatic lapse rate — for saturated air, typically around 4–6 °C per kilometre, because condensing water vapour releases latent heat and partly offsets the cooling.

Significance

Comparing the environmental lapse rate with the adiabatic rates determines atmospheric stability: if the environment cools faster than a rising parcel, the air is unstable and convection develops; temperature inversions — a negative lapse rate in which temperature rises with height — trap pollution near the ground. Lapse rates also govern the climatic difference between sea-level and mountain climates, and changes in the tropical lapse rate are used as a fingerprint of climate warming because moist processes amplify warming aloft.
