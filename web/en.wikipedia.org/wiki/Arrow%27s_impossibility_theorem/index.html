Arrow's impossibility theorem

Arrow's impossibility theorem
FieldSocial choice theory, economics
Proved byKenneth Arrow
Year1950–1951
Published inSocial Choice and Individual Values (1951)

Arrow's impossibility theorem is a fundamental result in social choice theory showing that no rank-order electoral system can be designed to satisfy a set of seemingly reasonable "fairness" criteria simultaneously, when there are three or more distinct alternatives. It was proved by economist Kenneth Arrow in his 1951 book Social Choice and Individual Values.

Statement

Suppose each voter ranks a set of alternatives. A social welfare function aggregates these individual rankings into a single collective ranking. Arrow showed that if the function satisfies unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, and independence of irrelevant alternatives, and produces a complete transitive ranking for every profile of individual preferences, then it must be a dictatorship: one voter's ranking always determines the outcome, regardless of everyone else.

The criteria

Unrestricted domain: the rule must work for any possible combination of individual preference orderings. Pareto efficiency: if every voter prefers A to B, society must rank A above B. Independence of irrelevant alternatives: the social ranking of A versus B depends only on voters' rankings of A versus B. Non-dictatorship: no single voter always prevails. Arrow proved these four conditions are mutually incompatible.

Interpretation and impact

The theorem does not say democracy is impossible; it shows that every voting rule involves trade-offs among desirable properties. It launched the modern field of social choice theory and helped earn Arrow the 1972 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences. Related results include the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem on strategic voting.

Ways around the theorem

Cardinal voting systems (such as range voting) escape the theorem by using more than ordinal rankings. Restricting preferences, for example to single-peaked preferences, also permits non-dictatorial rules, as in the median voter theorem.
