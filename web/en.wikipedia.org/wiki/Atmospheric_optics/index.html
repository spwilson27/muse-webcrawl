Atmospheric optics is the branch of physics and meteorology that studies how the distinctive optical properties of Earth's atmosphere cause a wide variety of optical phenomena. Light is refracted, reflected, scattered and diffracted by air, water droplets, ice crystals and aerosols to produce effects ranging from the everyday rainbow to rare arcs and glories.

Phenomena

  • Rainbows — refraction and internal reflection in raindrops, appearing at an angular radius of about 42° (primary) and 51° (secondary).
  • Halos — rings and arcs produced by hexagonal ice crystals in cirrus clouds, including the common 22° halo, sundogs (parhelia) and circumzenithal arc.
  • Scattering effects — the blue colour of the sky (Rayleigh scattering), red sunsets, and the twilight colours.
  • Mirages — bending of light in air layers of differing temperature, including the inferior mirage of hot roads and the superior mirage and Fata Morgana.
  • Glories and coronae — diffraction effects seen around shadows on clouds and around the Sun or Moon.
  • Other — green flash, crepuscular rays, airglow and aurorae.

History

Descartes gave the first quantitative account of the rainbow in 1637; Newton explained its colours. The scattering theory of Lord Rayleigh (1871) and Gustav Mie's solution for spheres (1908) underpin modern calculations, which are now performed with ray-tracing and Monte Carlo simulations of crystal habits.
