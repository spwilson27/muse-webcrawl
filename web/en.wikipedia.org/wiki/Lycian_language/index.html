Lycian is an extinct Anatolian language of the Indo-European family, spoken in Lycia in southwestern Anatolia (modern Turkey) during the late Iron Age. It is known from about 170 inscriptions, mostly funerary texts on rock-cut tombs and sarcophagi, dating mainly from the fifth and fourth centuries BC, and from legends on coins.

Classification and decipherment

Lycian is closely related to Luwian and belongs to the Luwic subgroup of Anatolian; a second variety, Milyan (sometimes called "Lycian B"), is attested in a small number of inscriptions and may be a distinct dialect or sister language. The Lycian alphabet derives from a Greek alphabet but includes additional letters for sounds such as nasal vowels. Decipherment proceeded through bilingual and trilingual inscriptions, most famously the Letoon trilingual (Lycian, Greek, and Aramaic, c. 337 BC) and the Greek–Lycian bilingual tomb texts.

Features

Lycian preserves characteristic Anatolian traits, including genitival adjectives in place of simple genitives, and developed distinctive features such as nasalised vowels (written with special letters) and extensive vowel umlaut. Typical tomb inscriptions follow a formula: "This tomb X built; [he is buried] with his wife and children; and if anyone harms it, may the gods punish him."
