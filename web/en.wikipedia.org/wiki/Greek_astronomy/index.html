Greek astronomy

The Ptolemaic system: planets move on epicycles carried on deferents about the Earth.

Greek astronomy is astronomy written in the Greek language during classical antiquity, from the pre-Socratic philosophers of the sixth century BCE to the astronomers of Roman Egypt in the second century CE. Building on Babylonian observations and Egyptian calendars, the Greeks transformed astronomy into a mathematical, model-building science — seeking geometrical schemes that would "save the phenomena" of the heavens — and thereby laid the foundations of Western astronomy. Greek words survive in the names of stars, constellations and technical terms such as planet ("wanderer").

Early developments

Thales of Miletus was credited in antiquity with predicting the solar eclipse of 585 BCE, though modern scholars doubt the story. The Pythagoreans placed the heavenly bodies on spheres and imagined a "central fire"; Anaximander pictured the stars as rings of fire. By the fourth century BCE, Plato's Academy set the programme of explaining planetary motions by combinations of uniform circular motions. Eudoxus of Cnidus constructed the first such system, of 27 homocentric spheres; Callippus refined it, and Aristotle adopted a version of it as physical cosmology, filling the heavens with aether.

Hellenistic astronomy

Aristarchus of Samos (c. 310–230 BCE) proposed a heliocentric system, placing the Sun at the centre — a view reported by Archimedes in The Sand Reckoner and rejected by contemporaries, partly for want of observable stellar parallax. Aristarchus also estimated the sizes and distances of the Sun and Moon. Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy. Apollonius of Perga introduced the epicycle and the eccentric as equivalent geometric devices, and Hipparchus of Nicaea (c. 190–120 BCE) combined Greek geometry with Babylonian data and arithmetic methods to create quantitative astronomy: he compiled a star catalogue, estimated the Moon's distance, and discovered the precession of the equinoxes.

Ptolemy and the Almagest

The culmination of Greek astronomy is the Almagest of Claudius Ptolemy (2nd century CE), a comprehensive treatise of mathematical astronomy including a star catalogue of 1,022 stars, theories of the Sun, Moon and planets using deferents, epicycles and the controversial equant, and tables enabling prediction of eclipses and planetary positions. Ptolemy's geocentric model remained the standard cosmology in the Islamic world and Europe until the work of Copernicus in the sixteenth century. Instruments such as the armillary sphere, the astrolabe and the geared Antikythera mechanism — an analogue computer of about 100 BCE that predicted eclipses and calendar cycles — demonstrate the sophistication of Greek astronomical practice.

Legacy

Greek astronomical texts were translated into Syriac and Arabic and developed further by Islamic astronomers, whose criticisms of Ptolemy's models helped prepare the way for the Copernican revolution. Most Greek constellation names, the zodiac, and the 48 Ptolemaic constellations remain in use today.
