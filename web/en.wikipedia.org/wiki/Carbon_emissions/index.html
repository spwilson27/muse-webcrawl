Carbon emissions

Emissions from a coal-fired power station. (Image placeholder)

Carbon emissions is shorthand for the release of carbon-containing greenhouse gases — principally carbon dioxide (CO₂) and methane (CH₄) — into the atmosphere, measured in tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent. Carbon dioxide emissions arise mainly from the burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) for energy and transport, from industrial processes such as cement and steel production, and from land-use change, especially deforestation. They are the dominant human contribution to climate change.

Sources and trends

Global CO₂ emissions from fossil fuels and industry have risen from around 6 billion tonnes in 1950 to roughly 37 billion tonnes per year in the early 2020s, with additional emissions of several billion tonnes from land-use change. The largest emitting sectors are electricity and heat generation, transport, manufacturing and construction, buildings, and agriculture (the latter especially for methane and nitrous oxide).

Historically, the United States and Europe account for the largest share of cumulative emissions. In recent decades China has become the largest annual emitter, followed by the United States, India and the European Union, though per-capita emissions remain highest in wealthy fossil-fuel-producing and consuming states. Total emissions are often summarised by the Kaya identity, which decomposes them into population, GDP per capita, energy intensity and the carbon intensity of energy.

Measurement

Emissions are reported in national inventories prepared under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change, typically divided into scope 1 (direct), scope 2 (from purchased energy) and scope 3 (other indirect) emissions for organisations. The concept of a carbon footprint applies the same accounting to products, companies and individuals.

Reduction

Reducing carbon emissions — decarbonisation — involves switching to low-carbon energy (renewables, nuclear), improving energy efficiency, electrifying transport and heating, reducing demand for emissions-intensive goods, and capturing residual emissions through carbon capture and storage or biological sequestration. The Paris Agreement commits parties to limit warming to well below 2 °C, which assessments indicate requires global CO₂ emissions to reach net zero around mid-century.
