Sleepwalking

Other namesSomnambulism, noctambulism
SpecialtySleep medicine, psychiatry
ClassificationNREM-related parasomnia (ICSD-3)

Sleepwalking, also known as somnambulism or noctambulism, is a phenomenon of combined sleep and wakefulness. It is classified as a sleep disorder belonging to the parasomnia family. It occurs during slow wave sleep stage, in a state of low consciousness, and features performance of activities that are usually done during a state of full consciousness.

Signs and symptoms

Episodes usually occur in the first third of the night, emerge from stage N3 (deep non-REM) sleep, and last from a few seconds to about half an hour. The sleepwalker may simply sit up and look around, or may walk, dress, eat, or leave the house, typically with open but glassy eyes and a blank expression. Recall of the episode afterwards is usually absent or fragmentary. Complex behaviours reported include cooking, driving and, rarely, violence — historically raised as a legal defence in criminal trials.

Causes

Sleepwalking is much more common in children (prevalence estimates of 10–30%) than adults (about 1.5–4%) and usually resolves by adolescence. It has a strong genetic component and is triggered by sleep deprivation, fever, alcohol, stress and certain medications, including sedative hypnotics such as zolpidem.

Treatment

Management focuses on safety — securing windows and doors, removing hazards — and on regular sleep schedules. Most children require no further treatment. In adults, evaluation may include polysomnography to exclude other disorders such as REM sleep behaviour disorder or nocturnal epilepsy.
