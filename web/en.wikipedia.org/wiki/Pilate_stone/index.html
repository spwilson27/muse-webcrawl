Pilate stone

The Pilate stone is a damaged limestone block bearing a Latin inscription that names Pontius Pilate, the prefect of Judaea who, according to the Gospels, presided at the trial of Jesus. Discovered in 1961 at Caesarea Maritima in Israel, it is the only archaeological find that mentions Pilate by name, and it confirms his title of praefectus (prefect). The original is held by the Israel Museum in Jerusalem; a replica stands at Caesarea.

Discovery

The stone was found during Italian excavations of the Roman theatre at Caesarea in June 1961, directed by Antonio Frova. It had been reused in late antiquity as a step in a staircase added to the theatre, which wore away part of the inscription but preserved the crucial lines.

Inscription

The surviving text, in four lines, is conventionally reconstructed as:

[DIS AUGUSTI]S TIBERIÉUM
[...PO]NTIUS PILATUS
[...PRAEF]ECTUS IUDA[EA]E
[...FECIT D]É[DICAVIT]

It records that Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea, built and dedicated a Tiberiéum — a building (probably a temple or shrine) in honour of the emperor Tiberius and the deified Augustus.

Significance

Before 1961, Pilate was known only from literary sources — the New Testament, Philo, Josephus and Tacitus. The inscription provided the first epigraphic evidence for his existence and office, and settled a scholarly debate by showing that his rank was prefect, not the procurator used anachronistically by Tacitus.
