Karst

Karst is a topography formed from the dissolution of soluble carbonate rocks such as limestone, dolomite and gypsum. It is characterised by underground drainage systems with sinkholes, caves, disappearing streams and large springs. The term derives from the Karst Plateau (Kras) of southwestern Slovenia and northeastern Italy, where such landscapes were first systematically studied. Karst terrain covers roughly a fifth of the Earth's ice-free land surface, and karst aquifers supply drinking water to a large share of the world's population.

Formation

Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the air and soil, forming weak carbonic acid that dissolves calcium carbonate along joints and bedding planes. Over thousands to millions of years this enlarges fissures into conduits and caverns; surface drainage is captured underground, leaving dry valleys, dolines (sinkholes) and, in the tropics, dramatic cone and tower karst such as that of Guilin in China and Ha Long Bay in Vietnam.

Features

Typical karst features include lapies (grooved rock surfaces), poljes (large flat-floored basins), ponors (where streams sink), resurgences, stalactites and stalagmites, and natural bridges. Cave systems in karst regions are among the longest in the world — Mammoth Cave in Kentucky exceeds 680 km of surveyed passages.

Significance and hazards

Karst areas present engineering challenges: rapid groundwater flow spreads contamination quickly, and cover-collapse sinkholes can damage buildings, as seen in parts of Florida and China. Conversely, karst towers and cave systems are major tourist attractions and refuges for endemic cave-adapted species.
