Deinterlacing

Deinterlacing is the process of converting interlaced video into a non-interlaced or progressive form, in which each frame consists of a complete image rather than alternating fields of odd and even lines. It is required when displaying older television material — such as 576i and 1080i broadcasts — on progressive displays such as computer monitors and modern flat-panel televisions.

Background

Interlaced video was developed to reduce broadcast bandwidth while maintaining refresh rate: each frame is split into two fields, captured at different instants. When the fields are displayed as a single frame without processing, motion between fields produces horizontal "combing" (or "tearing") artefacts along moving edges.

Methods

  • Weaving — simply combines two fields into one frame. Works perfectly for static images but produces combing on motion.
  • Bobbing — scales each field to full frame height by interpolation (line doubling), preserving motion smoothness at the cost of vertical resolution.
  • Blending — averages pairs of fields, avoiding combing but creating ghosting on motion.
  • Motion compensation — analyses motion between fields and reconstructs a full frame by aligning moving objects; the most accurate and most computationally expensive approach, used in quality scalers and broadcast converters.
  • Inverse telecine — for film material transferred to video, the original progressive frames can often be perfectly reconstructed by re-matching the fields.

Applications

Deinterlacing is performed by televisions, media players, video editing software, and open-source processing tools such as FFmpeg (which includes filters such as yadif and bwdif), and it is an important step in the upscaling and archiving of legacy broadcast material.
