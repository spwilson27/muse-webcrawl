Abdelkader ibn Muhieddine (6 September 1808 – 26 May 1883), known as the Emir Abdelkader, was an Algerian religious and military leader who led a struggle against the French colonial invasion of Algiers in the early 19th century. A Sufi scholar and statesman, he is regarded as the founder of the modern Algerian state and a national hero.

Resistance

After France seized Algiers in 1830, tribes in western Algeria proclaimed the young Abdelkader emir in 1832. He built a functioning state with a capital (eventually mobile, the Smala), a regular army, taxation, and diplomacy. The Treaty of Desmichels (1834) and the Treaty of Tafna (1837) temporarily recognized his authority over much of the interior. War resumed in 1839; French forces under General Bugeaud waged a brutal scorched-earth campaign, captured the Smala in 1843, and, after defeat in Morocco at Isly (1844), Abdelkader surrendered in 1847.

Exile

Imprisoned in France until 1852, he was released by Napoleon III and settled in Damascus. There he devoted himself to scholarship and Sufism. During the 1860 anti-Christian riots in Damascus he sheltered thousands of Christians, an act that won him honors worldwide, including from France, the United States, and the Vatican.

Legacy

Abdelkader died in Damascus in 1883; his remains were repatriated to Algeria in 1966. The town of Elkader, Iowa, is named after him.
