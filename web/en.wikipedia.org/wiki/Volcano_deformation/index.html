Volcano deformation

Interferometric radar reveals centimetre-scale swelling of a volcano

Volcano deformation refers to changes in the shape of a volcano and the surrounding ground caused by subsurface processes, principally the movement and pressurisation of magma, hydrothermal fluids and gas. Measuring deformation — uplift, subsidence and horizontal displacement, often summarised as inflation and deflation — is one of the principal methods of volcano monitoring and eruption forecasting.

Causes

When magma accumulates in a reservoir beneath a volcano, the ground above typically swells outward and upward; when magma erupts or drains away, the surface subsides. Deformation can also result from the intrusion of dikes and sills, pressurisation of hydrothermal systems, flank instability and landsliding, faulting, and loading by new lava. The classic model of a point pressure source in an elastic half-space (the Mogi model, 1958) relates surface uplift and radial displacement to the depth and volume change of the source and is still widely used to interpret observations.

Measurement techniques

Historical methods include levelling, tiltmeters and dry tilt arrays, and electronic distance measurement. Modern monitoring relies on continuous GNSS (GPS) receivers that record millimetre-level positions, and on satellite radar interferometry (InSAR), which maps deformation over whole volcanic regions between satellite passes. The dramatic bulging of Mount St. Helens' north flank in 1980 — growing at up to 1.5 m per day before it collapsed in the eruption of 18 May — remains the most famous example of deformation signalling an imminent eruption. Uplift at calderas such as Campi Flegrei (bradyseism) and Yellowstone is tracked closely to distinguish magmatic from hydrothermal causes. Not all deformation leads to eruption, so it is interpreted together with seismicity and gas emissions.
