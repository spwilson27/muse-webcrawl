Alexander Alexandrovich Blok (Russian: Александр Александрович Блок; 28 November 1880 – 7 August 1921) was a Russian lyric poet and dramatist, generally considered the greatest of the Russian Symbolists. His work was greatly admired by his contemporaries, and his verse, marked for its musicality and mystical imagery, had a profound influence on 20th-century Russian poetry.

Life

Blok was born in Saint Petersburg into a cultivated family: his father was a law professor and his maternal grandfather, Andrey Beketov, was rector of Saint Petersburg University. In 1903 he married Lyubov Mendeleyeva, daughter of the chemist Dmitri Mendeleev; she became the inspiration for his first book, Verses about the Beautiful Lady (1904), which cast his love in the mystical language of Vladimir Solovyov's doctrine of Sophia, the eternal feminine.

His later collections — Unexpected Joy (1907), and cycles such as "The Snow Mask" and "The Terrible World" — turned to urban themes, disillusion and ironic self-scrutiny. He also wrote verse dramas, including The Fair Show Booth (1906), staged by Vsevolod Meyerhold. Initially welcoming the October Revolution of 1917 as a purifying elemental force, Blok wrote his two most famous late works in early 1918: the essay "The Intelligentsia and the Revolution" and the long poem The Twelve, in which twelve Red Guards march through revolutionary Petrograd, with Jesus Christ pictured at their head — one of the most discussed images in Russian literature. Disillusioned and exhausted, he wrote little more, and died in Petrograd in August 1921, shortly before he was granted permission to travel abroad for medical treatment.

Legacy

Blok's poetry is central to the "Silver Age" of Russian literature. Anna Akhmatova, Marina Tsvetaeva and Boris Pasternak all acknowledged his influence, and his intonation — a blend of song, prayer and street speech — shaped generations of Russian poets.
