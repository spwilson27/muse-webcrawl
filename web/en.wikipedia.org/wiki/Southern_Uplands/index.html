The Southern Uplands are a range of hills in the southern part of Scotland and the northernmost part of England, forming the southernmost of Scotland's three main geographical regions (with the Central Lowlands and the Highlands). They run roughly 220 kilometres from the North Sea coast near Berwick-upon-Tweed westward to the Irish Sea coast of Galloway and Dumfriesshire, reaching into northern Cumbria and Northumberland.

Geography

The uplands consist of rolling, heather- and grass-covered hills dissected by broad valleys such as those of the Tweed, Annan, Nith and Clyde. Individual massifs include the Lammermuir Hills, the Moorfoot Hills, the Lowther Hills and the Galloway Hills, where the highest summit, the Merrick (843 m), stands. The region is sparsely populated; its towns — Peebles, Moffat, Dumfries, Galashiels and Hawick among them — lie mostly in the valleys.

Geology

The hills are built largely of hard Ordovician and Silurian sedimentary rocks — greywackes, shales and mudstones — deposited in the Iapetus Ocean and crumpled during the Caledonian orogeny. The Southern Uplands Fault, running the length of the region's northern edge, separates the uplands from the Midland Valley. Younger granite plutons, such as those of Criffel and Cairnsmore of Fleet, form some of the wilder western scenery.

Land use and culture

Sheep farming, commercial forestry (including the vast Galloway Forest Park, a Dark Sky Park) and grouse moor dominate the landscape. The region's woollen textile mills, Border ballads and reiver history colour its identity, and the Southern Upland Way, Scotland's first official coast-to-coast long-distance footpath, crosses the range from Portpatrick to Cockburnspath.
