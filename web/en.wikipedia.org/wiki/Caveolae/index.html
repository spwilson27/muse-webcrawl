Caveolae

In biology, caveolae (Latin for "little caves"; singular, caveola), which are a special type of lipid raft, are small (50–100 nanometre) flask-shaped invaginations of the plasma membrane in the cells of many vertebrates. They are composed primarily of glycosphingolipids and the integral membrane proteins caveolins, and they are especially abundant in endothelial cells, adipocytes, and muscle cells.

Structure

The caveolin family (caveolin-1, -2, and -3) consists of hairpin-loop proteins that oligomerise and bind cholesterol, driving the membrane curvature that gives caveolae their characteristic omega or flask shape. A coat of cavin family proteins on the cytosolic face helps stabilise the structure.

Functions

Caveolae are involved in endocytosis and transcytosis, signal transduction (concentrating receptors such as the insulin receptor and endothelial nitric oxide synthase), cholesterol homeostasis, and mechanosensing — flattening out when the membrane is stretched, thereby buffering membrane tension.

Clinical significance

Mutations in caveolin and cavin genes cause lipodystrophies, muscular dystrophies, and cardiac disorders. Certain viruses, including SV40, exploit caveolae as an entry route into cells.
