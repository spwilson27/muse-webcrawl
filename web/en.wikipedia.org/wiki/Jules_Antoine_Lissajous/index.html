Jules Antoine Lissajous

Jules Antoine Lissajous (4 March 1822 – 24 June 1880) was a French physicist best known for the Lissajous figures, patterns produced by combining two perpendicular harmonic oscillations.

Life

Lissajous was born in Versailles and studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, receiving his doctorate in 1847. He taught physics at the Lycée Saint-Louis and later served as rector of academies in Chambéry and Besançon.

Work on sound and vibration

Lissajous devised an optical method for visualizing sound vibrations: he mounted small mirrors on tuning forks and reflected a beam of light from them, projecting the combined motion onto a screen. When two forks vibrate at right angles, the resulting curves — Lissajous figures — reveal the frequency ratio of the tones, allowing precise comparison of pitch standards. His memoir on the optical study of vibration was published in 1857, and the method was used in establishing a standard concert pitch in France.

Legacy

Lissajous figures appear today on oscilloscopes, in laser shows, and in mathematics as examples of parametric curves. The asteroid 9478 and the Lissajous orbit used by space observatories such as Gaia and the James Webb Space Telescope at Lagrange points also bear related names.
