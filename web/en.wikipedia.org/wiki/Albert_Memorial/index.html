Albert Memorial

The Albert Memorial, Kensington Gardens

DesignerGeorge Gilbert Scott
TypeGothic Revival memorial

The Albert Memorial, directly north of the Royal Albert Hall in Kensington Gardens, London, was commissioned by Queen Victoria in memory of her beloved husband Prince Albert, who died of typhoid fever in 1861. Designed by Sir George Gilbert Scott in the Gothic Revival style, it was opened in 1872, with the statue of Albert — by John Henry Foley — ceremonially seated in 1876.

Design

The memorial takes the form of an ornate canopy or ciborium, 176 feet (54 m) tall, sheltering a gilded seated statue of the Prince, who holds the catalogue of the Great Exhibition of 1851. The canopy rests on a broad podium decorated with the Frieze of Parnassus, depicting 169 life-size figures of celebrated painters, poets, sculptors, musicians and architects.

At the four corners of the podium stand allegorical groups representing the four continents — Europe, Asia, Africa and America — while four further groups symbolise Victorian industry and enterprise: Agriculture, Manufactures, Commerce and Engineering. The mosaics, enamels, polished stones and semi-precious materials make the memorial one of the most elaborate monuments of the Victorian age.

History

Scott's design won an invited competition in 1863, and construction took more than ten years at a cost of about £120,000, raised largely by public subscription and a parliamentary grant. The surrounding area of South Kensington — developed with the profits of the Great Exhibition and filled with museums and colleges — became informally known as "Albertopolis". The memorial was comprehensively restored in the 1990s, when the gilding of the statue and canopy was renewed.
