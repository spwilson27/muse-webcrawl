C. S. Lewis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: C. S. Lewis in his Oxford rooms: tweeds, pipe in hand, a wardrobe of fur coats dimly visible behind him in jest]

Clive Staples Lewis (29 November 1898 – 22 November 1963) was a British writer, literary scholar and Anglican lay theologian. He held academic positions in English literature at both Magdalen College, Oxford (1925–1954) and Magdalene College, Cambridge (1954–1963). He is best known as the author of The Chronicles of Narnia, but he is also noted for his other works of fiction, such as The Screwtape Letters and The Space Trilogy, and for his non-fiction Christian apologetics.

A close friend of J. R. R. Tolkien and fellow Inkling, Lewis returned to Christianity in 1931 (describing himself as 'the most dejected and reluctant convert in all England'). His wartime radio talks became Mere Christianity. The seven Narnia books (1950–56), beginning with The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, have sold over 120 million copies and defined children's fantasy for generations. He died on the same day as John F. Kennedy and Aldous Huxley, a coincidence that eclipsed his obituaries.
