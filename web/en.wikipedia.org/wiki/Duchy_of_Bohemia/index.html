Duchy of Bohemia

Prague Castle, seat of the Bohemian dukes

The Duchy of Bohemia, also referred to as the Czech Duchy (Czech: České knížectví), was a monarchy and a principality of the Holy Roman Empire in Central Europe during the Early and High Middle Ages. It was formed around 870 by the Czechs of the Přemyslid dynasty on the territory of present-day Bohemia, and endured — through periods of fragmentation and imperial dependence — until it was elevated to the hereditary Kingdom of Bohemia in 1198.

Origins

Bohemia emerged from the collapse of Great Moravia around the turn of the tenth century. Bořivoj I, the first historically documented duke, was baptised (traditionally by Methodius) and founded Prague Castle around 870. His descendants — including Spytihněv I and Vratislaus I — consolidated Přemyslid rule. The reign of Wenceslaus I (Saint Wenceslas, murdered by his brother Boleslaus in 935 or 929) is surrounded by legend and by the continuing national veneration of the saint.

Within the Empire

Bohemian dukes acknowledged, resisted and negotiated imperial overlordship: Henry I of Germany imposed tribute in 929, and Bohemia became part of the East Frankish–German realm, while retaining its Slavic dynasty, church organisation (the Prague bishopric was founded in 973) and considerable autonomy. Boleslaus I and Boleslaus II expanded the duchy to its greatest medieval extent, temporarily controlling parts of Poland, Silesia and Slovakia.

Towards kingship

Bohemian rulers twice received temporary royal crowns — Vratislaus II in 1085 and Vladislaus II in 1158 — before Ottokar I secured the hereditary royal title in 1198, confirmed by the Golden Bull of Sicily in 1212. The duchy also came to hold one of the seven electoral votes of the Empire in the thirteenth century, a dignity formalised in the Golden Bull of 1356.
