Orthogonal group

The orthogonal group O(2) consists of all rotations and reflections of the circle.

In mathematics, the orthogonal group in dimension n, denoted O(n), is the group of distance-preserving transformations of a Euclidean space of dimension n that preserve a fixed point, where the group operation is given by composing transformations. Equivalently, it is the group of n × n orthogonal matrices, where the group operation is given by matrix multiplication. (An orthogonal matrix is a real matrix whose inverse equals its transpose.)

More generally, orthogonal groups are defined over arbitrary fields and for arbitrary non-degenerate quadratic forms; they are classic examples of algebraic groups and Lie groups.

Properties

An orthogonal matrix Q satisfies QTQ = QQT = I. Its determinant is either +1 or −1. The subgroup of matrices with determinant +1 forms the special orthogonal group SO(n), consisting of the rotations, while determinant −1 elements are improper rotations involving a reflection. The orthogonal group has two connected components; SO(n) is its identity component. The dimension of O(n) as a Lie group is n(n − 1)/2.

Low-dimensional examples

O(1) = {±1} and SO(1) is trivial. SO(2) is the group of rotations of the plane, isomorphic to the circle group; it is abelian. SO(3), the rotation group of three-dimensional space, is parametrised by axis-angle and by unit quaternions, which give its double cover SU(2) — a fact of central importance for the quantum mechanics of spin. SO(4) is exceptional in being a product-like group: its double cover is SU(2) × SU(2).

Related groups

The indefinite orthogonal groups O(p, q) preserve a quadratic form of signature (p, q); the Lorentz group O(1, 3) of special relativity is the most famous example. The complex and quaternionic analogues, the unitary and symplectic groups, complete the family of classical groups. Orthogonal groups also arise as the structure groups of Riemannian geometry, since the frame bundle of a Riemannian manifold reduces to O(n).
