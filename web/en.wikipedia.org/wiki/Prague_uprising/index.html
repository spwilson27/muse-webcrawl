The Prague uprising (Czech: Pražské povstání) was a partially successful attempt by the Czech resistance to liberate the city of Prague from German occupation at the end of the Second World War. It began on 5 May 1945, in the war's final days, and lasted until a ceasefire on 8 May, hours before the Red Army entered the city on 9 May. Roughly 1,700 Czechs and some 1,000 German soldiers were killed; some 1,600 barricades were erected across the city.

Citizens manning a barricade in Prague, May 1945.

Background

Prague had been under Nazi occupation since March 1939 as the capital of the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia. By early May 1945 Germany was collapsing: Berlin had fallen, and American forces under General Patton had reached Plzeň in western Bohemia but halted at an agreed demarcation line. On 5 May, after Czech-language radio staff resisted orders and rumours spread of approaching liberation, the Czech National Council called for revolt, and Prague Radio broadcast appeals for help in Czech — and later in English and Russian.

The uprising

Citizens tore down German signs, raised Czechoslovak flags, and built barricades; lightly armed resistance fighters, police and gendarmes seized key buildings including the radio station, which the SS attacked fiercely. German forces, including Waffen-SS units, counterattacked with armour and aircraft, and massacres of civilians occurred in several districts. On 6–7 May the insurgents received unexpected aid from the Russian Liberation Army of General Andrey Vlasov, whose division, turning against the Germans, fought in the south and west of the city before withdrawing westward toward the Americans.

End

On 8 May the Czech National Council negotiated a ceasefire permitting German forces to withdraw westward, in exchange for sparing the city further destruction. Germany's general surrender took effect that night. The Red Army entered Prague on the morning of 9 May, meeting little resistance.

Legacy

The uprising is commemorated as a symbol of Czech resistance; its politics — including the roles of the Vlasov army, the Western Allies' halt, and the Soviet liberation — remained sensitive throughout the communist period and are still debated by historians.
