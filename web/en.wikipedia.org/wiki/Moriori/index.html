The Moriori are the indigenous Polynesian people of the Chatham Islands (Rēkohu in the Moriori language), an archipelago about 800 kilometres east of New Zealand's South Island. Moriori developed a distinct culture adapted to the cool, oceanic environment of the islands, and are known for their tradition of pacifism, known as the law of Nunuku.

Origins

Moriori ancestors were Polynesian voyagers who settled the Chatham Islands centuries ago, likely arriving from mainland New Zealand around 1500 CE or earlier. Isolated from other Polynesian communities, they developed their own language and customs. Because the islands were too cold for most tropical Polynesian crops, Moriori lived as hunter-gatherers, relying on seals, seabirds, fish, shellfish, and the berries and roots of native plants such as karaka (kopi).

Culture and the law of Nunuku

Moriori culture is notable for its covenant of peace. According to tradition, the ancestor Nunuku-whenua forbade warfare and cannibalism, decreeing that disputes be settled without bloodshed; fighting, when unavoidable, was limited to blows with a staff and stopped at the first drawing of blood. This pacifist ethic, maintained for centuries, shaped Moriori society. Moriori are also known for rakau momori, intricate carvings of human and natural figures incised into the trunks of living kopi trees.

Invasion and decline

In 1791 the British ship Chatham arrived, giving the islands their European name. Sealers and whalers followed, bringing disease. In 1835, two Māori iwi (tribes) from the Wellington region, Ngāti Mutunga and Ngāti Tama, commandeered a British brig, the Lord Rodney, and invaded the Chathams with about 900 armed people. The Moriori, numbering around 2,000, met to decide their response and, following Nunuku's law, chose not to resist violently. The invaders killed hundreds of Moriori and enslaved the survivors, who were forbidden to marry among themselves or speak their language. By the early 1860s only about 100 Moriori survived. Tame Horomona (Tommy Solomon), who died in 1933, was long regarded as the last full-blooded Moriori, though Moriori descendants continue to identify with their heritage today.

Revival and recognition

Since the late 20th century, Moriori have worked to revive their culture and language and to correct the widespread myth — once taught in New Zealand schools — that Moriori were a pre-Māori people of mainland New Zealand who were displaced by Māori. In 2020, the Moriori Claims Settlement Act passed, delivering a Crown apology and financial redress for the historical injustices Moriori suffered, and the settlement was finalized in 2021 with the return of land and recognition of Moriori identity.
