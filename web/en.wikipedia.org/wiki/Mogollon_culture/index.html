Mogollon culture

The Mogollon culture was one of the major prehistoric cultural traditions of the Oasisamerica region of what is now the Southwestern United States and northern Mexico, flourishing roughly from 200 CE to about 1450 CE. It takes its name from the Mogollon Mountains of New Mexico, which in turn were named for the Spanish colonial governor Juan Ignacio Flores Mogollón.

Development

The culture was identified as a distinct tradition by archaeologist Emil Haury in the 1930s. Early Mogollon communities lived in pithouses and farmed maize, beans, and squash, supplementing their diet by hunting and gathering. Later periods saw above-ground pueblos of stone and adobe, great kivas, and regional branches including the Mimbres, whose black-on-white pottery — decorated with intricate geometric patterns and images of animals and people — is among the most celebrated ceramics of the ancient Southwest.

Decline and legacy

From the twelfth century onward, Mogollon settlements were reorganised and many were abandoned by the fifteenth century, with populations likely joining other Pueblo communities. Modern Pueblo peoples including the Hopi and Zuni are among the Mogollon's cultural descendants. The Gila Cliff Dwellings in New Mexico preserve notable Mogollon ruins.
