Doctor Manhattan is a fictional character who appears in the graphic novel series Watchmen, published by DC Comics. Created by writer Alan Moore and artist Dave Gibbons, the character was loosely based on the Golden Age hero Captain Atom. Doctor Manhattan is a former physicist who, in 1959, was transformed in an accident involving an "intrinsic field" experiment, gaining godlike control over matter and energy. He is depicted as a blue, glowing, near-omnipotent being whose perception of time is non-linear: he experiences his past, present and future simultaneously. As the only character in Watchmen with actual superpowers, his growing detachment from humanity drives much of the story's meditation on power, morality and the Cold War.

Publication history

Doctor Manhattan debuted in Watchmen #1 (September 1986). Moore originally conceived the series using Charlton Comics characters DC had recently acquired; when DC objected to rendering those characters unusable, Moore and Gibbons created analogues, and Captain Atom became Doctor Manhattan. The character later appeared in the prequel miniseries Before Watchmen: Doctor Manhattan (2012) and played a central role in Doomsday Clock (2017–2019), which connected the Watchmen universe to the mainstream DC Universe. In 2020 the character appeared in the HBO television sequel series Watchmen, portrayed by Yahya Abdul-Mateen II, while Billy Crudup played him in Zack Snyder's 2009 film adaptation.

Fictional character biography

Origin

Jonathan Osterman was born in 1929 to a watchmaker father. After the atomic bombing of Hiroshima, his father pushed him toward atomic physics instead of horology. Osterman earned a Ph.D. in atomic physics from Princeton University and in 1959 began work at the Gila Flats research base in Arizona, where he fell in love with fellow scientist Janey Slater. In August 1959, Osterman was accidentally locked inside a test chamber during an experiment with an intrinsic-field subtractor. The device activated, disintegrating him completely. Over the following months, fragments of consciousness gradually reassembled a body — first a nervous system, then a partially muscled form — until on November 22, 1959, Osterman returned fully, now a tall, hairless, blue-skinned being of immense power.

Career as Doctor Manhattan

The United States government named him "Doctor Manhattan", invoking the Manhattan Project to intimidate the Soviet Union. For over a decade he served as America's ultimate deterrent and, in 1971, was ordered by President Richard Nixon to intervene in the Vietnam War, which he ended within weeks. His presence altered the balance of the Cold War and advanced American technology, including electric vehicles. Simultaneously, his perception of time — experiencing all moments at once — estranged him from ordinary human concerns, straining his relationships with Slater and later with Laurie Juspeczyk, the second Silk Spectre.

Exile and return

In 1985, after being publicly accused of causing cancer in those close to him — a scheme orchestrated by Adrian Veidt — Manhattan exiled himself to Mars, where he built a vast clockwork glass structure. Persuaded by Laurie that humanity was worth saving, he returned to Earth and confronted Veidt in Antarctica, but ultimately accepted Veidt's plan to avert nuclear war through a manufactured catastrophe, agreeing to keep the secret and leaving Earth for another galaxy to create life of his own.

Powers and abilities

Doctor Manhattan possesses essentially unlimited control over matter at the subatomic level. He can grow to enormous size, duplicate himself, teleport across interplanetary distances, pass through solid objects, disintegrate matter with a gesture, and perceive time in its entirety, though this determinism prevents him from changing foreseen events. He does not need air, water, food or sleep, and is functionally immortal.

Characterisation

Moore described Manhattan as a study of how superhuman power would realistically estrange a person from humanity. His clinical, quantitative view of the universe, his nudity signalling indifference to social convention, and the hydrogen-atom symbol he etched into his forehead in place of a government-designed logo all reinforce his post-human outlook. Critics frequently read him as an allegory of nuclear weapons and of theological conceptions of God.

In other media

  • Film: Billy Crudup portrays Manhattan (with motion-capture) in Watchmen (2009).
  • Television: The 2019 HBO series reveals Manhattan living on Earth in human form, played by Yahya Abdul-Mateen II, in a storyline that won widespread critical acclaim.
  • Animation: Michael Cerveris voices the character in the animated Watchmen films (2024).
