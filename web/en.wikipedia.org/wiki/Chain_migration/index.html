Chain migration

Chain migration: earlier migrants assist later ones from the same community

Chain migration is a social process in which migrants from a particular town, region, or country follow others from the same community to a particular destination. The destination may be in another part of the same country or in a foreign country. Information, assistance, and encouragement provided by earlier migrants lower the costs and risks of migration for later arrivals, so migration flows tend to become self-sustaining and concentrated, producing ethnic enclaves and strong links between specific sending and receiving communities.

The term was popularised by the MacDonalds' 1964 study of Italian migration. In more recent political debate, particularly in the United States, "chain migration" has also been used as a (often pejorative) synonym for family reunification immigration policies, under which legal immigrants may sponsor relatives for admission.

Mechanisms

Chain migration typically begins with "pioneer" migrants, often young men seeking work. Once established, they send back money, information, and prepaid tickets, and provide housing and help finding employment to relatives and fellow villagers who follow. This produces distinctive patterns:

  • Disproportionate flows between particular villages and particular neighbourhoods or workplaces abroad.
  • Formation of ethnic enclaves, such as Little Italies and Chinatowns.
  • Continued migration even after the original economic causes have weakened, because the social network itself sustains the flow.

Historical examples

Classic studies document chains linking southern Italian villages to cities such as New York and Toronto, Sicilian communities to particular American towns, and Mexican villages to specific destinations in the United States. Similar patterns have been observed in internal migration, for example from rural districts to industrial cities during urbanisation.

Political usage

In 21st-century American political discourse, "chain migration" has frequently been used by opponents of family-based immigration to describe the process by which a single immigrant can eventually sponsor extended family members. Critics of the term argue that it is dehumanising and misleading, since family sponsorship involves long waiting periods and strict eligibility rules, and they prefer the term "family reunification". Supporters of the term argue it captures the multiplier effect of family-based admissions.
