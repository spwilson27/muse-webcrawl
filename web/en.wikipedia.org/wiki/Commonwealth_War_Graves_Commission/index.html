The Commonwealth War Graves Commission (CWGC) is an intergovernmental organisation of six member states whose principal function is to mark, record, and maintain the graves and places of commemoration of Commonwealth military personnel who died in the two World Wars. Founded by Royal Charter in 1917 as the Imperial War Graves Commission, it commemorates around 1.7 million men and women.

History

The Commission grew out of Fabian Ware's Graves Registration work on the Western Front during the First World War. Ware persuaded the British Empire's governments that the dead should be buried where they fell, commemorated uniformly regardless of rank or wealth, and not repatriated — a controversial but enduring policy of equality in death. The architect Edwin Lutyens, Reginald Blomfield, and Herbert Baker designed the great cemeteries; Rudyard Kipling, whose son was killed at Loos, advised on inscriptions, choosing "Known unto God" for unidentified graves and the words "Their Name Liveth For Evermore" for the Stones of Remembrance.

Principles and features

Each casualty is commemorated by name on a uniform headstone or on a memorial to the missing, such as the Menin Gate at Ypres or the Thiepval Memorial. Cemeteries are maintained as gardens, with horticulture considered integral to the design. The Cross of Sacrifice and Stone of Remembrance appear in larger cemeteries. The Commission also cares for the graves of non-Commonwealth and civilian dead in some locations and maintains an online casualty database.

Member states

The six member governments — the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, and India — fund the Commission in proportion to their numbers of war graves.
