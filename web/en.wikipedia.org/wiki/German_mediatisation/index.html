German mediatisation

The Holy Roman Empire before the mediatisations

The German mediatisation (German: deutsche Mediatisierung) was the major redistribution of territory within the Holy Roman Empire between 1803 and 1806, in which the great majority of the Empire's small imperial estates — prince-bishoprics, imperial abbeys, free imperial cities and small principalities and counties — lost their imperial immediacy and were absorbed into larger neighbouring states.

Background

Under the Treaty of Lunéville (1801), France annexed the west bank of the Rhine, and the dispossessed German princes were to be compensated with territory east of the river, chiefly from the ecclesiastical states. The plan was drawn up in 1802 by France and Russia and enacted by the Imperial Delegation's final report, the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 25 February 1803.

Effects

Of the roughly 300 states and 1,500 tiny imperial knightly estates of the old Empire, only about 40 states survived by 1806. Nearly all church lands were secularised, all but six of the 51 free imperial cities were mediatised, and some three million subjects changed rulers. In 1806, under Napoleonic pressure, sixteen states formed the Confederation of the Rhine and further mediatisations followed; Francis II dissolved the Holy Roman Empire in August 1806.

The mediatised princely and comital families (Standesherren) retained certain privileges, including legal equality with reigning houses, until 1918. The territorial simplification of Germany laid groundwork for later unification.
