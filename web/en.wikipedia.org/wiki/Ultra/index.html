Ultra

Ultra was the designation adopted by British military intelligence for wartime signals intelligence obtained by breaking high-level encrypted enemy radio and teleprinter communications at the Government Code and Cypher School (GC&CS) at Bletchley Park during the Second World War. The name conveyed that the material was more sensitive than the highest existing security classification, "Most Secret". Ultra principally referred to decrypts of the German Enigma cipher machines and, later, the Lorenz teleprinter ciphers (called "Tunny" by the British).

Bombe machines at Bletchley Park tested Enigma key settings; their output underlay much of the Ultra intelligence.

Production

Polish cryptologists had made the first breakthroughs against Enigma before the war and shared them with Britain and France in 1939. At Bletchley Park, mathematicians including Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman developed the Bombe to recover daily key settings, while other sections exploited procedural weaknesses and captured material. From 1943 the Colossus computers, designed under Tommy Flowers, mechanized attacks on the Lorenz cipher used by the German high command. Thousands of staff worked at Bletchley and its outstations by 1945.

Use and impact

Ultra intelligence contributed to Allied success in the Battle of the Atlantic, the North African campaign, the Mediterranean, and the planning and protection of the Normandy landings, including confirming that German commanders had accepted the Operation Fortitude deception. Strict handling rules and cover stories—such as attributing sightings to reconnaissance flights—protected the source. Historians have estimated that Ultra may have shortened the war, though its precise effect remains debated. The secret was kept until the 1970s, when F. W. Winterbotham's book The Ultra Secret (1974) made it public.
