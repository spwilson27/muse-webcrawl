Serpins trap proteases through a dramatic conformational change.

Serpins are a large superfamily of proteins, most of which function as protease inhibitors. The name is a contraction of serine protease inhibitor, reflecting the first-discovered members' targets—serine proteases—although some serpins inhibit cysteine proteases (e.g. caspases and cathepsins) and others have non-inhibitory roles, such as hormone transport (thyroxine-binding globulin, cortisol-binding globulin) and molecular chaperone activity (HSP47). More than a thousand serpins have been identified across animals, plants, fungi, bacteria, archaea, and certain viruses.

Mechanism

Inhibitory serpins work by an unusual "suicide substrate" mechanism. The target protease attacks an exposed reactive centre loop (RCL), mistaking it for a substrate, and forms a covalent acyl-enzyme intermediate. Before hydrolysis can complete, the serpin undergoes a massive conformational rearrangement: the cleaved RCL inserts into the centre of its β-sheet, dragging the attached protease some 70 Å across the molecule and crushing its active site. The result is an irreversible, kinetically trapped 1:1 complex that is cleared from circulation.

Biological roles and disease

Human serpins regulate blood coagulation (antithrombin), fibrinolysis (PAI-1), inflammation (α1-antitrypsin, α1-antichymotrypsin), and complement activation (C1-inhibitor). Mutations cause the serpinopathies: α1-antitrypsin deficiency (emphysema and liver disease), antithrombin deficiency (thrombophilia), C1-inhibitor deficiency (hereditary angioedema), and familial dementia/encephalopathy caused by neuroserpin polymerisation. The metastable fold that makes serpins effective inhibitors also makes them prone to pathological polymerisation.
