Fiscal conservatism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fiscal conservatism is a political and economic philosophy regarding fiscal policy — taxation, government spending and public debt — that advocates low taxes, reduced government spending, minimal government debt and balanced budgets. Fiscal conservatives hold that the state should live within its means, that deficits burden future generations, and that lower taxation encourages growth and individual responsibility.

The position is found across several parties and traditions: it is a defining strand of the modern Republican Party in the United States, of conservative and Christian-democratic parties in Europe, and of liberal parties in the classical sense. It differs from supply-side economics in emphasising debt reduction rather than growth alone, and from austerity programmes, which are its application in periods of fiscal consolidation. Because fiscal conservatives may be socially liberal or conservative, the term describes an economic stance rather than a complete ideology.

History and practice

The doctrine descends from classical-liberal political economy and nineteenth-century Gladstonian finance — retrenchment, balanced budgets and free trade. In the late twentieth century it was associated with deficit-reduction efforts across the political spectrum, including the Clinton administration's surpluses of the late 1990s in the United States. Opponents argue that rigid budget rules prevent necessary public investment and counter-cyclical stabilisation; Keynesian economists hold that deficits in recessions are appropriate. Actual governments frequently depart from fiscal-conservative rhetoric when in office, a gap noted by critics of all sides.
