Radiolaria

Scientific classification
Domain:Eukaryota
Clade:SAR (Rhizaria)
Phylum:Retaria
Subphylum:Radiolaria
Groups:Spumellaria, Nassellaria, Phaeodaria, Acantharia

The Radiolaria, also called Radiozoa, are unicellular eukaryotes (protists) that produce intricate mineral skeletons, typically of silica (opal). They are found as zooplankton throughout the world's oceans, most abundantly in the sunlit surface waters, and their remains are among the most important microfossils used in geology.

Morphology

Radiolarian cells are divided by a perforated capsular membrane into inner and outer cytoplasm. Radiating from the cell are stiff pseudopodia called axopodia and filopodia, supported by microtubules, which the organism uses to trap food particles such as phytoplankton, small crustaceans, and bacteria. Many radiolarians harbour photosynthetic algal symbionts. Their siliceous skeletons — lattices, spheres, and spines of astonishing geometric regularity — were famously illustrated by Ernst Haeckel in his Kunstformen der Natur (1904) and in the Challenger expedition reports.

Geological significance

When radiolarians die, their skeletons sink and accumulate on the deep ocean floor as radiolarian ooze, which lithifies into radiolarite and chert. Because radiolarian species evolved rapidly and were widely distributed, their fossils are invaluable for dating marine sedimentary rocks from the Cambrian to the present and for reconstructing past ocean conditions.
