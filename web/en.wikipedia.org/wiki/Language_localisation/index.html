Language localisation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Language localisation (also spelled localization; abbreviated L10n) is the process of adapting a product, service, document, or piece of software to a specific locale — a particular language, country or region, and its cultural conventions. Localisation goes beyond translation: it encompasses date and number formats, currencies, units of measurement, address and name formats, colour and imagery conventions, legal requirements, and even functionality.

Localisation versus translation and internationalisation

Within the language industry the standard model distinguishes three activities. Internationalisation (I18n) is the design work that makes a product adaptable to many locales without engineering changes — for example separating text strings from code and supporting Unicode and right-to-left scripts. Localisation is the adaptation for one specific locale, of which translation is the largest component. Globalisation (G11n) is the overall business strategy combining both.

Process

A typical localisation project involves string extraction, translation (increasingly assisted by machine translation and post-editing), cultural and legal review, integration of translated resources, layout adjustment for text expansion or contraction, and linguistic and functional quality assurance in the target locale. Translation memories and terminology databases (glossaries) are used to keep wording consistent across versions and products.

Applications

Software and video games are the classic localisation domains — games may additionally require dubbing, censorship edits, and cultural reworking of jokes, symbols, and imagery. Websites, marketing materials, films and television (subtitling and dubbing), packaging, and technical documentation are also routinely localised. The industry is served by language service providers and standardised through formats such as XLIFF and TMX.
