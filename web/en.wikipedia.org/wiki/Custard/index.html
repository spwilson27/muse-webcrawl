Custard

Custard is a variety of culinary preparations based on sweetened milk, cheese, or cream cooked with egg or egg yolk to thicken it, and sometimes also flour, corn starch, or gelatin. Depending on the recipe, custard may vary in consistency from a thin pouring sauce (crème anglaise) to the thick pastry cream used in éclairs, or a firm, set dessert baked in a mould.

Preparation

Custard is usually cooked in a double boiler (bain-marie) or heated very gently in a saucepan on the stove. Gentle heating is essential: if the temperature rises too high, the egg proteins over-coagulate and the mixture curdles. Typical proportions are roughly one egg yolk per 100 ml of milk or cream, sweetened to taste and flavoured with vanilla.

Varieties

  • Crème anglaise — a thin pouring sauce served with desserts.
  • Pastry cream (crème pâtissière) — thickened with starch, used to fill éclairs, fruit tarts and mille-feuille.
  • Crème brûlée — a rich baked custard with a hard caramelised sugar crust.
  • Flan / crème caramel — baked custard with a layer of soft caramel on top.
  • Baked custard and custard tarts, including the English custard tart and Portuguese pastel de nata.
  • Instant custard powder, popularised by brands such as Bird's, made with cornflour, flavourings and colourings instead of egg.

History

Custards and custard-filled tarts were known in medieval European cookery; the word derives from the Old French croustade, referring to the crust of a tart. By the 19th century, custard had become a staple of British cookery, served with puddings, pies and fruit.
