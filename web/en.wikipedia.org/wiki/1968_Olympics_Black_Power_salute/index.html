1968 Olympics Black Power salute

Date16 October 1968
VenueEstadio Olímpico Universitario, Mexico City
ParticipantsTommie Smith, John Carlos, Peter Norman
EventMen's 200 metres medal ceremony, 1968 Summer Olympics

The 1968 Olympics Black Power salute was a political demonstration by African-American athletes Tommie Smith and John Carlos during their medal ceremony at the 1968 Summer Olympics in Mexico City. After Smith won the men's 200 metres in a world record of 19.83 seconds and Carlos took bronze, the two men mounted the podium shoeless, wearing black socks, and each raised a black-gloved fist during the playing of the United States national anthem. The gesture became one of the most famous images in the history of sport and of the American civil rights movement.

The protest

Smith and Carlos, both students at San Jose State University influenced by sociologist Harry Edwards and the Olympic Project for Human Rights (OPHR), had considered boycotting the Games to protest racial injustice in the United States. Instead they competed, and used the podium to make their statement. Their symbolism was deliberate: the black gloves represented black power and unity; the shoeless black socks symbolised black poverty; Smith wore a black scarf for black pride, Carlos beads for the victims of lynching, and his tracksuit top was unzipped in solidarity with blue-collar workers. Carlos had forgotten his gloves, and Australian silver medallist Peter Norman suggested the two Americans share Smith's pair — hence Smith's right fist and Carlos's left fist were raised. Norman wore an OPHR support badge and stood in solidarity.

Reaction

IOC president Avery Brundage insisted the protest violated the Games' apolitical principles; under pressure, the United States Olympic Committee suspended Smith and Carlos and expelled them from the Olympic Village and the team. American media reaction was largely hostile — one prominent columnist compared them to Nazi stormtroopers — and both men received death threats and struggled to find work for years. Norman was ostracised by Australian athletics authorities and was not selected for the 1972 Games despite qualifying.

Legacy

Over the following decades the salute was recognised as an act of courage. Smith and Carlos became civil rights figures; a monumental statue of them was erected at San Jose State in 2005, with the silver-medal step left empty at Norman's request so visitors could stand in his place. In 2016 the two, together with Norman (who had died in 2006), were honoured at the Rio Olympics, and in 2019 Smith and Carlos were inducted into the United States Olympic Hall of Fame.
