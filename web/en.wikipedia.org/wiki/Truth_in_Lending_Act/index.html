Truth in Lending Act

Truth in Lending Act of 1968
EnactedMay 29, 1968 (as Title I of the Consumer Credit Protection Act)
Codification15 U.S.C. §§ 1601–1667f
Implementing regulationRegulation Z (12 C.F.R. Part 1026)
AdministratorConsumer Financial Protection Bureau

The Truth in Lending Act (TILA) of 1968 is a United States federal law designed to promote the informed use of consumer credit by requiring disclosures about its terms and cost to standardize the manner in which costs associated with borrowing are calculated and disclosed. TILA is implemented by Regulation Z, originally issued by the Federal Reserve Board and transferred to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau by the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010.

Provisions

The Act requires creditors to disclose key terms of a credit transaction, including the annual percentage rate (APR), the finance charge, the amount financed, and the total of payments. It gives consumers a right of rescission on certain transactions secured by their principal dwelling, regulates credit card billing practices, and limits liability for unauthorized card use. Later amendments added rules for adjustable-rate mortgages, high-cost home loans (through the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994), and integrated mortgage disclosures — the TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure (TRID) rule, effective in 2015.

History

TILA was introduced by Senator William Proxmire and signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson as part of the Consumer Credit Protection Act. It was the first major federal consumer-credit statute, and has been amended many times, including by the Fair Credit Billing Act (1974), the Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act (1980), and the Credit CARD Act of 2009.
