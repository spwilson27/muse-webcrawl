Prison reform

Placeholder image: Eastern State Penitentiary, Philadelphia

Prison reform is the attempt to improve conditions inside prisons, improve the effectiveness of a penal system, reduce recidivism, or implement alternatives to incarceration. It also focuses on ensuring the reinstatement of those whose lives are affected by crimes.

History

Early modern prisons were often merely places of detention before trial or punishment. Reformers of the 18th century, influenced by Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764), campaigned for proportionate, certain punishment. John Howard's The State of the Prisons (1777) exposed disease and squalor in English gaols. Quaker reformers promoted the penitentiary — a place of solitary reflection and reform — realised in the Pennsylvania system (Eastern State Penitentiary, 1829) and the rival congregate Auburn system. In England, Elizabeth Fry campaigned for women prisoners, and the Prisons Acts gradually standardised conditions.

Modern debates

Twentieth-century reform centred on rehabilitation, education, and prisoners' rights, while critics from the 1970s onward pointed to rising incarceration — most sharply in the United States — and racial and social disparities. Contemporary movements range from improving healthcare, sanitation and safety in custody, through restorative justice and sentencing reform, to prison abolition.

Organisations

Dedicated bodies include the Howard League for Penal Reform (founded 1866) and the Prison Reform Trust in the UK, and numerous national and international NGOs monitoring compliance with standards such as the UN Nelson Mandela Rules.
