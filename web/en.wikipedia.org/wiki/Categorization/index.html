Categorization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Categorization is the process in which ideas and objects are recognized, differentiated and understood. It involves grouping entities into categories, typically for some specific purpose, on the basis of shared characteristics. Categorization is considered one of the most fundamental processes in cognition, underlying perception, language, decision-making and inference.

Classical theory

The classical view, traceable to Aristotle, holds that categories are defined by sets of necessary and sufficient conditions: an entity belongs to a category if and only if it possesses all the defining features, and all members are equal in status. This view dominated philosophy and early cognitive science and underlies formal systems of classification such as biological taxonomy.

Prototype and exemplar theories

In the 1970s, Eleanor Rosch's experiments challenged the classical view by showing that category membership is graded: some members (prototypes) are judged more typical than others — a robin is a "better" bird than a penguin. Prototype theory holds that categories are represented by an abstract central tendency, while exemplar theory proposes that categorization proceeds by comparison with remembered individual examples. Both approaches explain typicality effects, family resemblance and fuzzy category boundaries.

Development and learning

Infants form perceptual categories within the first months of life, and category learning continues throughout development, shaped by language: labels invite children to attend to commonalities among objects. In machine learning, categorization corresponds to classification, where algorithms learn decision boundaries from labelled examples.
