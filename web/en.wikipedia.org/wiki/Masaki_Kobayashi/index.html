Masaki Kobayashi (14 February 1916 – 4 October 1996) was a Japanese film director, best known for the epic trilogy The Human Condition (1959–1961), the samurai film Harakiri (1962), and the ghost-story anthology Kwaidan (1964). His films are noted for their humanism, their critique of authoritarian institutions, and their striking black-and-white widescreen compositions.

Early life and career

Kobayashi studied Oriental art history at Waseda University and joined Shochiku studios in 1941 as an apprentice under Keisuke Kinoshita, but was soon drafted into the Imperial Japanese Army and served in Manchuria and the Ryukyu Islands, refusing promotion on pacifist grounds and spending time as a prisoner of war. These experiences shaped the anti-war and anti-authoritarian themes that run through his mature work.

Major works

The Human Condition, adapted from Junpei Gomikawa's novel, follows a pacifist supervisor (Tatsuya Nakadai) in wartime Manchuria across nine and a half hours of film. Harakiri, about a ronin who exposes the hypocrisy of a feudal clan, won the Special Jury Prize at Cannes. Kwaidan, Kobayashi's first colour film, adapted from Lafcadio Hearn's ghost stories, also won the Special Jury Prize at Cannes and was nominated for an Academy Award. Later works include Samurai Rebellion (1967), Hymn to a Tired Man (1968), and Kaseki (1975).
