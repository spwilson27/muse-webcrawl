Benzene

Benzene (C₆H₆) is the simplest aromatic hydrocarbon: six carbon atoms in a ring with a delocalised cloud of π electrons, drawn as alternating double bonds or as a ring inside a hexagon. It is a colourless, sweet-smelling, flammable liquid obtained from petroleum.

Benzene is a fundamental industrial feedstock for plastics, resins, nylon, detergents and drugs — but it is also a proven carcinogen, so its use as a solvent and in petrol is now tightly limited.

[Image: The benzene ring structure drawn as a hexagon with a circle inside]

Structure and history

Michael Faraday first isolated benzene from illuminating gas in 1825. Its structure puzzled chemists until August Kekulé proposed the ring in 1865 — famously, he said, inspired by a dream of a snake biting its tail. X-ray and quantum studies later showed all six C–C bonds are equal, between single and double in character: the classic example of resonance.

Aromaticity

Benzene's unusual stability and its preference for substitution over addition reactions define 'aromatic' chemistry, rationalised by Hückel's rule (4n+2 π electrons).
