Schooner

Schooner
TypeSailing vessel
RigFore-and-aft sails on two or more masts
OriginNetherlands / North America, 17th–18th century
UsesCargo, fishing, pilotage, yachting, naval

A schooner is a sailing vessel defined by its rig: it uses fore-and-aft sails on two or more masts, with the foremast no taller than the mainmast. Schooners were first used by the Dutch in the 16th or 17th century and were developed further in colonial British America, where they became the dominant rig for coastal trade and fishing in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Rig and advantages

The classic North American schooner carries gaff sails on each mast, with triangular topsails and headsails. Compared with a square-rigged ship, a schooner needs a smaller crew, points higher into the wind, and is cheaper to build and operate — ideal for short-sea trades. Schooners were built with two masts most commonly, but three, four, five, and even six masts were used on large cargo carriers; the only seven-masted schooner, the steel-hulled Thomas W. Lawson (1902), was also the largest schooner ever built.

Uses

Schooners served as coasters, Grand Banks fishing vessels (the racing fishing schooner Bluenose is a Canadian icon), pilot boats, privateers, slavers and their Royal Navy pursuers, and — in the topsail schooner form — naval and revenue vessels. The schooner rig also became a classic yacht rig, from the racing yacht America, winner of the race that created the America's Cup in 1851, to modern training ships.

Decline

Steam and diesel power displaced commercial sailing schooners during the early twentieth century; the last great fleets of wooden cargo schooners worked the North American east coast and the Baltic into the 1930s. Schooners survive today chiefly as yachts, charter vessels, and sail-training ships.
