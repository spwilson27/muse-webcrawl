The right ventricle is one of the four chambers of the human heart. It receives deoxygenated blood from the right atrium and pumps it through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary trunk, which carries it to the lungs for oxygenation (the pulmonary circulation). The right ventricle is the most anterior of the heart's chambers and forms most of the sternocostal (front) surface of the heart.

Structure

The right ventricle is triangular in outline and crescent-shaped in cross-section, wrapping around the thicker-walled, circular left ventricle. Its wall is only about 3–5 mm thick, roughly a third that of the left ventricle, because it pumps against the low resistance of the pulmonary circuit. The chamber is divided into an inflow part, guarded by the tricuspid valve whose leaflets are anchored by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; a trabeculated apical portion lined by ridges called trabeculae carneae (including the moderator band, or septomarginal trabecula, which carries part of the conduction system); and a smooth-walled outflow tract, the infundibulum (conus arteriosus), leading to the pulmonary valve. The supraventricular crest separates the inflow and outflow portions.

Function

The right ventricle normally ejects the same stroke volume as the left but at much lower pressures — peak systolic pressure is about 25 mmHg, versus about 120 mmHg on the left — making it a volume pump adapted to a low-pressure, high-compliance circuit. Its contraction proceeds partly by a bellows-like motion of the free wall toward the septum and partly by longitudinal shortening, reflected clinically in tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE). Right ventricular function is assessed by echocardiography, cardiac MRI, and right heart catheterisation.

Clinical significance

The thin-walled right ventricle tolerates volume overload better than pressure overload; acute pressure overload (as in massive pulmonary embolism) can cause rapid dilation and failure. Chronic pressure overload from pulmonary hypertension leads to hypertrophy and eventually cor pulmonale. Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy is an inherited disease in which myocardium is replaced by fibrofatty tissue, predisposing to ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death. Congenital conditions involving the chamber include tetralogy of Fallot and double outlet right ventricle.
