Seawall

A seawall (or sea wall) is a form of coastal defence constructed where the sea, and associated coastal processes, impact directly upon the landforms of the coast. The purpose of a sea wall is to protect areas of human habitation, conservation or leisure activities from the action of tides, waves, or tsunamis. As a seawall is a static feature it will conflict with the dynamic nature of the coast and impede the exchange of sediment between land and sea.

Design

Seawalls are typically massive structures of concrete, masonry, steel sheet piling or rock armour designed to resist the full force of storm waves. Common cross-sections include vertical walls; curved or stepped faces, which deflect wave energy upward or dissipate it; and sloped revetment-like faces armoured with riprap or precast concrete units. Designs must account for wave reflection and scour at the toe of the wall, overtopping rates, drainage of water trapped behind the wall, and sea-level rise over the structure's intended life. Famous examples include the Galveston Seawall in Texas (built after the devastating 1900 hurricane, raising and protecting the city), the malecón walls of Havana and San Juan, the defences along the Netherlands coast (in combination with dikes), and extensive seawalls built in Japan, which line roughly a third of its coastline and were extensively rebuilt after the 2011 Tōhoku tsunami.

Advantages and disadvantages

Seawalls provide a clear, high level of protection to the land immediately behind them and occupy a small footprint, which suits urban waterfronts. However, they are expensive to build and maintain; wave reflection can deepen water at the toe and accelerate erosion, causing the beach in front of the wall to narrow and eventually disappear ("coastal squeeze"); and they can transfer erosion problems to neighbouring undefended shoreline. For these reasons modern coastal engineering often favours "soft" or hybrid approaches — beach nourishment, dune restoration, and managed retreat — with hard seawalls reserved for high-value or densely developed coasts.

History

Harbour moles and coastal walls date to antiquity — the Romans built concrete harbour works, and Byzantium's sea walls protected Constantinople for a thousand years. Large municipal seawalls proliferated in the 19th century with seaside resorts and port cities, and major 20th-century storms and tsunamis repeatedly drove higher and stronger designs.
