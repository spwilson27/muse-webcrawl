The eagle rays are a family, Myliobatidae, of large rays found mainly in warm coastal waters of the Atlantic, Pacific and Indian Oceans. Unlike the closely related skates and many stingrays, eagle rays are active, open-water swimmers that propel themselves by flapping their broad, pointed pectoral "wings" in a motion reminiscent of birds in flight — the origin of their common name. Taxonomy of the group has been revised repeatedly; the devil and manta rays, once included here, are now usually placed in their own family, Mobulidae, and the cownose rays in Rhinopteridae.

Description and behaviour

Eagle rays have flattened bodies with a distinct head bearing a blunt, sometimes duckbill-like snout, and a long, whip-like tail typically armed with one or more venomous barbed spines near its base. Their mouths contain flat, pavement-like teeth fused into plates with which they crush hard-shelled prey — molluscs, oysters, snails and crustaceans — dug from sandy seabeds; they also take fish and worms. The spotted eagle ray (Aetobatus narinari), with white spots and rings over a dark back and a wingspan of up to about 3 metres, is one of the best-known species. The common eagle ray (Myliobatis aquila) inhabits the eastern Atlantic and Mediterranean.

Eagle rays are ovoviviparous: eggs hatch inside the mother and litters of up to about six pups are born live. Many species are social and seasonally gather in schools; some breach clear of the water. Several species are classified as endangered or critically endangered by the IUCN, threatened by bycatch in fisheries, habitat loss and slow reproduction.
