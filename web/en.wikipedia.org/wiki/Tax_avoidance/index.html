Tax avoidance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tax avoidance is the legal minimisation of tax liability through arrangements that comply with the letter of the law, distinguished from tax evasion, which is illegal concealment or misrepresentation. Between them lies a contested zone of “aggressive” avoidance that legislatures counter with general anti-avoidance rules (GAARs), specific anti-abuse provisions, and judicial doctrines (substance over form; the Ramsay principle in the UK).

Classic avoidance techniques include income-shifting to low-tax affiliates (transfer pricing by multinationals), debt-loading high-tax subsidiaries, treaty shopping, converting income into capital gains, and exploiting mismatches between national systems. The scale is large — estimates of global corporate tax losses to profit-shifting run into the hundreds of billions of dollars annually on commonly cited (and contested) figures — and the politics changed after the 2008 crisis and leaks such as the Panama Papers (2016): automatic exchange of tax information (the Common Reporting Standard), country-by-country reporting, and the 15 per cent global minimum tax are the resulting policy stack. Courts have long recognised the taxpayer's right to arrange affairs tax-efficiently (the Duke of Westminster case, 1936) while narrowing abusive schemes.
