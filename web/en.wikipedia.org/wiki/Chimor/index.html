Chimor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Decorated adobe walls with marine friezes inside a ciudadela at Chan Chan]

Chimor was the political grouping of the Chimú culture on the north coast of Peru, the largest Andean state before the Inca, flourishing from about AD 900 until its conquest by the Inca Empire around 1470. Its capital, Chan Chan — the largest adobe city in the world, with ten walled palace-compounds (ciudadelas) of decorated mud-brick — housed perhaps 40,000–60,000 people near modern Trujillo. The Chimú ruled a thousand kilometres of desert coast through irrigation engineering, craft production and a dynastic system in which each king built his own palace and had to conquer new wealth, since his predecessor's estate remained intact for his descendants' cult.

Chan Chan and the Chimú state

The ciudadelas of Chan Chan combined royal residence, court, storehouses and a burial platform where the dead king's household and wealth — and, by Spanish accounts, sacrificed retainers — accompanied him. Chimú artisans produced vast quantities of blackware pottery, featherwork, and gold, silver and tumbaga metalwork (the treasures that drew looters for centuries). A hydraulic society, Chimor extended inter-valley canals tens of kilometres; the Lambayeque and Chimú heartlands were stitched into a single coastal empire by about 1400.

Inca conquest and after

Around 1462–1470 the Inca prince (later emperor) Topa Inca Yupanqui conquered Chimor after hard fighting; its king Minchançaman was taken to Cuzco, its craftsmen deported, its gods' statues held hostage — the standard Inca technique of absorption. Chan Chan dwindled under Inca and then Spanish rule (a repartimiento town nearby); its adobe walls, remarkably preserved in the rainless desert, now erode under El Niño rains and are a UNESCO World Heritage Site in danger.
