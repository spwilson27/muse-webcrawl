A dog breed is a distinct population of domestic dogs that was bred by humans to possess a consistent set of inherited characteristics, including appearance, behaviour and working ability. Dog breeds are usually recognised by kennel clubs, which maintain breed standards and registries.

History

Dogs were the first domesticated animal, diverging from wolves more than 15,000 years ago. For most of that history dogs were selected informally for function — hunting, herding, guarding and hauling — producing regional types or landraces. The modern concept of the breed, with closed studbooks and written standards, emerged in Victorian Britain, where the Kennel Club was founded in 1873. Most of today's several hundred breeds date from this period of intensive selective breeding.

Recognition and genetics

The Fédération Cynologique Internationale recognises more than 340 breeds, grouped into ten categories; the American Kennel Club recognises around 200. Genetic studies show breeds form distinct clusters, though questions persist about the health effects of inbreeding and extreme conformation, driving reforms in breeding practice and veterinary guidance.

Crossbreeds

Dogs produced by crossing two breeds, such as the Labradoodle, are called crossbreeds or "designer dogs" and are generally not recognised as breeds by the major kennel clubs.
