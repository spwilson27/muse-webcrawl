Gift economy

A gift economy (or gift culture) is a mode of exchange in which valuables are given without an explicit agreement for immediate or future payment, in contrast to a market economy, where goods are exchanged for money, or barter, where they are swapped directly for other goods of agreed worth. Instead of explicit contracts, gift exchange is governed by social obligations of reciprocity and serves to build relationships, status and solidarity.

Mauss and the obligations of the gift

The founding study is Marcel Mauss's essay The Gift (Essai sur le don, 1925). Mauss argued that in archaic societies the gift is never free: it imposes three linked obligations — to give, to receive, and to reciprocate — and the thing given retains something of the giver (the Maori concept of hau), which demands its return. Gift exchange is thus a "total social phenomenon", at once economic, legal, religious and moral.

Classic cases

Bronisław Malinowski described the Kula ring of the Trobriand Islands, in which shell necklaces and armbands circulate ceremonially between islands, conferring prestige rather than utility goods. Among the Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest, the potlatch was a competitive feast at which chiefs validated their status by giving away — or even destroying — great quantities of property, obliging rivals to match the display or lose face. Anthropologists such as Marshall Sahlins placed gift exchange on a spectrum of reciprocity, from the generalised sharing of close kin to the negative reciprocity of haggling and theft between strangers.

Modern gift economies

Gift dynamics persist inside market societies: academic science (where findings are published for reputation and repaid in citations), open-source software and Wikipedia, blood and organ donation (Richard Titmuss's The Gift Relationship compared voluntary blood systems favourably with paid ones), hospitality and tipping customs, and the exchange of gifts at holidays and rituals all involve giving whose return is delayed, indirect or purely social.
