Pretectal area

Pretectal area
Part ofMidbrain (epithalamus region, rostral midbrain)
ComponentsOlivary pretectal nucleus, nucleus of the optic tract, anterior, medial and posterior pretectal nuclei
FunctionPupillary light reflex, optokinetic responses, REM sleep regulation, antinociception

The pretectal area, or pretectum, is a midbrain structure composed of several nuclei located in the rostral midbrain, just superior to the superior colliculus and anterior to the thalamus, at the junction of the midbrain and diencephalon. It receives direct input from retinal ganglion cells — including the intrinsically photosensitive (melanopsin-containing) ganglion cells — and functions as a relay centre for several subcortical visual reflexes, most famously the pupillary light reflex.

Anatomy

The pretectal complex is conventionally divided into several nuclei, including the olivary pretectal nucleus (PON), the nucleus of the optic tract (NOT), and the anterior, medial and posterior pretectal nuclei. Terminology varies somewhat between species and atlases. The pretectum lies near the posterior commissure, damage to which region is associated with the dorsal midbrain (Parinaud) syndrome.

Functions

Pupillary light reflex

Light falling on the retina is signalled via the optic nerve and optic tract to the olivary pretectal nucleus, which projects bilaterally to the Edinger–Westphal nuclei. Parasympathetic outflow from there travels with the oculomotor nerve (CN III) through the ciliary ganglion to constrict the pupil. Because each pretectal nucleus projects to both Edinger–Westphal nuclei, shining light in one eye normally constricts both pupils — the direct and consensual responses tested in every neurological examination.

Other functions

The nucleus of the optic tract participates in the optokinetic reflex, stabilising the retinal image during sustained head rotation by driving horizontal eye movements. The pretectum also contributes to the regulation of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, to vertical gaze pathways via neighbouring structures, and, through the anterior pretectal nucleus, to descending modulation of pain (antinociception) in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord.

Clinical significance

Lesions involving the pretectal region and dorsal midbrain — from pineal region tumours, hydrocephalus or stroke — can cause Parinaud syndrome: paralysis of upward gaze, convergence-retraction nystagmus, lid retraction, and light-near dissociation of the pupils, in which pupils react poorly to light but constrict on near effort.
