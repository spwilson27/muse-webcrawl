Basel Convention

The Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and Their Disposal is an international treaty designed to reduce the movement of hazardous waste between nations, and specifically to prevent transfer of hazardous waste from developed to less developed countries. It was adopted in Basel, Switzerland, on 22 March 1989 and entered into force on 5 May 1992. As of the 2020s it has about 190 parties; the United States has signed but not ratified it.

Provisions

The Convention requires that exports of hazardous waste take place only with the prior informed consent of the importing state (and any transit states), that shipments be accompanied by movement documents from the point of generation to disposal, and that waste be managed in an environmentally sound manner. It obliges parties to minimize waste generation and to ensure adequate disposal facilities within their own territory. Illegal traffic in hazardous waste is deemed criminal.

Amendments and development

The 1995 Ban Amendment, which entered into force in 2019, prohibits exports of hazardous waste from OECD and EU countries and Liechtenstein to other parties, even for recycling. In 2019 the parties adopted amendments bringing most plastic waste under the Convention's controls, effective 2021, and in 2022 they agreed to subject all electronic waste to prior informed consent. Notorious dumping incidents, such as the Khian Sea voyage and the 2006 Probo Koala dumping in Côte d'Ivoire, have been major drivers of the treaty's tightening.
