Gender assignment

Gender assignment, also known as sex assignment, is the discernment of an infant's sex, typically made at birth, on the basis of the child's external genitalia, and sometimes additional physical characteristics. In almost all cases, a child's gender is either male or female, in line with their biological sex. Gender assignment is typically concordant with a person's gender identity in adulthood, though in some cases a person's gender identity may differ from the gender they were assigned at birth.

Terminology

Related terms include "assigned female at birth" (AFAB) and "assigned male at birth" (AMAB), which are used in discussions of transgender and intersex people. The terms emphasise that the sex recorded at birth is an assignment made by others rather than an aspect of personal identity.

Assignment in intersex infants

In the case of intersex infants, whose sex characteristics do not fit typical binary notions of male or female bodies, gender assignment can be more complicated. Historically, medical practice often involved early surgical intervention to make an infant's genitalia conform to the assigned sex. Since the late 20th century, this practice has been increasingly criticised by intersex advocacy groups and human rights bodies, who argue that irreversible interventions should be postponed until the individual can participate in the decision.

History

The modern medical approach to gender assignment in ambiguous cases was strongly influenced by the work of psychologist John Money in the 1950s, who proposed that gender identity is largely a product of socialisation in the first years of life. Money's "optimal gender of rearing" model guided clinical practice for decades, though later follow-up studies and cases such as that of David Reimer led to significant reassessment of its claims.
