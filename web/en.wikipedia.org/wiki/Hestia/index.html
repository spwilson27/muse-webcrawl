Hestia (Ancient Greek: Ἑστία, "hearth") is the Greek goddess of the hearth, the home, domestic life and the sacrificial flame. The eldest child of the Titans Cronus and Rhea, she was one of the twelve Olympians, though in some lists she yields her place to Dionysus. Unlike the wandering Olympians, Hestia never left Olympus and takes part in almost no myths of adventure: her person is the fixed, sacred centre of the household and the city — the hearth fire itself.

Mythology

Swallowed at birth by Cronus and freed by Zeus, Hestia was courted by Poseidon and Apollo but swore perpetual virginity, taking refuge with Zeus, who granted her the central place in the house and the first share of every sacrifice. Because the first and last libations at a feast were poured to her, the phrase "to begin with Hestia" became proverbial. Her inviolable, motionless character — she is not to be imagined walking about — made her a symbol of stability and ordered domestic life.

Cult

Every Greek household's hearth was Hestia's altar, and each city maintained a public hearth (prytaneion) whose fire was never allowed to go out; colonists carried fire from the mother city's hearth to found new homes. At sacrifices she received the first offering. In Rome her counterpart Vesta was served by the Vestal Virgins, who guarded the sacred flame of the state. Hestia is comparatively rare in art, usually shown veiled and modestly dressed, sometimes with a kettle or flame.
