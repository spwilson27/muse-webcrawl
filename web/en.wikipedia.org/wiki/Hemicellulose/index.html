Hemicellulose

A hemicellulose is one of several types of matrix polysaccharides present in the cell walls of nearly all terrestrial plants. Whereas cellulose is a crystalline, strong, linear polymer of glucose resistant to hydrolysis, hemicelluloses are branched, amorphous heteropolymers with little strength, composed of several different sugars, and are readily hydrolysed by dilute acids and enzymes.

Composition

Hemicelluloses include xylans, glucuronoxylans, arabinoxylans, glucomannans, and xyloglucans. Their sugar monomers include xylose, mannose, galactose, glucose, arabinose, rhamnose, and uronic acids. Most hemicelluloses have a degree of polymerisation of only about 50–300 units, far shorter than cellulose chains, and many are branched, which keeps them amorphous and soluble in alkali.

Occurrence

Hemicelluloses make up roughly 20–35 per cent of lignocellulosic biomass. Hardwoods are rich in glucuronoxylan, softwoods in galactoglucomannan, and grasses in arabinoxylan. Together with cellulose microfibrils and lignin, they give the secondary cell wall its rigidity and resistance to microbial attack.

Extraction and uses

Hemicelluloses can be extracted from biomass with alkaline solutions and are a major feedstock for producing xylitol, furfural, and fermentable sugars for biofuels. Pre-treatment of lignocellulose for bioethanol production largely aims to remove or hydrolyse hemicellulose so that enzymes can reach the cellulose.

Biosynthesis

Hemicelluloses are synthesised in the Golgi apparatus from sugar nucleotide donors by glycosyltransferases, in contrast to cellulose, which is synthesised at the plasma membrane.
