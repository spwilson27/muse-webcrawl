Jasmine rice (Thai: ข้าวหอมมะลิ, khao hom mali) is a long-grain variety of fragrant rice (also known as aromatic rice). Its fragrance, reminiscent of pandan (Pandanus amaryllifolius) and popcorn, results from the rice plant's natural production of aroma compounds, of which 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline is the most salient. A rapid loss of aromatic intensity leads many Southeast Asian growers and consumers to prefer freshly harvested jasmine rice. Jasmine rice is a variety of Oryza sativa.

Jasmine rice is grown primarily in Thailand (Thai hom mali or Thai fragrant rice), Cambodia (phka rumduol or Cambodian jasmine rice), Laos, and southern Vietnam. It is moist and soft in texture when cooked, with a slightly sweet flavour. The grains are also shorter and thicker than basmati rice grains.

History

Jasmine rice was originally developed in Thailand, where the Khao Dawk Mali 105 variety (KDML105) was selected by farmers in the central Chao Phraya basin and later promoted by the Thai Rice Department in the 1950s. The variety proved especially well suited to the rain-fed lowlands of the northeastern Isan region, in provinces such as Surin, Sisaket, Buriram, and Ubon Ratchathani, where it became the dominant export rice.

The name "jasmine" refers to the colour of the cooked grain, which is white like the jasmine flower, rather than to any jasmine flavouring. The designation "Thai Hom Mali" is a protected certification mark for jasmine rice grown in specified Thai provinces and meeting official quality standards.

Cultivation

Jasmine rice is a photoperiod-sensitive, rain-fed lowland rice. It is typically transplanted during the rainy season (May–August in Thailand) and harvested from late October through December. Because it flowers in response to shortening day length in October, it is generally cropped once per year. The plants are relatively tall (often over 140 cm) and prone to lodging.

In Cambodia, the analogous variety Phka Rumduol was released in 1999 after development from Cambodian germplasm and has repeatedly won international "World's Best Rice" awards at the World Rice Conference, as has Thai Hom Mali.

Culinary uses

Steamed jasmine rice is a staple accompaniment to Thai dishes such as green curry, red curry, and stir-fries, and is used across Southeast Asian cuisines. It is the rice traditionally used for Thai fried rice (khao phat) and for congee-style rice porridge (jok). Because of its soft, slightly sticky texture, it is less suited than medium-grain rice to dishes such as risotto or paella, and it is not normally used for sushi.

To conserve the aroma, jasmine rice is usually rinsed sparingly (or not at all) before cooking, since the aroma compound 2-acetyl-1-pyrroline is water-soluble and diminished by washing and by storage. Freshly harvested ("new crop") rice commands a premium price.

Varieties

Three varieties dominate production in Thailand: Khao Dawk Mali 105, RD15, and the glutinous-adjacent jasmine types grown for domestic consumption. Cambodian jasmine rice (Phka Rumduol and Phka Rumduol derivatives) and Vietnamese jasmine varieties such as ST24 and ST25 — the latter named World's Best Rice in 2019 — compete in the same premium fragrant-rice market.

Brown jasmine rice, in which only the outermost husk is removed, retains the bran layer and more fibre and micronutrients than the polished white form.

Glycemic index

Jasmine rice has a relatively high glycemic index, commonly reported in the range of about 68–80, higher than that of basmati rice; frequent consumption in large portions has been discussed in public-health literature on type 2 diabetes risk in Asian populations.
