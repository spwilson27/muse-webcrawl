Louisiana (French: La Louisiane) was an administrative district of New France. Under French control from 1682 to 1769 and from 1801 to 1803, the area was named in honour of King Louis XIV by French explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de la Salle. It originally covered an expansive territory that included most of the drainage basin of the Mississippi River, stretching from the Great Lakes to the Gulf of Mexico and from the Appalachian Mountains to the Rocky Mountains.

History

La Salle claimed the Mississippi basin for France in 1682. Permanent colonisation began in 1699 under Pierre Le Moyne d'Iberville, with early settlements at Biloxi and Mobile before the founding of New Orleans in 1718 by Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne de Bienville. The colony was administered first by the Crown, then by John Law's Mississippi Company (1717–1731), whose speculative collapse — the "Mississippi Bubble" — damaged development. Settlement remained sparse; the economy rested on the fur trade, concessions worked partly by enslaved Africans, and alliances with Native American nations such as the Choctaw against the Natchez and Chickasaw.

Cession to Spain and retrocession

After defeat in the Seven Years' War, France ceded Louisiana west of the Mississippi, together with New Orleans, to Spain by the secret Treaty of Fontainebleau (1762); the Treaty of Paris (1763) transferred the eastern bank to Britain. Spanish Louisiana lasted until 1800, when Napoleon compelled Spain to retrocede the colony by the Third Treaty of San Ildefonso. France formally took possession only briefly before selling the territory to the United States.

Louisiana Purchase

In 1803, needing funds for war in Europe and unable to defend the territory, Napoleon sold Louisiana to the United States for $15 million — the Louisiana Purchase — roughly doubling the young republic's size. The transfer was formalised in New Orleans on 20 December 1803.

Legacy

French Louisiana left a lasting mark in place names, the civil-law tradition of the modern state of Louisiana, French and Creole languages, and the architecture of New Orleans' French Quarter (largely rebuilt under Spanish rule after fires in 1788 and 1794).
