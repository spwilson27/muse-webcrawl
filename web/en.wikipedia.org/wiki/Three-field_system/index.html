Three-field system

The three-field system is a regime of crop rotation used in medieval and early-modern Europe. A third of the land was planted in autumn with winter wheat or rye, a third in spring with peas, lentils, beans, barley or oats, and the remaining third left fallow.

History

Spreading from the Carolingian era onward, the system replaced the older two-field rotation across much of northern Europe by the High Middle Ages. It was typically combined with the open-field system of communal strips and common grazing on the fallow and stubble.

Advantages

Only one-third rather than one-half of land lay idle; spring-sown legumes fixed nitrogen and improved diets and livestock fodder; and spreading sowing between autumn and spring reduced the risk of total harvest failure and spread labour through the year.

Decline

From the 18th century, convertible husbandry, enclosure, and the Norfolk four-course rotation with turnips and clover made fallowing unnecessary and superseded the system.
