Classical element

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Classical elements were the proposed building blocks of the universe in ancient and pre-modern natural philosophy. In the Greek tradition formalized by Aristotle, the sublunary world was composed of four elements — earth, water, air, and fire — with a fifth, aether, constituting the heavens.

Greek formulation

Empedocles (c. 494–434 BCE) proposed four "roots" combined in varying proportions by the forces of Love and Strife. Aristotle added qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry) and a natural-place theory: earth and water move downward; air and fire upward. The elements could transmute into one another.

Other traditions

India and Tibet recognized mahabhuta — earth, water, fire, air, and space. Chinese wuxing (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) are not substances but phases in cyclical processes of transformation. Japanese godai adds void.

Medicine and alchemy

The four elements underwrote Hippocratic humoral medicine (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile). Medieval alchemy reworked elemental theory through Paracelsus's tria prima (salt, sulfur, mercury). Elemental thinking survived into the 18th century, when Lavoisier redefined "element" in the modern chemical sense.
