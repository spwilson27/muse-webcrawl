Transit method

The transit method, or transit photometry, is a method of detecting exoplanets by measuring the periodic dimming of a star's light as a planet passes (transits) in front of it from the observer's viewpoint. The depth of the dip reveals the planet's radius relative to the star, and the period between transits gives the orbital period.

Principle

During a transit, the fraction of light blocked is approximately (Rp/R★)2. An Earth-sized planet crossing a Sun-like star dims it by only about 84 parts per million, requiring extremely precise photometry. The transit probability falls with orbital distance, so the method is biased toward close-in planets with short periods. Repeated, consistent dips distinguish planets from stellar activity, though false positives such as eclipsing binary stars must be ruled out.

Missions and results

Space telescopes devoted to the method include CoRoT, NASA's Kepler mission (2009–2018), which confirmed more than 2,600 planets, and TESS (launched 2018), surveying bright nearby stars. Follow-up radial velocity measurements yield planet masses, and hence densities. Transit spectroscopy — studying starlight filtered through a planet's atmosphere during transit — has enabled the detection of atmospheric constituents with Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope.
