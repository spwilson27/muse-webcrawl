Cell death

Branch: Cell biology

Major pathways: Apoptosis, necrosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, pyroptosis

Cell death

Cell death is the event of a biological cell ceasing to carry out its functions. It may result from the natural process of old cells dying and being replaced by new ones, from damage or disease, or from regulated programmes that remove cells during development and immune function.

Programmed cell death

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is an orderly, ATP-dependent form of cell death characterised by cell shrinkage, chromatin condensation, membrane blebbing, and fragmentation into membrane-bound apoptotic bodies that are phagocytosed without inflammation. It is driven by caspase proteases through intrinsic (mitochondrial) and extrinsic (death-receptor) pathways.

Other regulated pathways

Other regulated forms include necroptosis, pyroptosis (an inflammatory, gasdermin-mediated death important in infection), ferroptosis (driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation), and autophagy-dependent cell death.

Necrosis

Necrosis is an unregulated form of death caused by acute injury, toxins, or loss of blood supply, resulting in cell swelling, membrane rupture, and inflammation of surrounding tissue.

Significance

Balanced cell death is essential for embryonic development, tissue homeostasis, and the elimination of damaged or infected cells. Too little death contributes to cancer; too much contributes to degenerative diseases and tissue injury in stroke and myocardial infarction.
