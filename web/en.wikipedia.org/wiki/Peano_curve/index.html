The Peano curve is the first known example of a space-filling curve: a continuous curve whose range contains the entire two-dimensional unit square. It was constructed by Giuseppe Peano in 1890, in a paper motivated by Georg Cantor's surprising result that the unit interval and the unit square have the same cardinality. Peano gave an explicit analytic construction showing that the mapping could be continuous, though necessarily not injective.

Construction

Peano's construction exploits base-3 (ternary) expansions. A number t in [0, 1] written in ternary is transformed digit-by-digit into a pair of ternary expansions giving the coordinates (x, y) of a point in the unit square; complementing selected digits ensures that nearby values of t map to nearby points, making the map continuous. Geometric versions proceed iteratively: the interval is divided into 9 equal parts and the square into 9 equal subsquares, in a pattern refined at each stage; the limit curve passes through every point of the square.

Properties and significance

The Peano curve demonstrated that continuity alone does not preserve dimension, sharpening the problem later resolved by the invariance of dimension theorem: no continuous bijection (homeomorphism) exists between the interval and the square. David Hilbert gave a more geometric variant (the Hilbert curve) in 1891. Space-filling curves now appear in analysis, image processing, data structures (such as spatial indexing), and the theory of fractals; the Peano curve's image has Hausdorff dimension 2, and approximants converge in ways used to measure curve complexity.
