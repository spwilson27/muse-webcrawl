Afro-Brazilian culture

Afro-Brazilian culture
RegionsThroughout Brazil; strongest in Bahia and the Northeast
Key elementsCandomblé, samba, capoeira, Afro-Brazilian cuisine

Afro-Brazilian culture is the culture produced and shaped by Afro-Brazilians, the descendants of the millions of enslaved Africans brought to Brazil during the Atlantic slave trade — more than to any other country in the Americas. Brazil abolished slavery only in 1888, the last country in the Western Hemisphere to do so, and African-derived religions, music, dance, cuisine, and language have profoundly influenced Brazilian national culture.

Religion

Afro-Brazilian religions preserved and transformed West and Central African traditions under Catholic cover. Candomblé, centred in Bahia, venerates the orixás (orishas) through drumming, song, dance, and offerings in terreiros (temples). Umbanda, which emerged in the twentieth century, blends African, Catholic, Kardecist spiritist, and Indigenous elements. Catholic Black brotherhoods (irmandades) dedicated to Our Lady of the Rosary and Saint Benedict provided early institutional space for Afro-Brazilian communal life and festivals.

Music and dance

Samba, Brazil's signature musical genre, grew from Afro-Brazilian rhythms and gatherings in Rio de Janeiro in the early twentieth century, and samba schools anchor the Rio and São Paulo carnivals. In Bahia, afoxé groups, the Afro-Brazilian carnival blocos afro such as Olodum, and samba-reggae carry explicit messages of Black pride. Capoeira, the acrobatic martial art developed by enslaved Africans and disguised in dance, is recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage.

Cuisine and language

Afro-Brazilian cuisine, especially in Bahia, features dishes such as acarajé (black-eyed pea fritters fried in dendê palm oil), vatapá, caruru, and moqueca, prepared and sold by baianas and recognized as national heritage. African languages such as Yoruba and Kimbundu contributed vocabulary, religious terminology, and rhythms of speech to Brazilian Portuguese.

Recognition and struggle

Despite the ideology of "racial democracy", Afro-Brazilians have faced persistent racism and inequality, and cultural expressions such as capoeira and Candomblé were historically criminalized and policed. November 20, Black Consciousness Day, commemorates Zumbi dos Palmares, leader of the great quilombo (maroon settlement) of Palmares, and the Afro-Brazilian movement has won recognition of quilombo land rights and affirmative-action policies in universities.
