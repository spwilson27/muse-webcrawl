Arrow–Debreu model

In mathematical economics, the Arrow–Debreu model is a general equilibrium model that describes an economy with a complete set of markets for every commodity at every date and location. Developed by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu in the 1950s, it provides the first rigorous proof of the existence of a competitive equilibrium under stated conditions.

Overview

The model assumes consumers with convex preferences and firms with convex production sets. Using fixed-point theorems, Arrow and Debreu proved that there exists a price vector at which supply equals demand in all markets simultaneously — a market-clearing equilibrium. Their 1954 paper "Existence of an Equilibrium for a Competitive Economy" is a landmark of twentieth-century economics.

Contingent commodities

A central device of the model is the contingent commodity: a good specified by its physical characteristics, the date and place of delivery, and the state of the world in which it is delivered. With markets for all contingent claims, uncertainty can be handled within the same framework as ordinary exchange.

Relation to welfare theorems

The model underpins the two fundamental theorems of welfare economics: every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient, and any Pareto efficient allocation can be supported as a competitive equilibrium after suitable transfers, given convexity assumptions.

Criticism and legacy

Critics note the model's strong assumptions — complete markets, perfect information, no transaction costs. Nevertheless it remains the benchmark for general equilibrium theory and influenced the development of finance theory and macroeconomic modelling.
