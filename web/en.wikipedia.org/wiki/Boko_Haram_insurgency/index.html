The Boko Haram insurgency began in 2009, when the jihadist group Boko Haram started an armed rebellion against the government of Nigeria. The conflict subsequently spread to neighbouring countries, including Cameroon, Chad, Niger and Mali. Boko Haram, formally known as the "Group of the People of Sunnah for Preaching and Jihad", had been founded in Maiduguri around 2002 by Mohammed Yusuf, and took its popular name from its opposition to Western-style education (boko).

Course of the conflict

In July 2009 the movement launched an uprising across northern Nigeria; its suppression by security forces left hundreds dead, including Yusuf, who died in police custody. Under his successor Abubakar Shekau the group regrouped and from 2010 waged an escalating campaign of bombings, assassinations and raids. In 2013 the Nigerian government declared a state of emergency in Borno, Yobe and Adamawa states.

The insurgency drew worldwide attention in April 2014 with the kidnapping of 276 schoolgirls from Chibok, which prompted the "#BringBackOurGirls" campaign. By early 2015 Boko Haram controlled large areas of Borno State and had pledged allegiance to the Islamic State, but a regional offensive by Nigeria, Chad, Niger and Cameroon pushed it back into the Sambisa Forest and the Lake Chad islands. In 2016 the movement split: the Islamic State recognised Abu Musab al-Barnawi's faction, Islamic State West Africa Province (ISWAP), while Shekau retained the Boko Haram name. Shekau died in May 2021 during clashes with ISWAP, after which many of his fighters surrendered.

Humanitarian impact

The conflict has killed tens of thousands of people and displaced more than two million, mainly within northeastern Nigeria. It has devastated education and health services in the region and contributed to severe food insecurity around Lake Chad. Both insurgent forces and government troops have been accused of human rights abuses.
