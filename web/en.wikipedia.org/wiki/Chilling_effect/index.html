Chilling effect

In a legal context, a chilling effect is the inhibition or discouragement of the legitimate exercise of natural and legal rights by the threat of legal sanction. The term is most often used in United States law in connection with freedom of speech: a law, lawsuit, or official action that is vague, overbroad, or punitive may deter people from engaging in constitutionally protected speech even where their speech would ultimately be upheld as lawful.

The phrase entered U.S. legal vocabulary in the early 1950s, during the McCarthy era, and was used by Supreme Court justices including William O. Douglas and in cases such as Wieman v. Updegraff (1952) and Lamont v. Postmaster General (1965), which struck down a requirement that recipients request delivery of "communist political propaganda" in writing because of its deterrent effect on expression.

Applications

Chilling effects are invoked against defamation law (with "libel chill" discouraging reporting), anti-SLAPP statutes designed to counter suits intended to silence critics, surveillance programs that may deter association and inquiry, and broad regulations of protest or online speech. Courts may treat a demonstrated chill as grounds for striking down a statute as overbroad or vague.

Criticism and related concepts

Critics note that some deterrence is the ordinary function of law, and scholars dispute how readily courts should credit speculative claims of self-censorship. Related doctrines include the overbreadth doctrine, void-for-vagueness, and prior restraint. In the surveillance context, researchers have documented "spiral of silence" effects and post-Snowden declines in readership of terrorism-related reference material as empirical evidence of chilling effects.
