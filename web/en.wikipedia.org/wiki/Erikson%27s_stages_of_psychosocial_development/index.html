Erikson's stages of psychosocial development is a theory by psychoanalyst Erik Erikson describing eight stages through which a healthy developing individual passes from infancy to late adulthood. At each stage the person faces a psychosocial crisis whose resolution shapes personality and leaves a corresponding virtue.

The stages

The stages are: (1) trust vs. mistrust in infancy; (2) autonomy vs. shame and doubt in early childhood; (3) initiative vs. guilt in the play age; (4) industry vs. inferiority at school age; (5) identity vs. role confusion in adolescence; (6) intimacy vs. isolation in young adulthood; (7) generativity vs. stagnation in middle adulthood; and (8) ego integrity vs. despair in late adulthood. Later editions, completed by Erikson's wife Joan, added a ninth stage addressing the challenges of very old age.

Significance

Building on Freud's psychosexual stages but emphasising social and cultural influences across the whole lifespan, Erikson's framework became one of the most influential theories in developmental psychology, notably for its account of the adolescent "identity crisis", a term Erikson coined.
