Kell antigen system

The Kell glycoprotein sits on the red-cell surface, linked to the XK protein.

The Kell antigen system (also known as the Kell–Cellano system) is a major human blood group system whose antigens are carried on the Kell glycoprotein, a zinc endopeptidase on the surface of red blood cells. It is named after "Mrs Kellacher", the patient in whom the first antibody, anti-K, was discovered in 1946.

Antigens

The system includes more than thirty antigens. The most important are the antithetical pair K (Kell) and k (Cellano), differing by a single amino acid, and the pair Kpa/Kpb. About 9% of people of European ancestry are K-positive. The Kell protein is linked to the XK protein; absence of XK causes the rare McLeod syndrome, with weakened Kell antigens and neurological and muscle abnormalities.

Clinical significance

Anti-K is the most common immune red-cell antibody after those of the ABO and Rh systems and can cause haemolytic transfusion reactions. In pregnancy, anti-K can cause haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn, characteristically suppressing fetal red-cell production and so causing anaemia with a relatively low bilirubin. Transfusion services therefore provide K-negative blood to girls and women of childbearing age when practicable.
