The Lviv pogroms were two waves of anti-Jewish violence carried out in the city of Lwów (now Lviv, Ukraine) in June and July 1941, shortly after the German invasion of the Soviet Union. The pogroms were instigated under German occupation, following the discovery of the bodies of prisoners murdered by the Soviet NKVD before its retreat, and were carried out by local mobs encouraged and assisted by German units and Ukrainian nationalist militia. Thousands of Jews were beaten, humiliated and murdered.

Background

Before the Second World War, Jews made up roughly a third of Lwów's population of over 300,000. The city was annexed by the Soviet Union in 1939. When German forces captured the city on 30 June 1941, the prisons were found full of the bodies of political prisoners killed by the NKVD. Nazi propaganda blamed "Jewish Bolshevism" for the murders, and Jews were rounded up and forced to exhume and handle the corpses, while crowds attacked them.

The pogroms

In the first pogrom, from 30 June to 2 July 1941, Jews were dragged from their homes, beaten in the streets and killed; estimates of the dead range from about 2,000 to several thousand. A second wave, known as the "Petliura Days", took place on 25–29 July 1941 and claimed further victims, commonly estimated at between 1,500 and 2,000. The pogroms were followed by the ghettoisation and eventual near-total destruction of Lviv's Jewish community in the Holocaust.
