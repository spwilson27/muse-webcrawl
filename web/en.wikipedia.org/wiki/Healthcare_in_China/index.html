Healthcare in China

Healthcare in China has undergone major reforms since the founding of the People's Republic in 1949. China now achieves near-universal health insurance coverage (over 95% of the population) through public social insurance schemes, while care delivery remains dominated by public hospitals, with patients often travelling to large tertiary hospitals in cities even for minor conditions. Life expectancy rose from about 35–40 years in 1949 to roughly 78 years in the early 2020s.

History

In the Mao era, rural cooperative medical schemes and "barefoot doctors" provided basic preventive and primary care to most of the population. Market reforms after 1978 dismantled most rural schemes and shifted costs onto patients, leaving many uninsured by the 1990s. From 2003 the government rebuilt coverage through the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme and the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance, later merged into resident schemes; a comprehensive reform plan in 2009 committed major funding to insurance, essential medicines and primary care infrastructure.

System structure

Basic medical insurance comprises an employee scheme (financed by employer and employee contributions) and a resident scheme (subsidised by government), covering inpatient and increasingly outpatient care, though reimbursement rates vary by locality and hospital tier. Delivery is organised in a three-tier system of village clinics, township and county hospitals, and large municipal/provincial hospitals, but weak gatekeeping means tertiary hospitals handle a disproportionate share of visits. Traditional Chinese medicine is integrated into the system alongside Western medicine.

Issues

Challenges include high out-of-pocket costs for serious illness, regional disparities, hospital revenue dependence on service volume, over-prescription historically linked to drug mark-ups (addressed by zero-markup reforms), tension between patients and medical staff, an ageing population, and rising burdens of non-communicable diseases such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Air pollution and smoking — China has over 300 million smokers — remain major public health concerns.
