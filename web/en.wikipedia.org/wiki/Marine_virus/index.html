Marine virus

Marine viruses are viruses found in seawater and other marine environments. By number they are the most abundant biological entities in the ocean — an estimated 1030 particles worldwide, roughly ten for every marine bacterium — and they infect organisms across all domains of life, from bacteria and archaea to fish and marine mammals. Despite their tiny total biomass (about 0.03 gigatonnes of carbon), their ecological impact is enormous.

Ecological role

Marine viruses, most of them bacteriophages, kill an estimated 20–40% of ocean bacteria every day. When infected cells lyse, their contents are released as dissolved organic matter that is taken up by other microbes — the "viral shunt", which diverts carbon away from higher trophic levels and back into the microbial loop. This process influences global nutrient cycling and is estimated to release billions of tonnes of carbon annually. Viruses also terminate algal blooms, including harmful blooms, and drive the evolution of their hosts through horizontal gene transfer; many cyanophages carry photosynthesis genes that they express in infected cyanobacteria, directly affecting global primary production and oxygen evolution.

Diversity

Metagenomic surveys since the early 2000s have revealed immense viral diversity, most of it previously unknown: double-stranded DNA phages of the order Caudovirales dominate counts, while giant viruses (such as Mimivirus relatives) infect marine protists. The Tara Oceans expeditions catalogued hundreds of thousands of viral populations in the surface ocean. RNA viruses, long neglected in marine studies, are now known to be abundant as well.

History

Although viruses were reported in seawater as early as the 1950s, their abundance was not appreciated until 1989, when transmission electron microscopy by Øivind Bergh and colleagues showed up to 108 viruses per millilitre of seawater — orders of magnitude more than expected — transforming understanding of ocean food webs.
