Doo-wop (also spelled doo wop or doowop) is a genre of rhythm and blues music that originated in African-American communities throughout the United States in the 1940s, mainly in the large cities of the Northeast, and developed into a mainstream style of American popular music in the 1950s and early 1960s. The style features vocal group harmony with nonsense syllables (giving the genre its name), a simple beat, and simple music and lyrics, often sung a cappella or with minimal instrumentation.

Characteristics

Doo-wop is built on vocal harmony: a tenor lead singer delivers the melody while a trio or quartet sings background harmonies and a bass singer provides rhythmic or harmonic foundation, often with syllables such as "doo-wop," "shoo-doo," and "dip da dip." Songs are frequently about love and courtship, using simple chord progressions such as the I–vi–IV–V "doo-wop progression" heard in "Earth Angel" and "Stand by Me."

History

The genre grew out of earlier African-American vocal traditions — gospel quartets, barbershop harmony, and groups such as the Ink Spots and the Mills Brothers — combined with rhythm and blues. Street-corner singing by teenagers in cities like New York, Philadelphia, and Baltimore produced hundreds of local groups. Hits included "Earth Angel" by the Penguins (1954), "Sh-Boom" by the Chords (1954), "Why Do Fools Fall in Love" by Frankie Lymon and the Teenagers (1956), "In the Still of the Night" by the Five Satins (1956), and "Come Go with Me" by the Del-Vikings (1957).

Doo-wop's popularity declined in the mid-1960s with the rise of Motown, soul, and the British Invasion, though it enjoyed revivals through oldies radio, the film American Graffiti (1973), and groups such as Sha Na Na.
