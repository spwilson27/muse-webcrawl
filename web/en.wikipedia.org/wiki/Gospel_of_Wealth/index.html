The Gospel of Wealth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919), steel magnate and author of the essay.

"The Gospel of Wealth" is an article written by Andrew Carnegie in June 1889, published in the North American Review, that describes the responsibility of philanthropy by the new upper class of self-made rich. Carnegie argued that the wealthy held their surplus fortunes as a trust for the benefit of the community and were morally obliged to distribute them during their lifetimes in ways that would promote the welfare and happiness of the common person.

Argument

Carnegie accepted that economic competition and the concentration of wealth were inevitable and even beneficial, but maintained that great fortunes must not be left to heirs — whom inherited wealth often harmed — nor given as indiscriminate charity, which he believed encouraged idleness. Instead, the rich man should act as "the mere agent and trustee for his poorer brethren," funding public goods such as libraries, universities, parks, and concert halls that gave people the means to help themselves.

His most famous line from the essay declares: "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

Legacy

Carnegie practised his doctrine on an unprecedented scale, giving away roughly $350 million — the bulk of his fortune — and funding more than 2,500 public libraries around the English-speaking world, as well as Carnegie Mellon University and the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace. The essay remains a foundational text of modern philanthropy and a reference point in debates over wealth inequality, inspiring later initiatives such as The Giving Pledge.
