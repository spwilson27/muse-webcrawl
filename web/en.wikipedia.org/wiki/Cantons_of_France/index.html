Cantons of France

The cantons of France are territorial subdivisions of the French Republic, intermediate between the arrondissement and the commune. Their principal function today is electoral: each canton elects two members (a man and a woman, standing as a pair) to the departmental council. Following a major redistricting enacted in 2013–2014 and applied at the 2015 departmental elections, metropolitan and overseas France has just over 2,000 cantons — about half the previous number — each drawn to contain a roughly equal population within a department.

History

Cantons were created during the French Revolution, by the decree of 22 December 1789 that reorganised local administration, and were initially both judicial districts (with a justice of the peace) and electoral districts. Over the following two centuries their number and boundaries changed repeatedly with population movement, producing wide disparities in electorate size that the 2013 reform sought to correct by imposing demographic equality as the governing criterion.

Organisation

A canton may consist of whole communes, fractions of large communes, or groups of small rural communes; the capital of the canton is its bureau centralisateur (formerly chef-lieu). Unlike departments and communes, cantons have no administration or legal personality of their own; they remain electoral constituencies and statistical units, although some state services are still organised by canton.

Role in elections

Departmental councillors are elected for six-year terms by a two-round binominal majority system: each canton returns a mixed-gender pair elected on a joint ticket. This parity requirement, introduced by the 2013 law, substantially increased the number of women in departmental assemblies.
