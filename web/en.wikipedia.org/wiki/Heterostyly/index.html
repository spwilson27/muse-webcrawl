Heterostyly

Heterostyly is a genetically controlled floral polymorphism in which a plant population produces two or three flower morphs that differ in the heights at which their stigmas and anthers are positioned (reciprocal herkogamy). The arrangement promotes cross-pollination between morphs and is usually coupled with a self-incompatibility system that prevents fertilisation within a morph.

Distyly and tristyly

In distylous species such as primroses (Primula), "pin" flowers have a long style and low anthers, while "thrum" flowers have a short style and high anthers, so a pollinator's body contacts male and female parts at corresponding positions on each morph. Tristyly, found in purple loosestrife (Lythrum salicaria) and some Oxalis, involves three morphs with mid-length organs as well.

Genetics

Distyly is controlled by a supergene — a tightly linked cluster of genes inherited as a single unit. In Primula, the thrum form is hemizygous for an S-locus segment of about 278 kb containing genes that control style length and anther position, a striking case of a Mendelian trait first studied by Charles Darwin.

Evolution

Darwin devoted his 1877 book The Different Forms of Flowers on Plants of the Same Species to heterostyly, concluding that it functions to promote legitimate (between-morph) pollination. The trait has evolved independently in at least 28 flowering-plant families, and its breakdown has repeatedly given rise to self-fertilising homostylous lineages.
