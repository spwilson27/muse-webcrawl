Duodenum

The duodenum is the first section of the small intestine in most vertebrates, including mammals, reptiles, and birds. In mammals it is a hollow, C-shaped tube about 25–38 centimetres (10–15 inches) long, connecting the stomach to the jejunum. Its name derives from the Latin duodenum digitorum, "twelve finger-widths", reflecting its length as measured by the anatomist Herophilus.

Structure and function

The duodenum is divided into four parts: superior, descending, horizontal, and ascending. It wraps around the head of the pancreas and receives the common bile duct and pancreatic duct at the major duodenal papilla (ampulla of Vater), through which bile and pancreatic juice enter. Brunner's glands in its wall secrete alkaline mucus that protects the duodenum from acidic chyme arriving from the stomach.

The duodenum is where most chemical digestion takes place. Chyme is neutralised and mixed with bile, which emulsifies fats, and with pancreatic enzymes that break down proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids. The hormones secretin and cholecystokinin, released by duodenal cells, coordinate this process. Some absorption, notably of iron and calcium, also occurs here. Clinically, the duodenum is a common site of peptic ulcers, and its diseases include duodenitis, coeliac disease (which primarily affects its villi), and obstruction.
