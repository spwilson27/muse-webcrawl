Swoon hypothesis

The swoon hypothesis is a theory concerning the resurrection of Jesus which holds that Jesus did not die on the cross, but merely fell unconscious ("swooned") and was later revived in the tomb, and that his reappearance to his disciples was therefore not a miraculous resurrection but a recovery from apparent death.

History of the hypothesis

Versions of the idea were proposed during the Enlightenment period of biblical criticism. Karl Friedrich Bahrdt suggested around 1780 that Jesus deliberately staged an apparent death using drugs, while Karl Venturini proposed that Jesus fell into a coma and revived in the cool tomb. Heinrich Paulus advanced a rationalistic version in the early nineteenth century. In the twentieth century the hypothesis was popularised for a general readership by Hugh J. Schonfield's 1965 bestseller The Passover Plot, and related ideas appear in some Islamic traditions, which hold that Jesus was not crucified at all.

Criticism

The hypothesis has been rejected by most historians and medical commentators. Arguments against it include the severity of Roman scourging and crucifixion, the Gospel account that a soldier pierced Jesus's side and observed a flow of blood and water, and the improbability that a half-dead man could have moved the tomb stone, evaded guards, and inspired his followers with the conviction that he had conquered death. Modern medical analyses of crucifixion generally conclude that survival was effectively impossible once the process had run its full course.
