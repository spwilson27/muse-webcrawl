Sir William Empson (27 September 1906 – 15 April 1984) was an English literary critic and poet, widely influential for his practice of closely analysing literary works, a practice fundamental to the New Criticism. His best-known work is his first, Seven Types of Ambiguity, published in 1930 when he was not yet 24.

Early life and education

Empson was born at Yokefleet Hall in Yorkshire. He was educated at Winchester College and Magdalene College, Cambridge, where he first read mathematics before switching to English and studying under I. A. Richards. His Cambridge career ended abruptly in 1929 when contraceptives were found in his rooms and he was rusticated, losing his fellowship prospects there.

Career

Empson taught in Japan at Tokyo University of Literature and Science (Bunrika) in the early 1930s, and then in China at Peking National University, where he taught through the Second Sino-Japanese War, joining the refugee university trek to Changsha and Yunnan. During the Second World War he worked in London for the BBC, editing reports for the Eastern Service. After the war he returned to Peking until 1952, then became Professor of English at the University of Sheffield, a post he held until his retirement in 1972. He was knighted in 1979.

Works

Seven Types of Ambiguity (1930) catalogued the ways poetic language can carry multiple simultaneous meanings. Some Versions of Pastoral (1935) broadened his analysis to prose and drama, and The Structure of Complex Words (1951) extended his method to keywords such as "honest" and "sense". Milton's God (1961) mounted a celebrated attack on the Christian God of Paradise Lost. Empson's own poetry — including "This Last Pain" and the villanelle "Missing Dates" — is noted for its intellectual compression and formal ingenuity. His collected poems appeared in 1940 and in later editions.

Legacy

Empson's defence of the richness of verbal meaning and of the reader's reasoned response made him one of the most influential critics of the twentieth century, though later theorists disputed his assumptions about authorial intention.
