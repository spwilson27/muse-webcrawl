GroEL is a protein of the chaperonin family — a molecular chaperone that assists the folding of other proteins in bacteria. Together with its co-chaperonin GroES it forms one of the best-understood folding machines in cell biology: a barrel-shaped complex that encapsulates a single unfolded protein chain in a protected chamber and gives it time and space to fold, driven by cycles of ATP binding and hydrolysis. In Escherichia coli, GroEL is essential for growth and folds a substantial fraction of the cytosolic proteome.

Structure and mechanism

GroEL consists of two rings of seven identical subunits stacked back to back, each subunit having an equatorial ATPase domain, a hinge-like intermediate domain and an apical domain that binds unfolded substrates and GroES. When ATP and GroES bind one ring (the cis ring), GroES caps the barrel, the cavity roughly doubles in volume and its walls switch from hydrophobic to hydrophilic, releasing the trapped substrate into a sequestered "Anfinsen cage" where it folds for about ten seconds. ATP hydrolysis then triggers release of GroES and the folded (or partly folded) protein. The landmark 1997 crystal structure of the GroEL–GroES–ADP complex by Z. Xu, A. L. Horwich and P. B. Sigler revealed this mechanism at atomic resolution.

Names and relatives

The names GroE and GroEL derive from bacteriophage growth: the genes were identified through mutations affecting the growth of phage λ in E. coli. The mitochondrial homologue is Hsp60, and the eukaryotic cytosolic counterpart is the CCT/TRiC chaperonin, an eight-subunit-per-ring complex with specialised subunits.
