Buddhist cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A temple meal of small bowls of vegetables, rice and soup arranged on a tray]
A temple meal of small bowls of vegetables, rice and soup arranged on a tray

Buddhist cuisine is the culinary tradition shaped by Buddhism's teaching of non-violence (ahimsa) toward sentient beings, expressed in vegetarian (and often vegan) cooking developed in the temples of East and Southeast Asia. Its most refined form is Japanese shōjin ryōri ('devotion cuisine') of Zen temples; Chinese Buddhist cooking created a celebrated repertoire of mock meats from gluten, tofu and mushrooms, and Korean temple food has become a byword for subtle vegetable cookery.

Principles

Mahayana scriptures (notably the Lankavatara Sutra) urge abstention from meat; East Asian monastics also avoid the 'five pungent vegetables' (garlic, onions, leeks and kin) said to inflame the senses. Theravada monastics, by contrast, traditionally eat what is given in the alms bowl, including meat not killed for them — so 'Buddhist cuisine' properly names the East Asian temple tradition. Meals are meditation: seasonal, local, frugal, nothing wasted, prepared (in shōjin doctrine) through the 'five flavours, five colours, five methods'.

Dishes and influence

Temple kitchens invented much of Asia's vegetarian vocabulary: tofu in its many forms, wheat gluten 'meats', yuba (tofu skin), konjac, fermented bean pastes and mushroom stocks of deep umami. Lay Buddhist restaurants in China, Taiwan and Vietnam serve banquet imitations of duck, pork and fish; modern chefs from Kyoto to New York draw on shōjin technique for the plant-based era, and Korea promotes temple food as national heritage.
