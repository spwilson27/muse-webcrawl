A chord, in music, is any combination of three or more notes sounded simultaneously. In Western tonal harmony the most basic chord is the triad, built from a root note with a third and a fifth above it. Chords underpin melody by providing harmonic accompaniment, and sequences of chords — chord progressions — give music much of its sense of direction and emotional character.

Triads

A triad consists of three notes stacked in thirds. Depending on the sizes of those thirds, four qualities arise: major (major third plus minor third), minor (minor third plus major third), diminished (two minor thirds), and augmented (two major thirds). A chord's notes may be rearranged into inversions, in which a note other than the root is lowest, and spread across octaves without changing the chord's identity.

Seventh chords and extensions

Adding a third above the fifth produces seventh chords, central to jazz and classical harmony alike. Adding still more thirds yields extended chords (ninths, elevenths, thirteenths). Sus chords replace the third with a second or fourth, and added-tone chords insert extra notes into a basic triad.

Function and progression

In tonal music, chords take on functions — tonic (home), dominant (tension seeking resolution), and subdominant (preparation) — traditionally labelled with Roman numerals (I, IV, V, and so on). Famous progressions include the I–V–vi–IV of much popular music, the ii–V–I of jazz, and the twelve-bar blues. The systematic study of how chords connect is called voice leading.

History

Simultaneous sounds appeared in medieval organum, but the chord as a self-contained harmonic unit emerged with the thoroughbass practice of the Baroque era and was theorized by Jean-Philippe Rameau in his Traité de l'harmonie (1722), which established the root and the fundamental bass. Later theorists extended chord vocabulary through chromaticism, and 20th-century composers and jazz musicians developed quartal harmony (chords built in fourths), clusters, and polychords.
