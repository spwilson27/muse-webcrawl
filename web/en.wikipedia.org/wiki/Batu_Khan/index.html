Batu Khan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The steppe capital of Sarai as imagined in a reconstruction drawing]

Batu Khan (c. 1205 – 1255) was a Mongol ruler and the founder of the Golden Horde, the khanate that governed the western Eurasian steppe and held suzerainty over the Rus' principalities for two centuries. A grandson of Genghis Khan (second son of Jochi), he commanded — with the great general Subutai as his strategist — the Mongol invasion of Europe (1236–1242): the conquest of the Volga Bulgars and Cumans, the destruction of the Rus' cities including Kiev (1240), and the victories at Legnica and Mohi (1241). Though heir-presumptive in prestige to the imperial succession, he withdrew to his appanage on the Volga, founded his capital at Sarai, and played kingmaker in the succession crises of the 1240s–50s.

The western campaign

Allotted the lands 'wherever Mongol horses' hooves reached' in the west, Batu led the great expedition voted by the kuriltai of 1235. His armies wintered on the steppe, annihilated Bulgar and Cuman power, and in successive winters took Ryazan, Vladimir-Suzdal and (in December 1240) Kiev, whose fall shocked the Christian world. In 1241 his columns won at Legnica in Poland and, with Subutai, destroyed King Béla IV's Hungary at Mohi; only the withdrawal of 1242 — following Ögedei's death, and with succession politics calling Batu home — spared the Latin west further campaigning.

The Golden Horde

Batu built his capital Sarai on the lower Volga and organised the ulus of Jochi: direct rule of the steppe, indirect suzerainty over the Rus' princes, who travelled to Sarai (and some to Karakorum) for their patents of rule (yarliqs) and paid the tribute remembered in Russian tradition as the 'Mongol yoke'. He backed Möngke's accession in 1251, securing his own house's position. He died in 1255; his successors (Sartaq, then his brother Berke, the Horde's first Muslim khan) made the Golden Horde an independent power that endured into the 15th century.
