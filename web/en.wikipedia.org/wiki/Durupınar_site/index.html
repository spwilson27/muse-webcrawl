Durupınar site

The Durupınar site is a geological formation located near the village of Üzengili in the Doğubayazıt district of Ağrı Province, eastern Turkey, about 29 kilometres (18 mi) south of the Greater Mount Ararat summit. It is a boat-shaped structure, roughly 164 metres long, which some have claimed to be the fossilised remains of Noah's Ark.

Discovery

The formation is named after İlhan Durupınar, a captain in the Turkish military who identified the unusual shape in aerial photographs taken during a NATO mapping mission in the 1950s; the photographs were brought to wider attention around 1959–1960. Sevket Kurtis and other researchers examined the images, and the site was visited by several expeditions over the following decades.

Exploration and claims

The American explorer Ron Wyatt visited the site in the late 1970s and 1980s and promoted it as the remains of Noah's Ark, claiming to have found deck timber, rivets and other artefacts. In 1987 the Turkish government declared the area a national park (Noah's Ark National Park) and erected a visitor centre. Wyatt's claims were publicised in books and documentaries but were never accepted by mainstream archaeologists or geologists.

Geological interpretation

Geologists who have studied the formation, including those who initially participated in expeditions to the site, generally interpret it as a natural structure — an elongated mudflow or syncline formation in limestone whose boat-like outline is coincidental. Samples said to be petrified wood were identified as ordinary rock, and the supposed artefacts as naturally occurring minerals and concretions. The site remains a minor tourist attraction and continues to be promoted by ark-search enthusiasts.
