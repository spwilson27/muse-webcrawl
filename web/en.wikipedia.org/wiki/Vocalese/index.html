Vocalese

Vocalese is a style of jazz singing in which words are set to previously recorded instrumental improvisations — the singer reproduces, with lyrics, every note of a famous solo. Unlike scat singing, which improvises wordlessly, vocalese is composed in advance: the lyricist transcribes an improvised performance and writes a text that fits its phrasing exactly, often with the lyric commenting on the original soloist or recording.

History and practitioners

Eddie Jefferson is credited as the originator, writing lyrics to solos in the 1940s; his celebrated version of Coleman Hawkins's "Body and Soul" and his setting of Charlie Parker's solo on "Parker's Mood" established the form. King Pleasure (Clarence Beeks) popularised it with his 1952 hit "Moody's Mood for Love", based on James Moody's alto solo on "I'm in the Mood for Love". Jon Hendricks raised vocalese to virtuoso ensemble art with the trio Lambert, Hendricks & Ross (1957–1964), writing and singing entire Count Basie band arrangements — including every horn solo — on albums such as Sing a Song of Basie. Hendricks later set Thelonious Monk compositions for choir and solo voices.

Other noted practitioners include Annie Ross (whose "Twisted", from a Wardell Gray tenor solo, became a standard covered by Joni Mitchell), Dave Lambert, Bob Dorough, Giacomo Gates, Kurt Elling, and the vocal group The Manhattan Transfer, whose Weather Report setting "Birdland" (lyrics by Hendricks) won a Grammy. Mark Murphy was acclaimed for his vocalese versions of bebop instrumentals. The style demands exact transcription, breath-defying phrasing, and lyrics that ride rapid eighth-note lines — and remains a revered, if specialised, corner of jazz singing.
