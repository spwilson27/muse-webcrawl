Society of the Snow (Spanish: La sociedad de la nieve) is a 2023 survival drama film directed by J. A. Bayona. It retells the story of Uruguayan Air Force Flight 571, which crashed in the Andes in October 1972 carrying the Old Christians rugby team to Chile; the survivors endured 72 days in the mountains and resorted to eating the bodies of the dead to stay alive. The film is based on Pablo Vierci's 2008 book of the same name, which drew on testimony from all sixteen survivors. It was Spain's submission for Best International Feature Film at the 96th Academy Awards, where it was nominated, and it also received a nomination for Best Makeup and Hairstyling.

Plot

The film is narrated from the perspective of Numa Turcatti, one of the passengers who did not survive. After the crash kills or fatally injures many of the 45 people aboard, the survivors shelter in the broken fuselage against sub-zero temperatures and avalanches, rationing scraps of food. When a radio reveals that the search has been called off, the group confronts starvation and collectively, agonisingly agrees to eat the dead. As weeks pass, expeditions probe the mountains; finally Nando Parrado and Roberto Canessa trek for ten days over the peaks into Chile and raise the alarm, and on 22–23 December 1972 helicopters rescue the sixteen survivors.

Production

Bayona first read Vierci's book while filming The Impossible (2012). Unlike the earlier Hollywood version Alive (1993), the production used Uruguayan and Argentine actors, filmed in Spanish, and named and dwelt on the dead as well as the survivors. Shooting took place in the Sierra Nevada in Spain, in Chile and Argentina, and at the actual crash site in the Valley of the Tears. The survivors and the families of the deceased were consulted extensively, and several survivors visited the set.

Reception

The film premiered out of competition at the Venice Film Festival in September 2023 and was released on Netflix in January 2024, becoming one of the platform's most-watched non-English-language films. Critics praised its technical realism, ensemble performances — particularly Enzo Vogrincic as Numa — and its treatment of the cannibalism theme as a story of solidarity rather than horror. It won twelve awards at the 38th Goya Awards, including Best Film and Best Director.
