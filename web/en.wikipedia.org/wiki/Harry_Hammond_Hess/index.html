Harry Hammond Hess

Harry Hammond Hess (May 24, 1906 – August 25, 1969) was an American geologist and a United States Navy officer in World War II. Considered one of the founding fathers of the unifying theory of plate tectonics, he is best known for his theories on seafloor spreading, specifically work on relationships between island arcs, seafloor gravity anomalies, and serpentinized peridotite, suggesting that the convection of the Earth's mantle was the driving force behind this process.

Born: May 24, 1906, New York City, U.S.

Died: August 25, 1969 (aged 63), Woods Hole, Massachusetts, U.S.

Alma mater: Yale University; Princeton University (PhD)

Known for: Seafloor spreading; discovery of guyots

Institution: Princeton University

Early life and career

Hess was born in New York City and studied geology at Yale, then completed his doctorate at Princeton in 1932. He joined the Princeton faculty and remained there for most of his career, becoming department chairman. In the 1930s he conducted gravity surveys of the Caribbean from submarines, identifying negative gravity anomalies associated with island arcs — work that foreshadowed his later thinking about crustal dynamics.

Naval service and guyots

During the Second World War, Hess commanded the attack transport USS Cape Johnson in the Pacific. Using the ship's echo sounder, he kept bathymetric records wherever he sailed and discovered numerous flat-topped seamounts, which he named "guyots" after Arnold Guyot, the 19th-century Princeton geographer. He rose to the rank of rear admiral in the Naval Reserve.

Seafloor spreading

In a paper circulated from 1960 and published in 1962 as "History of Ocean Basins", Hess proposed that new oceanic crust is continually created at mid-ocean ridges and spreads outward, eventually descending into the mantle at deep-sea trenches — the hypothesis, also developed independently by Robert Dietz, termed seafloor spreading. Hess humourously called the essay an exercise in "geopoetry". Combined with the Vine–Matthews interpretation of magnetic striping, the idea became a central pillar of the plate tectonics revolution of the late 1960s.

Hess also chaired the committee behind Project Mohole, the attempt to drill through the oceanic crust to the mantle, and played a leading role in the early American space programme's geology. He died of a heart attack in 1969 while chairing a meeting at Woods Hole.
