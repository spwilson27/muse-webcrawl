Atmospheric reanalysis
PurposeConsistent long-term record of past weather and climate
MethodData assimilation with a fixed forecast model

Reanalysis produces complete, gridded maps of past atmospheric conditions.

An atmospheric reanalysis is a scientific method for developing a comprehensive record of how the weather and climate are changing over time. In a reanalysis, observations from many sources — surface stations, ships, aircraft, radiosondes, and satellites — gathered over decades are combined, using data assimilation, with a single fixed numerical weather-prediction model. The result is a regular, physically consistent grid of atmospheric variables (temperature, wind, pressure, humidity, and many more) extending back far beyond the era of any single observing system, in a form directly comparable from year to year.

Why reanalysis is needed

Raw historical observations are unsuitable for many climate studies: instruments, stations, and observing practices changed over time, so a trend in the raw record may reflect the measurement system rather than the atmosphere. Because a reanalysis always uses the same model and assimilation scheme, inhomogeneities are greatly reduced (though not eliminated — changes in the observing system itself, such as the start of satellite data in 1979, still leave marks). Reanalyses are used for climatology, hydrological and energy planning, insurance and risk modeling, and as boundary conditions for regional climate models.

Major products

  • ERA5 — produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) for the Copernicus Climate Change Service; hourly data at ~31 km resolution from 1940 onward, succeeding ERA-Interim.
  • NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis (1996) — the pioneering global reanalysis, covering 1948 to the present.
  • MERRA-2 — NASA's reanalysis for the satellite era (1980–present), with aerosol fields.
  • JRA-55 — the Japan Meteorological Agency's 55-year reanalysis.
  • 20th Century Reanalysis — uses only surface pressure observations to extend the record back to the 19th century.
