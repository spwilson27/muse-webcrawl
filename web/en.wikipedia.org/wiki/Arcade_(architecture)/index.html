Arcade (architecture)

Arcade
ElementsArches, piers or columns, spandrels, imposts
Related formsColonnade, loggia, cloister, blind arcade
OriginsRoman architecture

An arcade is, in architecture, a succession of contiguous arches, each counter-thrusting the next, supported by columns, piers, or a covered walkway enclosed by a line of such arches on one or both sides. Such a walkway may be a passage or a gallery. Arcades go back to Roman engineering — as in the repetitive arched tiers of the Colosseum — and became a fundamental device of Romanesque, Gothic, Renaissance, and Islamic architecture.

Types

  • Blind arcade — arches applied decoratively to a solid wall rather than opening through it, common on Romanesque façades and apses.
  • Cloister arcade — the open arcades around a monastic garth.
  • Shopping arcade — a covered passageway lined with shops; the form flourished from the late 18th century (London's Burlington Arcade, 1819; Paris's passages couverts; Milan's Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II, 1865–77) and is a direct ancestor of the shopping mall.
  • Interlaced arcade — overlapping arches producing pointed intersections, seen in Norman and Islamic buildings.

History

Medieval churches used arcades to separate nave from aisles and to articulate triforium and clerestory levels. Renaissance architects such as Brunelleschi (the loggia of the Ospedale degli Innocenti, Florence, 1419) revived the classical round-arched arcade on slender columns. In Bologna, some 40 kilometres of street porticoes (portici) — arcaded sidewalks begun in the medieval period — are now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Gallery structure

An arcade's rhythm is defined by its piers and the arches springing from impost blocks above the capitals; the triangular wall areas between arches are called spandrels, and in the "arcuated" Roman manner the arch order may be framed by decorative half-columns and entablature, as on the Theatre of Marcellus.
