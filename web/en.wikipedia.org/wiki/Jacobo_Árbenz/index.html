Jacobo Árbenz

Juan Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán

Born: September 14, 1913, Quetzaltenango, Guatemala

Died: January 27, 1971, Mexico City, Mexico

Office: President of Guatemala (1951–1954)

Spouse: María Cristina Vilanova

Known for: Agrarian reform (Decree 900); overthrown in the 1954 CIA-backed coup

Juan Jacobo Árbenz Guzmán (September 14, 1913 – January 27, 1971) was a Guatemalan military officer and politician who served as President of Guatemala from 1951 to 1954. The second of the reformist governments of the Guatemalan Revolution, his administration is remembered for its agrarian reform program and for its overthrow in a coup organized by the United States Central Intelligence Agency, an event that helped set Guatemala on the path to a 36-year civil war.

Early life and rise

Árbenz was born in Quetzaltenango to a Swiss immigrant pharmacist father and a Guatemalan mother. He graduated from the Escuela Politécnica military academy as an outstanding cadet and rose through the army. In October 1944 he joined fellow officers Francisco Arana and Jorge Toriello in overthrowing the dictatorship that followed Jorge Ubico, and he served as defense minister in the revolutionary government of Juan José Arévalo. In the election of 1950 — among the freest in Guatemalan history — Árbenz won the presidency with over 60 percent of the vote, promising to modernize Guatemala and make it an economically independent, capitalist country.

Presidency and agrarian reform

Árbenz's centerpiece was Decree 900 (June 1952), an agrarian reform that expropriated uncultivated land on large estates — compensating owners with government bonds at the land's declared tax value — and redistributed it to some 100,000 peasant families. The reform touched the vast holdings of the United Fruit Company, which had declared its land at a fraction of the value it later demanded in compensation. His government also built the Atlantic highway and the port of Santo Tomás to break the United Fruit–linked railroad monopoly, and pursued a hydroelectric plant to end dependence on a U.S.-owned utility.

Overthrow

Portraying Árbenz as a communist beachhead in the Cold War, the Eisenhower administration authorized Operation PBSuccess. In June 1954, a small exile force led by Carlos Castillo Armas invaded from Honduras while CIA aircraft bombed and strafed the capital and psychological warfare sowed panic. The Guatemalan army refused to fight or to arm worker and peasant militias, and Árbenz resigned on June 27, 1954. His fall ended Guatemala's decade of reform; the ensuing military regimes and the civil war (1960–1996) cost an estimated 200,000 lives. Árbenz lived in exile in Mexico, Switzerland, and Cuba, dying in Mexico City in 1971. In 2011 the Guatemalan government formally apologized to his family for the state's role in his persecution and death.
