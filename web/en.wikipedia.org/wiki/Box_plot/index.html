A box plot summarises a distribution through its quartiles.

In descriptive statistics, a box plot (or box-and-whisker plot) is a method for depicting graphically groups of numerical data through their quartiles. Box plots may also have lines extending vertically from the boxes (whiskers) indicating variability outside the upper and lower quartiles, hence the alternative name. The plot was introduced by John Tukey in 1970 as part of exploratory data analysis.

Elements

A standard box plot displays the five-number summary of a set of data: the sample minimum, first quartile (Q1), median (Q2), third quartile (Q3) and sample maximum. The box itself spans Q1 to Q3, its length being the interquartile range (IQR), with a line at the median. The whiskers typically extend to the most extreme data points still within 1.5 × IQR of the quartiles; points beyond the whiskers are plotted individually as outliers.

Uses and variations

Box plots are nonparametric: they display variation in samples without assuming an underlying statistical distribution. They are especially useful for comparing distributions between groups — for example test scores across schools — because centres, spreads, symmetry and outliers can be compared at a glance. Variants include notched box plots (whose notches give a rough confidence interval for comparing medians), variable-width boxes scaled to group size, and the violin plot, which adds a kernel density trace to the box.

Limitations

Because a box plot compresses data to a handful of statistics, it can hide features such as multimodality — two datasets with identical five-number summaries may have very different shapes. For small samples, plots of the raw data are usually preferable.
