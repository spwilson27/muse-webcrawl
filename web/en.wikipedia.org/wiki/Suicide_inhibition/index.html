Suicide inhibition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A suicide inhibitor enters the active site and becomes covalently trapped.

In biochemistry, suicide inhibition, also known as suicide inactivation or mechanism-based inhibition, is an irreversible form of enzyme inhibition that occurs when an enzyme binds a substrate analog and forms an irreversible complex with it through a covalent bond during the "normal" reaction sequence. The inhibitor binds to the active site where it is modified by the enzyme to a reactive group that reacts irreversibly to form a stable inhibitor–enzyme complex.

Mechanism

A suicide inhibitor is designed to resemble the natural substrate. The enzyme begins its ordinary catalytic cycle on the molecule, but instead of releasing a product, it generates a highly reactive intermediate that attacks an amino-acid residue in the active site, forming a permanent covalent bond. Because the enzyme's own catalytic machinery creates the reactive species, such inhibitors can be extremely specific, and they inactivate the enzyme in stoichiometric amounts.

Examples and applications

Mechanism-based inhibition is exploited in several important drugs:

  • Penicillin inhibits the DD-transpeptidase that cross-links bacterial cell-wall peptidoglycan, binding covalently to its active-site serine.
  • Clavulanic acid is a suicide inhibitor of β-lactamase enzymes and is combined with amoxicillin (co-amoxiclav) to protect the antibiotic from bacterial resistance enzymes.
  • 5-Fluorouracil metabolites inhibit thymidylate synthase in cancer chemotherapy.
  • Eflornithine (DFMO) inhibits ornithine decarboxylase and is used against African sleeping sickness.
  • Selegiline and rasagiline are mechanism-based inhibitors of monoamine oxidase B used in Parkinson's disease.
  • Disulfiram inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase and is used in the treatment of alcohol dependence.
