Assistive technology

Assistive technologies range from simple grips to powered wheelchairs and eye-tracking communication devices.

Assistive technology (AT) is an umbrella term for assistive, adaptive and rehabilitative devices for people with disabilities, as well as the process of selecting, locating and learning to use them. AT promotes greater independence by enabling people to perform tasks they formerly could not accomplish, or had great difficulty accomplishing, by providing enhancements to, or changing methods of interacting with, the technology needed to accomplish such tasks.

Categories

Assistive technologies span mobility aids such as wheelchairs, walkers and prosthetic limbs; hearing aids and cochlear implants; vision aids including screen readers, Braille displays and magnification software; augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) devices, from letter boards to speech-generating tablets controlled by eye gaze; computer access tools such as alternative keyboards, switches and voice recognition; and environmental controls for operating lights, doors and appliances.

Legislation and provision

In the United States, the Assistive Technology Act of 1998 (amended 2004) funds state programmes, and the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act requires schools to consider AT for students with disabilities. The World Health Organization estimates that more than 2.5 billion people need one or more assistive products, with access far lower in low-income countries.

Design philosophy

Assistive technology overlaps with universal design: features first developed for disabled users — curb cuts, captions, voice assistants — often benefit everyone. A persistent problem is device abandonment, frequently attributed to poor matching between user, device and environment; assessment models such as the HAAT (Human Activity Assistive Technology) model address this.
