Principality of Wales

Placeholder image: the Principality (purple) and the Marcher lordships

The Principality of Wales (Welsh: Tywysogaeth Cymru) existed between 1216 and 1536, roughly, as a Welsh state ruled by a Prince of Wales recognised by the English Crown. Alongside it stood the Marcher lordships, semi-autonomous Norman lordships along the border; together they covered the territory of modern Wales.

Native principality

Llywelyn ab Iorwerth ("the Great") of Gwynedd united much of Wales and was acknowledged as Prince of Wales, formalised by the Council of Aberdyfi (1216). His grandson Llywelyn ap Gruffudd was recognised as Prince of Wales by Henry III in the Treaty of Montgomery (1267). After Llywelyn's death in 1282 during Edward I's conquest, the Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) incorporated the principality into the English Crown's administration, and Edward conferred the title on his son, the future Edward II, in 1301 — beginning the tradition of the English (later British) heir apparent holding the title.

Owain Glyndŵr

The revolt of Owain Glyndŵr, proclaimed Prince of Wales in 1400, briefly revived an independent Welsh principality with its own parliaments and institutions, before being suppressed by 1415.

Administration and end

The Crown's principality lands — the counties of Anglesey, Caernarfon, Merioneth, Flint, Carmarthen and Cardigan — were administered separately from the Marcher lordships until the Laws in Wales Acts (1535 and 1542) unified Wales with England's legal and parliamentary system, formally ending the principality as a jurisdiction, though the title Prince of Wales survives as that of the heir apparent.
