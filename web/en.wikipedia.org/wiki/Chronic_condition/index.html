Chronic condition

A chronic condition (also chronic disease or chronic illness) is a health condition that is persistent or long-lasting in its effects, typically defined as lasting three months or more and generally not curable by medication alone, though many can be controlled. Chronic conditions contrast with acute conditions, which develop suddenly and are of short duration.

Examples

Common chronic conditions include cardiovascular diseases such as hypertension and heart failure, diabetes mellitus, chronic respiratory diseases such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, arthritis, chronic kidney disease, many cancers, Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, depression and other mental illnesses, HIV/AIDS, and autoimmune diseases such as multiple sclerosis and lupus.

Burden of disease

Non-communicable chronic diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for roughly three-quarters of all deaths according to the World Health Organization. In the United States, a majority of adults have at least one chronic condition and a large share have two or more (multimorbidity), which grows more common with age. Chronic conditions account for the great majority of healthcare spending in developed health systems.

Risk factors and prevention

Major modifiable risk factors include tobacco use, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity, and harmful use of alcohol, together with metabolic factors such as raised blood pressure, blood glucose, and cholesterol. Prevention and management emphasize lifestyle change, long-term medication adherence, regular monitoring, and coordinated primary care.

Management

Because chronic conditions usually cannot be cured outright, care focuses on controlling symptoms, slowing progression, preventing complications, and maintaining quality of life. Self-management education, patient support groups, and multidisciplinary teams play central roles, and patients often face significant psychological, social, and financial burdens from living with long-term illness.
