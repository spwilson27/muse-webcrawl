Doxology

A doxology (from Ancient Greek δόξα, doxa, "glory", and -λογία, -logia, "saying") is a short hymn of praises to God in various forms of Christian worship, often added to the end of canticles, psalms, and hymns. The term also covers similar formulas of praise in Judaism and other religions, and, by extension, any expression of praise.

Christian doxologies

The most familiar is the Gloria Patri ("Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit..."), called the Lesser Doxology, in contrast to the Greater Doxology, the Gloria in Excelsis Deo. Trinitarian doxologies conclude the Psalms in the Roman breviary and many collects and hymns. The Lord's Prayer in many traditions closes with the doxology "For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever" — a formula found in later manuscripts of Matthew's Gospel and in the Didache.

The Protestant "Doxology"

In many English-speaking Protestant churches, "the Doxology" without qualification means the stanza beginning "Praise God, from whom all blessings flow", written by Bishop Thomas Ken in the late 17th century as the final verse of his morning and evening hymns, and sung to the tune Old 100th. It is commonly sung as an offering response or at the close of services.

Other traditions

Jewish prayer includes doxological formulas such as the Kaddish and the blessings (berakhot) that frame the liturgy, and the final words of psalms such as Psalm 150 function as a doxology to the Psalter. Hymns of praise in other traditions, including the Sikh and Hindu use of devotional chant, are sometimes described with the term by analogy.
