Counterurbanization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Counterurbanization is a demographic and social process whereby people move from urban areas to rural areas. The term was popularised by geographer Brian Berry in the 1970s to describe a reversal of the long-standing concentration of population in large cities, observed in the United States and later in other developed countries.

Causes

Motives include a desire for a better quality of life, lower housing costs, cleaner environments, and escape from congestion and crime, enabled by car ownership, improved transport links, retirement migration, and — increasingly — remote working. Businesses may follow, decentralising employment from metropolitan cores.

Effects and debate

Counterurbanization can revitalise declining villages but may also inflate rural house prices, strain services, and change community character. Geographers debate whether observed shifts represent a durable "clean break" from urbanisation or simply a stage in the redistribution of population within wider metropolitan regions, and many large cities resumed growing in later decades.
