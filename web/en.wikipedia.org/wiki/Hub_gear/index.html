Hub gear

A hub gear, internal-gear hub, internally geared hub or internally geared bicycle hub is a gear ratio changing system commonly used on bicycles that is implemented with planetary or epicyclic gears at the hub of the rear wheel. The gears are sealed within the hub shell, protecting them from dirt and damage, in contrast to derailleur gears, which are exposed.

How it works

In a typical three-speed hub, a single sun gear is fixed to the axle while planet gears mesh with it and with an outer ring gear. Depending on which element is driven and which is held by clutches selected by the shifter, the hub delivers a reduction gear, direct drive, or an overdrive gear. More elaborate designs use compound planet systems to provide 5, 7, 8, 11 or 14 speeds; the Rohloff Speedhub offers 14 evenly spaced ratios, while Shimano's Alfine and Nexus ranges and SRAM and Sturmey-Archer hubs cover the middle of the market.

Advantages and disadvantages

Hub gears can be shifted while stationary, require little maintenance, keep the chain in a fixed line, and work well with enclosed chains and belt drives — making them popular for city, utility and touring bicycles. Their disadvantages are greater weight, somewhat higher cost, and (in some models) slightly lower mechanical efficiency than a clean derailleur system.

History

Two- and three-speed epicyclic hubs were patented in the 1890s and early 1900s; Sturmey-Archer's three-speed became the standard fitting on British utility bicycles for much of the 20th century.
