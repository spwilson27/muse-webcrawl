Centrism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Centrism is a political outlook or position involving acceptance or support of a balance of social equality and a degree of social hierarchy while opposing political changes that would result in a significant shift of society strongly to the left or the right. Centre parties and voters typically draw from liberal, social-democratic and conservative traditions — free markets with welfare provision, gradual reform, and institutional stability — and are pivotal in multiparty systems, where they often hold the balance of power between blocs.

History and debates

The centre is a relational position: what counts as centrist shifts with the spectrum's poles. Historically strong centrist forces include European liberal and Christian-democratic parties and the "Third Way" social democracy of the 1990s; in two-party systems, centrism manifests as moderate factions and swing voters. Critics from both flanks charge centrism with incoherence or timidity; its defenders argue it reflects most voters' mixed preferences and sustains compromise. Political scientists distinguish positional centrism from "radical centrism", which proposes bold policies framed as beyond left and right.
