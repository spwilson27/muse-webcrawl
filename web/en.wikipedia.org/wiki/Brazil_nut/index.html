The Brazil nut (from the tree Bertholletia excelsa) is a South American tree in the family Lecythidaceae, and the name of its edible seed, one of the most valuable non-timber products of the Amazon rainforest. Despite the name, the largest exporter of Brazil nuts is Bolivia, ahead of Brazil and Peru. The trees are among the giants of the Amazon forest, growing up to 50 m (160 ft) tall with trunk diameters of 1–2 m, and may live for 500 years or more.

The fruit and the seed

Strictly speaking, the familiar Brazil "nut" is not a true nut in the botanical sense. The tree's fruit is a large woody capsule (pyxidium), 10–15 cm across and weighing up to 2 kg, containing 8–24 triangular seeds in segments rather like those of an orange, each seed encased in its own hard shell. The capsules ripen in the rainy season and fall to the forest floor, where they are gathered by collectors (castanheiros) and opened with machetes. The seeds depend for dispersal largely on agoutis, rodents capable of gnawing through the fruit.

Pollination and ecology

The tree's complex flowers can be pollinated only by large, strong bees — principally female orchid bees — whose males in turn depend on forest orchids for fragrances used in courtship. Brazil nut trees therefore fruit productively only in undisturbed rainforest, and attempts at plantation cultivation have generally failed, making the harvest an emblematic extractive economy that gives standing forest a direct commercial value.

Nutrition

Brazil nuts are exceptionally rich in selenium — a single nut can supply more than a day's recommended intake — and are also high in unsaturated fats, protein, magnesium, and thiamine. Because of the selenium content, excessive consumption can cause selenium toxicity (selenosis). The nuts are also notable for accumulating trace amounts of radium from the soil, making them slightly radioactive, though at levels considered harmless in normal consumption.

Trade

Brazil nuts are harvested almost entirely from wild trees in Brazil, Bolivia, and Peru, and exported chiefly to North America and Europe; European regulations on aflatoxin contamination in the shells have at times significantly affected the trade. The species is listed as Vulnerable owing to habitat loss in parts of its range.
