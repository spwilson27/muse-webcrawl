Constructive proof

In mathematics, a constructive proof is a method of proof that demonstrates the existence of a mathematical object by creating or providing a method for creating the object. This is in contrast to a non-constructive proof (also known as an existence proof or non-explicit proof), which proves the existence of an object without ever identifying it or showing how to find it.

The term "constructive" is closely associated with constructivism in the philosophy of mathematics, in which a mathematical object is held to exist only if it can be constructed. L. E. J. Brouwer, the founder of intuitionism, held that the law of the excluded middle could not be assumed valid when applied to infinite sets; constructivists therefore reject proofs that establish existence purely by contradiction, deriving an impossibility from the assumption that no such object exists.

Examples

A classic illustration concerns the claim that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational. The familiar non-constructive proof considers √2√2: if it is rational, the claim is proved; if it is irrational, then (√2√2)√2 = 2 is rational. The proof establishes existence without deciding which pair works. A constructive proof of the same claim exists, but it requires exhibiting explicit numbers and proving the required properties, such as a = √2 and b = 2·log23.

Constructive mathematics

Constructive methods are central to constructive analysis, constructive algebra, and type theory. Under the Curry–Howard correspondence, a constructive proof of an existence statement corresponds to a computer program that computes a witness, so constructively proved theorems can in principle be turned into algorithms. Errett Bishop's Foundations of Constructive Analysis (1967) showed that much of classical analysis could be developed constructively, giving the approach wide mathematical influence.
