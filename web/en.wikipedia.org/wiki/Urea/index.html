Urea


Urea, CO(NH2)2

Urea, also called carbamide, is an organic compound with the formula CO(NH2)2. It is a colourless, odourless, highly water-soluble solid. Urea is the principal nitrogen-containing waste product of protein metabolism in mammals and amphibians: it is produced in the liver by the urea cycle and excreted by the kidneys in urine. An adult human excretes roughly 25–30 grams per day.

Historical significance

Urea holds a famous place in the history of chemistry: in 1828 Friedrich Wöhler synthesised it from inorganic ammonium cyanate, the first time an "organic" compound had been made from non-living starting materials, undermining the doctrine of vitalism.

Uses

More than 90% of world production — well over 180 million tonnes a year, made industrially from ammonia and carbon dioxide — goes into nitrogen-release fertiliser; urea is 46% nitrogen by weight, the highest of any solid fertiliser. Other uses include urea-formaldehyde resins and adhesives, diesel exhaust fluid (AdBlue) for reducing NOx emissions, cattle feed supplements, skin creams that soften keratin, aircraft de-icing, and the manufacture of melamine and barbiturates.

Physiology and medicine

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is a routine clinical test of kidney function. In the body, skin and kidney medulla use urea in concentrating urine; sharks and rays retain high urea levels to balance seawater osmolarity.
