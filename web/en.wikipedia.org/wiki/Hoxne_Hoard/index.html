Hoxne Hoard

The Hoxne Hoard is the largest hoard of late Roman silver and gold discovered in Britain, and the largest collection of gold and silver coins of the fourth and early fifth centuries found anywhere in the former Roman Empire. It was found by metal detectorist Eric Lawes in the village of Hoxne in Suffolk, England, on 16 November 1992.

Discovery and excavation

Lawes was searching for a lost hammer at the request of the tenant farmer when his detector located silver spoons and coins. He reported the find rather than digging further, and archaeologists from the Suffolk Archaeological Unit excavated the site the following day, recovering the entire hoard intact. The objects had been packed in a wooden chest, with smaller boxes and organics, within an oak-lined pit.

Contents

The hoard contains 14,865 Roman coins — 569 gold solidi, 14,272 silver siliquae and 24 bronze nummi — dating up to the reign of Constantine III (AD 407–411), as well as silver tableware such as spoons, ladles and bowls, and gold jewellery including body chains and necklaces. Total weights are about 3.5 kg of gold and 23.75 kg of silver. Many silver items bear Christian chi-rho symbols and inscriptions naming their owners, such as Aurelius Ursicinus.

Significance

Because it was professionally excavated, the Hoxne Hoard gave an unusually complete picture of how such a treasure was packed and buried, probably in the turbulent years immediately after the end of Roman rule in Britain around AD 410. The hoard was declared treasure trove and valued at £1.75 million; it is now held by the British Museum, with highlights including the "Hoxne Tiger", the handle of a silver vessel in the form of a tigress.
