The Moulin Rouge (French for "Red Mill") is a cabaret in Paris, on the Boulevard de Clichy in the 18th arrondissement, at the foot of Montmartre. Founded in 1889 by Joseph Oller and Charles Zidler, it is marked by the red windmill on its roof and is famous as the birthplace of the modern can-can dance.

History

Opening during the Belle Époque, the Moulin Rouge quickly became a sensation. Its huge dance floor, mirrors and garden with a giant elephant allowed Parisians of all classes to mingle, and its dancers — performing the vigorous, high-kicking quadrille that evolved into the can-can — made stars of performers such as La Goulue and Jane Avril. The artist Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec was a regular, and his posters for the venue, beginning with Moulin Rouge: La Goulue (1891), became icons of the era.

Later years

After a fire in 1915 the hall was rebuilt and reopened in the 1920s under Jacques Charles, presenting revues starring Mistinguett. Operetta, cinema use and wartime disruption followed; in 1951 Georges France relaunched it, and in 1962 Jacki Clerico took over, expanding the auditorium with a giant aquarium and establishing the long-running dinner-show format. Since 1999 its revue has been Féerie, performed by the troupe of Doriss Girls. The venue inspired films including John Huston's Moulin Rouge (1952) and Baz Luhrmann's Moulin Rouge! (2001). The windmill's sails, which collapsed in April 2024, were restored in time for the Paris Olympics.
