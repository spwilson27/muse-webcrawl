The Acadians (French: Acadiens) are an ethnic group descended from the French settlers who colonised Acadia, in present-day Atlantic Canada, beginning in the early 17th century. Speaking a distinctive dialect of French and historically concentrated around the Bay of Fundy, they developed a largely self-governing, farming society that reclaimed marshland with dykes. Their history was transformed by the Expulsion of the Acadians (1755–1764), in which British forces deported more than 10,000 of them; many died, and the survivors scattered to other colonies, France, and Spanish Louisiana, where their descendants became known as Cajuns.

History

French settlement began at Port-Royal in 1605 and along the Bay of Fundy in the 1630s. Acadia changed hands repeatedly between France and Britain; the Treaty of Utrecht (1713) ceded peninsular Nova Scotia to Britain, and the Acadians, unwilling to swear an unconditional oath of allegiance that would oblige them to fight France or their Mi'kmaq allies, were known as the "Neutral French". During the French and Indian War, British authorities under Governor Charles Lawrence ordered their deportation — le Grand Dérangement — beginning with the Bay of Fundy campaign in 1755. Families were separated, lands confiscated, and some 11,500 Acadians transported to the British colonies, England and France; thousands died of disease, drowning and privation.

After 1764 some Acadians returned to the Maritimes, resettling in northern and eastern New Brunswick, Cape Breton and elsewhere, while others reached Louisiana via France — notably the seven ships of 1785 — and settled in the bayou country that became Acadiana.

Acadian renaissance

In the 19th century the Acadians rebuilt their institutions: the newspaper Le Moniteur Acadien (1867), national conventions beginning at Memramcook in 1881, an Acadian flag (1884) and national day, and the Société Nationale de l'Acadie. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem Evangeline (1847), though written by an American about fictional lovers separated in the Expulsion, made the Acadian story internationally famous. In the 20th century, reforms in New Brunswick under Premier Louis Robichaud, official bilingualism (1969) and the founding of the Université de Moncton strengthened Acadian language and culture. Congrès mondial acadien gatherings, held every five years since 1994, bring the diaspora together; a royal proclamation in Canada in 2003 acknowledged the wrongs of the deportation.

Culture and language

Acadian French preserves older French features and vocabulary distinct from Quebec French. Traditional culture centres on the Catholic parish, extended family, music, and the tintamarre — a noisy parade with pots and noisemakers held on National Acadian Day, 15 August. Cuisine includes fricot, poutine râpée, rappie pie and seafood dishes adapted to Maritime ingredients.
