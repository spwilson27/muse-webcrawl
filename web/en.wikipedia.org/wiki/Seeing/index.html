Seeing

Placeholder image: atmospheric turbulence blurring the image of a star.

In astronomy, seeing is the degradation of the image of an astronomical object caused by turbulence in the Earth's atmosphere. Variable refraction along the line of sight makes stars appear to twinkle and blurs the fine detail a telescope could otherwise resolve. Seeing is usually quantified by the angular size (full width at half maximum) of a star's image, measured in arcseconds; values around one arcsecond are considered good at professional observatory sites, while several arcseconds is typical at lower, more turbulent locations.

Measurement and causes

The strength of seeing is related to the turbulence and temperature gradients along the optical path, including the "dome seeing" produced by temperature differences inside an observatory. It is often parameterized by Fried's parameter r0, the diameter of a telescope aperture over which the image quality is roughly diffraction-limited. Astronomical seeing changes from night to night and minute to minute, and is generally better at high-altitude, dry sites with laminar air flow.

Mitigation

Techniques to overcome seeing include adaptive optics, which uses deformable mirrors guided by wavefront sensors (often with laser guide stars) to correct the image in real time; speckle imaging and lucky imaging, which exploit brief moments of good seeing; and space telescopes, which observe above the atmosphere entirely.
