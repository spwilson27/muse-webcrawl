Ali Baba is the hero of the folk tale "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves", one of the best-known stories associated with the One Thousand and One Nights. The tale does not appear in the oldest Arabic manuscripts of the Nights; it entered the collection through Antoine Galland's French translation (early 18th century), Galland having heard it from the Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab — it is one of the so-called "orphan tales" of the Nights.

Plot

Ali Baba, a poor woodcutter, spies a band of forty thieves opening their treasure cave with the magic words "Open, Sesame" ("Open Simsim"). He helps himself modestly to the gold; his rich, greedy brother Cassim follows, forgets the password inside the cave, and is cut to pieces by the returning thieves. Ali Baba retrieves the body, and the slave girl Morgiana has it sewn back together so the death seems natural. The thieves repeatedly try to locate and kill Ali Baba — marking his door, then hiding in oil jars in his courtyard — but Morgiana foils each attempt, finally killing the captain herself by pouring boiling oil into the jars and dispatching the disguised leader at a banquet. Ali Baba frees and marries her to his son, and alone keeps the secret of the cave.

Themes and reception

The tale contrasts Ali Baba's modest fortune with Cassim's lethal greed and makes the low-status Morgiana its true problem-solver — she is arguably the cleverest heroine of the Nights tradition. "Open Sesame" has become a byword for a password or key that magically grants access. The story has been endlessly adapted in pantomime, film, opera (notably Cherubini's Ali Baba), animation and children's literature, and Ali Baba's name is used in several languages as slang connected with thieves and stolen goods.
