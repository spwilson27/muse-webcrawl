The Nubian languages are a group of related languages spoken by the Nubian peoples along the Nile in southern Egypt and northern Sudan, together with outlier communities in the Nuba Mountains of Kordofan and in Darfur. They belong to the Eastern Sudanic branch of the proposed Nilo-Saharan family. The Nubian group is among the oldest attested language families of Africa south of Egypt.

Living languages

  • Nobiin (formerly called Mahas), spoken between Aswan and the Third Cataract region straddling the Egypt–Sudan border; the largest Nubian language, with perhaps 600,000 speakers.
  • Kenzi (Mattokki) around Kenuz in Egypt and Dongolawi (Andaandi) around Dongola in Sudan — often treated as one language, Kenzi-Dongolawi.
  • Hill Nubian (Kordofan Nubian), a cluster of dialects in the Nuba Mountains, probably reflecting an early southward migration.
  • Midob (Meidob), spoken around Malha in North Darfur.
  • Birgid, formerly spoken north of Nyala in Darfur, became extinct in the 20th century.

Old Nubian

The medieval Christian kingdoms of Nubia — Nobadia, Makuria and Alodia — wrote Old Nubian from about the 8th to the 15th century in a modified Coptic uncial alphabet supplemented with characters derived from Meroitic. The surviving corpus of parchment manuscripts and inscriptions (biblical translations, saints' lives, legal deeds, letters) was recovered mainly from Qasr Ibrim, Faras, and Old Dongola, and Old Nubian is regarded as ancestral to Nobiin. It is one of sub-Saharan Africa's earliest continuously written languages.

Present status

All Nubian languages are under pressure from Arabic. In Egypt, Nubian communities were displaced from their homeland in the 1960s by the flooding of Lake Nasser behind the Aswan High Dam, and the languages are classified as endangered to varying degrees. Revival efforts include Nubian-language teaching in Sudan and Egypt, radio broadcasts, and the encoding of the Old Nubian script, which was added to the Unicode standard in 2016.
