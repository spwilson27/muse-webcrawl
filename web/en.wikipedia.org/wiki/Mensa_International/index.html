Mensa International is the largest and oldest high-IQ society in the world. It is a non-profit organisation open to people who score at or above the 98th percentile on a standardised, supervised intelligence test. Mensa is formally composed of national groups and the umbrella organisation Mensa International, with a registered office in Lincolnshire, England.

History

Mensa was founded in England in 1946 by Roland Berrill, an Australian barrister, and Dr Lancelot Ware, a British scientist and lawyer. They met by chance on a train and conceived the idea of a society for people with high IQs; the society's first meeting was held at Lincoln College, Oxford, on 1 October 1946. Berrill and Ware envisaged an apolitical, intellectually elite society free from social, racial and religious distinctions.

Early growth was slow, but the society expanded internationally in the 1950s and 1960s, notably with the founding of American Mensa in 1960. Today there are national Mensa groups in around 90 countries and members on all continents.

Purposes and activities

Mensa's constitution lists three purposes:

  1. to identify and foster human intelligence for the benefit of humanity;
  2. to encourage research into the nature, characteristics and uses of intelligence;
  3. to provide a stimulating intellectual and social environment for its members.

Members organise local, national and international gatherings (including an Annual Gathering in several countries), participate in Special Interest Groups (SIGs) covering subjects from science fiction to finance, and receive publications such as the Mensa International Journal and national magazines, including the Mensa Bulletin in the United States.

Membership

The sole qualification for membership is a score at or above the 98th percentile on an approved intelligence test, such as the Stanford–Binet or Cattell Culture Fair tests administered under supervised conditions. Individuals may also submit evidence of scores from previously taken approved tests. Membership is open regardless of age; there have been members as young as two years old.

Criticism

Mensa has been criticised for a perceived elitism, for the limitations of IQ testing as a measure of intelligence, and for internal disputes, including the early split that led to rival societies. Some former members and commentators have questioned the value of membership beyond social networking.
