The Aran Islands (Irish: Oileáin Árann) are a group of three islands at the mouth of Galway Bay, off the west coast of Ireland. From west to east they are Inishmore (Inis Mór, "big island"), Inishmaan (Inis Meáin, "middle island"), and Inisheer (Inis Oírr, "east island"). Administratively part of County Galway, the islands are one of the few remaining areas where Irish is the predominant everyday language, and they form part of the Gaeltacht.

Geology and landscape

The islands are an extension of the karst limestone of the Burren in County Clare: bare grey limestone pavements, cut by deep fissures, rise to cliffs on the Atlantic side. Over centuries islanders built soil by hand, mixing sand and seaweed on the rock, and divided the land into a dense mosaic of tiny fields bounded by thousands of kilometres of drystone walls.

History and archaeology

The islands are famous for their prehistoric stone forts, most notably Dún Aonghasa on Inishmore, a semicircular cliff fort of concentric drystone walls and a defensive band of sharp upright stones (a cheval de frise), dating in its earliest phases to the late Bronze Age. Early Christian remains include churches and monastic sites associated with St Enda, who founded a monastery on Inishmore in the late 5th century.

Culture

The islands inspired J. M. Synge's book The Aran Islands (1907) and his play Riders to the Sea; Robert Flaherty's 1934 documentary Man of Aran romanticised island life. Traditional dress — the bainín homespun jackets and the patterned crios belts — influenced the internationally famous Aran sweater, whose cable patterns became a commercial emblem of Irish craft, though its marketed "clan stitch" lore is largely a 20th-century invention. The traditional boat is the Galway hooker and the light canvas currach.

Economy and transport

Tourism, fishing, and small-scale farming sustain the population of around 1,200. Ferries run from Rossaveal in County Galway and from Doolin in County Clare; Aer Arann Islands operates flights from Inverin to airstrips on each island.
