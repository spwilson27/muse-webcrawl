Ziryab

Born789 (probably Baghdad)
Died857, Córdoba
OccupationMusician, singer, composer, courtier
Known forTransforming music and court culture in al-Andalus

Abū l-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Nāfiʿ (789–857), better known by his nickname Ziryab (from Persian zaryāb, or interpreted as "blackbird", for his dark complexion and fine singing voice), was a celebrated musician, singer, composer, and arbiter of taste at the Umayyad court of Córdoba in al-Andalus. A pupil of the great Baghdad musician Ishaq al-Mawsili, he became the most influential figure in the musical and social culture of Islamic Spain.

Life

According to later accounts, Ziryab's brilliance at the Abbasid court of Harun al-Rashid aroused the jealousy of his teacher, who compelled him to leave Baghdad. After stops in Syria, Egypt, and Kairouan in Ifriqiya, he was invited to Córdoba by the emir al-Hakam I; arriving in 822, shortly after the emir's death, he was lavishly welcomed by his successor Abd al-Rahman II, who granted him a large salary, houses, and estates. Ziryab remained the court's leading musician and trendsetter until his death in 857.

Musical innovations

Ziryab is credited with adding a fifth string to the oud (lute), giving the instrument its classic form, and with using an eagle's quill rather than wood as a plectrum, producing a cleaner tone. He founded a music school in Córdoba, is said to have known thousands of songs by heart, and systematised the performance of the nūba — the suite form that became the basis of Andalusian music traditions still preserved in North Africa (such as Algerian and Moroccan Andalusi music and Tunisian ma'luf).

Influence on culture

Later chroniclers credit Ziryab with a sweeping refinement of Cordoban life: introducing seasonal fashions and new hairstyles, toothpaste and deodorants, fine leather goods and glassware in place of metal goblets, and a formalised order of courses at table — soup, then fish, meat, and finally sweet dishes and nuts — said to stand behind the modern sequence of courses in European dining. Though some attributions are probably legendary, Ziryab remains a symbol of the cultural brilliance of Umayyad Córdoba and of the transmission of Abbasid court culture to the West.
