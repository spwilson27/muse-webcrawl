Marine food web

A marine food web is a food web of marine life. At the base of the ocean food web are single-celled photosynthetic organisms such as diatoms and dinoflagellates, collectively called phytoplankton. These are eaten by tiny animals called zooplankton, which in turn are consumed by small fish and crustaceans, and so on up through successive trophic levels to apex predators such as sharks, tuna, and marine mammals.

Unlike terrestrial food webs, which are built mainly on plants eaten by herbivores, marine food webs are strongly influenced by the microbial loop: dissolved organic matter released by organisms is taken up by bacteria, which are eaten by protozoa and thus re-enter the classical food chain. Dead organisms and waste sink as "marine snow", fuelling deep-sea communities of decomposers and detritivores.

Trophic structure

Primary producers

In sunlit surface waters, phytoplankton perform most primary production, using dissolved nutrients and carbon dioxide. Near coasts, macroalgae (kelp and other seaweeds) and seagrasses also contribute. At hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, chemosynthetic bacteria form production bases independent of sunlight.

Consumers

Zooplankton — copepods, krill, larval animals — graze phytoplankton and are the key link to forage fish (herring, anchovies, sardines), squid, and ultimately large pelagic fish, seabirds, seals, dolphins and whales. Benthic food webs on the seafloor are built on detritus, shellfish and bottom-feeding invertebrates eaten by demersal fish and crabs.

Key dynamics

Energy transfer between trophic levels is inefficient — often around 10% — limiting food-chain length. Trophic cascades occur when changes at one level propagate through the web, as when overfishing of cod releases smaller fish and invertebrates, or when sea otter predation on sea urchins allows kelp forests to flourish. Gelatinous zooplankton (jellyfish) can dominate "jelly webs" when fish are depleted. Human pressures — overfishing, nutrient pollution, ocean warming and acidification — are restructuring marine food webs worldwide.
