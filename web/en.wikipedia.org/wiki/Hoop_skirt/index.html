Hoop skirt

A cage crinoline of steel hoops, c. 1860.

A hoop skirt or hoopskirt is a women's undergarment worn in various periods to hold the skirt extended into a fashionable shape. Its framework of hoops, made of materials such as steel, whalebone, wood, or reeds, supports the skirt in a bell or dome shape, relieving the wearer of the weight of the many layered petticoats previously needed for the same effect.

History

The hoop skirt traces its origins to the Spanish verdugado of the 15th century and the farthingale of the 16th century. Hoops returned to fashion in the 18th century as panniers, which extended the skirt sideways at the hips. The modern hoop skirt appeared in the 1850s as the cage crinoline, patented in Paris in 1856; light spring-steel hoops sewn into fabric tapes made the garment cheap to produce and far lighter than petticoats.

At their widest in the early 1860s, crinolines could reach a circumference of several metres. Satirical journals mocked their inconvenience in crowds, carriages, and doorways, and the flammable fabric caused a number of widely reported accidents. By the late 1860s the silhouette shifted toward the back, producing the crinolette and then the bustle. Hoop skirts survive today mainly in bridal and formal wear.

Construction

A typical cage crinoline consists of graduated hoops of spring steel, cane, or whalebone connected by vertical cotton tapes, fastened at the waist with a buckle or ties. Later designs used a half-cage open at the front to allow the wearer to sit more comfortably.
