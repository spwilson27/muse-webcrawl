Grassland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Grasslands are areas where the vegetation is dominated by grasses, maintained where climate (too dry for forest, too wet or disturbed for desert), fire and grazing herbivores prevent trees from taking over. They cover roughly a third of Earth's land surface and take regional names that map the planet: the Eurasian steppe, North America's prairies and Great Plains, South America's pampas and llanos, Africa's savannas (where scattered trees blur the definition), and Australia's rangelands. Sedges and forbs season them; almost no other biome feeds so many large mammals — or so many people, indirectly.

Ecology and human use

Grassland grasses grow from their bases, surviving grazing and fire that kill tree seedlings; their deep, dense root systems built the richest soils on Earth (the chernozems and mollisols of Ukraine, the pampas and the prairies) and lock away large carbon stocks underground — a climate service only recently tallied. Hoofed grazers evolved with the grasses: bison, saiga, wildebeest, kangaroos. Humans turned grasslands into the world's breadbaskets and ranchlands — most temperate grassland is now farmland (the US Corn Belt was tallgrass prairie; less than a few percent remains), making grasslands among the most converted and least protected biomes. Pastoral peoples — Mongol herders on the steppe, Maasai in East Africa, gauchos, cowboys — built whole cultures on moving herds across them, and steppe empires from the Scythians to the Mongols rode out of the grass sea to remake history. Restoration, rewilding with bison, and regenerative grazing are the modern grassland debates.
