John Howard (prison reformer)

John Howard FRS (2 September 1726 – 20 January 1790) was an English philanthropist and the first modern prison reformer. His investigations into conditions in British and European prisons and his landmark book The State of the Prisons in England and Wales (1777) led to the first legislation for prison sanitation and the abolition of gaolers' fees.

Early life

Howard was born in Hackney, London, the son of a prosperous upholsterer. On a voyage to Lisbon in 1756 his ship was captured by a French privateer and he was briefly imprisoned in France—an experience sometimes credited with awakening his interest in prisoners. He settled at Cardington in Bedfordshire and in 1773 became High Sheriff of Bedfordshire, in which role he inspected the county gaol.

Investigations and reforms

Shocked by what he found—including prisoners detained after acquittal because they could not pay the gaoler's discharge fees—Howard testified before the House of Commons in 1774, contributing to two Acts of Parliament. He then travelled tens of thousands of miles inspecting prisons, bridewells and lazarettos (quarantine hospitals) across Britain and Europe, documenting overcrowding, disease, lack of sanitation and the payment of fees by prisoners. His findings, published in successive editions of The State of the Prisons and An Account of the Principal Lazarettos in Europe (1789), proposed paid salaried gaolers, inspection, separate cells, clean water and religious and medical provision.

Death and legacy

Howard died of typhus ("gaol fever") at Kherson in 1790 while inspecting military hospitals. He was the first civilian to be honoured with a statue in St Paul's Cathedral. The Howard Association, founded in 1866 and later merged into the Howard League for Penal Reform, carries his name, and his statistical, evidence-based method influenced penal reformers worldwide.
