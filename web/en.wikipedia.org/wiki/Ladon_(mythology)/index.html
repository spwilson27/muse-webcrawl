Ladon (Greek: Λάδων) was a serpentine dragon in Greek mythology that guarded the golden apples in the Garden of the Hesperides, at the western edge of the world. He coiled around the apple tree and never slept; according to Hesiod, he was the offspring of the sea deities Phorcys and Ceto, while other sources make him a child of Typhon and Echidna.

Mythology

The golden apples had been a wedding gift to Hera, who set Ladon and the Hesperides — the nymphs of the evening — to guard them. Obtaining the apples was the eleventh labour of Heracles. In the best-known version, Heracles persuaded the Titan Atlas, who held up the sky nearby, to fetch the apples for him; Atlas, momentarily free, picked them while Heracles shouldered the heavens — and Ladon was not harmed. In alternative accounts, Heracles killed Ladon himself, shooting him with arrows over the garden wall, or put him to sleep with a drugged cake. Some authors say Hera placed the slain dragon among the stars as the constellation Draco, coiled around the north celestial pole.

Description

Ladon is most often described as having a hundred heads, each speaking with a different voice, though later art simplified him to a single-headed serpent. In Greek vase painting he appears as a great snake wound round the apple tree, confronting Heracles or being fed by the Hesperides.
