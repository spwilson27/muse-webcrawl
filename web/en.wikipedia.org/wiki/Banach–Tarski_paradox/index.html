Banach–Tarski paradox

Banach–Tarski paradox
FieldMathematical logic, geometry, set theory
Proved byStefan Banach, Alfred Tarski
Year1924
Key assumptionAxiom of choice

The Banach–Tarski paradox is a theorem in set-theoretic geometry which states the following: given a solid ball in three-dimensional space, there exists a decomposition of the ball into a finite number of disjoint subsets, which can then be put back together in a different way to yield two identical copies of the original ball. Indeed, the reassembly process involves only moving the pieces around and rotating them, without changing their shape. The pieces themselves, however, are not measurable "solids" but infinite scatterings of points.

Because of this, the theorem is often called a "paradox", although it is not a logical contradiction but a counterintuitive consequence of the axiom of choice.

Background

The result was published by Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski in 1924, building on earlier work by Felix Hausdorff (the Hausdorff paradox of 1914) and Giuseppe Vitali. A key ingredient is the existence of a free group on two generators inside the rotation group SO(3), which itself admits a paradoxical decomposition.

Sketch of the proof

The proof proceeds in outline as follows. First, the free group F2 on two generators is decomposed into four sets which, after multiplication by a single group element, can be reassembled into two copies of the whole group. This paradoxical decomposition is then transferred, via a suitable action, to the unit sphere, after removing a countable set of fixed points. Finally the result is extended from the sphere to the solid ball by considering radii, and the countable exceptional set is absorbed by a rotation about a suitable axis.

Interpretation

The paradox shows that there can be no finitely additive, rotation-invariant measure defined on all subsets of three-dimensional space that assigns the unit ball measure 1. The pieces in the decomposition are necessarily non-measurable sets, whose existence requires the axiom of choice. In the Solovay model of set theory, in which every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable, the paradox cannot be carried out.
