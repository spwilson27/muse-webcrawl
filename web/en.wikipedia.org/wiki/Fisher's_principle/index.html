Fisher's principle

Fisher's principle is an evolutionary model that explains why the sex ratio of most species that produce offspring through sexual reproduction is approximately 1:1 between males and females. W. D. Hamilton summarised Fisher's principle in the following way:

"1. Suppose male births are less common than female.
2. A newborn male then has better mating prospects than a newborn female, and therefore can expect to have more offspring.
3. Therefore parents genetically disposed to produce males tend to have more than average numbers of grandchildren born to them.
4. Therefore the genes for male-producing tendencies spread, and male births become more common.
5. As the 1:1 sex ratio is approached, the advantage associated with producing males disappears.
6. The same reasoning holds if females are substituted for males throughout. Therefore 1:1 is the equilibrium ratio."

Explanation

Fisher's principle was outlined by Ronald Fisher in his 1930 book The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection (but has been incorrectly attributed to Fisher as original). Fisher, however, attributed it to A. W. F. Edwards. Fisher framed his explanation in terms of parental expenditure: the total parental expenditure on male offspring must come to equal the total parental expenditure on female offspring at equilibrium, because each sex contributes, on average, an equal amount of ancestry to future generations. If one sex were rarer, individuals of that sex would have higher expected reproductive success, so natural selection would favour parents that produce more of the rarer sex until the ratio equalised.

History

The principle is one of the cornerstones of modern evolutionary biology and of sex allocation theory. It was one of the earliest applications of game-theoretic reasoning to biology, predating the formal development of game theory: the equilibrium it describes is an evolutionarily stable strategy. Fisher's explanation was elaborated by later writers, including Eric Charnov, and is related to Hamilton's theory of extraordinary sex ratios, which covers cases in which the 1:1 prediction breaks down, such as when mating takes place among siblings in structured populations.
