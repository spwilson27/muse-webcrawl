Organoid

An organoid is a miniaturised and simplified version of an organ produced in vitro in three dimensions that shows realistic micro-anatomy. They are derived from tissue, from embryonic stem cells, or from induced pluripotent stem cells that self-organize in three-dimensional culture thanks to their self-renewal and differentiation capacities.

History

The field took off in 2009 when Hans Clevers's laboratory showed that single Lgr5-expressing intestinal stem cells could build crypt–villus structures in a Matrigel matrix supplemented with growth factors. In 2011 the same approach yielded stomach, liver, and pancreatic organoids. In 2013, Madeline Lancaster and colleagues in Jürgen Knoblich's group reported cerebral organoids that recapitulated features of human brain development and modelled microcephaly, prompting both excitement and ethical discussion about "brains in a dish."

Types and applications

Organoids have been generated for intestine, kidney, lung, liver, retina, inner ear, and many other tissues. Tumour-derived "tumoroids" preserve the mutations and heterogeneity of a patient's cancer and are used to test drug responses, notably in cystic fibrosis care, where intestinal organoid swelling assays predict individual responses to CFTR modulators. Organoids also serve as models for infectious disease — for example, Zika virus infection of cerebral organoids demonstrated its effect on developing neurons.

Limitations

Organoids generally lack vasculature, immune cells, innervation, and mature size; they show batch-to-batch variability and often remain at a fetal-like developmental stage. Current research combines organoids into "assembloids," engineers them with microfluidic "organ-on-a-chip" perfusion, and uses bioprinting to impose structure.
