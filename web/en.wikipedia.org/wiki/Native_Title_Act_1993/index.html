Native Title Act 1993

The Native Title Act 1993 is a law passed by the Parliament of Australia that codifies the concept and processes of native title — the recognition in Australian law that Indigenous Australians hold rights and interests in land and waters under their traditional laws and customs. The Act was the Keating government's response to the High Court's decision in Mabo v Queensland (No 2) (1992), which rejected the doctrine of terra nullius and held that native title survived the acquisition of sovereignty by the Crown unless validly extinguished. The Act commenced on 1 January 1994.

Background: the Mabo decision

On 3 June 1992 the High Court held, by six judges to one, that the Meriam people of the Murray Islands in the Torres Strait held native title to their lands, rejecting the enlarged doctrine of terra nullius that had underpinned Australian land law. The decision created legal and political uncertainty about land titles across Australia, and after months of negotiation with Indigenous leaders, pastoralists, miners and state governments, Prime Minister Paul Keating introduced the Native Title Bill. His Redfern Park speech of December 1992 had framed recognition of dispossession as a foundation for reconciliation.

Main provisions

  • Recognition: defines native title and native title rights and interests (s 223), requiring rights possessed under traditional laws and customs, a continuing connection with the land, and recognition by the common law.
  • Validation: validates past acts (such as grants of title) done between the commencement of the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 and 1 January 1994 that would otherwise be invalid because of native title, with compensation payable in some cases.
  • Future acts: sets rules for acts done after 1 January 1994 affecting native title, including the "right to negotiate" before mining and certain other grants on land where native title exists.
  • Claims process: establishes the National Native Title Tribunal (NNTT) for registration, mediation and future-act functions, with the Federal Court making determinations of native title.
  • Extinguishment: provides that native title is extinguished by valid acts inconsistent with its continued existence, such as grants of freehold title.

Amendments and later developments

The High Court's Wik Peoples v Queensland decision (1996), holding that pastoral leases did not necessarily extinguish native title, prompted the Howard government's Native Title Amendment Act 1998 — the "ten-point plan" — which wound back some of the Act's protections. Native title has since been determined to exist over a large and growing share of the Australian continent, often through negotiated Indigenous Land Use Agreements (ILUAs). The framework remains central to Australian land law and to the unfinished politics of reconciliation that later produced the Uluru Statement from the Heart.
