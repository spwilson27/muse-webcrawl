François-René de Chateaubriand

Born4 September 1768, Saint-Malo, France
Died4 July 1848, Paris, France
OccupationWriter, politician, diplomat
MovementRomanticism

François-René, vicomte de Chateaubriand (4 September 1768 – 4 July 1848) was a French writer, historian, politician and diplomat who founded Romanticism in French literature. From an ancient aristocratic family in Brittany, he briefly served in the army and emigrated during the French Revolution.

Works

His most famous works include Atala (1801), René (1802), The Genius of Christianity (1802), which helped revive Catholic faith in post-revolutionary France, and his posthumously published autobiography Mémoires d'Outre-Tombe (Memoirs from Beyond the Grave), considered his masterpiece.

Political career

Chateaubriand served as ambassador to London and Rome and as Minister of Foreign Affairs (1822–1824) under the Bourbon Restoration. A royalist who nonetheless defended liberty of the press, he withdrew from public life after the July Revolution of 1830.

Legacy

His melancholic, nature-loving prose profoundly influenced French Romantic writers such as Victor Hugo, who as a youth vowed to be "Chateaubriand or nothing".
