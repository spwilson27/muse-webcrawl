The Cushitic languages are a branch of the Afroasiatic language family. They are spoken primarily in the Horn of Africa, with minorities speaking Cushitic languages to the north in Egypt and Sudan, and to the south in Kenya and Tanzania. The most widely spoken Cushitic languages are Oromo (including varieties sometimes treated as separate languages) and Somali.

The name comes from Cush, a figure in the Hebrew Bible associated with the region south of Egypt. The ancient Egyptian language is not Cushitic but belongs to a separate branch of Afroasiatic.

Classification

Cushitic is commonly divided into North Cushitic (Beja), Central Cushitic (the Agaw languages), East Cushitic (including Oromo, Somali, Afar, and Saho), and South Cushitic (spoken in Tanzania and Kenya, including Iraqw). The internal classification and even the unity of Cushitic as a single branch have been debated among linguists; some have proposed that Beja is an independent branch of Afroasiatic.

Characteristics

Cushitic languages typically distinguish ejective consonants and pharyngeal sounds, have grammatical gender, and mark case and focus in distinctive ways. Many are tonal or pitch-accent languages. Word order is often subject–object–verb (SOV).
