Beer in Canada

Beer in Canada has a long history reaching back to the earliest European settlements, and beer remains the most popular alcoholic beverage in the country. Canadian brewing was shaped by British, German and later American influences, by Prohibition-era policies that varied province by province, and by the consolidation of the industry under a small number of multinational brewing groups. Canada is also known internationally for the strength of its pale lagers and for regional styles such as ice beer and cream ale.

History

The first brewery in New France was established by Jean Talon, the Intendant of New France, in Quebec City in 1668. Brewing expanded steadily with British rule; by the nineteenth century, families such as the Molsons in Montreal and the Labatts and Carlings in London, Ontario, had built major brewing enterprises. Molson Brewery, founded in 1786, is the oldest brewery in North America.

During the First World War and the interwar period, most provinces introduced prohibition, though enforcement and duration varied widely. After repeal, provincial governments tightly controlled alcohol retail through bodies such as the Liquor Control Board of Ontario (LCBO) and the Société des alcools du Québec (SAQ).

Industry

By the late twentieth century the Canadian market was dominated by three firms: Molson, Labatt and Carling O'Keefe. Foreign takeovers followed: Labatt was acquired by Belgium's Interbrew (now part of AB InBev) in 1995, while Molson merged with Coors in 2005 to form Molson Coors. Since the 1980s, a craft beer movement has grown rapidly, with hundreds of microbreweries and brewpubs operating across the country.

Styles and culture

Canadian beer is commonly sold in cases of 12 or 24 bottles, colloquially called a "two-four." Canadian lagers are typically brewed to about 5% alcohol by volume, slightly stronger than comparable American mass-market lagers. Ice beer, introduced by Labatt in 1993, is produced by chilling beer until ice crystals form and removing them, concentrating the alcohol. Cream ale, despite its name a lager-like hybrid, is another characteristically Canadian style. Beer plays a central role in Canadian social life, from hockey broadcasts to cottage weekends.
