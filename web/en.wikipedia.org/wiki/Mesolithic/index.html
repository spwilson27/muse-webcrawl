Mesolithic

The Mesolithic (Greek: μέσος, mesos "middle" + λίθος, lithos "stone") or Middle Stone Age is the Old World archaeological period between the Upper Paleolithic and the Neolithic. The term Epipaleolithic is often used synonymously, especially for areas outside northern Europe, and for the corresponding period in the Levant and Caucasus. The Mesolithic has different time spans in different parts of Eurasia. It refers to the final period of hunter-gatherer cultures in Europe and the Middle East, between the end of the Last Glacial Maximum and the Neolithic Revolution. In Europe it spans roughly 15,000 to 5,000 BP; in the Middle East (the Epipaleolithic Natufian) roughly 20,000 to 10,000 BP. The term is less used of areas farther east, and not at all beyond Eurasia and North Africa.

Terminology

The term "Mesolithic" was introduced by Hodder Westropp in 1866 to fill the gap between the Paleolithic and Neolithic divisions proposed by John Lubbock. Early researchers doubted the period was distinct enough to merit its own name, but evidence of small, finely worked stone tools — microliths — convinced most archaeologists that a transitional period existed. In regions where hunter-gatherer lifeways continued without the same glacial retreat dynamics, scholars often prefer "Epipaleolithic".

Characteristics

The type of culture associated with the Mesolithic varies between areas, but it is generally associated with a decline in the group hunting of large animals in favour of a broader hunting-and-gathering economy. Small chipped stone tools called microliths, often hafted in wooden handles to form composite tools such as harpoons, arrows and fish-hooks, are characteristic. Fishing tackle, stone adzes, canoes and bows are known from some sites. The domestication of the dog as a hunting companion probably dates to this period, and in a few favoured environments — such as the Danish Ertebølle culture and the Natufian culture of the Levant — semi-sedentary or permanently settled villages appeared.

Notable sites

  • Star Carr (England) — a lakeside settlement dating to around 9000 BCE, famous for red deer antler headdresses.
  • Lepenski Vir (Serbia) — trapezoidal houses and sculpted boulders on the Danube.
  • Franchthi Cave (Greece) — a long sequence documenting the shift towards farming.
  • Mount Sandel (Ireland) — among the earliest known houses in Ireland, c. 7900 BCE.

Transition to the Neolithic

As the climate warmed after the Younger Dryas, rising seas drowned coastal plains and forests replaced tundra. In the Fertile Crescent, Epipaleolithic peoples such as the Natufians began harvesting wild cereals intensively, leading to the earliest experiments in cultivation. Elsewhere, Mesolithic lifeways persisted for millennia alongside — and eventually gave way to — farming communities spreading from the Near East.
