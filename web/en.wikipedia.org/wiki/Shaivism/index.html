Shaivism

Shaivism (Sanskrit: शैवसम्प्रदायः, Śaivasampradāyaḥ) is one of the major Hindu traditions that worships Shiva as the Supreme Being. It is one of the largest denominations of Hinduism, with followers — called Shaivas or Shaivites — numbering in the hundreds of millions, and it has profoundly influenced the religious, artistic, and philosophical culture of India, Nepal, and Southeast Asia.

Beliefs and practices

Shaivas regard Shiva as the creator, preserver, destroyer, revealer and concealer of the universe — the five actions (pañcakṛtya) symbolized by Shiva's dance as Nataraja. Devotees commonly worship Shiva in the form of the lingam, often accompanied by devotion to his consort Parvati (Shakti) and sons Ganesha and Kartikeya (Murugan). Sacred ash (vibhuti), the three-line tripundra mark on the forehead, and the rudraksha bead mala are characteristic Shaiva emblems. Major centres of pilgrimage include Mount Kailash, Varanasi (Kashi), the twelve Jyotirlinga shrines, Kedarnath, and the Pashupatinath temple in Kathmandu.

History and schools

Roots of Shaivism reach back to the Vedic god Rudra and possibly to seal imagery of the Indus Valley Civilization. Organized Shaivism coalesced in the early centuries CE around ascetic orders such as the Pāśupatas, and between roughly 400 and 1000 CE Shaiva theologians produced a vast Sanskrit literature in the Āgamas and Tantras. Major sub-traditions include:

  • Śaiva Siddhānta — the dominant Tamil tradition, dualist in its classical form, preserved in the hymns of the Nayanmar saints.
  • Kashmir Shaivism (Trika) — a nondualist (monistic) school associated with Abhinavagupta, teaching recognition (pratyabhijñā) of one's identity with Shiva-consciousness.
  • Lingayatism (Vīraśaivism) — founded in 12th-century Karnataka in the movement of Basava, centred on personal worship of the ishtalinga.
  • Nāth tradition — yogic lineages associated with Matsyendranath and Gorakhnath, influential on haṭha yoga.

Spread

Shaivism spread beyond India along trade routes: it shaped the religious culture of the Khmer Empire (where Shiva was the state god and Angkor's temples were oriented by Shaiva cosmology), Champa, Java and Bali, where Shaiva Hinduism survives to the present day. Today Shaivism is especially strong in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Nepal and among the Hindu diaspora.
