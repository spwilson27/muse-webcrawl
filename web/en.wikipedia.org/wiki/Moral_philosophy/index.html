Moral philosophy

Moral philosophy, more commonly known as ethics, is the branch of philosophy that involves systematizing, defending, and recommending concepts of right and wrong conduct. The field of ethics, along with aesthetics, concerns matters of value; these fields comprise the branch of philosophy called axiology. Moral philosophy investigates questions such as "What is the best way for people to live?" and "What actions are right or wrong in particular circumstances?"

Branches

The field is usually divided into three related areas of inquiry:

  • Metaethics investigates the nature of moral judgment itself — what moral claims mean, whether they can be true, and how we can know them — including debates between moral realism and anti-realism, cognitivism and non-cognitivism.
  • Normative ethics seeks standards of right and wrong. Its major theories include consequentialism (judging acts by their outcomes, as in utilitarianism), deontology (duty- and rule-based ethics, as in Kant), and virtue ethics (centred on character, as in Aristotle).
  • Applied ethics applies normative theories to specific contested issues, such as medical ethics and bioethics, business ethics, environmental ethics, animal ethics, and the ethics of war and punishment.

History

Moral inquiry begins with ancient traditions worldwide: Confucius and Mencius on ritual propriety and benevolence, Buddhist and Hindu teachings on dharma and non-violence, and in the West the Socratic question "How should one live?" Plato and Aristotle made virtue and flourishing (eudaimonia) central. Hellenistic schools — Stoics, Epicureans, and Sceptics — offered rival therapies for life. Medieval philosophers integrated ethics with theology; modern figures such as Hume, Kant, and Mill reshaped the field around sentiment, duty, and utility respectively. The 20th century saw an initial turn toward metaethics, followed by a revival of normative theory after John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971) and growing attention to applied and feminist ethics.
