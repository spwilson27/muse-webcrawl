Passive management

Passive management (also called passive investing) is an investment strategy that aims to replicate the performance of a market index or benchmark rather than to beat it through security selection or market timing. The passive investor typically buys and holds a portfolio mirroring an index such as the S&P 500 — most commonly through index mutual funds or exchange-traded funds (ETFs) — accepting the market return minus very low costs. It is the opposite of active management.

Rationale

Passive management rests on the efficient-market hypothesis and on arithmetic: as William Sharpe observed in "The Arithmetic of Active Management" (1991), before costs the average actively managed dollar must equal the market return, so after costs it must underperform the average passively managed dollar. Decades of studies (such as the SPIVA scorecards) show that most active funds underperform their benchmarks over long horizons, especially after fees. John C. Bogle, founder of Vanguard, launched the first retail index fund in 1976 and popularised the philosophy; the approach had intellectual roots in the random-walk research of Burton Malkiel's A Random Walk Down Wall Street (1973).

Implementation

Key features
VehiclesIndex mutual funds, ETFs
CostsOften under 0.1% per year for broad equity indexes
TurnoverLow — only index reconstitution changes holdings
PioneersJohn C. Bogle (Vanguard), Wells Fargo and American National Bank institutional funds (early 1970s)

Passive portfolios may replicate an index by holding every constituent in proportion (full replication) or by sampling. Because trading is minimal, turnover, transaction costs, and taxable distributions are low. By the early 2020s, passive funds accounted for roughly half of US equity fund assets, and index funds' share of total fund assets continued to climb.

Criticisms and debates

Critics argue that very large-scale passive investing weakens price discovery, concentrates voting power in a few giant asset managers (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street), and mechanically pours money into the largest companies regardless of fundamentals. Defenders reply that even at current scale, price-setting is dominated by active traders, and that the cost savings to ordinary savers are enormous and certain, unlike hypothetical outperformance.
