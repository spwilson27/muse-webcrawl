English fairy tales

English fairy tales are the traditional wonder tales of England, collected from oral storytelling in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and published in collections such as Joseph Jacobs's English Fairy Tales (1890) and More English Fairy Tales (1894). Although England's native tradition was recorded later and less systematically than the German tales of the Brothers Grimm or the French tales of Charles Perrault, it produced several stories known around the world.

Well-known tales

  • Jack and the Beanstalk — a boy trades a cow for magic beans, climbs the giant stalk, and steals the giant's treasures.
  • Jack the Giant Killer — a Cornish hero who outwits a succession of giants.
  • Tom Thumb — the tiny hero swallowed by a cow and a fish; in print by 1621, among the earliest English fairy tales published.
  • The Three Bears — first recorded by Robert Southey in 1837; the intruder, originally an old woman, later became Goldilocks.
  • Tom Tit Tot — the English version of the Rumpelstiltskin story, collected in Suffolk.
  • Molly Whuppie, Cap o' Rushes (an English Cinderella variant), Dick Whittington and His Cat, Henny Penny, Mr and Mrs Vinegar, The Fish and the Ring, and Childe Rowland, which Shakespeare echoes in King Lear.

Collectors and collections

Early tales circulated in cheap chapbooks. In the nineteenth century, folklorists including James Orchard Halliwell and later the members of the Folk-Lore Society began gathering nursery tales in print. Joseph Jacobs, an Australian-born folklorist, gave the tradition its canonical form; his two volumes retold about ninety tales in a lively, readable style with notes comparing them to continental parallels. Andrew Lang's coloured Fairy Books also included many English stories. In the twentieth century, Katharine Briggs's A Dictionary of British Folk-Tales (1970) provided the standard scholarly catalogue.

Characteristics

English tales are often noted for their brisk, humorous, unsentimental tone, their plucky trickster heroes (a succession of Jacks), and a mixture of fairy helpers, giants, dragons and talking animals. Later collectors sometimes complained that English tradition was thinner than that of Ireland, Scotland or Wales, partly because early industrialisation and print culture broke up oral transmission sooner.
