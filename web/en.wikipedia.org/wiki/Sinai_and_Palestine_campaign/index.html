The Sinai and Palestine campaign was part of the Middle Eastern theatre of the First World War, fought between British Empire and (from 1917) French and Arab forces on one side, and the Ottoman Empire supported by the German Empire on the other. Beginning with an Ottoman attempt to raid the Suez Canal in early 1915, the campaign ended with the Armistice of Mudros in October 1918, by which time Allied forces had advanced through Palestine and Syria to Aleppo.

Background and the defence of Egypt

After the Ottoman Empire entered the war in November 1914, the Suez Canal — a vital artery of the British Empire — became a target. An Ottoman expedition crossed the Sinai in January–February 1915 and was repulsed at the canal. British forces under Sir Archibald Murray advanced across the Sinai during 1916, building a railway and water pipeline, and defeated Ottoman attacks at Romani (August 1916) before advancing to El Arish and Rafah.

The Palestine offensive

Two British attacks on Gaza in March and April 1917 failed. Murray was replaced by General Edmund Allenby, who reorganised the Egyptian Expeditionary Force and, with an elaborate deception plan, broke the Gaza–Beersheba line in October–November 1917; the mounted charge of the Australian 4th Light Horse Brigade at Beersheba on 31 October became famous. Jerusalem fell in December 1917, Allenby entering the city on foot on 11 December. The Arab Revolt, coordinated in part by T. E. Lawrence, harassed Ottoman communications in the Hejaz and along the railway to Damascus.

Megiddo and the end of the campaign

After a pause during which troops were diverted to the Western Front, Allenby launched his final offensive in September 1918. The Battle of Megiddo shattered the Ottoman armies in Palestine; cavalry poured through the breach to Damascus (1 October) and Aleppo (26 October). The Armistice of Mudros, signed on 30 October 1918, ended hostilities. The campaign set conditions for the post-war mandates in Palestine, Transjordan, Syria and Lebanon.
