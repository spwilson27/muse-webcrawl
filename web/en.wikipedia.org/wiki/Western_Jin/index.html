Western Jin

The Western Jin (266 – 316 AD) was the first phase of the Jin dynasty of China, founded by Emperor Wu (Sima Yan) after he forced the abdication of the last Wei emperor. In 280 the dynasty conquered Eastern Wu, briefly reunifying China after the Three Kingdoms period. The dynasty is conventionally called "Western" Jin because its capital was at Luoyang, in contrast to the later Eastern Jin based at Jiankang (modern Nanjing).

Founding and reunification

The Sima clan had risen to dominance within Cao Wei during the regency of Sima Yi and his sons. Sima Yan, grandson of Sima Yi, took the throne in 266, founding Jin. His armies under generals such as Yang Hu and Du Yu conquered Eastern Wu in 280, ending nearly a century of division.

Decline

Emperor Wu enfeoffed many imperial princes with military commands, intending to secure the dynasty. After his death in 290, the incapable Emperor Hui became a pawn in court struggles. The resulting War of the Eight Princes (291–306) devastated northern China and fatally weakened central authority.

Fall

Amid the civil wars, non-Han peoples of the north, collectively known as the Five Barbarians, rose in revolt. The Xiongnu-led Han-Zhao state captured Luoyang in 311 (the Disaster of Yongjia) and took Chang'an in 316, where Emperor Min surrendered. Surviving Jin loyalists re-established the dynasty in the south as the Eastern Jin in 317/318.

Culture

The Western Jin period saw the flourishing of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove tradition of qingtan ("pure conversation"), the poetry of Pan Yue and Lu Ji, and advances in cartography by Pei Xiu. The calligrapher Wang Xizhi, later celebrated as the Sage of Calligraphy, was born near the end of the period.
