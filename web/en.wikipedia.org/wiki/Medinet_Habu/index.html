Medinet Habu (Arabic: مدينة هابو; ancient Egyptian: Djamet) is an archaeological locality on the west bank of the Nile at Luxor, Egypt, at the foot of the Theban hills. It is best known for the exceptionally well-preserved mortuary temple of Ramesses III, the second-largest temple enclosure in Egypt after Karnak.

The mortuary temple

Built during the reign of Ramesses III (c. 1186–1155 BC) and modelled on the Ramesseum of Ramesses II, the temple complex preserves its massive mudbrick enclosure walls, a fortified "Migdol" entrance gatehouse in Syrian style, a royal palace, administrative buildings and storerooms. Its walls carry some 7,000 m² of carved reliefs and inscriptions that are the principal historical source for the reign — including the famous depictions of Ramesses III's wars against the Sea Peoples (the earliest known depiction of a naval battle), campaigns against Libyans and Nubians, the royal calendar of festivals and offering lists, and the record of the harem conspiracy that ended the king's life.

Other remains

The site also includes a small 18th-Dynasty temple of Amun begun under Hatshepsut and Thutmose III and enlarged over a thousand years, chapels of the Divine Adoratrices of Amun of the Third Intermediate Period, and remains of the fortified town that grew up inside the enclosure walls in the late New Kingdom and Coptic periods — the town of Djeme, inhabited into the early Islamic era. Medinet Habu has been excavated and epigraphically recorded by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago since the 1920s.
