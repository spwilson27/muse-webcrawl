Hydride

In chemistry, a hydride is formally the anion of hydrogen (H−), or, more commonly, a compound in which hydrogen is bonded to a more electropositive element. Hydrides are traditionally divided into three classes according to their bonding: ionic (saline) hydrides, covalent hydrides and metallic (interstitial) hydrides.

Classes

Saline hydrides, such as sodium hydride (NaH) and calcium hydride (CaH2), contain the H− ion and react violently with water to give hydrogen gas and a hydroxide; they are used as strong bases and drying agents. Covalent hydrides are molecular compounds such as methane, ammonia, water and the boron hydrides; the complex hydrides lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH4) and sodium borohydride (NaBH4) are important reducing agents in organic synthesis, delivering hydride to carbonyl groups. Metallic hydrides are formed when hydrogen occupies interstitial sites in transition-metal lattices (e.g. palladium hydride, titanium hydride); they are studied for hydrogen storage and are relevant to hydrogen embrittlement of steels.

Hydride transfer

Hydride transfer is a fundamental step in many reactions, including the Cannizzaro reaction, Meerwein–Ponndorf–Verley reduction and biological redox chemistry, where the coenzymes NADH and NADPH act as hydride donors.
