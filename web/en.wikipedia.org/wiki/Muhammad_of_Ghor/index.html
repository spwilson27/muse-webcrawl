Mu'izz al-Din Muhammad ibn Sam (c. 1149 – 15 March 1206), commonly known as Muhammad of Ghor or Muhammad Ghori, was a ruler of the Ghurid Empire. Together with his elder brother Ghiyath al-Din Muhammad, he ruled an empire that stretched from Iran to Bengal. While his brother governed the western territories, Muhammad of Ghor led the Ghurid expansion into northern India, laying the foundations of Muslim rule in the Indian subcontinent that would endure for centuries.

Early life

Muhammad was born around 1149 in Ghor, a mountainous region in what is now central Afghanistan. The Ghurids, a dynasty of eastern Iranian origin, had risen to prominence under his family, the Shansabanis. Initially vassals of the Ghaznavids, they carved out an independent principality. In 1173 Muhammad was appointed governor of Ghazna by his brother, and from there he began probing southward toward India.

Campaigns in India

Unlike Mahmud of Ghazni, who raided India for plunder, Muhammad of Ghor sought permanent conquest. His first expedition in 1175 captured Multan and Uch. In 1178 he attempted to invade Gujarat but was decisively defeated by the Chaulukya (Solanki) forces at the Battle of Kasahrada near Mount Abu — his first major setback. He thereafter changed strategy, moving through the Punjab, annexing Peshawar (1179) and Lahore (1186), which brought the Ghaznavid line in Punjab to an end.

Battles of Tarain

Muhammad's advance into the Gangetic plain brought him into conflict with the Chahamana king Prithviraj Chauhan. At the First Battle of Tarain (1191), Muhammad was wounded and his army defeated. Returning the following year with a larger force, he won the Second Battle of Tarain (1192), a victory widely regarded as a turning point in Indian history. Prithviraj was captured and executed, and the Ghurids occupied Delhi and Ajmer. His general Qutb ud-Din Aibak went on to consolidate Ghurid power in northern India, while another commander, Muhammad Bakhtiyar Khalji, carried the conquest eastward into Bihar and Bengal.

In 1194 Muhammad defeated Jayachandra of Kannauj at the Battle of Chandawar, extending Ghurid control deep into the Doab.

Later campaigns

After the death of his brother in 1203, Muhammad became sole ruler of the Ghurid Empire. His later years were troubled by revolts, including a Khwarazmian threat in the west and a serious rebellion by the Khokhar tribes in the Punjab in 1205–1206, which he suppressed with difficulty.

Death and succession

On 15 March 1206, while encamped at Dhamiak on the banks of the Jhelum during his return from the Khokhar campaign, Muhammad of Ghor was assassinated. Accounts differ on the identity of his killers — some attribute the act to Khokhar tribesmen, others to Ismaili assassins. Having no surviving sons, his empire was partitioned among his Turkish slave-generals (mamluks); in India, Qutb ud-Din Aibak founded the Mamluk dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate.

Legacy

Muhammad of Ghor's conquests permanently altered the political map of South Asia, establishing an enduring Indo-Islamic state tradition. His patronage of Persian culture and the slave-general system he fostered shaped the character of the early Delhi Sultanate.
