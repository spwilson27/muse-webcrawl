Abandoned property

Abandoned property refers to property to which the owner has relinquished all rights, with no intention of reclaiming it or resuming ownership. In law, abandonment generally requires both an intention to abandon and an external act by which that intention is carried out. Property that is merely lost or mislaid is not abandoned, and the distinction matters because abandoned property may be claimed by the first person who takes possession of it, whereas lost property remains subject to the rights of the true owner.

Real property

Land and buildings are rarely treated as abandoned in common-law systems, because title to real property does not ordinarily pass by abandonment alone; however, prolonged neglect combined with non-payment of taxes may lead to tax sales, condemnation, or, historically, escheat to the state. Other doctrines related to long-term dispossession include adverse possession, under which a possessor who occupies land openly and continuously for a statutory period may acquire title.

Personal property and unclaimed property

Personal property can be abandoned — for example goods discarded as refuse — and finders may acquire good title to it. Distinct from true abandonment is unclaimed property (such as dormant bank accounts, uncashed cheques, and forgotten safe-deposit contents): under modern statutes in the United States and elsewhere, such assets are presumed abandoned after a dormancy period and must be turned over to the state under escheat or unclaimed-property laws, with the state holding them in custodial trust for the owner, who may usually reclaim them indefinitely.

Other contexts

Related concepts include abandoned vehicles and vessels (often subject to specific statutory procedures for removal and disposal), abandoned mines and industrial sites (which may carry environmental liabilities), and in intellectual property, the abandonment of trademarks through non-use, and of patent applications through failure to prosecute.
