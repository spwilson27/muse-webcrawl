Wood Badge

Wood Badge is a Scouting leadership programme and the related award for adult leaders in Scout associations around the world. The course aims to make Scouters better leaders by teaching advanced leadership skills, and the award consists of two wooden beads worn on a leather thong around the neck.

History

The first Wood Badge course was held in 1919 at Gilwell Park, England, organised by Francis Gidney under the direction of Robert Baden-Powell, the founder of Scouting. Baden-Powell took the wooden beads from a necklace he had acquired during his military service in Africa—said to have belonged to the Zulu king Dinizulu—and awarded them to course participants, giving the award its name. The accompanying scarf and woggle form the so-called Gilwell regalia, and graduates are members of the 1st Gilwell Park Scout Group.

The course

Modern Wood Badge courses combine classroom instruction with a practical outdoor phase, typically organised around patrols named after birds and animals of Gilwell (such as Bobwhite, Eagle, Fox, Owl, Bear, Buffalo, Quartermaster and Antelope in the American course). Participants work a "ticket"—a set of personal goals applying course skills in their home units—after which the beads are presented.

Around the world

Wood Badge training is offered by national Scout organisations in many countries, including the Boy Scouts of America, The Scout Association in the United Kingdom, and associations throughout the Commonwealth and beyond, each adapting the syllabus while retaining the beads, scarf and Gilwell traditions.
