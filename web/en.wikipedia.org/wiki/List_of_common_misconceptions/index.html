List of common misconceptions

This is a list of common misconceptions — widely held beliefs that are factually incorrect. Each entry states the misconception and the correct information.

History

  • Medieval people believed the Earth was flat. Educated people have known the Earth is spherical since antiquity; Eratosthenes measured its circumference around 240 BC. The "flat Earth" myth about the Middle Ages arose in the 19th century.
  • Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short. At about 5 feet 7 inches he was of average height for his time; the impression comes from the difference between French and British inches and from British propaganda.
  • Vikings wore horned helmets. There is no evidence for this; the image comes from 19th-century costume design for Wagner's operas.
  • Gladiators were given a "thumbs down" for death. The Latin phrase pollice verso means "with a turned thumb"; which gesture meant death is uncertain.
  • The Great Wall of China is visible from space. It is not visible to the unaided eye from orbit, as confirmed by astronauts.

Science and biology

  • Humans use only ten percent of their brains. Brain imaging shows virtually all parts of the brain are active over a day.
  • Evolution says humans evolved from chimpanzees. Humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor; neither evolved from the other.
  • Lightning never strikes the same place twice. It frequently does; the Empire State Building is struck many times a year.
  • Goldfish have three-second memories. Goldfish can retain learned behaviour for months.
  • Bats are blind. All bats can see; many also use echolocation.
  • The Coriolis effect determines drain direction. At household scales, drain direction is governed by basin shape and initial motion, not Earth's rotation.
  • Seasons are caused by Earth's varying distance from the Sun. They are caused by the axial tilt of the Earth.
  • Cracking knuckles causes arthritis. Studies show no such association.

Food and drink

  • Five-second rule. Bacteria transfer to dropped food immediately; the rule has no scientific basis.
  • MSG is broadly harmful. Monosodium glutamate is generally recognised as safe; controlled studies do not support widespread adverse effects.
  • Searing meat "seals in" juices. Searing creates flavour via the Maillard reaction but does not seal in moisture.
  • Alcohol kills brain cells. Moderate drinking does not kill neurons, though heavy abuse damages the brain in other ways.

Law and society

  • A person must wait 24 hours to report a missing person. No such waiting period exists in most jurisdictions; police encourage immediate reporting.
  • Double jeopardy prevents any retrial. The rule has exceptions and applies only within the same jurisdiction and offence.
