Two-factor theory of emotion

The two-factor theory of emotion, proposed by Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer in 1962, holds that emotion results from two components: physiological arousal and a cognitive label applied to that arousal. A person experiences undifferentiated bodily arousal and then searches the environment for an explanation, and the label chosen determines the emotion felt — fear, joy, anger and so on.

The Schachter–Singer experiment

In their classic study, participants received injections of epinephrine (adrenaline) and were either informed of its side effects (such as trembling and racing heart), left uninformed, or misinformed. Uninformed participants exposed to a euphoric or angry confederate were more likely to catch the confederate's mood, having no better explanation for their arousal. The experiment is a landmark of social psychology, though later replications have produced mixed results.

Implications and criticism

The theory underlies the concept of misattribution of arousal, famously illustrated by the Dutton and Aron "shaky bridge" study (1974), in which men who crossed a high suspension bridge were more likely to contact a researcher afterwards. Critics note that arousal is not entirely undifferentiated, that cognitive appraisal may precede arousal, and that the original study's effects were weaker than textbook accounts suggest. Appraisal theories by Richard Lazarus and others developed partly in response.
