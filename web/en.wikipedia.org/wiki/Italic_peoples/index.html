Italic peoples

The Italic peoples were a grouping of Indo-European peoples who inhabited the Italian peninsula from the Bronze Age onward, speaking Italic languages. The most historically consequential of these peoples were the Latins, from whose city — Rome — a world empire would eventually arise.

Branches

Linguists divide the Italic languages into two main branches:

  • Latino-Faliscan: Latin and the closely related Faliscan, spoken in Latium.
  • Osco-Umbrian (Sabellic): including Oscan, Umbrian, South Picene, Samnite and others, spoken across the Apennines and the south.

Other peoples often grouped among the Italics or neighbors include the Sabines, Volsci, Aequi, Hernici, and the Veneti of the northeast (whose language may be a separate Indo-European branch).

History

The Italic peoples likely migrated into Italy from the north around the end of the 2nd millennium BC, associated with the Terramare and Villanovan cultures. Through the late Republic the Italic peoples resisted or were absorbed by Rome in a series of wars, culminating in the Social War (91–88 BC), after which Roman citizenship was extended throughout Italy and Latin became the peninsula's sole surviving language of record.
