Androgyny

Androgyny is the possession of both masculine and feminine characteristics. In the biological sense, it can refer to organisms having both male and female reproductive organs or characteristics; in psychology and culture it describes a blend of traits, behaviors and self-presentation traditionally associated with both sexes. The word derives from the Greek andr- (man) and gynē (woman).

Etymology and history

The concept has ancient roots. In Plato's Symposium, Aristophanes describes primordial humans as spherical beings combining male and female, split by Zeus into two halves that thereafter seek to reunite. In religious traditions, deities such as the Hindu Ardhanarishvara combine male and female forms, and the Gnostic and alchemical traditions used the androgyne as a symbol of wholeness and perfection.

Psychology

In the 1970s, psychologist Sandra Bem developed the Bem Sex-Role Inventory, which classified people as masculine, feminine, androgynous or undifferentiated according to their endorsement of expressive (traditionally feminine) and instrumental (traditionally masculine) traits. Bem argued that androgynous individuals might be more flexible and mentally healthy, an idea that influenced research on gender roles, though later psychologists have questioned and refined the model.

Biology

In botany and zoology, androgyny overlaps with hermaphroditism: many flowering plants bear both staminate and pistillate structures, and some animals, such as snails and certain fish, possess both male and female reproductive capacity. In humans, the term has sometimes been applied historically to intersex conditions, though modern usage generally separates biological sex characteristics from gender expression.

Culture and fashion

Androgynous presentation has been a recurring feature of fashion and popular music, from the styles of the 1920s and the Romantic era to performers such as David Bowie, Prince, Annie Lennox and Grace Jones in the late twentieth century. Androgyny is also relevant to modern discussions of gender identity, including non-binary identities, though the concepts are not identical.
