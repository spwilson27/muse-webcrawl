Honorius

Honorius (9 September 384 – 15 August 423) was Roman emperor in the West from 393 to 423. The younger son of Theodosius I, he became ruler of the western half of the empire at age ten on his father's death in 395, while his brother Arcadius ruled the East. His reign coincided with the accelerating crisis of the western empire.

Reign

Real power was exercised successively by the general Stilicho (until his execution in 408), court factions at Ravenna — the capital from 402 — and later the general Constantius III, who married Honorius's sister Galla Placidia and briefly became co-emperor. Honorius's court abandoned Rome and Britain; in 410 the rescript traditionally read as ending Roman Britain was issued, and in the same year Alaric's Visigoths sacked Rome, an event that shocked the Roman world.

Assessment

Honorius is usually judged a weak and disengaged emperor, preoccupied with religion and court intrigue while barbarian peoples crossed the Rhine (406), usurpers proliferated, and provinces slipped from imperial control. Edward Gibbon's verdict was famously scathing. Nevertheless, the western empire under his successors survived another half-century.

Death

Honorius died of dropsy at Ravenna in August 423, leaving no heir; after the usurpation of Joannes, the eastern court installed his nephew Valentinian III.
