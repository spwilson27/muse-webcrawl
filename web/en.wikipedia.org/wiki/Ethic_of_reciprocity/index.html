Ethic of reciprocity

The ethic of reciprocity, better known as the Golden Rule, is the principle of treating others as one would want to be treated by them. It is sometimes expressed positively—"Do unto others as you would have them do unto you"—or negatively, as in Confucius's formulation: "Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself." Religious ethicist Harry J. Gensler described it as treating others only in ways one would be willing to be treated in the same situation.

Across traditions

Versions of the rule appear in most of the world's major religions and ethical traditions. In Christianity, Jesus states it in the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 7:12), adding "this sums up the Law and the Prophets". Rabbi Hillel the Elder gave the negative form—"What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow"—as a summary of the whole Torah. Confucius taught reciprocity (shu) as a lifelong guiding principle in the Analects, and the Mahabharata, the Hadith, and Jain, Buddhist, Sikh and Baháʼí texts contain parallel formulations. In 1993, the Parliament of the World's Religions adopted the Golden Rule as part of its Declaration Toward a Global Ethic.

Philosophical discussion

Philosophers have both endorsed and criticised the rule. Kant held that it is only a derivative application of his categorical imperative and cannot handle duties of self-regarding or juridical kinds. Defenders such as Marcus Singer argue the rule embodies a demand for impartiality and consistency in moral judgement. Critics note that a literal reading can fail where people's preferences differ—treating others as they wish to be treated (sometimes called the "platinum rule") is offered as a refinement.
