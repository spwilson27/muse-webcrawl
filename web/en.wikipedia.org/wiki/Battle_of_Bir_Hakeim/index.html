Battle of Bir Hakeim

The Battle of Bir Hakeim took place at an oasis and former Turkish fort in the Libyan desert from 26 May to 11 June 1942, during the Battle of Gazala in the Second World War. The 1st Free French Brigade under General Marie-Pierre Kœnig held the southern anchor of the Gazala Line against overwhelming Axis attacks for over two weeks, delaying Erwin Rommel's offensive and allowing British forces to escape encirclement, before breaking out successfully on the night of 10–11 June.

Background

Bir Hakeim, an old Turkish desert fort at a crossroads of caravan tracks, formed the southernmost "box" of the British Gazala Line west of Tobruk. Its garrison — the 1st Free French Brigade, including Foreign Legion battalions, Pacific and colonial troops, and fusiliers marins — numbered about 3,700 men with artillery and anti-tank guns dug into a perimeter protected by minefields.

Battle

When Rommel swung around the Gazala Line on 26 May 1942, Italian and German armored forces attacked Bir Hakeim and were repulsed with heavy tank losses. Thereafter the position was besieged, bombarded from the air (the Luftwaffe flew well over a thousand sorties against the box), and subjected to repeated ground assaults in early June. The French refused a surrender ultimatum — Kœnig's reply was a defiant "Nous ne sommes pas ici pour nous rendre" in spirit — and, their water and ammunition nearly exhausted, broke out through the minefields on the night of 10–11 June, most of the surviving garrison reaching British lines.

Significance

The stand at Bir Hakeim delayed Rommel for a fortnight, disrupted his timetable, and gave the British Eighth Army time to avoid encirclement. It was a major moral victory for the Free French, restoring French military prestige after 1940; General de Gaulle declared that at Bir Hakeim France had shown the world she was France.
