Tennessine is a synthetic chemical element with the symbol Ts and atomic number 117. It is the second-heaviest element that has been created and the penultimate element of the seventh period of the periodic table. Only a handful of atoms of tennessine have ever been made, in nuclear fusion reactions at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR) in Dubna, Russia, in collaboration with American laboratories.

Discovery

Tennessine was first synthesised in 2010 by a Russian–American collaboration led by Yuri Oganessian. The team bombarded a target of berkelium-249 — produced at Oak Ridge National Laboratory in Tennessee — with calcium-48 ions, obtaining atoms of isotopes 293Ts and 294Ts, which decayed within tens of milliseconds. The discovery was formally recognised by IUPAC in December 2015, completing the seventh row of the periodic table alongside nihonium, moscovium, and oganesson. The name tennessine, honouring the state of Tennessee's contribution to superheavy-element research, was approved by IUPAC in November 2016; the ending -ine follows the pattern of the halogen names.

Predicted properties

Tennessine is placed in group 17, the halogens, below astatine. Its chemistry has never been measured: with all known isotopes surviving only milliseconds, experiments on single atoms are not yet feasible. Theoretical calculations predict that relativistic effects will make tennessine differ significantly from the lighter halogens — it is expected to be a solid at room temperature, probably with a semimetallic or even metallic character, a melting point estimated around 340–550 °C, and chemistry in which the +1 oxidation state is unusually stable relative to the −1 state that dominates for chlorine or bromine.
