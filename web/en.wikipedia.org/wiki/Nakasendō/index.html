The Nakasendō (中山道, "central mountain route") was one of the five routes (Gokaidō) of the Edo period in Japan, and one of the two that connected the capital Edo (modern Tokyo) with Kyoto. Unlike the coastal Tōkaidō, the Nakasendō travelled inland through the mountains of central Honshū, passing through the modern prefectures of Tokyo, Saitama, Gunma, Nagano, Gifu and Shiga.

Route and post towns

The road ran about 534 km and had 69 post towns (shukuba) providing lodging, porters and horses. Travellers included daimyō processions on the sankin-kōtai system, merchants, and pilgrims. In the Kiso Valley the road passed the noted barrier stations and towns of Niekawa, Narai, Fukushima, Agematsu, Tsumago and Magome; the stretch between Tsumago-juku and Magome-juku is today one of Japan's best-known preserved walking trails.

Decline and preservation

Railways from the Meiji era eclipsed the highway, but Tsumago became a pioneer of heritage preservation in 1968, when residents adopted rules — no selling, renting or demolition of historic buildings, and hiding cars and power lines — that restored the town's Edo-period streetscape. Walking sections of the Nakasendō is now a popular form of heritage tourism. Hiroshige's print series The Sixty-nine Stations of the Kiso Kaidō (with Eisen) immortalised its post towns.
