Banked turn

Cars on the steeply banked turn of an oval racetrack.

A banked turn (or banking turn) is a turn or change of direction in which the vehicle banks or inclines, usually towards the inside of the turn. The road or track surface itself may be inclined: for a road or railroad this is usually referred to as superelevation or cant.

Physics

On a flat curve, a vehicle depends entirely on tyre friction to supply the centripetal force needed to follow the curve. Banking the surface tilts the normal reaction from the road so that part of it acts horizontally toward the centre of the turn, supplying centripetal force even without friction. For an ideal, frictionless banked curve of radius r taken at design speed v, the banking angle θ satisfies:

tan θ = v² / (r g)

Real roads use modest superelevation (often limited to about 4–10%) because vehicles also travel slowly or stop on the curve; railways specify a balance speed for their cant and limit "cant deficiency" for faster trains.

Applications

Banked curves are standard on motor-racing ovals (where banking can exceed 30 degrees, as at Daytona and Talladega), velodromes, bobsleigh and luge tracks, and high-speed railway lines, where tilting trains supplement cant. In aviation, an aircraft makes a coordinated banked turn by rolling toward the inside of the turn so that lift provides the centripetal force; in a properly coordinated turn the occupants feel force only along their seat's vertical axis.
