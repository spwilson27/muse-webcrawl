Ancel Benjamin Keys (January 26, 1904 – November 20, 2004) was an American physiologist who studied the influence of diet on health. As director of the Laboratory of Physiological Hygiene at the University of Minnesota, he led landmark research on starvation, military nutrition, and cardiovascular disease, and he hypothesised that dietary saturated fat raises blood cholesterol and contributes to heart disease — the "diet–heart hypothesis" that shaped dietary guidelines for decades.

Early work and the war years

Keys studied biology and physiology at Berkeley and earned a doctorate in oceanography at the Scripps Institution before working in Cambridge. In the 1930s he developed methods of altitude physiology and led an expedition to the Andes. During the Second World War he developed the K-ration, the lightweight combat ration named after him, and directed the Minnesota Starvation Experiment (1944–45), in which conscientious objectors volunteered for semi-starvation and refeeding; the results, published as The Biology of Human Starvation (1950), remain foundational for the study of eating disorders and famine relief.

Seven Countries Study

Beginning in the 1950s, Keys compared diets and heart-disease rates across populations and launched the Seven Countries Study (formally begun 1958), following nearly 13,000 men in the United States, Finland, the Netherlands, Italy, Yugoslavia, Greece, and Japan. The study found strong associations between saturated-fat intake, blood cholesterol, and coronary mortality, and highlighted the low heart-disease rates of Mediterranean populations eating diets rich in olive oil, cereals, vegetables, and fish. With his wife Margaret, Keys popularised this pattern in best-selling books such as Eat Well and Stay Well (1959), effectively introducing the "Mediterranean diet" to American audiences. He appeared on the cover of Time magazine in January 1961.

Criticism and legacy

Keys's work was contested from the start: the British physiologist John Yudkin argued that sugar, not fat, was the principal dietary culprit, and later critics charged Keys with selecting countries that fitted his hypothesis. Modern reassessments credit the Seven Countries Study with founding cardiovascular epidemiology while debating its conclusions; Keys retired to Pioppi, Italy — the village where he spent much of his later life practising the Mediterranean lifestyle he advocated — and lived to 100.
