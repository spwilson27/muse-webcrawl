Chouannerie


Placeholder image: Chouan guerrillas in Brittany

The Chouannerie was a royalist insurrection in western France — principally Brittany, Maine, and parts of Normandy and Anjou — against the French Revolution and later against Napoleon. Its fighters were called Chouans. The name is traditionally derived from the cry of the tawny owl (chat-huant), used as a signal by the insurgents, or from the nickname of an early leader, Jean Cottereau, called Jean Chouan.

Origins

Discontent grew from 1791 with the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, which alienated devout peasants from the Revolution, and exploded with the levée en masse of 1793 — the Republic's mass conscription decree. South of the Loire the same grievances produced the larger War in the Vendée; north of the river, resistance took the form of dispersed guerrilla warfare in the hedgerow country (bocage).

Course of the war

Chouan bands ambushed republican patrols, tax collectors and purchasers of national property. Leaders included Jean Chouan, Georges Cadoudal, and the comte de Puisaye, who sought British support and helped organise the disastrous émigré landing at Quiberon in 1795. General Hoche's combination of mobile columns, amnesties and religious toleration largely pacified the region by 1796, but resistance revived in 1799. Under Napoleon, Cadoudal conspired against the First Consul and was executed in 1804; sporadic Chouan activity flared once more during the Hundred Days in 1815.

Legacy

The Chouannerie was immortalised in literature by Honoré de Balzac's novel Les Chouans (1829) and treated in Victor Hugo's Quatrevingt-treize (1874), set in the neighbouring Vendée war. In Breton and western French memory the Chouans remain symbols of Catholic and regional resistance to the revolutionary state.
