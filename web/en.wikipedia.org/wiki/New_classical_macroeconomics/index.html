New classical macroeconomics

New classical macroeconomics, sometimes simply called the new classical school, is a school of thought in macroeconomics that emerged in the 1970s. It builds its analysis entirely on a neoclassical framework, emphasising the importance of rigorous microfoundations: households and firms are modelled as rational agents who optimise, markets continuously clear, and agents form rational expectations about the future.

Origins

The school grew out of a critique of the Keynesian orthodoxy that dominated post-war macroeconomics. Robert Lucas Jr.'s 1972 paper "Expectations and the Neutrality of Money" and his 1976 "Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique" — the famous Lucas critique — argued that the parameters of reduced-form Keynesian models were not invariant to policy changes, because agents would adjust their expectations and behaviour. Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace developed the related policy ineffectiveness proposition, holding that anticipated monetary policy has no systematic effect on real output.

Key ideas

  • Rational expectations: agents use all available information and do not make systematic forecasting errors.
  • Continuous market clearing: prices and wages adjust so that supply equals demand in all markets.
  • Microfoundations: aggregate relationships must be derived from individual optimising behaviour.
  • Real business cycle theory: developed by Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott, attributing business cycles largely to real (technology) shocks rather than monetary disturbances.
  • Ricardian equivalence: following Robert Barro, government debt does not affect consumption because rational households anticipate future taxes.

Influence and criticism

New classical ideas reshaped mainstream macroeconomics, leading eventually to the "new neoclassical synthesis" that combines rational expectations and microfoundations with the nominal rigidities emphasised by New Keynesian economists. Critics have questioned the realism of perfect information, instantaneous price adjustment and representative-agent assumptions, and the school was challenged by the events of the 2008 financial crisis.
