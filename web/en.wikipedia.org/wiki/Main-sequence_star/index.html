Main-sequence star

A main-sequence star is a star that generates energy by the nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium in its core, and is in hydrostatic equilibrium — the outward pressure from fusion balancing the inward pull of gravity. Main-sequence stars form a prominent band on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram, running from hot, luminous, massive stars at upper left to cool, dim, low-mass stars at lower right. The Sun is a main-sequence star of spectral type G2 V. Stars spend the great majority of their active lives on the main sequence.

Properties

The position of a star on the main sequence is determined mainly by its mass, with chemical composition and age having secondary effects. More massive main-sequence stars are hotter, larger, and far more luminous: luminosity rises roughly with the 3.5 power of mass. A star's lifetime on the main sequence is inversely related to its luminosity, so massive stars exhaust their core hydrogen in a few million years, while the least massive red dwarfs can remain on the main sequence for trillions of years — far longer than the present age of the universe. The Sun's main-sequence lifetime is about 10 billion years, of which roughly half has elapsed.

Energy generation

In lower-mass stars like the Sun, hydrogen fusion proceeds mainly through the proton–proton chain reaction; in stars above about 1.3 solar masses, the CNO cycle dominates. Energy reaches the surface through radiative and convective zones in proportions that vary systematically along the main sequence: massive stars have convective cores and radiative envelopes, while low-mass stars are largely or wholly convective.

Evolution

A star forms from the collapse of a molecular cloud and joins the main sequence — the zero-age main sequence — once core hydrogen fusion begins. As hydrogen is consumed and helium accumulates in the core, the star slowly brightens and expands. When core hydrogen is exhausted, the star leaves the main sequence, evolving into a subgiant and then a red giant. Main-sequence stars are sometimes called "dwarf stars", though only the coolest are named red or white dwarfs in other contexts; the term spans the full range from O-type giants of luminosity class V to faint M dwarfs.
