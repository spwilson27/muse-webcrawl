Medical genetics

Medical genetics is the branch of medicine that involves the diagnosis and management of hereditary disorders. Medical genetics differs from human genetics in that human genetics is a field of scientific research that may or may not apply to medicine, while medical genetics refers to the application of genetics to medical care — including research into the causes and inheritance of genetic disorders, diagnosis, management, and genetic counseling.

Scope

Medical genetics encompasses many different areas, including clinical practice of physicians, genetic counselors, and nutritionists, clinical diagnostic laboratory activities, and research into the causes and inheritance of genetic disorders. Examples of conditions that fall within the scope of medical genetics include birth defects and dysmorphology, intellectual disability, autism, mitochondrial disorders, skeletal dysplasias, connective tissue disorders, cancer genetics, teratogens, and prenatal diagnosis.

Subspecialties

  • Clinical genetics — the diagnosis and management of genetic conditions in individuals and families.
  • Clinical biochemical genetics — diagnosis and treatment of inborn errors of metabolism.
  • Clinical cytogenetics — study of chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome and Turner syndrome.
  • Clinical molecular genetics — DNA-based testing for single-gene disorders such as cystic fibrosis and Huntington disease.
  • Cancer genetics — hereditary cancer syndromes such as BRCA1/BRCA2-related breast and ovarian cancer and Lynch syndrome.
  • Reproductive genetics — carrier screening, prenatal and preimplantation genetic testing.

Genetic counseling

Genetic counseling is the process by which patients or relatives at risk of an inherited disorder are advised of the consequences and nature of the disorder, the probability of developing or transmitting it, and the options open to them in management and family planning. Genetic counselors work alongside clinical geneticists as part of the care team.

Testing and technology

Diagnostic methods range from karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to chromosomal microarray analysis and next-generation sequencing, including gene panels, whole-exome sequencing and whole-genome sequencing. Newborn screening programs detect treatable metabolic conditions such as phenylketonuria shortly after birth.

History

The field grew out of early 20th-century studies of inborn errors of metabolism, such as Archibald Garrod's work on alkaptonuria (1902). Victor McKusick's catalog Mendelian Inheritance in Man (later OMIM) systematized knowledge of genetic disorders from the 1960s onward, and in the United States the American Board of Medical Genetics was founded in 1980.
