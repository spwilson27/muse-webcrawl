Phenolic acid

Phenolic acids are a class of plant compounds — a major subgroup of the polyphenols — characterised by a phenolic ring bearing a carboxylic acid group. They are widespread secondary metabolites in the plant kingdom and are abundant in fruits, vegetables, cereals, coffee, tea and wine, where they contribute to colour, flavour and defence against pests and pathogens.

Classification

Phenolic acids are divided into two main families by their carbon skeleton:

  • Hydroxybenzoic acids (C6–C1), derived from benzoic acid, including gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, vanillic acid, syringic acid and salicylic acid.
  • Hydroxycinnamic acids (C6–C3), derived from cinnamic acid, including caffeic acid, ferulic acid, sinapic acid and p-coumaric acid. These are more common in foods, often occurring esterified — chlorogenic acid in coffee, for example, is an ester of caffeic and quinic acids.

Occurrence and biology

Ferulic acid is abundant in cereal brans, where it cross-links cell-wall polysaccharides; gallic acid and its derivatives (gallotannins) are found in tea, berries and oak. In plants the acids are synthesised via the shikimate pathway from phenylalanine and tyrosine.

Health research

Phenolic acids are antioxidants in vitro, scavenging free radicals, and have been studied for possible anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial and anticarcinogenic effects. Their bioavailability varies considerably: many are absorbed in the gut, while others are extensively metabolised by gut microbiota into smaller phenolic compounds. Scientific consensus on specific health claims remains limited.
