Eritrea–Ethiopia Boundary Commission

The Eritrea–Ethiopia Boundary Commission was an arbitral body established under the Algiers Agreement of 2000 to delimit the border between Eritrea and Ethiopia.

The Eritrea–Ethiopia Boundary Commission (EEBC) was an independent arbitral commission established under the Algiers Agreement of 12 December 2000, which ended the Eritrean–Ethiopian War (1998–2000). Its mandate was to delimit and demarcate the border between the two states on the basis of the colonial treaties of 1900, 1902 and 1908 and applicable international law. The parties agreed in advance that the Commission's determination would be "final and binding".

Composition and procedure

The Commission sat in The Hague, supported by the Permanent Court of Arbitration. Each party appointed two commissioners and the four chose a president — the British jurist Sir Elihu Lauterpacht. The UN Secretary-General served as depositary, and a chief surveyor was appointed to carry out demarcation in the field.

The 2002 decision

On 13 April 2002 the Commission delivered its delimitation decision, allocating the disputed territory in each of the three sectors of the border. Crucially, the flashpoint town of Badme — where the war had begun in May 1998 — was awarded to Eritrea. Ethiopia initially claimed victory, then, as the implications became clear, rejected the decision where it disfavoured it, demanding "dialogue" and variations in demarcation. Eritrea insisted on full implementation and increasingly obstructed the Commission's field work in protest at international inaction.

Virtual demarcation and aftermath

Unable to place pillars on the ground, the Commission in November 2007 completed demarcation "virtually", by coordinates, and dissolved itself. The border stalemate froze relations in a state of "no war, no peace" for a decade; Ethiopia continued to occupy Badme and other territory awarded to Eritrea. Only in 2018, under Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed, did Ethiopia announce full acceptance of the Algiers Agreement and the Commission's decision, leading to a rapprochement between the two countries — though physical demarcation remained incomplete, and the later Tigray War again transformed the region's politics.
