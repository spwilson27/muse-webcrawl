Rice noodles

Rice noodles
RegionChina and Southeast Asia
Main ingredientsRice flour, water (sometimes tapioca or corn starch)
FormsFresh, dried; thin vermicelli to wide flat sheets
Typical dishesPhở, pad thai, char kway teow, Guilin mifen, laksa

Rice noodles are noodles made from rice flour and water, sometimes with added tapioca or corn starch to improve transparency and chewiness. They are a staple of East and Southeast Asian cuisines, eaten in soups, stir-fries and salads, and are naturally gluten-free. Rice noodles range from hair-thin vermicelli to wide ribbons and rolled sheets.

Production

Rice is soaked, ground with water to a batter or dough, and then steamed in sheets and cut, extruded through dies into boiling water, or pressed into strands. Fresh noodles are sold within a day or two in markets across Asia; dried noodles, which keep for months, are rehydrated by soaking or brief boiling. High-quality noodles are judged by their translucency, elasticity and resistance to disintegrating in soup.

Varieties and dishes

  • Rice vermicelli (mǐfěn in Chinese, bún in Vietnamese, bee hoon in Singapore/Malaysia) — thin strands used in spring rolls, Singapore-style fried noodles, and salads.
  • Phở noodles (bánh phở) — flat white ribbons served in Vietnam's beef or chicken noodle soup.
  • Shahe fen / hor fun — wide flat noodles from Shahe, Guangdong, used in Cantonese beef chow fun and Malaysian char kway teow.
  • Guilin mifen — round rice noodles from Guangxi, served with gravy and crispy pork.
  • Mixian and migan — Yunnanese round noodles, as in "crossing-the-bridge noodles" (guoqiao mixian).
  • Lai fun — short, thick, spaghetti-like noodles; cheung fun — steamed rice noodle rolls, a dim sum staple.
  • Pad thai noodles — medium flat dried noodles, soaked and stir-fried with tamarind sauce in Thailand.
  • Idiyappam — South Indian and Sri Lankan rice noodle "string hoppers," steamed in nests.

History

Rice noodle production is generally traced to southern China, where rice rather than wheat predominates; techniques spread with Chinese migration throughout Southeast Asia, where local cuisines transformed them into signature dishes. Dried noodle technology and, later, instant rice noodles industrialised the food in the twentieth century.

Nutrition

Plain rice noodles are mostly carbohydrate with small amounts of protein and are low in fat; they are a common wheat substitute for people avoiding gluten, though cross-contamination and blended flours must be checked.
