Prairie School

The Prairie School was an architectural style of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, most common in the Midwestern United States. Its leading figure was Frank Lloyd Wright, alongside architects such as Louis Sullivan (Wright's mentor), George Washington Maher, Walter Burley Griffin, Marion Mahony Griffin, William Gray Purcell and George Elmslie. The style sought to create a genuinely American architecture suited to the flat prairie landscape: horizontal lines, low-pitched roofs with broad overhanging eaves, open interior plans and integration with the land.

Characteristics

Prairie houses emphasise the horizontal: long low rooflines echoing the prairie horizon, bands of windows, and natural materials — brick, stone and wood — left undisguised. Inside, Wright "destroyed the box" of the traditional house, opening rooms into one another around a central hearth, with built-in furniture and art-glass windows in abstracted natural patterns. Siting was integral: houses nestle into their plots with terraces and planters extending the architecture into the landscape.

History

The style grew out of the Chicago architectural scene after the Great Fire of 1871 and the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, whose neoclassical "White City" the Prairie architects rejected. Wright's Oak Park studio became its engine room; his Wasmuth Portfolio (1910) carried the ideas to Europe, where they influenced the Bauhaus and De Stijl. The style faded after the First World War as clients' tastes shifted to period revivals, but it profoundly shaped modern residential architecture — the open plan, the ranch house and Wright's later Usonian homes all descend from it.
