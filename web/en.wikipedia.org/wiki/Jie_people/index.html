The Jie (Chinese: 羯; Wade–Giles: Chieh) were members of a small, tribe-like ethnic group in northern China in the early 4th century CE. They were counted among the "Five Barbarians" (Wu Hu) of the period, although Chinese records describe them as a distinct tribe of the Xiongnu confederation. The Jie founded the Later Zhao dynasty (319–351), one of the Sixteen Kingdoms.

History

The Jie rose to prominence under Shi Le, a Jie chieftain who had been enslaved during a famine-driven sale of Xiongnu tribesmen by a Jin dynasty official. Shi Le escaped, became a rebel and bandit leader, and eventually founded the Later Zhao state in 319, ruling much of northern China. His nephew and successor Shi Hu was notorious for cruelty and extravagance.

After Shi Hu's death in 349, the general Ran Min — a Han Chinese adopted into the Shi family — seized power, proclaimed the state of Ran Wei, and issued an infamous order to exterminate the Jie and other non-Han peoples, identified, according to the sources, by their high noses and full beards. Tens of thousands were killed, and the Jie disappear from history thereafter.

Language and origins

Only a single sentence of the Jie language survives, recorded in Chinese transcription in connection with the Buddhist monk Fotudeng: the Jie words include a form often compared with Turkic or Yeniseian words for "attack/army." Scholars have variously proposed that the Jie spoke a Turkic or a Yeniseian language; the Yeniseian hypothesis, linking them to the Ket people of Siberia, has attracted attention because the surviving phrase can be plausibly read in a Yeniseian language. Their origins remain debated.
