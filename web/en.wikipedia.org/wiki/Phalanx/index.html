Phalanx

The phalanx (Ancient Greek: φάλαγξ, plural phalanxes or phalanges) was a rectangular mass military formation, usually composed entirely of heavy infantry armed with spears, pikes, or similar polearms. The term is particularly used of the ancient Greek and Macedonian armies.

Greek hoplite phalanx

The classical phalanx was formed by hoplites — citizen-soldiers armed with a large round shield (aspis), a thrusting spear (dory), and a short sword. Standing shoulder to shoulder in ranks typically eight men deep, each soldier's shield covered his own left side and his neighbour's right, presenting an unbroken wall of shields and spear points. Battles often became pushing contests (othismos) in which cohesion and weight of numbers were decisive.

Macedonian phalanx

Philip II of Macedon and his son Alexander the Great reformed the phalanx around the sarissa, a pike some 5.5–6 metres long wielded with both hands. Phalangites wore smaller shields slung from the shoulder, and the first five ranks' pike tips projected beyond the front of the formation. The Macedonian phalanx acted as the anvil of Alexander's army, pinning the enemy while the Companion cavalry delivered the hammer blow.

Decline

The phalanx was formidable on level ground but rigid and vulnerable on broken terrain and to attacks on its flanks and rear. The more flexible Roman manipular legion defeated the phalanxes of Macedon and the Hellenistic kingdoms, notably at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) and Pydna (168 BC), ending its dominance.
