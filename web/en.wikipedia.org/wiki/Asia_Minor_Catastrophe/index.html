Asia Minor Catastrophe

Date: 1922

Location: Western Anatolia, Ottoman Empire/Turkey

Outcome: Greek defeat; population exchange; end of Greek presence in Asia Minor

The Asia Minor Catastrophe (Greek: Μικρασιατική Καταστροφή) is the term used in Greece for the events of 1922 that ended the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922): the defeat of the Greek Army of Asia Minor, the Great Fire of Smyrna, the killing and expulsion of Greek Orthodox and Armenian inhabitants, and the subsequent compulsory population exchange between Greece and Turkey under the Treaty of Lausanne (1923). It ended a Greek presence in Asia Minor stretching back some three thousand years.

Background

After the Ottoman defeat in the First World War, Greece, encouraged by the Allied powers and inspired by the Megali Idea, landed troops at Smyrna in May 1919 under the Treaty of Sèvres. Greek forces advanced deep into Anatolia but were halted at the Sakarya River in 1921 and destroyed in the Turkish Great Offensive of August 1922 under Mustafa Kemal.

Catastrophe

Turkish forces entered Smyrna on 9 September 1922; four days later a fire destroyed the Greek and Armenian quarters, killing thousands. Crowds of refugees fled to the quays and were evacuated by Allied and Greek ships, while many perished. In the following months the surviving Christian population of Anatolia and Eastern Thrace fled or was expelled. The 1923 population exchange made the displacement permanent and reciprocal: roughly 1.2–1.5 million Orthodox Christians moved to Greece and some 400,000 Muslims to Turkey.

Consequences

The catastrophe toppled the Greek government; the "Trial of the Six" executed leading politicians and commanders blamed for the defeat. Refugee settlement transformed Greek society, doubling the population of cities such as Athens and Thessaloniki and leaving a lasting imprint on Greek politics, music (rebetiko) and collective memory.
