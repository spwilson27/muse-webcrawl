Polearm

A polearm or pole weapon is a close combat weapon in which the main fighting part of the weapon is fitted to the end of a long shaft, typically of wood, extending the user's reach and striking power. Polearms were among the most common weapons of pre-modern infantry from antiquity through the early modern period, used by peasant levies and professional soldiers alike, because they were cheap to manufacture, effective against cavalry, and easily adapted from agricultural tools.

Types

The simplest polearm is the spear, known since prehistory. Medieval Europe produced a bewildering variety of specialised forms, often hard to classify sharply: the halberd combined an axe blade, spear point, and hook; the glaive and the Japanese naginata mounted a single-edged blade; the bill derived from the agricultural billhook; the pollaxe was a knightly weapon for armoured foot combat; the pike, up to 6 metres long, armed the massed infantry squares of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. In China the ji (halberd) and ge (dagger-axe) were ancient prestige weapons, while the Japanese yari paralleled the spear and pike. Many polearms — such as the bec de corbin and war hammer variants — were designed to pierce or crush plate armour.

Use and decline

Fighting in dense formations, polearms let infantry present a hedge of points against charging cavalry and push against opposing infantry; treatises such as those of Joachim Meyer describe sophisticated individual fencing techniques with the halberd and staff for duels and self-defence as well. The bayonet, fixed to a musket, effectively turned every infantryman's firearm into a short pike, and by the eighteenth century the true polearm had largely vanished from the battlefield, surviving ceremonially (for example with the Swiss Guard) and in martial arts traditions.
