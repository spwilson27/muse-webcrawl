Samuel Plimsoll (10 February 1824 – 3 June 1898) was an English politician and social reformer, now best known for the Plimsoll line — the mark indicating the maximum depth to which a ship may be safely loaded — and for his campaign against dangerously overloaded and unseaworthy "coffin ships." He was affectionately known as "the sailors' friend."

Campaign against coffin ships

After a career in the coal trade and railway promotion, Plimsoll was elected Liberal MP for Derby in 1868. He devoted himself to merchant shipping reform after learning that unscrupulous owners sent overloaded, over-insured, rotten vessels to sea, profiting from insurance when they sank with their crews. His 1873 book Our Seamen: An Appeal galvanised public opinion. In a famous outburst in the House of Commons in July 1875 he denounced shipowners in the chamber and shook his fist at the Speaker, an episode that — though it forced an apology — moved Disraeli's government to act.

Legislation and the Plimsoll mark

The Merchant Shipping Act 1876 required a series of load-line marks on British ships, though it initially left their placement to owners, a loophole only closed in 1890 when the Board of Trade was given control and the disc-and-line mark — universally called the Plimsoll line or Plimsoll mark — was fixed by regulation. Load lines became mandatory internationally through conventions in the twentieth century and remain standard on merchant vessels worldwide.

Later life

Plimsoll lost his Derby seat in 1880 and later served as honorary president of the National Sailors' and Firemen's Union. He died at Folkestone in 1898. His name also survives in the "plimsoll shoe," a canvas trainer whose coloured band between upper and sole resembled a load line.
