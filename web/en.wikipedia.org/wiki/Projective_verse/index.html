Projective verse

Projective verse is a term coined and a theory elaborated by the American poet Charles Olson in his influential 1950 essay "Projective Verse". The theory proposes a compositional method in which the poem's form is determined by the poet's breath and by the immediate movement of perception, rather than by inherited metrical or stanzaic patterns.

Principles

Olson summarized the method in the slogan "form is never more than an extension of content," borrowed from Robert Creeley. Its key elements include "composition by field," in which the page is treated as a field of energies; the syllable as the unit arriving by way of the ear; and the line as the unit arriving by way of the breath. Olson also urged "objectism"—getting rid of the lyrical interference of the individual ego—so that one perception must immediately and directly lead to a further perception.

Influence

Projective verse was closely associated with the poets of Black Mountain College, including Robert Creeley and Robert Duncan, and influenced the Beats and later avant-garde and Language poetry. The theory was debated through Olson's correspondence with Creeley, and remains a central reference point in discussions of open form and free verse in American poetry. See also free verse.
