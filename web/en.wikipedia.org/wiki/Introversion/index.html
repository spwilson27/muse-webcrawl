Introversion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Introversion is a personality trait characterized by a focus on internal feelings rather than on external sources of stimulation. Introverts and extroverts are typically viewed as two extremes of a continuum: introverts prefer quieter, less stimulating environments and tend to feel drained after social interaction, recovering their energy through periods of solitude and reflection. The related term extraversion describes the opposite orientation, in which energy is drawn from social engagement and external activity.

History of the concept

The terms introversion and extraversion were popularized by the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung in his 1921 work Psychological Types. Jung described introversion as an attitude-type characterized by orientation in life through subjective psychic contents, and extraversion by orientation through external, objective matters. The concepts were later incorporated into trait theories of personality, including Hans Eysenck's model, which linked introversion to higher baseline cortical arousal, and the Big Five personality traits, where extraversion appears as one of the five broad dimensions.

Characteristics

Introverts typically enjoy solitary activities such as reading, writing, and contemplative hobbies, and may prefer interacting with a small number of close friends rather than large groups. Research suggests that introverts are more sensitive to external stimulation, including the neurotransmitter dopamine, which may explain why highly stimulating environments feel overwhelming to them. Introversion is distinct from shyness: shyness involves fear of social judgment, whereas introversion is a preference for low-stimulation environments. An introvert may be socially confident yet still prefer quiet settings.

Ambiversion

Most people fall somewhere between the two poles. An ambivert displays a balance of introverted and extroverted tendencies, adjusting behavior to the situation. Because personality traits are normally distributed, the majority of the population is thought to be ambiverted to some degree.

Measurement

Introversion–extraversion is assessed by instruments such as the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (as the E–I scale), the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, and the NEO Personality Inventory used in Big Five research. Self-report questionnaires ask respondents about their sociability, assertiveness, and preference for excitement or calm.

Cultural perspectives

Attitudes toward introversion vary across cultures. Some Western societies, particularly the United States, have been described as favoring an "extrovert ideal" that rewards assertiveness and sociability, while many East Asian cultures traditionally value humility, reserve, and group harmony. Authors such as Susan Cain, in her 2012 book Quiet: The Power of Introverts in a World That Can't Stop Talking, have argued for greater appreciation of introverted temperaments.
