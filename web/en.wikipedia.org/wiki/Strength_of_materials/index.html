Strength of materials

A beam in bending — a central problem of the strength of materials.

The strength of materials (also called mechanics of materials) is the behaviour of solid objects subject to stresses and strains. The subject studies the strength of engineering materials and their mechanical behaviour in general: stress, strain, deformation and the relations between them, and the failure of members under load. It is a core subject in mechanical, civil, and aerospace engineering, applied to the design of beams, columns, shafts, pressure vessels, and machine parts.

Basic concepts

Stress is internal force per unit area, σ = F/A. Strain is the relative deformation, ε = ΔL/L. For many materials at small deformations the two are proportional (Hooke's law), σ = Eε, where E is Young's modulus. A tensile test produces a stress–strain curve from which are read the yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, ductility, and toughness of the material.

Loading types

  • Axial loading — tension and compression of bars; thermal expansion effects; statically indeterminate assemblies.
  • Torsion — twisting of shafts, with shear stress proportional to radius; angle of twist formulas.
  • Bending — beams under transverse loads; shear force and bending moment diagrams; the flexure formula σ = My/I; deflection curves (Euler–Bernoulli beam theory).
  • Shear — transverse shear stress in beams, τ = VQ/(It).
  • Combined loading — superposition of axial, bending, torsion and pressure effects; principal stresses and Mohr's circle; failure criteria such as von Mises and Tresca.
  • Columns — buckling instability, governed by Euler's critical load formula Pcr = π²EI/(KL)².

Material behaviour

The field distinguishes elastic from plastic deformation, creep at high temperature, fatigue under cyclic loading (characterized by S–N curves), and fracture mechanics, which treats crack growth through stress intensity factors. Design uses allowable stresses or load-and-resistance factors with safety margins.

History

Galileo's Two New Sciences (1638) opened the quantitative study of beam strength. Hooke's law (1678), Euler's column formula (1757), Coulomb's torsion and bending work, Navier's 1826 treatise, and the 19th-century work of Saint-Venant, Mohr, and others built the classical theory, later extended by Timoshenko in the 20th century.
