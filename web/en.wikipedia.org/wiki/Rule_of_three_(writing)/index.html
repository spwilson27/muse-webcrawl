Rule of three (writing)

Rule of three (writing)
Type: Rhetorical and writing technique
Related concepts: Tricolon, hendiatris
Latin: Omne trium perfectum ("everything in threes is perfect")

The rule of three is a writing principle which suggests that a series of three events, characters or phrases creates a pattern that is inherently more satisfying, effective and memorable than other numbers of items. It is one of the oldest concepts in rhetoric and storytelling, expressed in Latin as omne trium perfectum — everything that comes in threes is perfect.

Use in writing and speech

Writers apply the rule throughout a work — in sentences, paragraphs, scenes, chapters and whole plots. Fairy tales are rich in threes: three wishes, three bears, three little pigs, three billy goats gruff. In rhetoric, the three-part list (tricolon) appears in famous phrases such as "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness", Julius Caesar's "veni, vidi, vici", and Abraham Lincoln's "government of the people, by the people, for the people".

In comedy the rule underpins the "comic triple": two items establish a pattern and the third breaks it for humorous effect. In professional writing and presentation design, the principle is used to structure arguments into three points, since three is the smallest number that forms a recognisable pattern, and audiences tend to retain information grouped in threes.

Related concepts

The rule of three in writing is distinct from, but parallel to, the rule of thirds in visual composition and the dramatic three-act structure. A three-word phrase embodying a single idea is called a hendiatris.
