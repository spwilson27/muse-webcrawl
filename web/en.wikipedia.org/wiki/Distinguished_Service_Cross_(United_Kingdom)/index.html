Distinguished Service Cross (United Kingdom)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Distinguished Service Cross

The Distinguished Service Cross (DSC) is a third-level military decoration awarded for gallantry during active operations against the enemy at sea to officers of the British Armed Forces and, formerly, to officers of other Commonwealth countries. The DSC was instituted in 1901 by King Edward VII as the Conspicuous Service Cross and was renamed the Distinguished Service Cross in October 1914.

Recipients are entitled to use the post-nominal letters "DSC" after their names. A Bar is awarded for a further act of gallantry that would have warranted the cross, worn on the ribbon.

History

The award was originally created in 1901 as the Conspicuous Service Cross, for award to warrant officers and subordinate officers for distinguished service before the enemy. It was renamed in 1914, and eligibility was extended in 1931 to members of the Merchant Navy, and during the Second World War to officers of Allied navies and armies serving with His Majesty's ships.

Since the 1993 review of the honours system, as part of the drive to remove distinctions of rank in gallantry awards, the Distinguished Service Medal, formerly awarded to ratings, has been discontinued, and the DSC is now open to all ranks.

Description

The DSC is a plain silver cross with rounded ends, with the Royal Cypher in the centre surmounted by a crown. The reverse is plain except for the hallmark and the year of award engraved on the lower arm. The ribbon is formed of three equal vertical stripes of dark blue, white and dark blue.
