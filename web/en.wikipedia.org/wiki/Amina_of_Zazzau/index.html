Amina of Zazzau

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The ancient city walls of Zaria, traditionally attributed to Queen Amina]

Amina (also Aminatu; mid-16th century) was a Hausa warrior queen (magajiya) of the city-state of Zazzau — modern Zaria, in northern Nigeria — credited in the Kano Chronicle and Hausa oral tradition with expanding Zazzau's territory to the Niger and the edge of the forest, and with the great earthen walls (ganuwar Amina, 'Amina's walls') whose remains ring several Hausa towns. Daughters of Queen Bakwa Turunku, Amina is said to have ruled for 34 years, leading cavalry armies in person, exacting tribute in kola nuts and eunuchs, and refusing marriage in the famous (and likely embellished) tales of her lovers. Her historicity is debated — some scholars place her in the 15th, others the 16th century, and the chronicle evidence is late — but she stands as West Africa's archetypal warrior queen.

The tradition

The Kano Chronicle (compiled in the 19th century from earlier lists) places Amina as the 22nd ruler of Zazzau and credits her with conquests 'as far as Kwararafa and Nupe', the building of walled camps on campaign that grew into towns, and the introduction of kola cultivation's tribute. Oral traditions add the colour: her mastery of war from age sixteen in her grandfather's armies, her month-short marriages, her death at Atagara. The walls named for her — Zaria's surviving ramparts run some 15 kilometres — may predate or postdate any single ruler; attaching them to Amina is how Hausa memory keeps her.

Historians' debate

Sceptics note the chronicle's late compilation, king-list compression and the absence of contemporary external records, proposing Amina as a composite or a 15th-century figure displaced in time. Defenders answer that Hausa women's rule was real (the magajiya institution, Zazzau's female rulers), that oral kingdoms' traditions carry genuine structure, and that mid-16th-century Zazzau's expansion needs an agent the tradition supplies. In modern Nigeria she is a national icon — statues in Lagos and Zaria, the figure invoked by every retelling from school texts to television drama.
