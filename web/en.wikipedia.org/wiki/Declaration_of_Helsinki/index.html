The Declaration of Helsinki is a set of ethical principles regarding human experimentation developed originally in 1964 for the medical community by the World Medical Association (WMA). It is widely regarded as the cornerstone document on human research ethics. It is not a legally binding instrument in international law, but it has influenced national legislation and the practice of research ethics committees worldwide.

History

The declaration was adopted at the WMA's 18th General Assembly in Helsinki in June 1964, in response to the inadequacies of the earlier Nuremberg Code — in particular its silence on research conducted on patients by their own physicians. It distinguished between therapeutic and non-therapeutic research and introduced the requirement of review by an independent committee.

Principles

  • The health of the patient is the physician's first consideration.
  • Every clinical trial should be preceded by careful assessment of risks in comparison with benefits.
  • Free and informed consent must be obtained from research participants.
  • Research must be based on prior laboratory and animal work and a thorough knowledge of the literature.
  • Special protection is owed to vulnerable populations.

Revisions and controversies

The declaration has been revised repeatedly. The 2000 revision's clauses on placebo use and post-trial access to treatment proved controversial, prompting the WMA to add "notes of clarification" in 2002 and 2004. The 2013 Fortaleza revision addressed research in low-resource settings, and a further revision was adopted in 2024, the declaration's 60th anniversary year, expanding attention to participant vulnerability and community engagement.
