Strategic voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Strategic voting, also called tactical voting, occurs when a voter casts a vote not for their most preferred candidate or party, but for a less preferred option, in order to prevent an even less desirable outcome. It is a pervasive feature of elections under plurality and many other voting systems: voters desert candidates they judge unlikely to win in favour of viable alternatives.

Examples and mechanisms

In first-past-the-post elections, supporters of smaller parties often vote for the leading candidate closest to their views to avoid "wasting" their vote or enabling their least favourite candidate — a dynamic described by Duverger's law and closely related to the spoiler effect. In two-round systems, voters may vote sincerely in the first round and strategically in the second. Tactical voting campaigns — including websites recommending the best-placed challenger in each constituency — became prominent in United Kingdom general elections and in French presidential elections, where voters engage in vote utile ("useful voting").

Theory

The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem shows that every non-dictatorial voting system with more than two outcomes is, in principle, manipulable by strategic voting. Systems differ in how much they reward it: score voting and approval voting invite "bullet voting" by some, while positional and Condorcet methods invite "burying" a strong rival beneath less threatening candidates. Proportional systems such as the single transferable vote — using quotas like the Droop quota — generally reduce the incentive because votes for smaller parties are rarely wasted. Strategic considerations also feature in coalition ("lend-lease") voting under list systems, whether open or closed.
