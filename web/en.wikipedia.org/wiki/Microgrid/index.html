Microgrid

A microgrid integrates distributed generation, storage and loads

A microgrid is a local electrical grid with defined electrical boundaries, acting as a single and controllable entity. It is able to operate in grid-connected mode or island mode (disconnected from the main utility grid), and can transition between the two. Microgrids typically combine distributed energy resources such as solar photovoltaic panels, wind turbines, combined heat and power units, and battery energy storage, together with controllable loads and a control system that balances supply and demand.

Characteristics

The United States Department of Energy defines a microgrid as "a group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid." Key features include local generation close to consumption, the ability to island during outages — improving resilience for hospitals, military bases, campuses and remote communities — and intelligent controls that forecast demand and renewable output.

Applications

Microgrids serve remote and island communities that lack reliable grid access, university and industrial campuses, data centres, and military installations. They became prominent after disasters such as Hurricane Sandy (2012), during which facilities with microgrids, including Princeton University and NYU, maintained power while surrounding areas were blacked out. Falling costs of solar generation and batteries have driven rapid growth in community and commercial microgrid projects worldwide.
