The Upper Paleolithic (or Upper Palaeolithic) is the third and last subdivision of the Paleolithic or Old Stone Age. Very broadly, it dates to between 50,000 and 12,000 years ago, according to some theories coinciding with the appearance of behavioral modernity in early modern humans, until the advent of the Neolithic Revolution and agriculture.

Overview

During the Upper Paleolithic, anatomically modern humans spread from Africa across Eurasia, reaching Australia by about 50,000 years ago and the Americas near the period's end. In Europe, Neanderthals declined and disappeared around 40,000 years ago, shortly after modern humans arrived. Denisovans similarly vanished from Asia. The period coincides with the later part of the Last Glacial Period, including the Last Glacial Maximum around 26,000–20,000 years ago.

Technology and culture

Upper Paleolithic industries are characterized by blade-based stone toolkits produced from prepared cores, and by an explosion of worked bone, antler, and ivory: spear-throwers (atlatls), harpoons, eyed needles for tailored clothing, fishhooks, and oil lamps. Named European cultures include the Aurignacian, Gravettian, Solutrean, and Magdalenian. Dogs were domesticated during this period, probably before 15,000 years ago.

Art

The era produced the earliest undisputed representational art: the painted caves of Chauvet (c. 36,000 years ago), Altamira, and Lascaux; portable "Venus figurines" such as the Venus of Willendorf (c. 28,000 years ago); the ivory Lion-Man of Hohlenstein-Stadel, among the oldest known sculptures; and musical instruments such as bone flutes from the Swabian Jura (c. 40,000 years ago). Elaborate burials, personal ornaments, and long-distance exchange of shells and exotic stone indicate complex symbolic life — sometimes called the "Upper Paleolithic Revolution" or the "Great Leap Forward."
