Gatekeeping (communication)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gatekeeping is the process through which information is filtered for dissemination, whether for publication, broadcasting, the Internet, or some other mode of communication. The academic theory of gatekeeping is a key concept in communication studies, examining how editors and other "gatekeepers" select which events become news and how those stories are shaped.

Theory and history

The concept derives from Kurt Lewin's 1947 work on channels and gatekeepers in social groups. David Manning White's 1950 study of a telegraph editor ("Mr. Gates") founded empirical gatekeeping research in journalism. Later models, such as Shoemaker and Vos's Gatekeeping Theory (2009), analyse gatekeeping at five levels: the individual communicator, communication routines, the organization, social institutions, and the social system. The related concept of agenda-setting examines how gatekeepers' selections influence what audiences think about.

Digital gatekeeping

The internet was widely expected to disintermediate traditional gatekeepers, since anyone can publish. In practice, new gatekeepers have emerged: search engines, social media platforms and their recommendation algorithms now exercise gatekeeping power through ranking, moderation and demonetization — so-called algorithmic gatekeeping — prompting debate about transparency and accountability.
