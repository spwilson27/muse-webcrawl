Peloponnesian War

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Athenian triremes, the warships that sustained Athens' naval empire]

The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) was fought between the Delian League, led by Athens, and the Peloponnesian League, led by Sparta, for the mastery of the Greek world. Thucydides' unfinished history of the war — a founding work of political realism — remains the principal source. Athens' naval empire and radical democracy faced Sparta's land power; after twenty-seven years of fighting, plague, civil strife and a catastrophic Athenian expedition against Sicily, Athens surrendered in 404 BC and its empire was dissolved.

Course of the war

Spartan invasions of Attica (the Archidamian War) drove Athens behind its Long Walls, where plague (430 BC) killed perhaps a quarter of the population, including Pericles. The Peace of Nicias (421 BC) failed to hold. Athens' Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC) ended in annihilation at Syracuse. Sparta, now financed by Persia, built a fleet; Lysander destroyed the Athenian navy at Aegospotami (405 BC), starved Athens into surrender, demolished the Long Walls and installed the oligarchic Thirty Tyrants (soon overthrown).

Consequences

The war exhausted the Greek city-states and discredited both Athenian imperialism and Spartan hegemony. Within a generation Thebes broke Spartan power, and within two, Macedon under Philip II subordinated Greece — a decline Thucydides' analysis of fear, honour and interest was written to explain. The war remains a canonical case study in international relations.
