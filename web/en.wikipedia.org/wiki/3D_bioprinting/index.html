3D bioprinting

3D bioprinting
FieldBiomedical engineering; tissue engineering
MaterialsBioinks: cell-laden hydrogels (alginate, gelatin methacrylate, collagen)
MethodsExtrusion, inkjet, laser-assisted, vat photopolymerisation
ApplicationsTissue models, drug testing, grafts, organ research

Three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting is the use of 3D printing-like techniques to deposit living cells, growth factors and biomaterials — collectively called bioinks — layer by layer to fabricate structures that imitate natural tissues. It combines additive manufacturing with tissue engineering, and its long-term ambition is the production of transplantable organs; its present successes are chiefly in tissue models for research and simple tissue constructs.

Approaches

The most common method is extrusion-based bioprinting, in which a pneumatic or mechanical dispenser lays down continuous filaments of cell-laden hydrogel. Inkjet (drop-on-demand) printers deposit tiny droplets with high speed and cell viability but limited viscosity, while laser-assisted bioprinting uses laser pulses to transfer cells from a donor film with very high precision. Light-based methods, including stereolithography and volumetric printing, cure photosensitive bioinks and can produce fine structures rapidly. After printing, constructs are matured in bioreactors, during which cells remodel the material and, in some methods, self-assembling spheroids fuse into continuous tissue.

Applications

Bioprinted skin, cartilage, bone and vascular patches have reached preclinical and limited clinical investigation, and bioprinted tissue models — liver tissue for toxicity testing, tumour models for drug screening, and "organ-on-chip" systems — are used commercially in pharmaceutical research. Printing whole solid organs remains far beyond current capability, mainly because a functional vascular network must be created to keep thick tissue alive, and because organs require many specialised cell types arranged with microscopic precision.

History

The field traces to experiments in the early 2000s adapting inkjet printers to deposit cells, and to the first commercial bioprinters such as Organovo's NovoGen platform around 2009. Since then the technology has expanded rapidly, with bioprinting experiments conducted aboard the International Space Station, where microgravity allows soft constructs to hold their shape without support scaffolds.
