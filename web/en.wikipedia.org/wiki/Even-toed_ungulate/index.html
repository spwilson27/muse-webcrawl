The even-toed ungulates (order Artiodactyla, from Ancient Greek ártios, "even", and dáktylos, "finger, toe") are hoofed mammals whose weight is borne equally by the third and fourth toes, as opposed to the odd-toed ungulates (perissodactyls), in which the weight is mostly borne by the third toe alone. The roughly 270 terrestrial species of artiodactyl include pigs, peccaries, hippopotamuses, antelopes, deer, giraffes, camels, llamas, alpacas, sheep, goats and cattle.

Many are of great dietary, economic and cultural importance to humans, and domesticated species such as cattle, sheep and goats are central to agriculture worldwide.

Evolution and taxonomy

The artiodactyls first appeared in the early Eocene, around 54 million years ago. Molecular and morphological evidence indicates that cetaceans (whales, dolphins and porpoises) evolved from within the even-toed ungulates, and are most closely related to hippopotamuses. For this reason many taxonomists now unite the two in the clade Cetartiodactyla. The traditional Artiodactyla, excluding whales, is thus paraphyletic with respect to cetaceans, a situation comparable to the concept of an evolutionary grade.

Anatomy

The defining feature of the group is the double-pulley astragalus (ankle bone), which gives the hind foot great flexibility. In most species the axis of the foot passes between the third and fourth digits, a condition called paraxonic. The number of toes is reduced in many lineages: deer, cattle and antelopes walk on two toes, while pigs retain four and the hippopotamus has four splayed toes.

Digestion

Many even-toed ungulates are ruminants, fermenting plant material in a multi-chambered stomach (rumen, reticulum, omasum and abomasum) with the aid of symbiotic microbes. This allows them to extract energy from cellulose-rich forage. Camels and chevrotains have three-chambered stomachs, while pigs and hippos are non-ruminant foregut or hindgut fermenters.

Suborders

Traditionally the order is divided into the suborders Suina (pigs and peccaries), Tylopoda (camels and llamas), and Ruminantia (chevrotains, deer, giraffes, pronghorn, antelopes, cattle, sheep and goats), with the hippopotamus family placed within or near the group now called Whippomorpha alongside cetaceans.
