Settlement archaeology

Settlement archaeology (German: Siedlungsarchäologie) is a branch of modern archaeology concerned with the study of past settlements — their form, distribution, development, and relationship to the surrounding landscape and environment. Rather than focusing on individual monuments or artefacts, it examines communities at scales ranging from the single household and village to regional settlement systems.

Origins

The approach has roots in early 20th-century German scholarship, where scholars such as Gustaf Kossinna used settlement archaeology (often entangled with nationalist aims) to trace the supposed territories of ethnic groups — an association later generations have critically re-examined. In the English-speaking world, the field was transformed from the 1940s by regional survey projects, notably Gordon Willey's Virú Valley study in Peru (1949), which defined settlement pattern research, and by the processual archaeology of the 1960s.

Methods

Settlement archaeologists combine field survey (systematic surface collection across a landscape), excavation of houses and settlements, aerial photography and remote sensing (including LiDAR), geographic information systems (GIS), and environmental data such as soils, water, and vegetation. Analysis addresses settlement hierarchies, site catchment, population estimates, and patterns of colonisation, abandonment, and continuity over long spans of time (longue durée).

Applications

The approach has been applied worldwide — to Mesopotamian urbanisation, Maya settlement in the lowlands revealed by LiDAR, Iron Age hillfort landscapes in Europe, and early farming villages of the Near East — and it informs modern heritage management and cultural resource planning by identifying significant sites across whole regions.
