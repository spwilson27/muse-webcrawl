The short-beaked echidna (Tachyglossus aculeatus)

Echidnas (/ɪˈkɪdnəz/), sometimes known as spiny anteaters, are quill-covered monotremes belonging to the family Tachyglossidae. The four living species of echidna and the platypus are the only living mammals that lay eggs. Echidnas live in Australia and New Guinea, feeding on ants, termites and other invertebrates which they catch with a long sticky tongue. They are named after Echidna, a creature from Greek mythology who was half-woman and half-snake.

Description

Echidnas are medium-sized, solitary mammals covered with coarse hair and spines — modified hairs made of keratin, similar to the quills of a porcupine, which they can erect defensively; threatened animals burrow with only spines exposed or roll into a spiny ball. They have elongated, slender snouts (beaks) that serve as both mouth and nose, equipped with electroreceptors used to detect prey underground. Their short, strong limbs bear large claws adapted for digging. Echidnas have the second-lowest active body temperature of any mammal (after the platypus), around 32 °C, and correspondingly low metabolic rates; they are long-lived, with captive individuals surviving 50 years. Despite a relatively small brain relative to body size for early records, the echidna's cerebral cortex is large and highly folded, and the animals show notable learning ability.

Species

  • Short-beaked echidna (Tachyglossus aculeatus) — widespread across Australia and southern New Guinea; five subspecies.
  • Sir David's long-beaked echidna (Zaglossus attenboroughi) — Cyclops Mountains, New Guinea; critically endangered.
  • Eastern long-beaked echidna (Zaglossus bartoni) — New Guinea highlands; vulnerable.
  • Western long-beaked echidna (Zaglossus bruijnii) — western New Guinea; critically endangered.

The long-beaked species feed principally on earthworms and are substantially larger, reaching about 1 m in length.

Reproduction

Females lay a single leathery egg about 22 days after mating, placing it directly into a simple pouch; the young ("puggle") hatches after about 10 days, hairless and translucent, and laps milk secreted from milk patches on the mother's skin — monotremes have no nipples. The puggle is left in a nursery burrow once its spines develop (at about 45–55 days) and continues to suckle until about seven months old. Males form "trains" following a female during the winter breeding season, and male echidnas possess a unique four-headed penis.

Evolution

Echidnas diverged from the platypus lineage an estimated 19–48 million years ago; the oldest echidna fossil material is from the early Miocene, and giant echidnas (such as Murrayglossus, formerly Zaglossus hacketti) lived in Pleistocene Australia. Echidnas appear on the Australian five-cent coin, and are the faunal emblem of no state but are beloved nationally; one named Millie was a mascot of the Sydney 2000 Olympics.
