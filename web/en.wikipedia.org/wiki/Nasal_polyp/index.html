Nasal polyp

Nasal polyps (NP) are noncancerous growths of the lining of the nose or sinuses. Symptoms include trouble breathing through the nose, loss of smell, decreased taste, postnasal drip, and a runny nose. The growths are sac-like, movable, and nontender, though occasionally facial pain can occur. They typically occur in both nostrils and can affect several percent of the adult population.

Causes and associations

Nasal polyps arise from chronic inflammation of the nasal and sinus mucosa (chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, CRSwNP), often of a type-2 (eosinophilic) character. They are associated with asthma, allergic rhinitis, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (Samter's triad), cystic fibrosis, and allergic fungal rhinosinusitis. Unlike polyps elsewhere in the body, nasal polyps are not associated with an increased risk of cancer.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is by nasal endoscopy, with CT imaging used to assess sinus involvement and plan surgery. Polyps appear as pale, grape-like, insensitive masses, distinguished from the pink, sensitive inferior turbinates.

Treatment

First-line treatment is intranasal corticosteroid sprays; oral corticosteroids can shrink polyps temporarily but carry systemic side effects. Endoscopic sinus surgery (polypectomy with widening of the sinus openings) is used when medical therapy fails, though recurrence is common. Biologic agents targeting type-2 inflammation — such as dupilumab, omalizumab, and mepolizumab — are approved for severe CRSwNP.
