Aconitine

Aconitine
FormulaC34H47NO11
Molar mass645.75 g/mol
SourceAconitum species (monkshood)
ClassDiterpenoid alkaloid; neurotoxin and cardiotoxin

Aconitine is a highly toxic alkaloid found in plants of the genus Aconitum, especially Aconitum napellus (monkshood or wolfsbane). It is a potent neurotoxin and cardiotoxin that acts on voltage-gated sodium channels. A few milligrams can be lethal to an adult human, and aconite has a long history of use as an arrow poison, a homicidal poison, and, in carefully prepared and diluted forms, a traditional medicine.

Mechanism of toxicity

Aconitine binds to site 2 of voltage-dependent sodium channels, holding them open and preventing inactivation. The resulting persistent sodium influx causes initial neuronal and muscular excitation — tingling, burning and numbness around the mouth and limbs — followed by paralysis, cardiac arrhythmias, and potentially fatal ventricular fibrillation. There is no specific antidote; treatment is supportive.

Sources and history

The name derives from the Greek akoniton. Aconite extracts were smeared on arrows and spears in Asia and Europe for hunting wolves and in warfare, and monkshood appears in folklore as a protection against werewolves and vampires. In traditional Chinese medicine, processed aconite root (fuzi) is used after prolonged boiling or steaming that hydrolyses aconitine to far less toxic derivatives; improper preparation still causes poisonings.

Poisoning

Symptoms typically begin within minutes to two hours of ingestion: paraesthesia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, muscle weakness, hypotension, and dangerous ventricular arrhythmias. Fatal outcomes most often arise from refractory arrhythmia or respiratory paralysis. Documented modern cases include accidental poisonings from herbal preparations and a small number of deliberate poisonings.
