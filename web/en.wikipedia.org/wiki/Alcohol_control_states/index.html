Alcohol control states, also called control jurisdictions, are those U.S. states that exercise a government monopoly over the wholesaling — and in most cases the retailing — of distilled spirits and, in some states, wine. Instead of licensing private liquor stores, the state itself sells spirits through state-run stores or designated agency outlets. Seventeen states, along with Montgomery County in Maryland, operate some form of control system.

Origins

When the Twenty-first Amendment repealed national Prohibition in 1933, it left alcohol regulation to the states. Some states, influenced by temperance traditions and a report commissioned by John D. Rockefeller Jr., chose direct state control as a middle path between prohibition and an open market, arguing that a public monopoly would discourage promotion of drinking while capturing revenue for the state.

How the systems work

Control arrangements vary. In "monopoly" retail states such as Pennsylvania, Virginia, North Carolina and Utah, spirits are sold only in state stores (or state-contracted agencies), while beer and wine may be sold privately under separate rules. In "wholesale control" states the state runs the wholesale distribution of spirits but licenses private retailers. Control boards (often called ABC boards — Alcoholic Beverage Control) set prices, select products, and direct profits to state and local treasuries; collectively the systems generate billions of dollars in annual revenue.

Debate

Supporters credit control systems with lower per-capita spirits consumption and alcohol-related harm, uniform enforcement, and revenue without raising other taxes. Critics argue the monopolies limit selection and convenience, inflate prices, and are an anachronism. Some jurisdictions have moved toward privatisation, most notably Washington state, whose voters ended the state spirits monopoly in 2012.
