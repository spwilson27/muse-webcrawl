Sphere

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A shaded sphere with its centre, radius and great circle marked]

A sphere is a three-dimensional surface consisting of all points at a fixed distance (the radius) from a centre point — the roundest possible solid boundary, the surface of an ordinary ball. Its volume is (4/3)πr³ and its surface area 4πr², results proved by Archimedes, who considered them his finest work and asked for a sphere inside a cylinder to be carved on his tomb.

Spheres matter far beyond geometry class: planets are nearly spherical because gravity pulls matter toward a centre; bubbles are spherical because surface tension minimises area; and the celestial sphere remains astronomy's working picture of the sky.

Generalisation

Mathematicians extend spheres to every dimension: the n-sphere is the set of points at unit distance in (n+1)-dimensional space. The Poincaré conjecture — proved by Grigori Perelman in 2003 — characterises the 3-sphere among all closed 3-dimensional shapes.

A great circle, the largest circle drawable on a sphere, gives the shortest surface route between two points, which is why flight paths look curved on flat maps.
