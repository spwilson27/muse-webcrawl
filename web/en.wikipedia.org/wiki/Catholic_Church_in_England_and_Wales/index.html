Catholic Church in England and Wales

The Catholic Church in England and Wales is part of the worldwide Catholic Church in full communion with the Holy See. Its origins date to the 6th and 7th centuries, when Pope Gregory the Great sent St Augustine of Canterbury and other missionaries to convert the pagan Anglo-Saxons.

Following the English Reformation of the 16th century, when Henry VIII broke with Rome and the Church of England was established, Catholicism was suppressed and its adherents persecuted for nearly 300 years. The hierarchy of dioceses was formally restored by Pope Pius IX in 1850 with the bull Universalis Ecclesiae.

Organisation

The Church in England and Wales is organised into five ecclesiastical provinces — Westminster, Birmingham, Liverpool, Southwark and Cardiff — comprising 22 dioceses. The Archbishop of Westminster is traditionally regarded as the leader of the Church in England and Wales and presidents of the Catholic Bishops' Conference of England and Wales. Its principal church is Westminster Cathedral in London.

Demographics

There are an estimated five to six million Catholics in England and Wales, including large communities descended from Irish immigrants and more recent arrivals from Poland, other parts of Europe, Africa, Asia and Latin America. Mass attendance, which declined through much of the 20th century, has been sustained in many urban parishes by immigration.

History highlights

Key events include the mission of St Augustine (597), the schism under Henry VIII (1534), the Catholic emancipation of 1829, and the restoration of the hierarchy in 1850. In 1982 Pope John Paul II made the first visit by a reigning pope to Britain, and in 2010 Pope Benedict XVI made a state visit, during which he beatified Cardinal John Henry Newman, who was canonised in 2019.
