Lion Air Flight 610 was a scheduled domestic passenger flight from Jakarta to Pangkal Pinang, Indonesia, which crashed into the Java Sea 13 minutes after takeoff on 29 October 2018. All 189 people aboard the nearly new Boeing 737 MAX 8 were killed. It was the first fatal accident involving the 737 MAX and, at the time, the deadliest accident involving a 737.

Accident

The aircraft, registration PK-LQP, had been delivered to Lion Air only in August 2018. On the previous flight, from Denpasar to Jakarta, the crew had experienced unreliable airspeed and altitude indications and repeated nose-down trim commands, and reported a "flight control problem"; maintenance replaced an angle-of-attack (AoA) sensor. On the accident flight, a faulty AoA sensor fed erroneous data to the flight-control system. Shortly after takeoff the stick shaker activated, and the then-little-known Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS) repeatedly commanded nose-down stabiliser trim in response to the false high angle of attack. The pilots fought the trim inputs more than twenty times while struggling with conflicting airspeed and altitude readings; the aircraft eventually dived at high speed into the sea at about 06:31 local time, some 34 nautical miles off the coast.

Investigation and the MCAS crisis

Indonesia's KNKT final report (October 2019) identified nine contributing factors, including: MCAS's reliance on a single AoA sensor, the absence of MCAS information in flight manuals and training, deficient design and certification oversight, maintenance errors in the sensor installation, and the crew's difficulties managing multiple alerts. Together with the crash of Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 five months later — in which the same system behaved similarly, killing 157 — the accident triggered the worldwide grounding of the 737 MAX from March 2019, a crisis that cost Boeing tens of billions of dollars, led to congressional investigations, criminal charges and a $2.5 billion deferred prosecution agreement in the United States, and forced sweeping reforms in aircraft certification. The MAX returned to service from late 2020 after software redesigns (dual-sensor comparison, single activation, reduced authority) and retraining mandates.
