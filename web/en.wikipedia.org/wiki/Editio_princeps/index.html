Editio princeps

In textual scholarship, an editio princeps (Latin for "first edition"; plural: editiones principes) is the first printed edition of a work that previously existed only in manuscripts. Such editions are of great importance in the history of classical, medieval and Renaissance literature, since the printing of a text for the first time often fixed its form for subsequent generations of readers and scholars.

History

Many editiones principes of Greek and Latin classics were produced during the Renaissance, notably by printers and scholar-publishers such as Aldus Manutius in Venice and Johann Froben in Basel. The Homeric epics were first printed in Florence in 1488, edited by Demetrios Chalkokondyles. Works of authors such as Aristotle, Plato and Virgil appeared in print in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries, often based on whichever manuscripts the editor could obtain.

Scholarly significance

Because an editio princeps was frequently based on a small number of manuscripts, sometimes of poor quality, its text may contain errors that later editors worked to correct. Nevertheless, where the manuscripts used have since been lost, the editio princeps may itself serve as an important witness to the text. The term is also applied outside classics, for example to the first printed editions of works by medieval and early modern authors.
