Sidney Gottlieb

Sidney Gottlieb (born Joseph Scheider; 3 August 1918 – 7 March 1999) was an American chemist and spymaster who headed the Central Intelligence Agency's Technical Services Staff and directed Project MKUltra, the CIA's clandestine mind-control and chemical-interrogation research programme of the 1950s and 1960s. He also oversaw CIA efforts to develop poisons and assassination devices aimed at foreign leaders. In retirement his career became public during the Church Committee investigations of 1975, making him one of the most notorious figures of the Cold War CIA.

Early life

Gottlieb was born in the Bronx to Hungarian Jewish immigrant parents. A childhood club foot left him with a limp and excluded him from military service in the Second World War; he instead earned a doctorate in biochemistry from Caltech, specialising in the chemistry of fungi, and worked for the US Department of Agriculture before joining the CIA in 1951.

MKUltra

From 1953 Gottlieb ran MKUltra, an umbrella programme of more than 100 subprojects exploring LSD and other psychoactive drugs, hypnosis, sensory deprivation, and interrogation techniques. Under his direction the CIA dosed unwitting subjects — including mental patients, prisoners, and ordinary citizens (Operation Midnight Climax used agency safehouses in San Francisco and New York) — with LSD. Army scientist Frank Olson died in a fall from a New York hotel window in 1953 after being secretly dosed; Gottlieb's role in the affair became a major scandal when it was revealed in 1975. Gottlieb also directed assassination-related work, including the preparation of poisoned items intended for Congolese leader Patrice Lumumba and a poisoned handkerchief and other devices associated with plots against Fidel Castro.

Later life

Gottlieb retired from the CIA in 1973 after destroying most MKUltra files on the orders of Director Richard Helms — a fact that limited later investigations. He testified before Congress under immunity and spent part of his retirement doing humanitarian and hospice work in India and Virginia. He died in 1999. Stephen Kinzer's 2019 biography Poisoner in Chief drew renewed attention to his career.
