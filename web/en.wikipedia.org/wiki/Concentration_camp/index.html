Concentration camp

A concentration camp is a facility for the internment of people, usually political prisoners, members of persecuted ethnic or religious groups, or other civilians, held without normal judicial process, typically under harsh conditions. The term became widely known through its use by Nazi Germany, which operated an extensive system of concentration camps (Konzentrationslager) between 1933 and 1945, but camps of this kind have existed in many countries and conflicts.

History

The English term derives from the Spanish campos de concentración used during the Cuban War of Independence in the 1890s, when Spanish forces under General Valeriano Weyler confined rural civilians in "reconcentration" camps. Britain used the term for camps holding Boer civilians during the Second Boer War (1899–1902), in which high mortality from disease and malnutrition caused public scandal. Imperial Russia, the Soviet Union (the Gulag system), and other states also ran large internment-camp systems.

Nazi concentration camps

The first Nazi camp, Dachau, opened in March 1933 for political opponents of the regime. The system expanded to hold Jews, Roma, Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, homosexuals, Jehovah's Witnesses, and others. Distinct from concentration camps proper, the regime also built dedicated extermination camps such as Treblinka and Sobibor, and combined sites such as Auschwitz-Birkenau, where around a million people, most of them Jews, were murdered as part of the Holocaust.

Later and other uses

Other twentieth-century examples include Japanese internment camps for enemy aliens, the United States' incarceration of Japanese Americans during the Second World War, camps in North Korea, and camps in the Yugoslav wars of the 1990s. The term remains contested in modern political debate when applied to detention facilities.
