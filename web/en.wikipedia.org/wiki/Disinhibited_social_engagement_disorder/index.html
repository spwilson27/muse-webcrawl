Disinhibited social engagement disorder (DSED) is a mental disorder of childhood characterised by a pattern of behaviour in which a child actively approaches and interacts with unfamiliar adults in an overly familiar, indiscriminate way. It arises, by definition, from extremes of insufficient care — social neglect, repeated changes of primary caregivers, or rearing in settings with high child-to-caregiver ratios, such as some institutions.

Diagnosis

Under the DSM-5, the child must show at least two of: reduced or absent reticence in approaching unfamiliar adults; overly familiar verbal or physical behaviour inappropriate to cultural and age boundaries; diminished or absent checking back with a caregiver when away from them; and willingness to go off with an unfamiliar adult with little or no hesitation. The behaviour must not be limited to impulsivity (as in ADHD) but must reflect genuinely disinhibited social engagement, and the child must be at least nine months old developmentally.

History and research

Before DSM-5 (2013), these symptoms were classified as the disinhibited subtype of reactive attachment disorder; research — above all the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, which followed children raised in Romanian institutions — showed that the inhibited and disinhibited presentations have different courses and correlates, justifying separate diagnoses. DSED symptoms can persist even after children are placed in adequate caregiving environments, though they often diminish.

Treatment

Treatment centres on providing the child with a stable, emotionally available attachment figure and a safe, structured environment; caregiver-focused interventions that increase sensitivity and consistency, such as attachment-based therapies, are the mainstay, rather than medication for the disorder itself.
