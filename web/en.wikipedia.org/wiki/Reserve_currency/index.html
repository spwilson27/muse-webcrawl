Reserve currency

A reserve currency (or anchor currency) is a foreign currency held in significant quantities by central banks and other monetary authorities as part of their foreign exchange reserves, and widely used in international trade, investment and transactions. The United States dollar is the world's dominant reserve currency, followed by the euro, the Japanese yen, sterling and the Chinese renminbi.

Characteristics

A reserve currency is generally issued by a large, open economy with deep and liquid financial markets, a stable value, and a strong institutional and legal framework. Issuing the dominant reserve currency confers advantages — sometimes called the "exorbitant privilege" — including lower borrowing costs and the ability to run persistent deficits; it also entails obligations, such as supplying safe assets to the world (the Triffin dilemma).

Composition

According to the IMF's Currency Composition of Official Foreign Exchange Reserves (COFER) data, the U.S. dollar has accounted for roughly 58–59% of allocated global reserves in recent years, the euro about 20%, the yen about 5–6%, sterling about 5%, and the renminbi around 2–3%. The IMF's Special Drawing Rights basket comprises the dollar, euro, renminbi, yen and sterling.

History

Sterling dominated in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries under the gold standard; the dollar took primacy after the 1944 Bretton Woods Agreement and retained it after the system's collapse in 1971–1973, supported by the depth of U.S. Treasury markets and dollar-denominated trade invoicing (including petrodollars). Debates over "de-dollarisation" have grown since the 2010s but no rival has yet matched the dollar's share.
