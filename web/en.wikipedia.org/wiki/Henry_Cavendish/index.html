Henry Cavendish (10 October 1731 – 24 February 1810) was an English natural philosopher and scientist who was an important experimental and theoretical chemist and physicist. He is noted for his discovery of hydrogen, which he termed "inflammable air". He described the density of inflammable air, which formed water on combustion, in a 1766 paper. Antoine Lavoisier later reproduced his experiment and gave the element its name.

Work

Cavendish showed that water is a compound of hydrogen and oxygen, measured the specific heats of substances, and studied the properties of gases. His most famous experiment, performed in 1797–98 with a torsion balance devised by John Michell, determined the density of the Earth — from which the gravitational constant could be derived — with remarkable accuracy; it is known as the Cavendish experiment. Famously reclusive and eccentric, he published relatively little; many of his electrical researches were only recognised when James Clerk Maxwell edited his papers decades after his death, revealing that he had anticipated results including Ohm's law and Coulomb's law. The Cavendish Laboratory at the University of Cambridge is named in his honour.
