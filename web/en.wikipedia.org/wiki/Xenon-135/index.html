Xenon-135

Xenon-135 (135Xe) is an unstable isotope of xenon with a half-life of about 9.14 hours. It is a fission product of uranium-235 and is the most powerful known neutron-absorbing nuclear poison, with an enormous thermal neutron absorption cross section of roughly 2.6 million barns. Its behaviour strongly influences the operation of nuclear reactors.

Production and removal

Xenon-135 is produced mainly by the beta decay of iodine-135 (half-life 6.6 hours), which is itself a direct fission product and the decay product of tellurium-135. In an operating reactor the xenon is removed both by its own radioactive decay and by "burnup" — neutron capture converting it to the essentially stable xenon-136. At steady power an equilibrium concentration is reached.

Xenon poisoning and the iodine pit

When a reactor is shut down or its power is reduced, xenon burnup stops while iodine-135 continues to decay into xenon-135, so the poison concentration rises for roughly 11 hours before declining — the so-called iodine pit or xenon transient. During this window a reactor may be unable to restart until the xenon decays, a phenomenon known as being "poisoned out". Transient xenon effects cause spatial power oscillations in large cores and must be managed by operators.

Role in the Chernobyl disaster

Xenon-135 accumulation was a contributing factor in the Chernobyl disaster of 26 April 1986. A delayed safety test left Reactor No. 4 operating at low power for hours, building up xenon that suppressed reactivity; when operators withdrew control rods to raise power and then the test proceeded, the reactor's positive void coefficient and rod design flaws allowed a power excursion once the xenon began to be overcome, culminating in the explosion.
