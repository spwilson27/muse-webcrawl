Henri Tajfel (born Hersz Mordche Tajfel; 22 June 1919 – 3 May 1982) was a Polish social psychologist, best known for his pioneering work on the cognitive aspects of prejudice and for social identity theory, which he developed together with his student John Turner. Tajfel was a Fellow of the British Academy and president of the European Association of Experimental Social Psychology, which he helped to found.

Early life

Tajfel was born in Włocławek, Poland, to a Jewish family. He left Poland to study chemistry at the University of Toulouse in France — Polish universities operated quotas restricting Jewish students. Called up at the outbreak of the Second World War, he served in the French army, was captured by the Germans, and survived the war as a prisoner of war, his Jewish identity undetected. When he returned, he learned that none of his immediate family, and few of his friends, had survived the Holocaust. The experience of the war and of the destruction of Polish Jewry shaped the central question of his career: how ordinary people come to discriminate against, and even exterminate, members of other groups.

After the war Tajfel worked for relief organisations with displaced persons and orphans, then studied psychology at the Université libre de Bruxelles, graduating in 1951. He emigrated to Britain, became a British citizen, and in 1954 took his doctorate at Birkbeck College, London. He lectured at Durham and Oxford, and from 1967 until his death he was professor of social psychology at the University of Bristol.

Minimal groups and social identity

At Bristol, Tajfel and his colleagues devised the minimal group paradigm. In the classic experiments, schoolboys were assigned to groups on trivial or even explicitly random criteria — for instance, a purported preference for paintings by Klee or Kandinsky — and were then asked to allocate points (later exchangeable for money) to anonymous members of their own group and of the other group. The boys systematically favoured their own group, even when doing so meant receiving less in absolute terms, demonstrating that mere categorisation into groups is sufficient to trigger intergroup discrimination.

Together with John Turner, Tajfel built these findings into social identity theory: people derive part of their self-concept from the groups they belong to, and they strive for a positive social identity, which they achieve by favourably comparing their in-group with out-groups. When a group's status is unsatisfactory, members may pursue individual mobility, social creativity, or social competition. The theory became one of the most influential frameworks in social psychology and underlies later work on intergroup relations, organisational identity, and political mobilisation.

Death and legacy

Tajfel died of cancer in Oxford in 1982. The European Association of Social Psychology awards the Henri Tajfel Award in his honour, and the Tajfel Medal recognises lifetime contributions to the field. His edited volumes Differentiation Between Social Groups (1978) and Social Identity and Intergroup Relations (1982) remain standard references.
