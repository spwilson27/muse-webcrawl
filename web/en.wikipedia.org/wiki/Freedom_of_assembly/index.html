Freedom of assembly

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedom of peaceful assembly is the individual right to gather with others for expressive purposes — marches, rallies, pickets, vigils and meetings — protected by Article 21 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, the First Amendment of the US Constitution (“the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government”) and Article 11 of the European Convention on Human Rights, and elaborated in the UN Human Rights Committee's General Comment 37 (2020). States may impose only lawful, necessary and proportionate restrictions (time, place and manner rules; notification regimes that must not function as permission systems).

The right underwrites labour movements, civil-rights marches and protest politics generally (see Montgomery bus boycott for the assembly-and-boycott repertoire), and its pressure points are policing tactics (kettling, mass arrest), counter-demonstrations, assembly near sensitive sites, online-organised gatherings, and emergency restrictions (pandemic bans litigated worldwide). Since the 2010s many democracies have tightened protest law (the UK's Police, Crime, Sentencing and Courts Act 2022 and Public Order Act 2023; various US state anti-riot statutes), drawing criticism from UN rapporteurs and rights groups — the recurring cycle of the freedom's history.
