Gravitational binding energy

The gravitational binding energy of a system is the minimum energy which must be added to it in order for the system to cease being in a gravitationally bound state. A gravitationally bound system has a lower (more negative) gravitational potential energy than the sum of the energies of its parts when they are completely separated — a value consistent with the fact that a bound system requires energy input to separate it.

Newtonian approximation

For a spherical body of uniform density, the gravitational binding energy U is given approximately by U = −(3/5) GM²/R, where G is the gravitational constant, M is the mass of the sphere and R its radius. For the Earth, using a more realistic two-layer density model, the binding energy is about 2.49 × 1032 joules — roughly the total energy the Sun radiates in one week.

General relativity and negative mass

In general relativity, binding energy contributes negatively to the total (ADM) mass of a system: a bound star weighs less than the sum of the masses of its dispersed constituents. The fractional mass defect of ordinary astronomical bodies is tiny (about 10−10 for Earth), but reaches percent levels for neutron stars. Unlike a classical picture in which binding energy might be arbitrarily negative, relativistic bodies remain positively massive.
