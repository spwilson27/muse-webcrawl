Nudibranchs are a group of soft-bodied marine gastropod molluscs which shed their shells after their larval stage. The name means "naked gills", referring to the feathery external gills and horn-like rhinophores on their backs. They are renowned among divers and photographers for their extraordinary colours and forms.

Biology

More than 3,000 species are described, ranging from a few millimetres to over 60 cm (such as the Spanish dancer, Hexabranchus sanguineus). Lacking a protective shell, many defend themselves with chemical deterrents, stinging cells (kleptocnidae) stolen from hydrozoan prey, or camouflage. Their vivid colouration often advertises toxicity — aposematism.

Feeding and reproduction

Nudibranchs are carnivorous, grazing on sponges, hydroids, anemones, bryozoans, tunicates and barnacles; some aeolids store functional chloroplasts or nematocysts from their food. All are simultaneous hermaphrodites, exchanging sperm reciprocally and laying ribbon-like egg masses.
