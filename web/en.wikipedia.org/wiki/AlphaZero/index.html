AlphaZero is a computer program developed by artificial intelligence research company DeepMind to master the games of chess, shogi and Go. The algorithm uses an approach similar to AlphaGo Zero, combining a deep neural network with Monte Carlo tree search, but generalises it to multiple games with no game-specific knowledge beyond the basic rules.

Training and results

AlphaZero learns entirely by self-play reinforcement learning, starting from random play — a tabula rasa approach. On 5 December 2017 the DeepMind team released a preprint reporting that, after only a few hours of self-play training (about nine hours for chess, twelve for shogi and thirteen days equivalent for Go in the final published version), AlphaZero defeated the world-champion programs Stockfish in chess, Elmo in shogi, and its own predecessor AlphaGo Zero in Go.

In a 100-game chess match against Stockfish 8, AlphaZero won 28 games and drew 72, losing none. The games it played displayed a dynamic, attacking style that commentators described as creative and "alien" — including long-term piece sacrifices that conventional engines initially undervalued. A peer-reviewed paper describing the system appeared in Science in December 2018.

Algorithm

A single residual neural network takes the board state as input and outputs a policy (a probability distribution over moves) and a value estimate of the position. The network is trained from self-play games, in which Monte Carlo tree search guided by the network generates improved move probabilities as training targets. The same architecture and hyperparameters were used for all three games.

Impact

AlphaZero's methods influenced the design of open-source projects such as Leela Chess Zero and the broader field of reinforcement learning. Chess players and authors, notably Matthew Sadler and Natasha Regan in their book Game Changer (2019), studied its games for new strategic ideas. DeepMind later generalised the approach in MuZero, which learns a model of its environment as well as the policy.
