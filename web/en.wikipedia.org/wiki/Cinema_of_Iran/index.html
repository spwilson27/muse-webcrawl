Cinema of Iran

The cinema of Iran (Persian cinema) comprises the film industry of Iran and the art films it has produced, which have achieved wide international recognition since the late 20th century. Iranian cinema is noted for its poetic, realist style, its frequent use of child protagonists and non-professional actors, and the creative responses of its directors to censorship constraints.

Early history

Film arrived in Iran in 1900, when Mozaffar ad-Din Shah Qajar's court photographer Mirza Ebrahim Khan Akkas Bashi filmed the Shah's visit to Europe; the first public cinema opened in Tehran in 1904. The first Iranian feature, Abi and Rabi (1930), was directed by Ovanes Ohanian, and Abdolhossein Sepanta made the first Persian-language sound film, The Lor Girl (1933), in India. Before the 1979 revolution, a popular commercial cinema known as filmfarsi coexisted with a burgeoning new wave, marked by Dariush Mehrjui's The Cow (1969) and work by Masoud Kimiai and Sohrab Shahid-Saless.

Post-revolution cinema

After the Iranian Revolution, cinema was restructured under Islamic guidelines administered by the Ministry of Culture and Islamic Guidance, with strict rules on dress (notably the compulsory hijab for actresses on screen) and content. Despite — and partly because of — these constraints, a distinctive art cinema emerged that won international acclaim: directors such as Abbas Kiarostami (Where Is the Friend's Home?, Close-Up, Taste of Cherry, Palme d'Or 1997), Mohsen Makhmalbaf, Jafar Panahi, Majid Majidi, Asghar Farhadi (A Separation and The Salesman, both Academy Award winners for Best Foreign Language Film), Bahman Ghobadi, Rakhshan Bani-Etemad and Samira Makhmalbaf.

Themes and style

Post-revolution Iranian films often feature rural settings, children's perspectives (as in Majidi's Children of Heaven, 1997), open endings, documentary–fiction hybrids and subtle social critique conveyed through allegory. Constraints on depicting touch or uncovered women led to inventive visual grammars that foreign critics came to see as a hallmark of the national cinema.

Industry and festivals

The Fajr International Film Festival in Tehran is the country's main showcase. Iran produces dozens of features annually for a domestic market dominated historically by comedies and melodramas, alongside its celebrated art-house exports.
