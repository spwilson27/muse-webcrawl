Cyrano de Bergerac

Hercule-Savinien de Cyrano de Bergerac (1619–1655).

Hercule-Savinien de Cyrano de Bergerac (6 March 1619 – 28 July 1655) was a French novelist, playwright, epistolarian, and duelist. A bold and innovative author, his work was part of the libertine literature of the first half of the seventeenth century. Today, he is best known as the inspiration for Edmond Rostand's play Cyrano de Bergerac (1897), which, although it includes elements of his life, also contains inventions about his person and his famously large nose.

Life

Born in Paris to a family with lands at Bergerac in the Senneville valley (not the famous Bergerac of Périgord), Cyrano served as a soldier in the Cadets of Gascony and was wounded at the sieges of Mouzon and Arras during the Thirty Years' War. After leaving the army he studied under the philosopher Pierre Gassendi and turned to writing: satirical letters, the comedy Le Pédant joué, the tragedy La Mort d'Agrippine, and attacks on the minister Mazarin. He died in 1655, at about 36, from injuries associated with a falling beam — an accident some contemporaries rumoured to be an assassination.

Works

Cyrano's most original writings are his two prose romances of imaginary voyages, L'Autre Monde: ou les États et Empires de la Lune (1657) and les États et Empires du Soleil (1662), published posthumously. Blending fantasy with materialist philosophy and satire, they describe journeys to the Moon and Sun and encounters with their inhabitants, and are regarded as early landmarks of science fiction, anticipating later voyages extraordinaires and influencing writers from Swift onward.
