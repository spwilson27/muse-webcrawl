Nerve agent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nerve agents
ClassOrganophosphate chemical weapons
MechanismIrreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase
Main typesG-series (tabun, sarin, soman); V-series (VX); Novichok agents
AntidotesAtropine, pralidoxime, diazepam
StatusBanned under the Chemical Weapons Convention (1993)

Nerve agents, sometimes also called nerve gases (though they are liquids, not gases), are a class of organic chemicals that disrupt the mechanisms by which nerves transfer messages to organs. The disruption is caused by the blocking of acetylcholinesterase (AChE), an enzyme that catalyzes the breakdown of acetylcholine, a neurotransmitter. Nerve agents are among the most lethal chemical weapons known.

History

The first nerve agents were synthesized in Germany in the 1930s by Gerhard Schrader's team at IG Farben during pesticide research: tabun (1936) and sarin (1938), followed by soman (1944). Britain developed VX in the 1950s, and the Soviet Union developed the Novichok family from the 1970s. Nerve agents were used in the Iran–Iraq War, in the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack by Aum Shinrikyo, and in Syria during its civil war; the 2018 Salisbury poisoning involved a Novichok agent.

Effects and treatment

Exposure causes symptoms summarized by the mnemonic SLUDGE (salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastrointestinal cramps, emesis) plus miosis (pinpoint pupils), bronchospasm, seizures, and death by respiratory failure. Treatment involves decontamination and antidotes: atropine, an oxime reactivator such as pralidoxime, and a benzodiazepine anticonvulsant.

Legal status

Nerve agents are listed as Schedule 1 substances under the Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993, which bans their development, production, stockpiling, and use; declared stockpiles have been destroyed under international supervision, most recently with the completion of US stockpile destruction in 2023.
