Relief pitcher

In baseball and softball, a relief pitcher or reliever is a pitcher who enters the game after the starting pitcher is removed because of fatigue, ineffectiveness, injury, or ejection, or for other strategic reasons, such as inclement weather delays or pinch hitter substitution. Relief pitchers are further divided informally into various roles, based on depth, availability, the inning in which they enter, and the situation.

Relief pitchers are generally quicker in their throwing motions, allowing them to be ready on short notice, and tend to focus on a small repertoire of pitches delivered with maximum effort over short outings.

Roles

Common relief roles include the closer, who pitches the final inning(s) of a close game to record a save; the setup man, who pitches the inning before the closer; middle relievers, who bridge the gap between starter and late-inning specialists; long relievers, who can pitch several innings when a starter exits early; and specialists, historically including the left-handed specialist brought in to face one or a few batters. In recent years teams have also used "openers" — relievers who start a game and pitch only the first inning or two.

History

In the early decades of baseball, starting pitchers were expected to complete games, and relief appearances were rare. The role of the dedicated reliever grew through the mid-20th century with pitchers such as Firpo Marberry and Joe Page, and by the 1970s and 1980s the bullpen had become a structured unit. The save statistic, adopted officially in 1969, and the emergence of star closers such as Rollie Fingers, Goose Gossage, and later Mariano Rivera elevated the profile of relief pitching. Modern analytics have pushed teams toward ever more specialized and frequent bullpen usage.
