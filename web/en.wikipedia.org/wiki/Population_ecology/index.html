Population ecology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Population ecology is the branch of Ecology that studies how populations of a species change in size and composition over time and space, and why. It links individual births, deaths, immigration and emigration to population-level patterns, and underpins wildlife management, fisheries, pest control and conservation.

Core concepts

Populations grow exponentially when resources are unlimited, but real growth is bounded, giving the logistic curve toward Carrying capacity. Ecologists build life tables and survivorship curves, model age structure, and study density-dependent factors (competition, disease, predation) versus density-independent ones (weather, disasters). Predator–prey cycles, described by Lotka–Volterra models, and metapopulations — networks of local populations linked by dispersal — are classic topics.

Applications

Population viability analysis estimates extinction risk for endangered species; maximum sustainable yield models guide fisheries (often imperfectly); and epidemiological models are population ecology applied to pathogens. Charles Elton and later Robert MacArthur and G. Evelyn Hutchinson shaped the field in the 20th century.
