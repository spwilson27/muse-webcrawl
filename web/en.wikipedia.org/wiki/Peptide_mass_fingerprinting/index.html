Peptide mass fingerprinting

Peptide mass fingerprinting (PMF), also known as protein fingerprinting, is an analytical technique for protein identification in which the protein of interest is cleaved into peptides by a protease such as trypsin, and the absolute masses of those peptides are measured precisely by a mass spectrometer such as MALDI-TOF or ESI-TOF. The method was developed in 1993 by several groups independently.

Method

In a typical workflow, the unknown protein — often a spot excised from a two-dimensional gel — is digested in-gel or in solution with a sequence-specific protease. Trypsin is used most commonly because it cleaves reliably after lysine and arginine residues, generating peptides of a convenient size. The resulting mixture is analysed by mass spectrometry, producing a list of peptide masses: the "fingerprint".

Software then performs a theoretical digest of every protein sequence in a database using the same cleavage rules, and compares the predicted peptide masses with the measured set. A statistical score indicates how many masses match and how likely such a match would arise by chance; the best-scoring database entry is taken as the identification. Widely used tools include Mascot, ProFound, and MS-Fit.

Sample preparation

Protein samples are typically reduced and alkylated (for example, cysteine residues are carbamidomethylated with iodoacetamide) before digestion, and these fixed modifications, along with variable modifications such as methionine oxidation, are specified in the database search. Gel-derived samples are destained and dehydrated before in-gel digestion, and the peptides are extracted and often desalted on a C18 microcolumn before spotting with matrix for MALDI analysis.

Advantages and limitations

PMF is fast, inexpensive, and suitable for high-throughput identification of proteins from organisms whose genomes have been sequenced. It works best when the sample contains a single protein (or a very simple mixture) and when the protein is present in the reference database. It is less effective for complex mixtures, for proteins with extensive post-translational modifications, and for species without sequenced genomes; in those cases tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) peptide sequencing methods such as peptide fragment fingerprinting are preferred.
