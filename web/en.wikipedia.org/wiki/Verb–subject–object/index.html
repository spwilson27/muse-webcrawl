Verb–subject–object

In linguistic typology, verb–subject–object (VSO) is a sentence structure in which the verb comes first, followed by the subject and then the object: thus "Ate the man the fish". VSO is the third most common word order among the world's languages, after SVO and SOV, characterising roughly 9–10% of languages surveyed in the World Atlas of Language Structures.

Distribution and examples

VSO order is characteristic of the Insular Celtic languages (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Welsh in its literary form), where sentences typically begin with the verb: Irish Chonaic mé an fear, literally "Saw I the man". It is also the classical order of Semitic languages such as Classical Arabic and Biblical Hebrew, and is found in many Austronesian languages (Tagalog, Māori, Hawaiian), Nilotic languages, and in some families of the Americas. Spanish permits VSO in questions and emphatic contexts, though its basic order is SVO.

Properties

In phrase-structure terms, VSO order often arises when the verb raises out of the verb phrase past the subject, a head-movement analysis developed in generative grammar. Many VSO languages mark grammatical relations with rich case systems or verb agreement. The order also correlates with other typological parameters in the tradition of Joseph Greenberg's word-order universals, such as prepositions preceding their objects and genitives following nouns.
