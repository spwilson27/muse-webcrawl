Treaty of Versailles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Treaty of Versailles was the peace treaty between Germany and the Allied powers that formally ended the First World War's state of war between them. Signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles — exactly five years after the Sarajevo assassination — and in force from 10 January 1920, it was the principal product of the Paris Peace Conference and contained the Covenant of the League of Nations as its first part.

Its terms stripped Germany of about a tenth of its European territory and population (Alsace-Lorraine to France, Posen and West Prussia to Poland, Eupen-Malmedy to Belgium, northern Schleswig after plebiscite to Denmark), all colonies (distributed as League mandates), limited the army to 100,000 men without conscription, tanks or aircraft, demilitarised the Rhineland, imposed reparations (fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks), and — in Article 231, the “war guilt clause” — assigned Germany and its allies responsibility for the war as the basis for reparations. Germany signed under protest after an Allied ultimatum.

Legacy

The treaty was attacked from birth: John Maynard Keynes's The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919) called it a Carthaginian peace that would breed revanchism; the US Senate refused ratification, and America made a separate peace in 1921. In Germany the treaty (“Diktat”, “Schmachfrieden”) was a standing grievance exploited by nationalists and the Nazis. Later historians have varied between seeing Versailles as too harsh to be legitimate and too lenient to be durable — Germany remained potentially the strongest power in Europe — and the debate structures much writing on the origins of the Second World War.
