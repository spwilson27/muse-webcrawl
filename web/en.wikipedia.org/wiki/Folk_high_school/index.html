Folk high school

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Folk high schools (Danish: folkehøjskole; Norwegian: folkehøgskole; Swedish: folkhögskola; Finnish: kansanopisto) are institutions for adult education that generally do not grant academic degrees. They are most numerous in the Nordic countries and in Germany, and are rooted in a philosophy of education for life rather than education for examinations or vocations.

History

The idea originated with the Danish philosopher, theologian and writer N. F. S. Grundtvig (1783–1872), who envisioned schools where ordinary people — especially farmers — would gain civic awareness, national culture and personal formation (dannelse, or Bildung) through dialogue, history, literature, song and communal living rather than textbooks and examinations. The first folk high school opened at Rødding in Schleswig in 1844; others, such as Askov, followed. After Denmark's defeat in 1864 the schools became vehicles of national cultural revival.

Philosophy and practice

Folk high schools are typically residential boarding institutions. Students, commonly young adults aged about 18–25, live at the school for courses lasting from a few weeks to a year. There are no entrance requirements, no grades and no final examinations. Teaching emphasises the "living word" — conversation, lectures, communal singing, and practical subjects ranging from arts and crafts to politics, outdoor life, music and media. The schools receive state support in the Nordic countries but are self-governing, each with its own character and subject profile.

Spread and influence

The model spread to Norway, Sweden and Finland in the later 19th century, and to Germany and other countries in the 20th. Adaptations appeared elsewhere, including the Danish-inspired Highlander Folk School founded in Tennessee in 1932, which became an important training ground for the American labour and civil rights movements.

Today

Denmark has around 70 folk high schools, Norway about 80 (including Christian and independent schools), Sweden over 150, and Finland several dozen. They remain a distinctive part of the Scandinavian tradition of non-formal adult education and lifelong learning.
