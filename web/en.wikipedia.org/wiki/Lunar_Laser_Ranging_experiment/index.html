Lunar Laser Ranging
MethodTiming pulses of laser light reflected from retroreflectors on the Moon

The Lunar Laser Ranging experiment measures the distance between surfaces of the Earth and the Moon using laser ranging. Stations on Earth aim a pulsed laser at retroreflector arrays left on the lunar surface by the Apollo and Lunokhod missions and time the return of individual photons, giving the Earth–Moon distance to centimetre — and nowadays millimetre — precision.

Laser ranging to the retroreflectors left on the Moon by Apollo and Lunokhod

History

The first retroreflector was deployed by Apollo 11 in July 1969, followed by larger arrays on Apollo 14 and Apollo 15 (the biggest, with 300 corner cubes), and French-built reflectors on the Soviet Lunokhod 1 and Lunokhod 2 rovers. The first successful returns were obtained at the McDonald Observatory in Texas and at the Lick Observatory in 1969. Dedicated stations such as APOLLO at Apache Point Observatory in New Mexico and the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur in France now achieve millimetre precision.

Scientific results

More than five decades of ranging have determined that the Moon is receding from Earth at about 3.8 cm per year due to tidal friction; measured the Moon's libration and confirmed it has a fluid core; tested the equivalence principle and general relativity (including the Nordtvedt effect) to high precision; constrained any variation in the gravitational constant G; and refined models of Earth's rotation, plate tectonics and precession.
