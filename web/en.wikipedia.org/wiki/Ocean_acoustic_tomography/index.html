Ocean acoustic tomography

Ocean acoustic tomography is a technique for measuring temperatures and currents over large distances in the ocean by timing how long sound pulses take to travel between underwater transmitters and receivers. Proposed in the late 1970s by Walter Munk and Carl Wunsch, it applies the principles of medical tomography to the ocean: because the speed of sound in seawater depends strongly on temperature, small changes in travel time reveal changes in the average temperature along the sound's path.

Method

Sound at low frequencies travels enormous distances in the ocean, channeled in the deep sound (SOFAR) channel where refraction traps it between pressure and temperature gradients. A pulse sent from a moored source arrives at a distant receiver as a sequence of arrivals along distinct ray paths (or mode combinations), each of which has sampled a different depth range. Inverting the measured travel times yields a three-dimensional estimate of temperature and, from differences between reciprocal transmissions in the two directions, of current velocity along the paths.

The Heard Island experiment and ATOC

In January 1991 the Heard Island Feasibility Test transmitted coded low-frequency sound from near Heard Island in the southern Indian Ocean; signals were detected at receivers on both coasts of the Americas and around the world, some 18,000 km away, demonstrating global-scale acoustic thermometry. The follow-on Acoustic Thermometry of Ocean Climate (ATOC) program deployed sources in the North Pacific in the 1990s to measure basin-scale warming.

The program drew controversy over the possible effects of loud low-frequency sound on marine mammals such as whales; environmental review and mitigation measures, and the confrontation between scientists and activists at the time, shaped later regulation of ocean acoustics. Later networks, such as the Philippine Sea experiments and moored tomographic arrays combined with Argo floats, have continued to use the method for measuring ocean heat content — a central variable in climate science.
