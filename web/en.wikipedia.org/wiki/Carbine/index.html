Carbine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A carbine is a long gun that has a barrel shorter than that of a conventional rifle or musket. The term originally denoted the shorter firearms carried by cavalry troopers (carabiniers), who needed a weapon handier on horseback than a full-length infantry long arm; many early carbines were shortened versions of standard muskets, sometimes firing a reduced charge.

History

Carbines appeared by the late 16th century and became standard for cavalry, artillerymen, and other specialists through the muzzle-loading era — examples include the British Pattern 1856 cavalry carbine and the Sharps and Spencer carbines of the American Civil War. In the bolt-action era nearly every army issued carbine variants of its service rifle, such as the Karabiner 98k. The semi-automatic M1 carbine of the Second World War, chambered for its own intermediate cartridge, was produced in greater numbers than any other US military small arm of the war.

Modern usage

Modern assault rifles in short-barrelled form — such as the M4 carbine — are universally called carbines. Shorter barrels trade muzzle velocity, effective range, and increased muzzle blast for compactness and manoeuvrability, suiting vehicle crews, paratroopers, and close-quarters fighting. Pistol-calibre carbines are also popular in civilian sport shooting.
