Fezzan (Arabic: فزان, Fazzān; Latin: Phazania) is a historic region of southwestern Libya, lying deep in the Sahara desert. Together with Tripolitania in the north-west and Cyrenaica in the east, it forms one of the three traditional provinces of the country.

Mostly covered by sand seas (ergs), gravel plains, and rocky plateaus, Fezzan is sparsely populated; its settlements cluster around oases where groundwater supports date palms and small-scale agriculture. Its largest city is Sabha, a major transport hub for the Libyan south.

Geography

Fezzan covers much of Libya south and west of the Haruj al-Aswad volcanic field. The Ubari Sand Sea in the north-west contains the remarkable Ubari Lakes, remnants of a much wetter prehistoric climate when a giant paleolake (Lake Megafezzan) occupied the basin. In the south-west the Acacus Mountains (Tadrart Acacus) rise near the Algerian border; their rock art, depicting animals and human figures from a greener Sahara, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. To the south, the Tibesti Mountains extend into Chad.

The climate is hyper-arid, with summer temperatures among the highest recorded anywhere on Earth and almost no rainfall.

History

In antiquity the region was the homeland of the Garamantes, a Berber people who built an irrigation-based civilisation centred on Germa, using underground channels (foggaras) to farm the desert. The Romans knew the land as Phazania and campaigned against the Garamantes in the 1st century BC and 1st century AD.

After the Arab conquests of the 7th century, Fezzan became a link in the trans-Saharan trade routes carrying gold, salt, and enslaved people northward to the Mediterranean coast. From the 13th century the Awlad Muhammad dynasty ruled from Murzuq; the region was nominally Ottoman from the 16th century, and was occupied by Italy in 1914 and again in the 1920s–30s during the colonial conquest of Libya. Fezzan was administered by France from 1943 until Libyan independence in 1951, when it became a province of the Kingdom of Libya.

During and after the Libyan civil wars of the 2010s, Fezzan's smuggling routes and oil fields — notably the El Sharara field, Libya's largest — made it strategically contested by rival factions and foreign actors.

Economy

Agriculture around the oases produces dates, grain, and vegetables, sustained by fossil groundwater. The region hosts major oil production around Ubari and Murzuq, and Sabha serves as the principal market and transport centre for southern Libya, linking the coast with Chad and Niger.
