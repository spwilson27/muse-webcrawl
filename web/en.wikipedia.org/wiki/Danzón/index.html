Danzón is the official national dance and musical genre of Cuba, and one of the most influential forms in Latin American popular music. It developed in the 1870s in Matanzas from the contradanza and the danza, blending European salon forms with Afro-Cuban rhythmic elements. Elegant, restrained and highly formalised, danzón is danced by couples in ballrooms, traditionally with pauses between sections during which the dancers stop, converse and fan themselves while the orchestra holds the floor.

History

The first composition officially titled a danzón, "Las Alturas de Simpson", was written by Miguel Faílde and premiered in Matanzas on 1 January 1879. Early danzón orchestras were orquestas típicas built around wind instruments; in the early twentieth century the charanga francesa format — French flute, violins, piano and Afro-Cuban percussion — became standard through bands such as Antonio María Romeu's and later Arcaño y sus Maravillas.

Musical structure

A classic danzón follows a rondo-like structure of repeated, contrasting sections, often opening with a stately introduction and proceeding through a lyrical violin section and a flute-led section before a brilliant finale. In the 1930s and 1940s Orestes López added a syncopated final section known as the nuevo ritmo, whose rhythmic cells gave rise to the mambo, and Enrique Jorrín's experiments within the form produced the cha-cha-chá in the early 1950s.

Danzón in Mexico

Danzón also took deep root in Mexico, especially in Veracruz and Mexico City, where salons such as the Salón México became famous and danzón remains a living public tradition, danced weekly in plazas by older couples. Famous danzones include "Almendra" by Abelardito Valdés and "Las Alturas de Simpson".
