Periodontology or periodontics is the specialty of dentistry that studies the supporting structures of teeth (the periodontium), as well as diseases and conditions that affect them. The periodontium includes the gingiva (gums), alveolar bone, cementum, and the periodontal ligament. A periodontist is a dentist who specialises in the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of periodontal disease and in the placement of dental implants.

Scope of practice

Periodontists manage the full spectrum of gum disease, from gingivitis to advanced periodontitis, using non-surgical therapy (scaling and root planing), periodontal surgery (flap surgery, osseous surgery), regenerative techniques (guided tissue regeneration, bone grafting, growth factors), mucogingival and plastic surgery (grafting to treat gum recession), crown lengthening, and the surgical placement and maintenance of dental implants and treatment of peri-implant diseases. They also manage the interplay between periodontal health and systemic conditions such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease.

Training and organisations

After dental school, periodontists complete two to three years of additional specialty training, recognised by bodies such as the American Academy of Periodontology (founded 1914) and the European Federation of Periodontology. In 2017 the two organisations jointly produced the World Workshop classification of periodontal and peri-implant diseases and conditions, which introduced staging and grading of periodontitis analogous to oncology.

History

Diseases of the gums were described in ancient Egyptian and Chinese texts, and Pierre Fauchard recognised their treatment in the 18th century. The specialty crystallised in the early 20th century with figures such as Grace Rogers Spalding, and advanced dramatically with the microbiological revolution of the 1960s — when Harald Löe's experimental gingivitis studies proved the causal role of dental plaque — and with the advent of osseointegrated implants developed by Per-Ingvar Brånemark.
