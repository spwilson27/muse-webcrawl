Unidentified decedent

Forensic facial reconstructions are one tool used to identify unknown decedents.

An unidentified decedent, or unidentified person (also referred to with the placeholder names John Doe or Jane Doe in North America, and formerly "Lil Miss" or similar local nicknames), is a deceased person whose identity cannot be established by police and medical examiners. Such cases arise when a body is found without identification documents, when decomposition or trauma prevents visual recognition, or when no missing-person report matches the deceased.

Identification methods

Investigators traditionally rely on fingerprints, dental records and DNA profiles, checked against missing-person databases such as the US National Missing and Unidentified Persons System (NamUs) and NCIC. When these fail, forensic anthropologists may produce facial reconstructions or composite sketches to appeal to the public. Since 2018, investigative genetic genealogy — comparing a decedent's DNA against consumer genealogy databases to build family trees — has resolved many decades-old Doe cases, in parallel with its use to identify serial offenders such as the Golden State Killer.

Notable cases

Famous long-unidentified decedents include the "Somerton Man" found on an Adelaide beach in 1948 (identified in 2022 as Carl "Charles" Webb), the Isdal Woman found near Bergen, Norway in 1970, and the "Boy in the Box" in Philadelphia (identified in 2022 as Joseph Augustus Zarelli). The Doe Network and similar volunteer organisations publicise cases internationally. Volunteer groups such as the DNA Doe Project specialise in genetic-genealogy identifications of Does.

Burial

Unidentified decedents who remain unnamed may be buried in potter's fields or indigent cemeteries, often with case numbers rather than names on their markers; samples are normally retained so that identification remains possible decades later.
