Kollel

A kollel (Hebrew: כולל‎, plural kollelim) is an institute for full-time, advanced study of the Talmud and rabbinic literature, structured primarily for married men who have completed their yeshiva studies. Unlike a yeshiva, which serves unmarried students, a kollel typically pays its members a modest stipend so that they can devote themselves entirely to Torah study after marriage.

The word kollel means "gathering" or "collection" — a collective of scholars. The modern kollel movement is widely credited with reshaping Orthodox Jewish life in the twentieth century, creating a large class of full-time Torah scholars and supplying rabbis, teachers, and communal leaders to Jewish communities worldwide.

History

The first kollels were established in nineteenth-century Eastern Europe. The Kovno Kollel, founded in 1877 under the influence of Rabbi Yisrael Salanter and directed by Rabbi Isaac Blaser, is often cited as the prototype: a small group of outstanding married scholars supported communally so that they could study without worldly occupation. A parallel institution, the Kollel Perushim in Jerusalem, supported Lithuanian scholars who had emigrated to the Land of Israel.

The model spread dramatically after the Second World War. Rabbi Aharon Kotler founded Beth Medrash Govoha in Lakewood, New Jersey, in 1943, with a kollel at its heart; it grew into one of the largest institutions of advanced Talmud study in the world. In Israel, government support for kollel students became a central — and politically contested — feature of the relationship between the state and the Haredi community.

Structure

Members of a kollel, known in Yiddish as yungeleit, generally study in pairs (chavrusa) in a shared study hall (beis medrash), following a set curriculum, often concentrating on a single tractate or area of halakha over a study term (zman). Many kollels are attached to a yeshiva, whose advanced students "graduate" into the kollel; others are independent or community-based. Community kollels, which multiplied in North America and elsewhere from the 1990s, combine members' personal study with outreach teaching for local residents.

Finances and debate

Kollels are funded by a combination of charitable donations, tuition-like contributions, and — in Israel — public funds. The stipends are usually small, and kollel families often supplement them with a spouse's income. The growth of long-term kollel study has been debated both inside and outside the Orthodox world: supporters describe it as preserving and deepening Torah scholarship; critics question its economic sustainability and, in Israel, its connection to exemption from military service.
