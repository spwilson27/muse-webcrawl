Guanches

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A stone idol attributed to the Guanches of Tenerife

The Guanches were the Indigenous inhabitants of the Canary Islands in the Atlantic Ocean some 100 kilometres (62 mi) west of Africa. Strictly, the name referred to the natives of Tenerife, but it is commonly applied to the aboriginal peoples of all seven islands. It is believed that they migrated to the archipelago around 1,000 BC or earlier, and genetic and linguistic evidence links them to the Berber peoples of North Africa.

Society and culture

Guanche society was organised in kingdoms (on Tenerife, nine menceyatos ruled by menceyes) with a stratified social order of nobles, priests, warriors, and commoners. They herded goats and sheep, grew barley and wheat, lived in caves and stone houses, and practised mummification of their dead — the Guanche mummies of Tenerife, preserved in lava caves, are comparable in technique to those of ancient Egypt. Their language, Guanche, is extinct but survives in place names and a few recorded words and inscriptions in a Libyco-Berber type script.

Conquest

The Castilian conquest of the islands unfolded over most of the fifteenth century (1402–1496); Tenerife, the last island to fall, resisted under leaders such as Bencomo until the battles of Acentejo and Aguere. Disease, warfare, enslavement, and assimilation sharply reduced the Indigenous population, though modern Canary Islanders retain substantial Guanche ancestry, and Guanche heritage is a prominent element of Canarian identity, celebrated in place names, folklore, and the survival of practices such as the whistled language Silbo Gomero of La Gomera (of pre-Hispanic origin in technique, applied to Spanish).
