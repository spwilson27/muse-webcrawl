Annus mirabilis

Annus mirabilis (pl. anni mirabiles) is a Latin phrase that means "marvelous year", "wonderful year" or "miraculous year". This term was originally used for the year 1666, and today it is applied to several years during which events of major importance are remembered.

1666

John Dryden published the poem Annus Mirabilis in 1667 about the events of 1666: despite the Great Fire of London and the plague, England won naval victories against the Dutch, which Dryden framed as signs of divine favour. The same period saw Isaac Newton's plague-year retreat to Woolsthorpe, during which he laid foundations of calculus, optics and gravitation.

1905: Einstein's annus mirabilis

In 1905 Albert Einstein published four groundbreaking papers — on the photoelectric effect, Brownian motion, special relativity, and mass–energy equivalence (E = mc²) — revolutionising physics. These are known as the Annus mirabilis papers.

Other uses

The phrase has also been applied to 1543 (Copernicus and Vesalius), 1759 (British victories in the Seven Years' War) and various years in sports and popular culture. Its antonym is annus horribilis.
