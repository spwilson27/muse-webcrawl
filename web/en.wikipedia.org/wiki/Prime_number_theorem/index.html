Prime number theorem

In mathematics, the prime number theorem (PNT) describes the asymptotic distribution of prime numbers among the positive integers. It formalises the intuitive idea that primes become less common as they become larger, by precisely quantifying the rate at which this occurs. The theorem states that the prime-counting function π(x), the number of primes less than or equal to x, satisfies

π(x) ~ x / ln x   as x → ∞,

meaning that the ratio of π(x) to x / ln x approaches 1. Equivalently, the n-th prime number pn satisfies pn ~ n ln n.

History

Based on tables of primes, Legendre and Gauss conjectured the result in the late 18th century (Gauss's form used the logarithmic integral li(x), a better approximation). Chebyshev proved in the 1850s that π(x) is of the correct order of magnitude and that if the limit existed it had to equal 1. In 1896, Jacques Hadamard and Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin independently proved the theorem using complex analysis, showing that the Riemann zeta function has no zeros on the line Re(s) = 1.

Elementary proofs

For decades it was believed that any proof required complex analysis, but in 1949 Atle Selberg and Paul Erdős published "elementary" proofs (using only real analysis, though intricate ones). An analytic proof via Tauberian theorems was given by D. J. Newman in 1980, notable for its brevity.

Error term and the Riemann hypothesis

The size of the error in the approximation is intimately connected with the zeros of the zeta function. The Riemann hypothesis is equivalent to the statement that π(x) − li(x) = O(√x log x), the strongest plausible error estimate. De la Vallée Poussin established a classical zero-free region giving an error of order x·exp(−c√(log x)).

Related results

Analogues of the theorem hold in other settings: the prime ideal theorem in algebraic number theory, and the distribution of irreducible polynomials over finite fields. Dirichlet's theorem has a corresponding "prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions".
