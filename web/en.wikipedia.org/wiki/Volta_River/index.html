Volta River

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Volta River
CountriesBurkina Faso and Ghana (basin also in Côte d'Ivoire, Mali, Togo, Benin)
Main tributariesBlack Volta, White Volta, Red Volta (named for their water colours)
LengthAbout 1,500–1,600 km (main stem, White Volta–Volta)
MouthGulf of Guinea at Ada, Ghana
Famous featureLake Volta — one of the largest artificial lakes in the world by surface area, formed by the Akosombo Dam (1965)

The Volta River is the major river system of West Africa, draining a basin of some 400,000 km² across six countries and flowing south through Ghana to the Gulf of Guinea. It forms where the Black Volta (Mouhoun), White Volta (Nakambé) and Red Volta — named for the colour of their waters in flood — converge on the Ghanaian plains; the Portuguese named the river Volta ("twist/turn") for its winding course, a name that also echoes in the former French colony of Upper Volta, now Burkina Faso, source of the Black and White branches.

Lake Volta and the Akosombo Dam

The river's modern story is dominated by a single structure: the Akosombo Dam (completed 1965), Kwame Nkrumah's flagship project, which dammed the Volta gorge to power an aluminium smelter and Ghana's industrialisation, flooding 8,502 km² — about 4% of Ghana's land area — to create Lake Volta, among the largest reservoirs on Earth by surface. The lake displaced some 80,000 people, created a vast inland fishery and transport artery, and left Ghana dependent on its rains: drought years (1983, 1998, 2007) repeatedly throttled the turbines and the economy. Upstream in Burkina Faso, smaller dams and gold-mining pollution press on the Black Volta; at the coast, the river's sediment-starved delta erodes at Ada and Keta. The Volta is thus West Africa's compact parable of development: one river feeding power, fish, farms and floods across a region of 25+ million basin residents, governed since 2007 by the Volta Basin Authority of its six states.
