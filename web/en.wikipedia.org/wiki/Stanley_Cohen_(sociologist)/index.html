Stanley Cohen (sociologist)

Born: 23 February 1942, Johannesburg, South Africa
Died: 7 January 2013 (aged 70), London, England
Known for: Moral panic theory; Folk Devils and Moral Panics (1972); States of Denial (2001)
Fields: Criminology, sociology
Institutions: University of Durham; Hebrew University of Jerusalem; London School of Economics

Stanley Cohen (23 February 1942 – 7 January 2013) was a South African-born British sociologist and criminologist, best known for his concept of the moral panic and for his later work on human rights denial. His 1972 book Folk Devils and Moral Panics, based on his doctoral research at the London School of Economics, became one of the most influential works in the sociology of deviance.

Early life and career

Cohen was born in Johannesburg, South Africa. He studied social work and sociology at the University of the Witwatersrand, where he became active in anti-apartheid politics. He emigrated to Britain in 1963. After completing his PhD at the LSE, he taught at the University of Durham from 1967, where he was involved in the radical criminology movement and was a founding figure of the National Deviancy Conference.

In 1980 Cohen moved to Israel, where he became a professor of criminology at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. There he became active in human rights work, including with the organisation B'Tselem, documenting abuses in the occupied territories. He returned to Britain in 1996 as Martin White Professor of Sociology at the LSE, a post he held until his death.

Moral panics and folk devils

In Folk Devils and Moral Panics: The Creation of the Mods and Rockers (1972), Cohen studied media and public reactions to disturbances involving the youth subcultures known as Mods and Rockers at English seaside resorts in the mid-1960s. He argued that the societal reaction was grossly disproportionate to the actual threat, and that through a process of exaggeration, distortion and symbolisation the media cast the groups as "folk devils" — visible reminders of what society should not be. His definition of a moral panic — a condition, episode, person or group that "emerges to become defined as a threat to societal values and interests" — became a standard reference point in media studies and criminology.

Later work

Cohen's Visions of Social Control (1985) examined the dispersal of disciplinary mechanisms beyond the prison into the community. States of Denial: Knowing about Atrocities and Suffering (2001) turned to the psychology and sociology of denial — how individuals, governments and societies know about, yet simultaneously fail to know about, atrocities and human suffering. The book applied his earlier concerns about social reaction to the field of human rights.

Legacy

Cohen was elected a Fellow of the British Academy in 2009. The concept of moral panic remains one of the most widely cited ideas in sociology, routinely applied to media coverage of crime, immigration, drugs and youth culture.
