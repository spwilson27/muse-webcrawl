Sapping

Sapping is a term used in siege warfare to describe the digging of a tunnel or trench — called a sap — toward or beneath an enemy fortification, with the aim of undermining its walls, placing explosive charges, or approaching the defences under cover. The soldiers who performed this dangerous work were known as sappers, a term that survives today as the name of military combat engineers in many armies.

History

Undermining was practised from ancient times: besiegers dug galleries beneath city walls, propping the tunnel with timber that was later burned so the wall above collapsed into the void. Assyrian, Greek and Roman armies all employed mining and counter-mining. In medieval warfare the technique was used against castles, notably at sieges such as Acre and Rochester, where King John's miners famously brought down a corner of the keep in 1215 using pig fat to fuel the fire that burned the props.

Early modern and Vauban

With the rise of artillery fortresses, sapping changed character. The great French engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban (1633–1707) systematised siege approaches: zigzag trenches called saps were pushed toward the fortress under cover of gabions, fascines and parapets, punctuated by parallel trenches that ringed the target. The sap roller — a large protective wicker cylinder — shielded the lead sapper as the trench crept forward.

Later use

Sapping remained central to siege and trench warfare into the First World War, where Russian saps and short approach trenches were driven into no man's land. Modern combat engineers retain the title "sapper" in British, Commonwealth, French (sapeur) and other armies, though their role now spans demolition, bridging, mine clearance and fortification.
