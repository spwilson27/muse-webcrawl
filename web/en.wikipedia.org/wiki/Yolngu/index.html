Yolngu

The Yolngu (also Yolŋu) are an Aboriginal Australian people of Arnhem Land in the Northern Territory of Australia. "Yolngu" means "person" in the Yolngu languages, and the term came into general use when the Yolngu adopted it for themselves in the mid-20th century. Their population is roughly 6,000–7,000, centred on communities including Yirrkala, Galiwin'ku (Elcho Island), Milingimbi, Ramingining, Maningrida, and Gapuwiyak.

Society and law

Yolngu society is organized into two exogamous moieties, Dhuwa and Yirritja, which partition people, clan estates, languages, and the natural and spiritual worlds; marriage and ceremonial responsibilities are structured by moiety and by an eight-term skin-name (subsection) system. Land and sea country is held by patrilineal clans (bäpurru) under Yolngu law, known as Madayin, which governs ceremony, sacred objects, designs (miny'tji), song cycles, and resource management. The Yolngu maintain one of the oldest continuous cultures in the world, with rock art in the region dating back tens of thousands of years, and their society historically traded with Macassan trepangers from Sulawesi.

Land rights history

The Yolngu played a foundational role in the Australian Aboriginal land rights movement. In 1963, protesting a mining lease on the Gove Peninsula, Yolngu leaders sent the Yirrkala bark petitions — typed documents mounted on bark paintings — to the Australian Parliament, the first traditional documents recognized by the Parliament. When the subsequent Gove land rights case (1971) ruled against them under the doctrine of terra nullius, the campaign continued, leading to the Aboriginal Land Rights (Northern Territory) Act 1976. In 2008 the Federal Court's Blue Mud Bay decision recognized Yolngu ownership of most of the intertidal zone in their sea country.

Culture

The Yolngu are renowned for bark painting, the didgeridoo (yiḏaki), of which they are the traditional custodians and masters, and music through the band Yothu Yindi, whose 1991 hit "Treaty" brought Yolngu concerns to national prominence. The annual Garma Festival at Gulkula, hosted by the Yothu Yindi Foundation, is a major forum for Indigenous culture and policy.
