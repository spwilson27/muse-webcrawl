Islamic law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Islamic law — in its broad sense sharia, the divinely ordained path, and in its juristic sense fiqh, human understanding of it — is the religious law forming part of the Islamic tradition. It derives from the Quran and the Sunnah (the example of the Prophet Muhammad, preserved in hadith), elaborated through consensus (ijma) and analogical reasoning (qiyas), with further methods varying by school and era.

Development and schools

Classical jurisprudence crystallised between the 8th and 10th centuries in the legal schools (madhhabs): Hanafi (the most widespread), Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali among Sunnis, and Ja'fari among Twelver Shia, plus minority traditions. Fiqh covers worship, family law (marriage, divorce, inheritance), commerce, crime and governance, classifying acts from obligatory to forbidden. Pre-modern Islamic societies administered law through qadi courts, muftis' fatwas and rulers' supplementary justice, in legal pluralism with local custom.

Modern application

Colonialism and nation-states transformed the field: most Muslim-majority states adopted European-style codes for commerce and crime while retaining Islamic family law in varying degrees; a few (Saudi Arabia, Iran) claim comprehensive sharia-based systems, and movements advocating "sharia implementation" are politically contested within Muslim societies themselves. Islamic finance — structured to avoid interest (riba) and excessive uncertainty — is a major global industry. Scholarly debate distinguishes historical sharia as a moral-juristic tradition from modern codified versions, and examines compatibility and tension with constitutionalism and human-rights law.
