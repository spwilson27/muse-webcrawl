Decentralised social network

A decentralised social network (spelled decentralized social network in American English) is an online social networking service implemented on a distributed or federated architecture, in which no single company or central server controls the whole network. Instead, many independently operated servers, often run by volunteers, organisations, or the users themselves, interoperate using open protocols so that their members can communicate across server boundaries.

Overview

Decentralised social networks arose as an alternative to centralised platforms such as Facebook, Twitter (now X), and Instagram, whose operators control user data, moderation policy, and the algorithms that shape what users see. In a decentralised or federated network, each server (often called an "instance") sets its own rules and moderation, while open protocols let posts, follows, and messages flow between servers. Users can choose a server whose policies they trust, or run their own, and can usually migrate between servers while retaining their social connections.

Protocols and software

  • ActivityPub — a W3C standard protocol (2018) used by Mastodon, Pixelfed, PeerTube, and many other services, collectively often called the "Fediverse".
  • OStatus — an earlier federation protocol used by GNU social and early Mastodon versions.
  • Diaspora — a decentralised network launched in 2010 using its own protocol, organised around user-run "pods".
  • AT Protocol — the protocol behind Bluesky, designed for account portability and composable moderation.
  • Nostr — a protocol based on cryptographic keypairs and simple relays.
  • Matrix and XMPP — federated protocols used mainly for messaging that also support social features.

Advantages and challenges

Advocates argue decentralisation improves resilience against censorship and single points of failure, gives communities control over moderation, and reduces dependence on advertising-driven business models. Critics note challenges including fragmented user experience, difficulty discovering content across servers, inconsistent moderation that can allow harmful communities to persist, and the burden placed on volunteer server administrators. The model also complicates account recovery and identity verification.

History

Early internet social systems such as email, Usenet, and IRC were inherently federated or decentralised. The idea returned to prominence in the 2010s with Diaspora, and gained wider attention after 2016 with Mastodon, and again in 2022–2023 when changes at Twitter prompted large migrations to Mastodon and Bluesky, and Meta's Threads began experimenting with ActivityPub federation.
