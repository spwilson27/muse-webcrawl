The Sultanate of Adal (Somali: Saldanadda Cadal; Harari: ኣዳል) was a medieval Sunni Muslim sultanate in the Horn of Africa, centred on the region of the modern-day Somali Region of Ethiopia, Djibouti and the northwestern part of Somalia. Emerging from the earlier Sultanate of Ifat in the early 15th century, Adal became the principal Muslim power in the Horn and a long-standing rival of the Christian Ethiopian Empire.

History

Adal arose as the Walashma dynasty, formerly rulers of Ifat, re-established their power eastward after Ethiopian expansion pushed them from the Shewa plateau. Its capital moved from Zeilah on the Red Sea coast to the inland city of Harar, which became a major centre of Islamic learning and trade under Adal's rule.

Under Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Ahmed Gurey"), Adal launched a sweeping campaign of conquest against Ethiopia beginning in 1529, winning victories such as the Battle of Shimbra Kure and overrunning much of the Ethiopian highlands by the mid-1530s. The intervention of a Portuguese expedition under Cristóvão da Gama, and Ahmad's death at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543, reversed Adal's fortunes. His successor, Emir Nur ibn Mujahid, consolidated the sultanate around Harar and built the city's famous defensive wall.

Weakened by conflict and by the migrations of the Oromo peoples into its territory, the sultanate declined in the later 16th century; in 1577 its remnant was succeeded by the Emirate of Harar.

Trade and society

Through the port of Zeilah, Adal controlled trade routes linking the Ethiopian interior to Arabia and the wider Indian Ocean world, exporting ivory, gold, slaves, myrrh and frankincense in exchange for textiles and manufactured goods. Its population drew on Somali, Harari, Afar and other peoples of the Horn, and its armies were famed for their cavalry and archers.
