Perseus (constellation)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Perseus is a constellation in the northern sky, named after the Greek mythological hero Perseus, who slew the Gorgon Medusa and rescued Andromeda. It is one of the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer Ptolemy and remains one of the 88 modern constellations. It lies between Cassiopeia and Auriga, crossed by the plane of the Milky Way, and is best seen in northern winter evenings.

Notable stars

The brightest star is Mirfak (Alpha Persei), a yellow-white supergiant of magnitude 1.8 lying about 510 light-years away, surrounded by the Alpha Persei Cluster. Algol (Beta Persei) is the prototype of the eclipsing binary stars: every 2.87 days the brighter star is partially eclipsed by its companion and the brightness drops from magnitude 2.1 to 3.4 for about ten hours. Its name derives from the Arabic raʾs al-ghūl, "the Demon's Head", as it represents the eye of Medusa.

Deep-sky objects

Perseus contains the Double Cluster (NGC 869 and NGC 884), a pair of open clusters visible to the naked eye; the California Nebula (NGC 1499); the Little Dumbbell Nebula (M76); and the immense Perseus Cluster of galaxies (Abell 426), one of the most massive objects in the nearby universe. The radiant of the Perseid meteor shower, peaking around 12–13 August each year, lies in Perseus; the shower is produced by debris from comet Swift–Tuttle. In 1901 the bright nova GK Persei erupted in the constellation.

Mythology

In Greek myth Perseus, son of Zeus and Danaë, beheaded Medusa using a mirrored shield, rescued Andromeda from the sea-monster Cetus, and married her. The surrounding constellations — Andromeda, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Cetus and Pegasus — preserve the whole cast of the legend.
