Bloody Sunday (1972)

Date30 January 1972
LocationBogside, Derry, Northern Ireland
Deaths14
InjuredAt least 13

Bloody Sunday, sometimes called the Bogside Massacre, was a massacre on 30 January 1972 in the Bogside area of Derry, Northern Ireland, during the Troubles. Soldiers of the British Army's Parachute Regiment shot 26 unarmed civilians during a protest march organised by the Northern Ireland Civil Rights Association against internment without trial. Thirteen men were killed outright and a fourteenth died of his wounds four months later. Many of the victims were shot while fleeing or while tending the wounded.

Background

The march had been banned, and the Army planned to arrest rioters who customarily attacked troops at the barricades after marches. Soldiers of 1 Para opened fire in the Rossville Street and Glenfada Park areas. The Army initially claimed its soldiers had fired on gunmen and bomb-throwers; the hastily convened Widgery Tribunal of April 1972 largely accepted the soldiers' accounts, exonerating them and casting suspicion on some victims — findings long rejected by the victims' families and nationalist opinion.

Saville Inquiry

The Saville Inquiry, established in 1998, sat for twelve years and reported on 15 June 2010. It concluded that the killings were "unjustified and unjustifiable": none of those shot was in possession of a firearm, soldiers had fired first, and some soldiers had knowingly put forward false accounts. Prime Minister David Cameron formally apologised in the House of Commons on the day of publication, describing the events as "both unjustified and unjustifiable".

Legacy

Bloody Sunday became one of the most significant events of the Troubles, galvanising nationalist opinion and greatly increasing recruitment to the Provisional IRA. It is commemorated annually in Derry, and the Museum of Free Derry preserves its history. The U2 song "Sunday Bloody Sunday" references the event.
