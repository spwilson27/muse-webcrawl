Elliptic integral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In integral calculus, an elliptic integral is one of a class of functions defined as integrals that cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions. They arose originally from the problem of computing the arc length of an ellipse — hence the name — and from the period of a pendulum. Elliptic integrals were studied intensively in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries by Fagnano, Euler, Legendre, Abel, and Jacobi, and their inversion led to the theory of elliptic functions.

Standard forms

Legendre showed that every elliptic integral can be reduced to a combination of elementary functions and three standard kinds, written in terms of the amplitude φ and the parameter m = k2:

  • First kind: F(φ | m) = ∫ (1 − m sin²θ)−1/2 dθ
  • Second kind: E(φ | m) = ∫ (1 − m sin²θ)1/2 dθ
  • Third kind: Π(n; φ | m), involving an additional parameter n

When φ = π/2 the integrals are called complete and are written K(m) and E(m). The complete integral of the first kind gives the period of oscillation and appears in the formula for the circumference of an ellipse together with the second kind: C = 4a·E(m), where a is the semi-major axis.

History

Giulio Fagnano discovered curious identities among these integrals in the early 1700s, prompting Euler to develop a general addition theorem. Adrien-Marie Legendre devoted forty years to classifying them and compiling tables. In the 1820s Niels Henrik Abel and Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi independently inverted the integrals, creating elliptic functions — doubly periodic generalisations of the trigonometric functions — and transforming the subject.

Computation and applications

Elliptic integrals are efficiently evaluated using the arithmetic-geometric mean and Landen transformations, and appear wherever an inverse-square or quartic structure occurs: the exact period of a simple pendulum, geodesics on an ellipsoid, the magnetic field of a current loop, and the deflection of light in some relativistic models.
