Alan Wilson Watts (6 January 1915 – 16 November 1973) was a British-American philosopher, writer and speaker known for interpreting and popularising Eastern philosophies — particularly Zen Buddhism, Taoism and Hinduism — for Western audiences. A former Anglican priest, he wrote more than 25 books, beginning with The Spirit of Zen (1936) at the age of twenty-one, and became a beloved lecturer on radio and television during the 1950s and 1960s counterculture.

Life

Watts was born in Chislehurst, Kent, and educated at King's School, Canterbury. Drawn early to Asian art and religion, he joined the Buddhist Lodge in London as a teenager and moved to the United States in 1938, training briefly for the Zen life in New York before studying theology and being ordained an Episcopal priest in 1945. He left the priesthood in 1950, moved to California, and taught at the American Academy of Asian Studies in San Francisco, where he built a wide following through his lectures on KPFA radio in Berkeley.

Work and ideas

In books such as The Wisdom of Insecurity (1951), The Way of Zen (1957), Psychotherapy East and West (1961) and The Book: On the Taboo Against Knowing Who You Are (1966), Watts argued that the sense of a separate ego is an illusion, that human beings are continuous with the universe rather than strangers in it, and that anxiety arises from trying to secure a self that cannot be secured. He presented Zen, in an accessible and witty style, as a liberation from conceptual fixation — "a finger pointing at the moon", not the moon itself.

Watts was associated with the Beat and hippie generations, experimented with psychedelics (writing The Joyous Cosmology in 1962), and lived his later years between a houseboat in Sausalito and a cabin on Mount Tamalpais, where he died in 1973.

Legacy

Recordings of Watts's lectures have enjoyed a large afterlife: they are broadcast by community radio, sampled in electronic music and film scores, and streamed widely online, making him one of the most listened-to philosophical voices of the internet age. Scholars credit him with a central role in the Western reception of Zen, while noting his interpretations were personal and unsystematic by design.
