Abscess

An abscess is a localised collection of pus within body tissue, usually caused by a bacterial infection. Pus consists of dead and living white blood cells, bacteria, and tissue debris. Abscesses can develop almost anywhere in the body — commonly in the skin and soft tissues, but also in organs such as the liver, lungs, brain and teeth — and typically present with pain, redness, warmth, swelling and sometimes fever.

Causes and pathophysiology

Most abscesses are caused by bacteria, especially Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA strains), entering through a break in the skin or spreading from a nearby infection. The immune response walls off the infection with a capsule of inflamed tissue, which limits spread but also prevents antibiotics from penetrating well. Risk factors include diabetes, immunosuppression, intravenous drug use and chronic skin conditions.

Diagnosis

Skin abscesses are usually diagnosed clinically by a tender, fluctuant swelling. Ultrasound can confirm a fluid collection and guide drainage; CT or MRI is used for deep or internal abscesses, such as peritonsillar, dental, intra-abdominal, liver or brain abscesses.

Treatment

The mainstay of treatment is incision and drainage: the cavity is opened, pus evacuated, and the wound often left open or packed to heal by secondary intention. Antibiotics alone are usually insufficient for larger collections but are added when there is systemic illness, extensive cellulitis, deep location, or immune compromise. Undrained abscesses may rupture spontaneously, form fistulae, or seed infection to the bloodstream, causing sepsis.
