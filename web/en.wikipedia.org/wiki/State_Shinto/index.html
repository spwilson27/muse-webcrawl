State Shinto

State Shinto (国家神道, Kokka Shintō) was Imperial Japan's ideological use of the native Shinto traditions to promote national unity and emperor worship from the Meiji Restoration (1868) until the end of the Second World War.

State Shinto
Period: 1868–1945
Ended: Shinto Directive, 1945
Key institutions: Jinja Honcho predecessors, Yasukuni Shrine, Ise Grand Shrine

Development

The Meiji government separated Shinto from Buddhism (shinbutsu bunri), ranked shrines in a state hierarchy, and declared shrine rites civic rather than religious, allowing compulsory participation despite constitutional religious freedom. Schoolchildren venerated the Imperial Rescript on Education and the emperor's portrait.

Wartime

During the 1930s–40s State Shinto supported militarism and ultranationalism; Yasukuni Shrine enshrined the war dead as eirei. The Allied occupation's Shinto Directive (December 1945) disestablished state support; the 1947 Constitution separated religion and state.

Debate

Scholars debate whether State Shinto was a religion, an invented tradition, or a set of civic rituals; the term itself is contested.
