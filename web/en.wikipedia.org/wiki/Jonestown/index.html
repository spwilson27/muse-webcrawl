Jonestown

Jonestown was the informal name of the Peoples Temple Agricultural Project, a remote settlement in the jungle of northwestern Guyana established by the Peoples Temple, an American religious movement led by Jim Jones. On November 18, 1978, a total of 918 people died at the settlement, at a nearby airstrip, and in the Guyanese capital of Georgetown — the greatest single loss of American civilian life in a deliberate act until the September 11 attacks.

Background

Jim Jones founded the Peoples Temple in Indiana in the 1950s and moved it to California, where it attracted a large, racially integrated membership with a message combining Christian teaching, socialism, and social activism. Facing mounting scrutiny over allegations of abuse and financial misconduct, Jones leased land from the government of Guyana and promoted Jonestown as a utopian socialist community; members began relocating there in 1977. By 1978 the settlement housed more than 900 people under increasingly authoritarian conditions, with residents subjected to long workdays, armed guards, and rehearsed "White Night" suicide drills.

November 18, 1978

U.S. Congressman Leo Ryan visited Jonestown in November 1978 to investigate reports that residents were being held against their will. As Ryan's party and a group of defectors departed from the Port Kaituma airstrip, Temple gunmen opened fire, killing Ryan, three journalists, and one defector. Back at the settlement, Jones ordered a mass murder-suicide: residents drank a grape-flavoured drink laced with cyanide and sedatives. 909 people died at Jonestown itself, including Jones and more than 300 children; others died in related incidents in Georgetown and at the airstrip, bringing the total to 918.

Aftermath and legacy

The events provoked horror and intense debate about cults, charismatic authority, and the vulnerability of the Temple's largely poor and Black membership. Jonestown gave rise to the idiom "drinking the Kool-Aid" (the drink was actually Flavor Aid), referring to unquestioning obedience — an expression survivors and victims' families have criticised as trivialising. The site was largely reclaimed by jungle, and memorials stand at the Evergreen Cemetery in Oakland, California, where many victims are buried.
