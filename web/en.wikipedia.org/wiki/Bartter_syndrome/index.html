Bartter syndrome

Bartter syndrome is a rare inherited disease characterised by a defect in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle in the kidney. It results in renal wasting of sodium, potassium and chloride, producing hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis, low or normal blood pressure despite raised renin and aldosterone, and often hypercalciuria.

Types and genetics

The condition is caused by mutations in genes encoding ion transporters of the loop of Henle, including the Na–K–2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2, type 1), the ROMK potassium channel (type 2), the ClC-Kb chloride channel (type 3, the classic form), and barttin (type 4, associated with sensorineural deafness). Inheritance is autosomal recessive.

Signs and treatment

Severe (antenatal) forms present with polyhydramnios, premature birth, excessive urination, salt craving and growth failure; milder classic forms may present in childhood with muscle weakness and cramps. Management centres on potassium and magnesium supplementation, salt and water replacement, and prostaglandin synthesis inhibitors such as indomethacin. The related condition Gitelman syndrome involves the distal convoluted tubule instead and typically presents later with hypocalciuria.

The syndrome is named after American endocrinologist Frederic Bartter, who described it in 1962.
