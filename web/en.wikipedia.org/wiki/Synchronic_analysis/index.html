Synchronic analysis

Synchronic analysis is the study of a phenomenon — most often a language — as it exists at a particular point in time, without reference to its historical development. The term is contrasted with diachronic analysis, which studies change over time. The distinction was introduced by the Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure and became foundational to structural linguistics.

Origins

In his posthumously published Course in General Linguistics (1916), Saussure argued that a language should be studied as a complete system of interrelated signs at a given moment — the langue of a speech community — rather than only through the history of its individual elements. A synchronic description captures the rules and oppositions that are operative for speakers at that time, regardless of etymology.

Applications

Synchronic approaches underlie most modern descriptive linguistics: grammars of contemporary languages, phonemic inventories, and corpora sampled from a single period are all synchronic. The distinction is also used in anthropology, literary studies, and other fields that contrast a "snapshot" of a system with its evolution.
