Judaea

Judaea (Latin: Iudaea; Greek: Ἰουδαία, Ioudaía) was a Roman province from 6 to 132 AD, formed from the former Herodian kingdom in the southern Levant, encompassing the regions of Judea proper, Samaria, and Idumaea, with its capital first at Caesarea Maritima and later Jerusalem. After the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 AD) it was merged into Syria Palaestina.

History

After the deposition of Herod Archelaus in 6 AD, Judaea came under direct Roman rule, governed by prefects (later procurators) of equestrian rank subordinate to the legate of Syria. The Census of Quirinius that year provoked resistance led by Judas the Galilean. Pontius Pilate, prefect 26–36 AD, presided over the trial and crucifixion of Jesus.

Tensions between Jewish inhabitants, Hellenised cities, and Roman governors erupted in the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 AD), ending with the destruction of the Second Temple in Jerusalem by Titus in 70 AD and the fall of Masada. A brief restoration of fuller provincial status with a senatorial legate followed the Kitos War and unrest.

Bar Kokhba revolt and aftermath

Hadrian's founding of Aelia Capitolina on Jerusalem's site and bans on Jewish practice sparked the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135). Its suppression devastated Judea's Jewish population; Hadrian renamed the province Syria Palaestina and barred Jews from Jerusalem except on Tisha B'Av.
