Alternative education

Alternative education encompasses many pedagogical approaches differing from mainstream or traditional education. Such approaches may be found in public, private, charter, independent or home-based settings, and are often characterized by a strong emphasis on the individual needs of learners, smaller learning communities, flexible curricula, and non-traditional methods of assessment.

The term covers two broad families of provision. The first consists of schools founded on distinctive educational philosophies, such as Montessori, Waldorf (Steiner), and democratic or free schools such as Summerhill. The second consists of programs intended for students for whom the conventional school setting has not worked — for example students at risk of dropping out, those with behavioural difficulties, or those who have been expelled — where "alternative" provision may offer smaller classes, counselling and individually paced credit recovery.

History

Alternative education has roots in the progressive education movement of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, associated with thinkers such as John Dewey, who argued that learning should be grounded in experience and the interests of the child. Maria Montessori opened her first Casa dei Bambini in Rome in 1907; Rudolf Steiner founded the first Waldorf school in Stuttgart in 1919; and A. S. Neill founded Summerhill in 1921 on principles of self-government by pupils and staff.

Philosophical approaches

Common threads in alternative approaches include respect for the learner's autonomy, learning through projects and experience rather than direct instruction alone, mixed-age grouping, narrative or portfolio assessment instead of grades, and governance structures that involve students. Homeschooling and its more radical variant, unschooling, place these choices within the family rather than an institution.

Provision for at-risk students

In the United States and other systems, public alternative schools and programs serve students whose needs are not met in regular schools. Research suggests small size, caring relationships with teachers, and flexibility are their most consistently valued features, though provision and quality vary widely between districts.

Criticism and evaluation

Critics of philosophy-based alternative schools question the evidence for some methods and note that such schools may disproportionately serve affluent families. Critics of at-risk provision warn that alternative placements can become a low-expectations track unless resourcing and pathways back to mainstream qualifications are protected.
