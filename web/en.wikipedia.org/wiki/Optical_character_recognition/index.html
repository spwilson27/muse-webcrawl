Optical character recognition

Optical character recognition or optical character reader (OCR) is the electronic or mechanical conversion of images of typed, handwritten or printed text into machine-encoded text, whether from a scanned document, a photo of a document, a scene photo or subtitle text superimposed on an image.

Process

OCR pipelines typically perform preprocessing (deskewing, binarisation, noise removal), layout analysis to segment the page into text regions, character segmentation and recognition, and post-processing using dictionaries or language models to correct errors. Early systems worked by matching glyphs against stored templates or by feature analysis, and required training for each typeface.

Modern systems

Contemporary engines use neural networks — convolutional networks for image features and recurrent or transformer models for sequence modelling — and handle many scripts and handwriting. Widely used systems include the open-source Tesseract engine, originally developed at Hewlett-Packard and later by Google, and ABBYY FineReader. Accuracy on clean printed text routinely exceeds 99%, while handwriting, degraded historical print and complex layouts remain harder.

Applications

OCR is used for digitising books and archives (as in Google Books and library digitisation projects), extracting data from invoices and forms, automatic number-plate recognition, converting scanned documents into searchable PDFs, reading assistance for the blind, and decoding cheques and postal addresses.

History

Precursors date to the 1910s–1920s reading machines for the blind; the first commercial OCR systems appeared in the 1950s–1960s, standardised typefaces such as OCR-A (1966) were designed for machine readability, and Ray Kurzweil's 1976 omni-font reading machine was a landmark in assistive technology.
