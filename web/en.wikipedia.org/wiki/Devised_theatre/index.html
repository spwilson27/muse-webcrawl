Devised theatre

Devised theatre — frequently called collective creation when the process is shared — is a method of theatre-making in which the script originates not from a single playwright but from the collaborative work of an ensemble, typically led by a director or facilitator. The final performance text emerges from improvisation, discussion, physical exploration and research during rehearsals.

History

Devising grew out of the experimental theatre of the 1960s and 1970s, when companies such as the Living Theatre, the Open Theater, Théâtre du Soleil and Joint Stock sought alternatives to the hierarchy of playwright, director and actor. In Britain, groups like Monstrous Regiment and, later, Complicité and Frantic Assembly made devising a mainstream practice. The approach also became central to theatre in education and applied theatre.

Process

A devising process usually begins with a stimulus — an image, a historical event, a piece of music — rather than a script. Performers generate material through improvisation, games and tasks; the company then selects, structures and refines this material, often with a writer or dramaturg shaping the emerging text. Authorship is consequently shared, which can complicate copyright and credit.
