Do not resuscitate

Do not resuscitate (DNR), also called do not attempt resuscitation (DNAR) or allow natural death, is a medical order, written by a physician on the basis of a patient's wishes or best interests, instructing healthcare providers not to perform cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) if the patient's heart stops or breathing ceases. A DNR order concerns only resuscitation: it does not withdraw other treatments such as ventilation, medication, nutrition, or comfort care, though in practice the scope is sometimes misunderstood.

Background

CPR, developed in the 1960s, became the default response to cardiac arrest in hospitals, applied to nearly every dying patient unless specified otherwise. From the 1970s onward, hospitals formalised "no code" decisions into DNR orders, and legal frameworks developed around advance directives, living wills, and durable powers of attorney for healthcare. In the United States, the Patient Self-Determination Act of 1990 required institutions to inform patients of their rights to make such decisions. Out-of-hospital and portable forms (such as POLST/MOLST orders) extend the instruction to paramedics and community settings; some patients wear DNR bracelets or, controversially, tattoos.

Decision-making and ethics

Orders are normally made after discussion between physician, patient and family, considering the very low survival rates of CPR in advanced illness, frailty or metastatic disease, and the burdens of the procedure (fractured ribs, brain injury, prolonged intensive care). Where a patient lacks capacity, decisions follow advance statements or the views of surrogates and best-interests assessments, with rules varying by jurisdiction. Debates persist about "slow codes", unilateral DNR decisions, and ensuring that a DNR is not misread as "do not treat".
