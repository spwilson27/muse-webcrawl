Central heating

Central heating
[Image: A domestic gas boiler and radiators in a central heating system]
Heat sourceBoiler, furnace, or heat pump
DistributionWater, steam, or air
EmittersRadiators, underfloor pipes, convectors, ducts

Central heating is a system for distributing heat throughout a building from a single central source, such as a furnace, boiler or heat pump, in contrast to heating each room with separate stoves or fires. It is usually combined with other systems to control the building climate and may form part of an HVAC (heating, ventilation and air conditioning) installation.

History

The ancient Romans used the hypocaust, in which hot air from a furnace circulated under floors and through walls. Korean ondol systems similarly heated floors with flue gases. Modern central heating developed in the 19th century: steam systems were installed in mills and public buildings, and hot-water radiator systems, pioneered by engineers such as Franz San Galli in Russia in the 1850s, spread through European and North American homes in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

Types

Wet systems circulate hot water from a boiler through radiators or underfloor heating loops, the dominant form in much of Europe. Steam systems, common in older North American buildings and district heating, distribute steam that condenses in radiators. Warm-air systems use a furnace and ductwork, often combined with air conditioning. District heating supplies heat to many buildings from a central plant, frequently using combined heat and power.

Energy sources and efficiency

Boilers burn natural gas, oil, biomass or coal; condensing boilers recover latent heat from flue gases and exceed 90% efficiency. Electric heat pumps, including ground-source systems, are increasingly favoured for their low carbon emissions. Controls such as thermostats, thermostatic radiator valves, weather compensation and zoning improve efficiency and comfort.
