L-DOPA

L-DOPA, also known as levodopa and L-3,4-dihydroxyphenylalanine, is an amino acid that is made and used as part of the normal biology of some plants and animals, including humans. Humans, as well as some other animals, make L-DOPA from the amino acid L-tyrosine via the enzyme tyrosine hydroxylase. It is the precursor to the neurotransmitters dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine, collectively known as catecholamines.

Aside from its natural and essential biological role, L-DOPA is also used in the clinical treatment of Parkinson's disease and dystonia. Levodopa is sold under many brand names in combination with a dopa decarboxylase inhibitor such as carbidopa (as co-careldopa, e.g. Sinemet) or benserazide (as co-beneldopa, e.g. Madopar).

Medical use

L-DOPA crosses the protective blood–brain barrier, whereas dopamine itself cannot. Once inside the brain it is converted to dopamine by the enzyme aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase, replenishing the dopaminergic deficit of Parkinson's disease. To prevent its breakdown in the periphery — which causes nausea and other side effects — it is almost always co-administered with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor that does not cross the blood–brain barrier.

Side effects

Common adverse effects include nausea, orthostatic hypotension, somnolence and, with long-term use, motor complications such as dyskinesias and "on–off" fluctuations in response. Psychiatric effects including hallucinations and impulse-control disorders may also occur.

History

L-DOPA was first isolated from the broad bean (Vicia faba) by Markus Guggenheim in 1913. Its role in Parkinson's disease emerged from the work of Arvid Carlsson in the 1950s, who demonstrated that it reversed reserpine-induced akinesia in animals; clinical application followed in the 1960s through the work of George Cotzias and others, transforming the treatment of Parkinson's disease.
