Balance of trade

[Image: loaded export containers being lifted onto a ship at a trade port]

The balance of trade is the difference between the value of a country's exports and imports of goods (and sometimes services) over a period. A country with exports exceeding imports runs a trade surplus; the reverse is a trade deficit. It is the largest component of the current account in the balance of payments.

Patterns and politics

Persistent surpluses (China, Germany, oil exporters) mirror persistent deficits (notably the United States, whose goods deficit exceeds a trillion dollars a year), the two being accounting counterparts connected by capital flows: a deficit country must borrow or sell assets to foreigners. Mercantilists treated surpluses as the aim of policy; modern economists treat the bilateral balance as meaningless and the overall balance as a symptom — of savings, investment and exchange rates — rather than a scorecard, though trade balances remain politically charged and targets of tariff campaigns. Services and income flows mean overall current accounts can diverge sharply from the goods balance (the UK and US earn large services and investment-income credits).
