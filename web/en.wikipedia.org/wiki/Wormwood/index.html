Wormwood

Scientific nameArtemisia absinthium
FamilyAsteraceae
Native rangeTemperate Europe, Asia, and North Africa
Key compoundThujone; absinthin (bitter principle)
UsesFlavoring of absinthe and vermouth; traditional medicine

Wormwood (Artemisia absinthium) is a herbaceous perennial plant in the aster family, native to temperate regions of Eurasia and North Africa and widely naturalized elsewhere. It is renowned as an ingredient of the spirit absinthe and as one of the most bitter substances known, owing to the compound absinthin.

Description

The plant grows to about a meter tall, with spirally arranged silvery-green leaves covered in fine silky hairs, and small pale-yellow flowers appearing in summer. All parts are intensely bitter. It thrives in dry, uncultivated ground.

Uses

Wormwood has a long history in herbal medicine, where it was used against intestinal worms — hence its English name — and as a digestive bitter. In the drinks trade it flavors absinthe, vermouth, and some other spirits and aperitifs. Its essential oil contains thujone, a neurotoxin in concentrated doses, which led to restrictions on absinthe in the early 20th century; modern regulations limit thujone content in beverages.

In culture

"Wormwood" appears in the Bible as a symbol of bitterness and calamity, including the star named Wormwood in the Book of Revelation. The Ukrainian name of the town of Chernobyl is commonly said to derive from a local word for a related Artemisia species, a coincidence widely noted after the 1986 disaster. In literature, wormwood symbolizes bitterness and regret, as in Hamlet's aside "Wormwood, wormwood."
