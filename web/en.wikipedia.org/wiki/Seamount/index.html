A seamount is a large submarine landform that rises at least 1,000 m (3,300 ft) from the surrounding seafloor, typically of volcanic origin, whose summit does not reach the ocean surface. Seamounts are found in every ocean basin and are most abundant in the Pacific Ocean. Estimates suggest there are more than 100,000 seamounts worldwide, but only a small fraction have been explored. Flat-topped seamounts whose summits have subsided below sea level are called guyots.

Seamounts form principally through volcanism, commonly near mid-ocean ridges or above mantle hotspots, where chains such as the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain record the motion of a tectonic plate over a stationary plume. They tend to be steep-sided cones rising from abyssal depths, and some — such as Loihi, off Hawaii — are still growing and may eventually emerge as islands.

Ecology

Seamounts are biological hotspots. Their slopes accelerate currents and induce local upwelling, concentrating phytoplankton and supporting deep-sea coral and sponge gardens, dense aggregations of fish such as orange roughy, and visiting pelagic predators, sharks and marine mammals. Many seamount species are endemic and slow-growing, making these ecosystems vulnerable to deep-sea trawling and to prospective seabed mining for cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts.

Exploration

Because of their depth, seamounts were largely mapped from shipboard echo sounders and satellite altimetry; the number catalogued from satellite data runs into the tens of thousands, and new mapping campaigns continue to add thousands more. Despite their abundance, fewer than one per cent have been sampled or surveyed in detail by submersibles or remotely operated vehicles.
