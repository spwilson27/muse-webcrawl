Loco moco is a dish featured in contemporary Hawaiian cuisine. In its classic form it consists of white rice topped with a hamburger patty and a fried egg, smothered in brown gravy. It is a staple of Hawaii's plate-lunch culture and diners throughout the islands, and numerous variations replace or supplement the hamburger with Spam, bacon, kalua pork, teriyaki beef or chicken, Portuguese sausage, or seafood.

History

The dish is generally credited to the Lincoln Grill in Hilo, Hawaii, where in 1949 proprietors Richard and Nancy Inouye created it for a group of hungry teenage regulars — members of a local sports club called the Lincoln Wreckers — who wanted something cheap, filling and different from a sandwich. According to the usual account, one of the boys, George Okimoto, had the nickname "Crazy" (loco in Spanish), and "moco" was added because it rhymed (and, in Spanish slang, means snot — a joke among the teenagers). The boys were charged about 25 cents for the original bowl of rice, hamburger patty and gravy; the egg was added later.

Popularity

From Hilo the loco moco spread across Hawaii and then to the US mainland and Japan, becoming one of the most recognisable dishes of modern Hawaiian cooking alongside the plate lunch and Spam musubi. It appears on menus from drive-ins to upscale restaurants, and competitive and novelty versions pile on multiple patties and eggs.

Variations

Variations include the use of mushroom or onion gravy, fried rice in place of plain rice, and alternative proteins such as mahi-mahi, shrimp, or chicken katsu. Some restaurants serve a "super" or "sumo" loco with several patties and eggs. The dish is commonly eaten with macaroni salad as part of a plate lunch.
