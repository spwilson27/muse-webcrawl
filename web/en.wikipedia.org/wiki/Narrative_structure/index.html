Narrative structure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Narrative structure is the structural framework underlying the order and manner in which a narrative is presented to a reader, listener, or viewer. It encompasses the organisation of a story's events — its plot — into patterns such as acts, chapters, and episodes, and the relation between the sequence of events as they happened (the fabula or story) and the sequence in which they are told (the syuzhet or discourse).

Classical models

Aristotle's Poetics held that a whole plot has a beginning, a middle, and an end, unified by causal connection. The German novelist Gustav Freytag analysed the five-part structure of classical drama — exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and dénouement — in a model commonly drawn as Freytag's pyramid. In the twentieth century, Vladimir Propp's morphology of folktales identified 31 narrative functions, Tzvetan Todorov proposed an equilibrium–disruption–restoration cycle, and Joseph Campbell popularised the hero's journey or monomyth.

Common frameworks

The three-act structure (setup, confrontation, resolution), championed in screenwriting by Syd Field, divides a story at turning points or plot points. Alternatives include the four-act structure used in television, serial and episodic structures, frame narratives, parallel plots, and nonlinear arrangements employing flashbacks (analepsis) and flashforwards (prolepsis), as well as in medias res openings. East Asian traditions include the four-part kishōtenketsu structure, which can proceed without conflict.

Analysis

The study of narrative structure is central to narratology, the theory and study of narrative. Scholars distinguish levels of structure (such as Gérard Genette's categories of order, duration, and frequency) and examine how structure shapes suspense, character development, and theme.
