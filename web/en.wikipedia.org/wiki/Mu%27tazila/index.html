Mu'tazila

The Mu'tazila was an early school of Islamic theology that emerged in Basra and Baghdad in the 8th century CE. Emphasizing reason and rational thought in matters of faith, its adherents held that religious truths could be known through human intellect and that revelation must be interpreted in light of reason.

Five principles

Mu'tazilite doctrine is summarized in five principles: the absolute unity of God (tawhid), divine justice, the promise and the threat (the certainty of reward and punishment), an intermediate position for the grave sinner (neither believer nor unbeliever), and the duty of commanding right and forbidding wrong.

From divine unity they argued that the Qur'an, being distinct from God, must be created rather than eternal — a doctrine that became the center of controversy during the Mihna, the inquisition under the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun beginning in 833.

History and influence

Flourishing under early Abbasid patronage, the school declined after the Mihna was abandoned and traditionalist theology, especially Ash'arism, gained ground. Nevertheless, Mu'tazilite ideas deeply influenced later Islamic philosophy and jurisprudence, and its doctrines survived notably within Zaydi Shi'a theology. Thinkers such as Wasil ibn Ata, al-Jahiz, and al-Zamakhshari are associated with the school.
