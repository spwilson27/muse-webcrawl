The endometrium is the inner epithelial layer of the uterus, together with its mucous membrane. It lines the uterine cavity and is the site of implantation of the blastocyst during pregnancy. The endometrium is one of the most dynamic tissues in the human body, undergoing cyclical growth, differentiation, and shedding under hormonal control throughout the reproductive years.

Structure

The endometrium consists of a simple columnar epithelium resting on a thick connective-tissue stroma containing tubular uterine glands. It is conventionally divided into two layers: the stratum basalis, a deep permanent layer adjacent to the myometrium that regenerates the lining after shedding, and the stratum functionalis, a superficial transient layer that proliferates, becomes secretory, and is shed during menstruation. Blood is supplied by spiral arteries arising from the arcuate and radial arteries of the uterus.

Menstrual cycle

During the proliferative (follicular) phase, rising estrogen from the developing ovarian follicle drives regrowth of the functional layer. After ovulation, progesterone from the corpus luteum converts the endometrium to its secretory phase: glands become tortuous and secrete glycogen-rich fluid, preparing for implantation. If pregnancy does not occur, falling hormone levels cause constriction of the spiral arteries, ischemia, and shedding of the functional layer as menstrual flow.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the endometrium include endometriosis (endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus), endometrial hyperplasia, endometrial polyps, Asherman syndrome, and endometrial cancer, one of the most common gynecological malignancies. Endometrial thickness measured by transvaginal ultrasound is used clinically in evaluating postmenopausal bleeding and in assisted reproduction.
