Lagrange's four-square theorem

Lagrange's four-square theorem, also known simply as the four-square theorem, states that every natural number can be represented as the sum of four integer squares. For example, 3 = 1² + 1² + 1² + 0² and 31 = 5² + 2² + 1² + 1².

History

The theorem appears in Diophantus's Arithmetica in implicit form, and was restated by Bachet in 1621. Fermat claimed to have a proof but never published one. Euler worked on the problem for decades, proving key lemmas, and Joseph-Louis Lagrange gave the first complete proof in 1770, building on Euler's four-square identity, which shows that the product of two numbers each expressible as a sum of four squares is itself so expressible.

Proof outline

By Euler's identity it suffices to prove the theorem for prime numbers. For an odd prime p, one shows that mp is a sum of four squares for some small multiplier 1 ≤ m < p (using the fact that the sets of quadratic residues and shifted residues mod p must overlap), and then a descent argument reduces m to 1.

Related results

The theorem is a special case of the Waring problem (g(2) = 4). Jacobi's four-square theorem counts the number of representations. The Legendre three-square theorem characterizes numbers that need only three squares, and the sum-of-two-squares theorem those needing only two.
