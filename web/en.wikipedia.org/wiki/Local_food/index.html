Local food

Local food is food that is produced within a short distance of where it is consumed, often as part of a social and political movement — the local food movement — that promotes buying food from nearby farms and producers rather than from global industrial supply chains. People who prefer local food are sometimes called locavores, a term coined in 2005 in the San Francisco Bay Area and chosen as the Oxford American Dictionary's word of the year in 2007.

Definitions

There is no single official definition of "local". Distances used range from the same county, to within 100 miles (160 km), to within a day's drive. In the United States, the 2008 Food, Conservation, and Energy Act described locally or regionally produced food as transported less than 400 miles from its origin, or produced within the same state. Retailers, farmers' markets and community-supported agriculture (CSA) schemes each apply their own standards.

Motivations

Supporters cite several benefits: fresher and tastier produce picked at maturity; support for small farms and the local economy; preserving regional crop varieties and agricultural landscapes; shorter transport distances ("food miles") and hence lower transport emissions; and greater transparency and trust between producers and consumers. Local food systems are distributed through farmers' markets, CSAs, farm shops, food hubs and farm-to-table restaurants.

Criticism

Critics argue that transport is often a small share of food's total environmental footprint — production methods, especially for meat and dairy, usually dominate — so "local" is not automatically more sustainable. Local production can also be less efficient in unsuitable climates (e.g. heated greenhouses versus field-grown imports), and local food can be more expensive and less available out of season. The concept nonetheless remains influential in food policy, urban agriculture and school and hospital procurement.
