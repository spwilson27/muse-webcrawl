Education in ancient Greece

Education in ancient Greece varied greatly between city-states, but its most influential models were those of Athens, which prized learning for the citizen, and Sparta, which subordinated the individual wholly to the state. The Greek word paideia (παιδεία) denoted the rearing and education of the ideal citizen and became a foundational concept of Western education.

Athens

Athenian boys from citizen families began schooling at about age seven, escorted by a slave attendant (paidagogos). Three teachers provided elementary education: the grammatistes taught reading and writing, the kitharistes music and lyric poetry (with Homer memorised extensively), and the paidotribes physical training in the palaestra. Arithmetic was learned more incidentally. There was no state curriculum and fees were private. Girls were educated at home, if at all.

From the fifth century BCE, the Sophists offered higher instruction in rhetoric, politics and philosophy for fees. Plato founded the Academy (c. 387 BCE) and Aristotle the Lyceum, giving Athens enduring institutions of higher learning. At eighteen, Athenian youths entered the ephebeia, a period of military and civic training.

Sparta

In Sparta, boys were taken from their families at age seven into the state-run agoge, a communal, military upbringing emphasising endurance, obedience, stealth and combat over literacy. Spartan girls, unusually for Greece, also received public physical education.

Hellenistic period

In the Hellenistic era the gymnasium became standard across the Greek world, and education became more formalised and bookish, centred on grammar, rhetoric and the classical authors, with great libraries at Alexandria and Pergamum.
