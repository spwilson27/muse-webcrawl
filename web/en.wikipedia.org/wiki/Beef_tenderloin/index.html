Beef tenderloin is an oblong cut of beef taken from the short loin, consisting chiefly of the psoas major muscle. Because this muscle does very little work, the tenderloin is the most tender cut of the animal, and it is usually the most expensive beef cut per unit weight.

Anatomy and cuts

The tenderloin sits beneath the ribs, next to the backbone, running from the hip to the rib cage. A whole tenderloin typically weighs about 2–3 kg (4–6 lb) and tapers from a thick "butt" end to a thin tail. Butchers divide it into sections:

  • Tail — the thin end, often used for tips or stir-fry.
  • Chateaubriand — steaks from the thick centre/butt portion, traditionally roasted or grilled for two.
  • Filet mignon — small, round steaks cut from the centre and tail-end of the tenderloin, prized for tenderness.
  • Tournedos — small medallions from the thinner end in French butchery.

When left attached to the bone with the strip steak side, the corresponding section forms part of the T-bone and porterhouse steaks.

Preparation

The tenderloin is lean and mild-flavoured, so it is commonly roasted whole (often tied to an even shape), grilled as steaks, or prepared in dishes such as beef Wellington, in which the centre cut is coated with pâté and mushroom duxelles and baked in puff pastry. Because of its low fat content it is usually cooked rare to medium-rare and benefits from sauces or compound butters.
