The CANDU (Canada Deuterium Uranium) is a Canadian pressurized heavy-water reactor design used for electricity generation. The design was developed by Atomic Energy of Canada Limited beginning in the 1950s. Its defining features are the use of heavy water (deuterium oxide) as both moderator and coolant, and natural (unenriched) uranium as fuel.

Design

Unlike light-water reactors, a CANDU has no single large pressure vessel. Instead, hundreds of horizontal pressure tubes pass through a large, low-pressure tank called a calandria filled with heavy-water moderator. Short fuel bundles are pushed through the tubes by remote fuelling machines, allowing the reactor to be refuelled while running at full power. Because heavy water absorbs far fewer neutrons than ordinary water — a property tied to the deuterium whose discovery won Harold Urey a Nobel Prize — CANDU achieves neutron economy good enough to burn natural uranium, and can even use spent fuel from other reactors or thorium.

History and deployment

The prototype Nuclear Power Demonstration (NPD) reactor entered service in 1962, followed by the commercial Pickering and Bruce stations in Ontario, among the largest nuclear plants in the world. CANDU units were exported to Argentina, China, India (whose PHWRs derive from the design), Pakistan, Romania and South Korea. Later developments include the CANDU 6, the Enhanced CANDU 6, and the ACR-1000.

Safety and characteristics

CANDU safety features include two independent shutdown systems, a large cool moderator that acts as a heat sink, and a negative void coefficient in some configurations; online refuelling reduces excess reactivity requirements. The design's use of unenriched fuel avoids dependence on enrichment facilities, though heavy water production is itself costly.
