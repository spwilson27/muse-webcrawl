Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism

The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism is a general class of mechanisms in mechanism design theory that implement a socially efficient outcome in dominant strategies when agents have quasilinear preferences. Participants report their valuations over possible outcomes; the mechanism then selects the outcome maximising the total reported value, and charges each participant a payment equal to the externality their participation imposes on the others. It generalises the Vickrey (second-price) auction and the earlier ideas of William Vickrey (1961), Edward H. Clarke (1971) and Theodore Groves (1973).

VCG: choose the efficient outcome; charge each agent their externality

Definition

Let each agent i report a valuation function v̂ᵢ over a set of outcomes. The mechanism picks the outcome x* maximising the sum of reported valuations. Agent i pays pᵢ = (best total value of the other agents if i were absent) − (total value the other agents obtain at x*). Under quasilinear utility, truthful reporting is a weakly dominant strategy, and the mechanism therefore selects the genuinely value-maximising outcome. The special case where the payments are computed with respect to a single agent's report relative to a fixed rule is known as the pivot (Clarke) mechanism.

Applications

Beyond auctions, VCG mechanisms have been proposed for public project provision (the Clarke tax), traffic and congestion management, allocating landing slots, sharing network resources, and procurement. In online advertising, generalised second-price auctions historically used by search engines were inspired by, but are not exactly, VCG mechanisms.

Limitations

The Green–Laffont theorem shows that (under mild conditions) Groves-type schemes are essentially the only efficient dominant-strategy mechanisms with quasilinear preferences, so VCG's drawbacks are fundamental: payments are not budget-balanced, revenue can be very low, the mechanism can be manipulated by colluding groups or agents splitting into false identities, it assumes agents can report complete valuation functions, and computing the optimal outcome may be intractable for large combinatorial problems. Practical mechanism design often trades away some of VCG's theoretical elegance for robustness, simplicity and revenue.
