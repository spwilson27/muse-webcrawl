United States census

Decennial census
First: 1790
Most recent: 2020
Conducted by: United States Census Bureau

The United States census (plural: censuses) is a decennial census mandated by Article I, Section 2 of the United States Constitution, which states: "Representatives and direct Taxes shall be apportioned among the several States... according to their respective Numbers." The modern census is conducted by the United States Census Bureau.

Purpose

Census results determine each state's representation in the U.S. House of Representatives and the Electoral College through apportionment, and guide the distribution of hundreds of billions of dollars in federal funding. The 2020 census counted 331,449,281 residents.

History

The first census in 1790, supervised by Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson, counted 3,929,214 people. The Constitution's three-fifths compromise counted enslaved persons as three-fifths for apportionment until abolished by the Fourteenth Amendment. The permanent Census Bureau was created in 1903. Technological milestones include Herman Hollerith's punched-card tabulators in 1890 and the UNIVAC I computer in 1950.

Confidentiality

Individual responses are protected by Title 13 of the United States Code and are released publicly only after 72 years. Participation is legally mandatory, though prosecutions for refusal are rare.
