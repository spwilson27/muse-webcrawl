William Morton Wheeler (March 19, 1865 – April 19, 1937) was an American entomologist, myrmecologist and professor at Harvard University. He was the leading authority of his generation on ants and the social behaviour of insects, and one of the founders of the study of social insects as a scientific discipline.

Career

Wheeler began his career in embryology, studying in Germany under Anton Dohrn's influence, before becoming curator of invertebrate zoology at the American Museum of Natural History in New York (1903–1908). In 1908 he moved to Harvard, where he was professor of economic entomology at the Bussey Institution and later dean of its faculty; he assembled what became one of the world's great ant collections at Harvard's Museum of Comparative Zoology.

Work on ants

His book Ants: Their Structure, Development and Behavior (1910) remained the standard synthesis for decades. Wheeler described many hundreds of ant taxa on collecting expeditions across the Americas, Australia and the Pacific, studied the colony as an integrated unit, and popularised the idea of the ant colony as a "superorganism" — a term he borrowed from Herbert Spencer and developed in his 1911 essay "The Ant-colony as an Organism". His later works, including The Social Insects (1928), ranged over bees, wasps and termites and helped lay the groundwork for modern sociobiology.
