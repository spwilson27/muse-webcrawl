Siete Partidas

The Siete Partidas ("Seven Parts") is a Castilian law code compiled in the 13th century under King Alfonso X of Castile ("Alfonso the Wise"). One of the most famous products of medieval jurisprudence, it is a comprehensive legal encyclopedia in seven parts covering civil, criminal, procedural, family, commercial, constitutional and canon-law topics, drawing heavily on Roman law as revived in the European universities. It became a foundational text of Spanish law and, through the Spanish empire, of the law of much of Latin America and parts of the southern and western United States.

Compilation and structure

The code was prepared by a team of jurists between about 1256 and 1265 (the dates are conventionally given as c. 1256–1265). Alfonso, who aspired to legal uniformity in his realms and perhaps to the imperial crown, intended it as a unifying body of law. Each of the seven partidas opens with a letter of the king's name (A-L-F-O-N-S-O):

  • Partida I — sources of law and canon law; the Church, sacraments and ecclesiastical matters.
  • Partida II — emperors, kings and lords; government, war and the duties of rulers and subjects.
  • Partida III — the administration of justice: judges, advocates, procedure and evidence.
  • Partida IV — family law: marriage, divorce, children, slavery and freedom.
  • Partida V — contracts, loans, commerce and obligations.
  • Partida VI — wills, inheritance and guardianship.
  • Partida VII — crimes and punishments, including provisions on Jews, Muslims and heretics.

Reception and influence

Because the great towns and nobility resisted royal law in favour of their local fueros, the Partidas did not take full legal effect in Castile until the Ordenamiento de Alcalá of 1348 under Alfonso XI, which established them as subsidiary law where local law was silent. From there their influence grew enormously. They were carried to Spanish America and the Philippines, where they shaped colonial law for centuries and left traces in modern Latin American codes. In the United States, the Partidas influenced the law of former Spanish and Mexican territories — notably property, water and community-property law in states such as Louisiana, Texas and California — and the code was translated into English in the 19th and 20th centuries (notably the five-volume translation edited by Samuel Parsons Scott, 1931). Jurists have praised the code's learning and literary style, while noting provisions — on slavery, for example — that reflect its medieval origins.
