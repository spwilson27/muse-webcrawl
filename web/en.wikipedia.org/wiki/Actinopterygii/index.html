Actinopterygii

Actinopterygii
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Class: Actinopterygii
Known species: over 30,000

Actinopterygii, members of which are known as ray-finned fish or actinopterygians, is a class of bony fish that comprise over 50% of living vertebrate species. They are so called because of their lightly built fins made of webs of skin supported by radially extended bony spines called lepidotrichia, as opposed to the bulkier, fleshy lobed fins characteristic of the Sarcopterygii.

The actinopterygians are the dominant vertebrates in aquatic settings, especially the oceans. They are found in deep oceanic trenches to high mountain streams and include familiar groups such as tuna, cod, herring, and angelfish.

Anatomy

Ray-finned fishes have fins supported by rays, a swim bladder in many species for buoyancy, and scales that may be ganoid, cycloid, or ctenoid, or absent. The skeleton is increasingly ossified in derived groups, and the jaws and skull are highly kinetic in teleosts, aiding suction feeding.

Classification

Traditional classification divides Actinopterygii into Chondrostei (including sturgeons and paddlefish, plus bichirs in some schemes) and Neopterygii (gars, bowfin, and the vast Teleostei). Teleosts include more than 30,000 species in about 40 orders.

Evolution

The earliest known ray-finned fishes date to the Devonian, about 400 million years ago. They diversified greatly in the Mesozoic and Cenozoic, becoming the dominant fishes of both marine and freshwater habitats.
