Glider (sailplane)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A modern fibreglass sailplane soaring over the countryside.

A glider or sailplane is a type of glider aircraft used in the leisure activity and sport of gliding (also called soaring). This unpowered aircraft can use naturally occurring currents of rising air in the atmosphere to remain airborne. The word sailplane is also used in North America; in British English glider is the usual term.

Design

Sailplanes have long, slender wings giving a high aspect ratio and low drag, enabling glide ratios that in modern racing machines exceed 60:1 — meaning the aircraft can travel 60 kilometres forward while losing one kilometre of altitude in still air. Most modern sailplanes are built of glass- and carbon-fibre composites, have wingspans between 15 and 25 metres, and carry water ballast that can be dumped before landing. Controls include air brakes (spoilers) for glidepath control, and many gliders have flaps for varying wing camber.

Staying airborne

Glider pilots gain height in thermals (rising warm air), ridge lift (wind deflected upward by slopes), and lee waves (standing waves downwind of mountains). Flights of over 3,000 kilometres and altitudes above 15,000 metres have been achieved in wave lift.

Launching

Because sailplanes are unpowered, they are launched by aerotow behind a powered aircraft, by a ground winch or vehicle tow, or occasionally by bungee launch from a slope. Some self-launching motor gliders have retractable engines for take-off but rely on soaring for cross-country flight.

Competition

Gliding competitions task pilots with racing around closed courses, scored by speed and distance through turn points verified by GPS flight recorders. Classes are defined by wingspan and whether flaps are permitted.

History

Modern gliding developed in Germany in the 1920s after the Treaty of Versailles restricted powered aviation. Pioneers such as Otto Lilienthal had earlier made the first controlled glider flights in the 1890s.
