Implantation (embryology)

Implantation is the stage of embryonic development in mammals in which the blastocyst hatches from its protective shell, the zona pellucida, adheres to the wall of the uterus, and embeds itself in the endometrium. In humans, implantation begins about six to seven days after fertilisation and is usually complete by the eleventh or twelfth day. Successful implantation marks the beginning of pregnancy in the clinical sense, as it is only then that the embryo begins to exchange signals and nutrients with the mother.

A blastocyst attaching to and invading the endometrium during implantation.

Stages

Implantation proceeds through three broad stages. In apposition, the blastocyst loosely aligns with the endometrial surface, oriented so that the inner cell mass faces the uterine wall. In adhesion, specialised molecules on the trophoblast cells — including integrins and selectins — bind firmly to the receptive endometrium. In invasion, the syncytiotrophoblast secretes enzymes that erode the uterine epithelium and stroma, burrowing the embryo into the lining and tapping into maternal blood vessels to form the earliest circulation of the placenta.

The implantation window

The endometrium is receptive to implantation only during a short interval in each menstrual cycle, roughly days 20–24 of an idealised 28-day cycle, known as the window of implantation. This receptivity is prepared by oestrogen and progesterone. Molecular signals, notably the cytokine LIF (leukaemia inhibitory factor) and the hormone human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) secreted by the implanting embryo, coordinate the dialogue between embryo and uterus. Detection of hCG in blood or urine is the basis of pregnancy tests.

Clinical significance

Failure of implantation is a major cause of infertility and of very early pregnancy loss; it is estimated that a large fraction of human conceptions fail to implant or are lost shortly afterwards. In assisted reproduction, embryos created by in vitro fertilisation are transferred to the uterus in the hope that implantation will occur. Abnormal implantation — too deeply, or in the wrong location, as in ectopic pregnancy — can be dangerous.
