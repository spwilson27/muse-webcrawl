Cybele was an Anatolian mother goddess, venerated in Phrygia as a goddess of mountains, nature, and fertility, and adopted by Greek colonists in Asia Minor and eventually by Rome, where she was worshipped as Magna Mater ("Great Mother"). She is typically depicted enthroned, wearing a mural crown, flanked by lions, and holding a drum (tympanum) and a bowl.

Mythology

In Phrygian tradition Cybele was associated with Mount Ida and other sacred mountains. Greek myth merged her with Rhea, mother of Zeus. Her most famous myth concerns her consort Attis, a shepherd whose madness, self-castration, death, and (in later versions) resurrection were mourned and celebrated in her spring festival. Her priests, the Galli, were eunuchs who served her in ecstatic rites with wild music and dancing.

Cult in Rome

In 204 BC, during the Second Punic War, Rome officially imported Cybele's cult from Pessinus in the form of a sacred black stone, following a consultation of the Sibylline Books; her temple was dedicated on the Palatine Hill in 191 BC. Her festival, the Megalensia, was held in April. The ecstatic and self-castrating aspects of her priesthood were long restricted for Roman citizens, but under the Empire her cult spread widely, and the March festival cycle of Attis became prominent.
