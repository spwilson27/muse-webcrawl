Motel

Motel
EtymologyBlend of "motor" + "hotel"
First known useMotel Inn, San Luis Obispo, California (opened 1925)
Defining featuresGuest rooms opening directly onto a parking area; designed for motorists
Typical layoutLow-rise (1–2 storeys), L-, U-, or linear-plan around a car park

A motel, also known as a motor hotel, motor lodge, or motor inn, is a hotel designed for motorists, usually with guest rooms that open directly onto a parking lot so travellers can enter their rooms straight from their vehicles. The word is a portmanteau of "motor" and "hotel." Motels arose in the United States in the 1920s with the spread of automobile travel and long-distance highway journeys, and became an iconic feature of American roadside culture.

History

As automobile ownership boomed in the 1920s, drivers on long journeys needed lodging beyond city-centre hotels. Early answers included free municipal auto camps and cabin camps of small individual cottages. The first establishment to call itself a motel was the Motel Inn (originally the Milestone Mo-Tel) in San Luis Obispo, California, designed by Arthur Heineman and opened in December 1925. Through the 1930s–1950s, motels proliferated along highways such as U.S. Route 66, competing with eye-catching architecture, swimming pools, air conditioning, free ice, and tall neon signs. Entrepreneurs like Kemmons Wilson, founder of Holiday Inn (1952), standardized the motel into chain-operated roadside hotels. The Interstate Highway System after 1956 reshaped the business, shifting traffic from town-centre routes to highway interchanges, and many classic independent motels declined, though others survive, some preserved as historic landmarks along Route 66.

Layout and operation

Unlike urban hotels, motels are low-rise buildings with exterior corridors or direct room access from the parking area, minimal lobby and public space, and few staff. They are usually located beside major roads on inexpensive land. Rooms typically include an en-suite bathroom and, in North America, often a television and coffee maker; amenities are modest compared with full-service hotels. Many motels are independently owned and operated, frequently by families who live on site.

International spread and cultural presence

The motel concept spread to Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and parts of Europe and Latin America, where in some countries (such as Brazil and South Korea) "motel" came to denote short-stay or couples' lodging rented by the hour. In the United States, motels feature prominently in film and literature as settings of transience and anonymity — from Psycho (1960), with its fictional Bates Motel, to countless road movies. In recent decades some older motels have been repurposed as budget apartments or supportive housing.
