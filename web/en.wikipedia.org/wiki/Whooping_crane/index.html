The whooping crane (Grus americana) is an endangered crane species native to North America, named for its whooping calls. It is, along with the sandhill crane, one of only two crane species found in North America, and at up to about 1.5 m (5 ft) tall with a wingspan of roughly 2.3 m (7.5 ft) it is among the continent's tallest flying birds. Adults are snowy white with black wingtips visible in flight, a red crown and a long, dark, pointed bill.

Decline

The species once ranged across the mid-continent from the Arctic coast to central Mexico. Hunting and habitat destruction reduced it catastrophically: by 1941 only about 21 wild birds remained — a single flock wintering at Aransas National Wildlife Refuge on the Texas Gulf Coast and breeding in remote Wood Buffalo National Park in Canada, plus a tiny non-migratory remnant in Louisiana that soon disappeared. The whooping crane became an icon of the early endangered species movement and was among the first species protected under US endangered species legislation.

Recovery

Intensive conservation — habitat protection, captive breeding at centres such as the Patuxent Wildlife Research Center and the International Crane Foundation, and reintroduction programmes — has raised the population to over 800 birds in total by the 2020s. Reintroduction efforts include an eastern migratory flock taught for years to follow ultralight aircraft from Wisconsin to Florida by Operation Migration, and a restored non-migratory flock in Louisiana. The wild Aransas–Wood Buffalo flock remains the only self-sustaining population and has grown to several hundred birds. Threats persist, including collisions with power lines, habitat loss on the wintering grounds, drought, and illegal shooting.

Life history

Whooping cranes mate for life and breed in shallow northern wetlands, where pairs defend large territories and usually raise a single chick. Their lifespan in the wild is estimated at 22 to 24 years, with captive birds living much longer. They feed on crabs, crayfish, frogs, small fish, plants and grain, probing mudflats and shallow marshes.
