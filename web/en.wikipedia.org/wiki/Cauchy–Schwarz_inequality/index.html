Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality (also called the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality) is a fundamental inequality in linear algebra, analysis and probability theory. It states that for vectors u and v in an inner product space, the absolute value of their inner product is at most the product of their norms:

|⟨u, v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖ ‖v‖

Equality holds if and only if u and v are linearly dependent.

Special cases

For finite sums, the inequality reads (Σ aibi)² ≤ (Σ ai²)(Σ bi²) for real numbers ai, bi. For integrals, |∫ f(x)g(x) dx|² ≤ ∫|f(x)|² dx · ∫|g(x)|² dx. In probability, it implies that the absolute correlation between two random variables is at most 1, and that |Cov(X,Y)| ≤ σXσY.

History

Augustin-Louis Cauchy published the sum version in 1821 in his Cours d'Analyse. Viktor Bunyakovsky obtained the integral version in 1859, and Hermann Amandus Schwarz gave the general proof for integrals in 1888, from which the abstract inner-product formulation follows. A standard proof notes that ‖u − tv‖² ≥ 0 for all scalars t and optimises over t.

Applications

The inequality proves the triangle inequality for inner product spaces, underlies the definition of angle between vectors, and is used throughout functional analysis, quantum mechanics (where it yields the uncertainty principle in its general form), statistics and optimisation. Generalisations include Hölder's inequality and the Bessel inequality.
