Cretan War (1645–1669)

Crete was contested between Venice and the Ottoman Empire for 24 years

The Cretan War, also known as the War of Candia, was fought between the Republic of Venice (with its allies) and the Ottoman Empire for control of the island of Crete. It began in 1645 when Ottoman forces landed on the Venetian-held island, and ended in 1669 with the fall of Candia (Heraklion) after a siege of more than twenty years — one of the longest sieges in history. Venice ceded most of Crete, retaining only a few island fortresses.

Background

Crete had been a Venetian colony since the aftermath of the Fourth Crusade. By the seventeenth century it was a vital Venetian base in the eastern Mediterranean. The immediate pretext for war came in 1644, when Knights of Malta seized an Ottoman vessel carrying pilgrims and officials to Mecca and took shelter in Crete, giving Sultan Ibrahim a casus belli.

Course of the war

Ottoman troops landed near Chania in June 1645 and quickly overran most of the island, but the fortified capital, Candia, resisted. The ensuing siege lasted from 1648 to 1669, marked by trench warfare, mining, and repeated assaults, while Venice tried to blockade the Dardanelles and won naval victories in the Aegean, including battles off the Dardanelles in 1654–1657. Venice also fought the Ottomans in Dalmatia, where it gained territory. European volunteers and aid from France and the Papal States supported Candia, but after the final great assaults of 1669 the Venetian commander Francesco Morosini negotiated an honourable surrender in September 1669.

Consequences

The loss of Crete ended Venice's role as a major eastern Mediterranean power, though it retained the offshore fortresses of Gramvousa, Souda, and Spinalonga until 1715. Crete remained under Ottoman rule for more than two centuries. The war exhausted both empires, and its cost and duration made it a byword for attritional siege warfare.
