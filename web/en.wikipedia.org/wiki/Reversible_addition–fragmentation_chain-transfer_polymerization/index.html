Reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer polymerization

Reversible addition–fragmentation chain-transfer (RAFT) polymerization is a method of controlled radical polymerization discovered at the Australian Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation (CSIRO) and reported in 1998. It is one of the most versatile forms of reversible-deactivation radical polymerization, allowing polymers of predetermined molecular weight, narrow molecular-weight distribution and complex architectures to be made from a wide range of monomers.

Mechanism

RAFT works by adding a thiocarbonylthio chain-transfer agent — typically a dithioester, trithiocarbonate, xanthate or dithiocarbamate — to an otherwise conventional radical polymerization. Propagating radicals add to the C=S bond of the agent, forming an intermediate radical that fragments to release a new radical and a dormant polymer chain capped with the thiocarbonylthio group. Rapid equilibration between active and dormant chains means all chains grow intermittently and at a similar overall rate, giving even growth and "living" character: the chains can be re-extended to form block copolymers.

Applications

Because RAFT tolerates many functional groups and reaction conditions, including aqueous media, it is used to prepare block, graft, star and comb polymers, and materials for drug delivery, coatings, dispersants and nanotechnology. The sulfur-containing end groups can cause colour and odour in products, and various methods exist to remove or transform them after polymerization.
