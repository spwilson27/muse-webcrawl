Willem Johan Kolff (14 February 1911 – 11 February 2009) was a Dutch physician and biomedical engineer regarded as the father of artificial organs. He is best known for inventing the first practical hemodialysis machine during the Second World War and for his later work on the artificial heart.

Early life and the artificial kidney

Kolff studied medicine at Leiden University and completed his doctorate at the University of Groningen. Working in Kampen during the German occupation of the Netherlands, he built a rotating-drum artificial kidney in 1943 from cellophane tubing, orange-juice cans and other improvised materials. After a series of unsuccessful attempts, in 1945 he successfully treated a 67-year-old woman in uremic coma, who regained consciousness after eleven hours of dialysis and lived for several more years.

Career in the United States

Kolff emigrated to the United States in 1950, joining the Cleveland Clinic, where he further developed dialysis and worked on heart–lung machines. In 1967 he moved to the University of Utah, where he directed the Institute for Biomedical Engineering and the artificial organs programme. There, in 1982, his team implanted the Jarvik-7 artificial heart into Barney Clark, the first recipient of a permanent artificial heart, who survived 112 days.

Legacy

Kolff's work laid the foundation for modern dialysis, which sustains millions of patients with kidney failure worldwide, and for the broader field of artificial organs. He received numerous honours, including the Albert Lasker Award for Clinical Medical Research in 2002.
