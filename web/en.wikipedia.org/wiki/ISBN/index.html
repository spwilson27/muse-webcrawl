The International Standard Book Number (ISBN) is a numeric commercial book identifier that is intended to be unique for every book and book-like product (such as an audiobook) published internationally. Publishers purchase or receive ISBNs from an affiliate of the International ISBN Agency and assign them to each separate edition and variation of a publication.

History

The system grew out of the 9-digit Standard Book Numbering (SBN) code created in the United Kingdom in 1966 for booksellers including W. H. Smith. The 10-digit ISBN format was adopted as international standard ISO 2108 in 1970. Since 1 January 2007, ISBNs have been 13 digits long, aligning them with the EAN "Bookland" barcode system (prefixes 978 and 979).

Structure

An ISBN-13 consists of five elements: a GS1 prefix (978 or 979), a registration group element identifying the country or language area, a registrant (publisher) element, a publication element identifying the specific title and edition, and a check digit. Each format of a book — hardcover, paperback, e-book — receives its own ISBN.

Check digit

For ISBN-10, the check digit is computed modulo 11 and may be the letter X (representing 10). For ISBN-13, digits are weighted alternately by 1 and 3 and summed modulo 10, following the EAN-13 scheme.
