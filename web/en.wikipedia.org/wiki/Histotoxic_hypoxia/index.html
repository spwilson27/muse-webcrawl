Histotoxic hypoxia

Not to be confused with hypoxia (medical) in general.

Histotoxic hypoxia (also called histogenous hypoxia) is a form of hypoxia in which the tissues of the body are unable to use the oxygen delivered to them, even though blood flow and oxygen content are normal or even elevated. It is one of the four classical categories of hypoxia, alongside hypoxic (hypoxemic) hypoxia, anemic hypoxia, and stagnant (circulatory) hypoxia.

Causes

The classic cause of histotoxic hypoxia is cyanide poisoning. Cyanide ions bind to the ferric iron of cytochrome c oxidase (complex IV) in the mitochondrial electron transport chain, preventing cells from carrying out aerobic respiration. Because the tissues cannot extract oxygen, venous blood retains an abnormally high oxygen content and may appear bright red.

Other agents that can produce a histotoxic picture include hydrogen sulfide, which also inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, and certain metabolic poisons. Alcohol and some narcotics have historically been listed as causes of impaired cellular oxygen utilization.

Clinical features

Signs may include rapid breathing, confusion, seizures, lactic acidosis, and cardiovascular collapse in severe poisoning. A characteristic clue is a narrow difference between arterial and venous oxygen content, since the tissues fail to extract oxygen from the blood.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying poison. In cyanide poisoning, antidotes such as hydroxocobalamin, sodium thiosulfate, and nitrites (which induce methemoglobinemia to bind cyanide) are used, together with supportive care and high-flow oxygen.
