Aseptic processing

Aseptic processing is a processing technique wherein commercially thermally sterilized liquid products (typically food or pharmaceutical products) are packaged into previously sterilized containers under sterile conditions to produce shelf-stable products that do not need refrigeration. Aseptic processing has almost completely replaced in-container sterilization of liquid foods, including milk, fruit juices, concentrates, cream, yogurt, salad dressings, liquid eggs, and ice cream mix.

Process

In aseptic processing, the product is sterilized separately from the package, typically by rapid heating — for example ultra-high temperature (UHT) treatment — and then cooled and filled into pre-sterilized containers in a sterile environment. Packages are commonly made of paperboard laminated with plastic and aluminium foil, as in Tetra Pak cartons, or of plastic, metal or glass. Because the food is heated for a shorter time than in retort canning, aseptic products generally retain more flavour and nutrients while remaining shelf stable for months without preservatives.

History

The principles of aseptic canning were developed in the early 20th century, with key advances by W. McK. Martin in the 1930s and the work that led to the Dole aseptic canning system in the 1940s and 1950s. Tetra Pak, founded in Sweden in 1951, commercialised aseptic carton packaging for milk and juice on a large scale. In the United States, aseptic packaging was approved for hydrogen peroxide sterilisation of packaging material in 1981, enabling rapid growth of juice boxes and similar products.

Pharmaceutical applications

In the pharmaceutical industry, aseptic processing is used to manufacture sterile drug products — such as injectables, ophthalmic solutions and biologics — that cannot withstand terminal sterilization. Such work is carried out in cleanrooms and isolators under strict regulatory control, with media fill simulations used to validate the process.
