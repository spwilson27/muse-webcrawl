A protein kinase is an enzyme that modifies other proteins by chemically adding phosphate groups to them (phosphorylation), usually transferring the γ-phosphate of ATP to a serine, threonine or tyrosine residue on the substrate. Phosphorylation usually changes the target protein's activity, location or interactions, and is one of the most common mechanisms of cellular signal transduction and regulation.

Types

Most eukaryotic protein kinases belong to a single large superfamily recognizable by a homologous catalytic domain of about 250–300 amino acids. They are divided by the residue they phosphorylate:

  • Serine/threonine kinases — e.g. protein kinase A (PKA), protein kinase C (PKC), CaMK, MAP kinases, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), AKT.
  • Tyrosine kinases — receptor tyrosine kinases such as the insulin and EGF receptors, and non-receptor kinases such as Src, Abl and JAK.
  • Dual-specificity kinases — e.g. MEK enzymes, which phosphorylate both threonine and tyrosine in MAP kinase activation loops.
  • Histidine kinases — central to two-component signalling in bacteria, plants and fungi.

Structure and regulation

The kinase catalytic domain is bilobed: a smaller N-terminal lobe rich in β-sheets binds ATP through the glycine-rich loop and the conserved lysine of the VAIK motif, while the larger, mostly helical C-terminal lobe binds substrate. The activation segment, including the DFG motif and activation loop, is reorganized by phosphorylation and regulatory interactions, switching the enzyme between inactive and active conformations. Kinases are further regulated by second messengers (cAMP, Ca²⁺, diacylglycerol), cyclins, scaffold proteins and by removal of the phosphate by protein phosphatases.

Signalling

Kinases form cascades that amplify and route signals, the best known being the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK MAP kinase pathway. Some 518 protein kinase genes — about 2% of human genes — make up the human kinome. Deregulation of kinases by mutation or overexpression is a hallmark of cancer and other diseases.

Kinase inhibitors as drugs

Protein kinases are among the most important drug targets of the twenty-first century. Imatinib, which inhibits the BCR-Abl tyrosine kinase, revolutionized treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia; dozens of small-molecule kinase inhibitors against EGFR, HER2, CDK4/6, BTK, JAK and other kinases are now approved medicines.
