Balance spring

Balance spring (hairspring)

Used inMechanical watches and clocks
FunctionReturns the balance wheel, giving it a regular period of oscillation
Inventedc. 1675 — Christiaan Huygens and Robert Hooke (disputed priority)
MaterialsBlued steel; Elinvar and Nivarox alloys; modern silicon

A balance spring, or hairspring, is a fine spiral spring attached to the balance wheel of a mechanical timepiece. Together, the balance wheel and spring form a harmonic oscillator whose period of oscillation is (ideally) unaffected by the wheel's amplitude — a property called isochronism — and which therefore regulates the rate at which the watch's gears advance. It is the watch's timekeeping element, functionally analogous to the pendulum in a clock.

History

Before its invention, portable timepieces used plain balance wheels whose rate varied erratically. Around 1675 both Christiaan Huygens and Robert Hooke developed the spiral balance spring, leading to a bitter priority dispute; Huygens had a working watch made by the Paris clockmaker Isaac Thuret. The improvement raised accuracy from perhaps a quarter-hour per day to a few minutes, making the practical marine chronometer possible. Later refinements included Thomas Earnshaw's and John Arnold's work, Abraham-Louis Breguet's overcoil (the "Breguet spiral", 1795), in which the outer turn of the spring is raised and bent inward for more concentric breathing, and Charles-Édouard Guillaume's self-compensating Elinvar alloys, which greatly reduced temperature error.

Function and adjustment

The escapement gives the balance a small impulse each swing and counts its oscillations. The period depends on the wheel's moment of inertia and the spring's stiffness: T = 2π√(I/κ). Watchmakers regulate the rate with curb pins and a regulator index that effectively lengthens or shortens the spring, or, in free-sprung balances, by adjusting timing screws on the wheel. Modern springs vibrate at 18,000–36,000 beats per hour (2.5–5 Hz).

Materials

Early springs were blued carbon steel, vulnerable to rust, magnetism and temperature change. Twentieth-century alloys — Elinvar, then Nivarox — made springs virtually non-magnetic and thermo-compensated. Since the 2000s, hairsprings etched from single-crystal silicon (for example Swatch Group's and Rolex's silicon springs) have offered near-perfect geometry, total immunity to magnetism, and freedom from lubrication.
