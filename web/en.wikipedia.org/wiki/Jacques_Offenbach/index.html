Jacques Offenbach (20 June 1819 – 5 October 1880) was a German-born French composer, cellist, and impresario. He is remembered for his nearly 100 operettas of the 1850s to the 1870s and his uncompleted opera The Tales of Hoffmann. He was a powerful influence on later composers of the operetta genre.

Career

Born Jakob Offenbach in Cologne, he studied cello at the Paris Conservatoire and played in the orchestra of the Opéra-Comique. In 1855 he opened his own theatre, the Bouffes-Parisiens, and scored a sensation with Orpheus in the Underworld (1858), whose irreverent parody of classical myth — and famous "Infernal Galop," now universally associated with the can-can — delighted Second Empire Paris.

Works

His long series of successes with librettists Henri Meilhac and Ludovic Halévy included La belle Hélène (1864), La vie parisienne (1866), La Grande-Duchesse de Gérolstein (1867), and La Périchole (1868). The satire in these works poked fun at the court and society of Napoleon III.

Final years

Offenbach's popularity survived the fall of the Second Empire, though his fortunes fluctuated amid costly productions and an American tour in 1876. He died in Paris in 1880 while racing to finish The Tales of Hoffmann, which premiered posthumously in 1881.
