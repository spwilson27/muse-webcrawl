The United States Department of Justice (DOJ), also known as the Justice Department, is a federal executive department of the United States government tasked with the enforcement of federal law and the administration of justice. It is equivalent to the justice or interior ministries of other countries. The department is headed by the U.S. Attorney General, who reports directly to the president of the United States and is a member of the president's Cabinet; it was established on July 1, 1870, during the presidency of Ulysses S. Grant — although the office of Attorney General itself dates to the Judiciary Act of 1789.

Organization

The department oversees the principal federal law-enforcement agencies:

  • Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI)
  • Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA)
  • Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF)
  • United States Marshals Service
  • Federal Bureau of Prisons

Its litigating divisions handle criminal prosecutions, civil suits, antitrust (the Antitrust Division), civil rights, tax, and environmental law. The 93 United States Attorneys — one for each federal judicial district — conduct most federal trials. The Office of the Solicitor General represents the United States before the Supreme Court. Other components include the Office of Legal Counsel, the National Security Division (created in 2006), the Civil Rights Division (created by the Civil Rights Act of 1957), and the Office of Justice Programs, which funds research and state and local assistance.

History

Originally a part-time office, the Attorney General's role grew with the government; the 1870 Act — championed amid Reconstruction to enforce civil rights law against the Ku Klux Klan — created the Department to consolidate federal legal work and added the Solicitor General. The department's Bureau of Investigation (1908) became the FBI. Its powers expanded through Prohibition enforcement, New Deal litigation, the organised-crime campaigns of Robert F. Kennedy's tenure as Attorney General (1961–64), the civil rights era, and post-9/11 counterterrorism. The department's norm of independence from White House political direction in individual cases has been a recurring theme in American politics, notably tested during Watergate's "Saturday Night Massacre" of 1973.

Headquarters

The department's headquarters, completed in 1935 between the White House and the Capitol on Pennsylvania Avenue, was renamed in 2001 for former Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy. Its limestone Art Deco fabric is decorated with sculptures and murals on themes of justice, including the aluminium statue Spirit of Justice.
