Count Ioannis Antonios Kapodistrias (11 February 1776 – 9 October 1831) was a Greek statesman who served as the first head of state of independent Greece, as Governor from 1828 until his assassination in 1831. Before that he had a distinguished career as a diplomat of the Russian Empire, serving as joint foreign minister and playing a significant role at the Congress of Vienna. He is considered one of the founders of the modern Greek state and the architect of its early institutions.

Early life and career in Corfu

Kapodistrias was born in Corfu, then part of the Republic of Venice, into a distinguished Corfiote family registered in the island's Libro d'Oro, of distant descent from the town of Capo d'Istria in Istria. He studied medicine, philosophy and law at the University of Padua. When the Septinsular Republic — the first autonomous Greek state of the modern era — was established under Russo-Ottoman protection in 1800, Kapodistrias entered its service, becoming its chief minister and supervising the drafting of its liberal constitution of 1803. After the Ionian Islands passed to France in 1807, he left for Russia.

Russian diplomatic service

Kapodistrias entered the Russian foreign ministry in 1809 and rose rapidly. During the Napoleonic Wars he undertook missions to the Army of the Danube and to Switzerland, where he helped negotiate Swiss neutrality and supported the Swiss federal constitution. At the Congress of Vienna (1814–15) he was one of Tsar Alexander I's most trusted advisers, counterbalancing Metternich, and in 1816 he was appointed joint foreign minister of Russia alongside Karl Nesselrode. His sympathy for the Greek cause made his position untenable after the outbreak of the Greek War of Independence in 1821 — Alexander disavowed the revolt — and Kapodistrias took leave and withdrew to Geneva in 1822, formally resigning in 1827.

Governor of Greece

In April 1827 the Greek National Assembly at Troezen elected Kapodistrias the first Governor (Kyvernitis) of Greece for a seven-year term. He arrived at Nafplio in January 1828, inheriting a country devastated by war, factional conflict and Egyptian occupation. With extraordinary energy he set about building a state: he reorganised the army, established schools and an orphanage on Aegina, founded the first Greek university-level institutions, introduced the phoenix as the national currency, reformed the judiciary, promoted agriculture (famously encouraging potato cultivation) and negotiated the borders of the new state with the Great Powers, culminating in the London Protocol of 1830, which recognised Greek independence.

His centralising, authoritarian style — he suspended the liberal constitution of Troezen and governed through an appointed Panhellenion — alienated powerful interests: the primates of the Peloponnese, the shipowners of Hydra, and the Maniot clan chiefs. Opposition hardened into revolt in 1831, notably on Hydra and in Mani.

Assassination and legacy

On 9 October 1831 Kapodistrias was shot and stabbed outside the church of Saint Spyridon in Nafplio by Konstantinos and Georgios Mavromichalis, members of the Maniot clan whose chief, Petrobey Mavromichalis, Kapodistrias had imprisoned. He died instantly. His brother Augustinos briefly succeeded him amid chaos before the Powers imposed a monarchy under Otto of Bavaria.

Kapodistrias is widely regarded in Greece as a national hero and the founder of the modern Greek state. The University of Athens is named the National and Kapodistrian University in his honour, and his portrait appeared on Greek coinage. Historians debate the balance between his state-building achievements and his authoritarian methods.
