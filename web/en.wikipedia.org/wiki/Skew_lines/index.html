Skew lines

Two skew lines in three-dimensional space

In three-dimensional geometry, skew lines are two lines that do not intersect and are not parallel. Equivalently, they are lines that do not lie in a common plane. A simple example is the pair of lines through opposite edges of a regular tetrahedron.

Properties

Two lines in three-dimensional space can be in one of three configurations: they may intersect at a single point, be parallel (coplanar and non-intersecting, possibly coincident), or be skew. In higher dimensions, pairs of lines are generically skew: randomly chosen lines in 3D almost surely do not meet and are not parallel.

Distance

The distance between two skew lines is defined as the length of the unique line segment that is perpendicular to both lines, connecting a point on one line to a point on the other. If the lines are given parametrically, with points p and q and direction vectors u and v, the distance can be computed using the scalar triple product: the distance equals the absolute value of (u × v) · (p − q) divided by the magnitude of u × v.

Generalizations

The notion extends to higher dimensions and to other pairs of objects: two subspaces are called skew if they do not intersect in the expected way, and a family of lines pairwise skew to each other is called a skew configuration. Related concepts appear in line geometry and in the theory of reguli on hyperboloids.
