Area studies

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Area studies divides the world into regions studied through an interdisciplinary lens.

Area studies are interdisciplinary fields of research and scholarship pertaining to particular geographical, national/federal, or cultural regions. The term exists primarily as a general description for what are, in practice, many heterogeneous fields of research, encompassing both the social sciences and the humanities. Typical area studies programs involve history, political science, sociology, cultural studies, languages, geography, literature, and related disciplines. In contrast to cultural studies, area studies often include diaspora and emigration from the area.

History

Interdisciplinary area studies became increasingly common in the United States and in Western scholarship after World War II. Before that war, American universities had just a few faculty who taught or conducted research on non-Western subjects. Foreign-area studies were virtually nonexistent. After the war, both liberals and conservatives were concerned about the United States' ability to respond effectively to perceived threats from the Soviet Union and China in the context of the emerging Cold War, as well as to the fallout from the decolonization of Africa and Asia.

In this context, the Ford Foundation, the Rockefeller Foundation, and the Carnegie Corporation of New York convened a series of meetings producing a broad consensus that to address this knowledge deficit, the US must invest in international studies. Participants argued that a big brain trust of internationally oriented political scientists and economists was an urgent national priority. There was a central assumption, however, that area specialists in the non-Western world would be rooted in the languages and cultures of the regions concerned.

The Social Science Research Council and the American Council of Learned Societies, with Ford Foundation funding, worked together to build the institutional basis for area studies. Meanwhile, programs expanded at major universities, often organized around world regions such as Latin America, the Middle East, South Asia, Southeast Asia, East Asia, Africa, and Eastern Europe.

Fields

Common area studies fields include African studies, Asian studies (including East Asian, South Asian, and Southeast Asian studies), European studies, Latin American studies, Middle Eastern studies, Russian and Eurasian studies, and American studies. Each field combines language training with coursework in the history, politics, economics, and cultures of the region.

Criticism and debates

Area studies have been debated since their inception. Critics, including scholars associated with Edward Said's critique of Orientalism, argued that Cold War-era area studies sometimes served state interests and reproduced essentialist views of the regions studied. Others have defended the field's linguistic depth and local knowledge against purely theoretical approaches in the social sciences. Since the end of the Cold War, the field has increasingly emphasized transnational flows, globalization, and comparative themes that cross regional boundaries.
