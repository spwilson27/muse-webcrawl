Madeleine de Scudéry

Born15 November 1607, Le Havre, France
Died2 June 1701 (aged 93), Paris, France
Pen nameSapho
Notable worksArtamène, ou le Grand Cyrus; Clélie

Madeleine de Scudéry (15 November 1607 – 2 June 1701), often known simply as Mademoiselle de Scudéry, was a French writer whose enormous heroic romances made her the most celebrated novelist in Europe during her lifetime. She was a central figure of the précieuses — the cultivated salon women who shaped French literary taste and manners in the mid-17th century — and adopted the name Sapho, after the Greek poet Sappho, in her circle.

Works

Her novels were initially published under the name of her brother Georges de Scudéry. Artamène, ou le Grand Cyrus (1649–1653), at ten volumes and some 13,000 pages, is often described as the longest novel ever published. Its successor, Clélie, histoire romaine (1654–1660), contained the famous Carte de Tendre — an allegorical map of the country of Tenderness charting the routes of love through villages such as Esteem and Inclination — which became an emblem of préciosité. The novels were romans à clef: their ancient Persian and Roman characters thinly disguised members of the French court and salon society, and readers circulated keys to decode them.

Salon and later life

From the 1650s she hosted her own salon, the Samedis de Mademoiselle de Scudéry (Saturdays), which drew writers and aristocrats and fostered the conversational literature she later published as Conversations (1680s). Though the précieuses were mocked — notably by Molière in Les Précieuses ridicules (1659) — Scudéry retained royal favour, receiving a pension from Louis XIV, and was the first woman honoured by the Académie française when her discourse on glory won its prize for eloquence in 1671. She died in Paris in 1701, nearly blind and deaf but still a literary institution.
