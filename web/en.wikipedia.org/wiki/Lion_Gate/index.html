Lion Gate

Lion Gate
Location: Mycenae, Argolis, Greece
Built: c. 1250 BCE
Civilization: Mycenaean

The Lion Gate is the main entrance of the Bronze Age citadel of Mycenae in southern Greece. It was erected during the 13th century BCE, around 1250 BCE, in the northwestern side of the acropolis and is named after the relief sculpture of two lionesses in a heraldic pose above its massive lintel.

Architecture

The gate is built in the Cyclopean masonry style characteristic of Mycenaean fortifications, with enormous limestone blocks. The passageway is about 3 metres wide and is closed by a double wooden door. Above the lintel — itself a block weighing some 20 tons — a triangular relieving space is filled by a slab of lighter limestone carved in relief: two lionesses flank a central column, their forepaws resting on an altar-like structure. The heads, probably of bronze or another material, are lost. The column is thought to represent a deity or the palace itself, guarded by the lions.

Significance

The Lion Gate is the sole surviving monumental piece of Mycenaean sculpture and the only monument of Bronze Age Greece to bear an iconographic motif that survived from antiquity; it was always visible, described by Pausanias in the 2nd century CE, and remained a landmark for travellers long before excavations at Mycenae began in the 19th century.
