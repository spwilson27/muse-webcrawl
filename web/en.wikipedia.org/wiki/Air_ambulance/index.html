Air ambulance
TypeEmergency medical service
AircraftHelicopters; fixed-wing aircraft

An air ambulance is an aircraft specially equipped to transport sick or injured people in a medical emergency over distances or terrain impractical for a conventional ground ambulance. Air ambulances fall into two broad categories: helicopter emergency medical services (HEMS), which respond to accident scenes and transfer patients between hospitals over short ranges, and fixed-wing services, which move patients over longer distances, including international repatriations.

History

Air evacuation of casualties began in the First World War, when modified aircraft carried wounded soldiers from front-line airstrips, and expanded greatly in the Second World War. The Korean War popularized helicopter casualty evacuation — immortalized in the television series M*A*S*H — and the Vietnam War demonstrated that rapid helicopter evacuation and early surgical care dramatically improved survival. Civilian programs adapted the military model from the 1970s onward; one of the first hospital-based services was Flight for Life in Denver, founded in 1972, and charity-funded air ambulances became a feature of many national systems, notably in the United Kingdom, Germany (ADAC and DRF Luftrettung), Switzerland (Rega), Australia (Royal Flying Doctor Service), Canada, and the United States.

Operations and equipment

Air ambulances carry much of the equipment of an intensive care unit: cardiac monitors and defibrillators, ventilators, infusion pumps, blood products, and advanced airway kit. Crews typically pair an experienced pilot with critical-care clinicians — flight nurses, paramedics, and in many European systems an emergency physician. Helicopters are dispatched for time-critical conditions such as major trauma, stroke, and heart attack, where minutes saved can change outcomes; fixed-wing aircraft, flown by services such as the Royal Flying Doctor Service across the Australian outback, provide primary care and transfers over vast sparsely populated regions.

Costs and debate

In the United States, air ambulance transport is frequently provided by private companies and can generate very large bills, prompting legislative debate about "surprise billing" that was partly addressed by the No Surprises Act of 2022. In many other countries services are publicly funded or supported by charitable donations; most UK air ambulance services are charities that receive no direct government funding for daily operations.
