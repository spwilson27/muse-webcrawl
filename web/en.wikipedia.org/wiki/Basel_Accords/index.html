The Basel Accords are a series of international banking standards issued by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS), which is hosted by the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, Switzerland. The accords set out frameworks for capital adequacy, risk management and supervisory review that member jurisdictions implement through their own laws and regulations; they are standards of soft law rather than treaties, but are adopted worldwide.

Basel I

The 1988 Basel Capital Accord (Basel I) introduced a minimum capital requirement of 8 per cent of risk-weighted assets, with assets grouped into broad risk categories (for example, 0 per cent for OECD government debt and 100 per cent for most corporate lending). It aimed to strengthen the stability of the international banking system and reduce competitive inequality between banks of different countries. A 1996 amendment added capital requirements for market risk.

Basel II

Published in 2004, Basel II organised regulation around three pillars: minimum capital requirements refined by more risk-sensitive methods (including banks' internal ratings, with supervisory approval), a supervisory review process, and market discipline through disclosure. It extended capital charges to operational risk. Critics later argued that its reliance on banks' internal models and credit ratings contributed to undercapitalisation revealed in the 2008 financial crisis.

Basel III

Agreed in 2010–11 in response to the crisis, Basel III raised the quality and quantity of capital (emphasising common equity Tier 1), introduced a leverage ratio, capital buffers (conservation and countercyclical), and new global liquidity standards — the liquidity coverage ratio and the net stable funding ratio. Implementation began in 2013 and has proceeded in phases; the finalised reforms, sometimes called "Basel IV" in the industry, address variability in risk-weighted assets and introduce an output floor for internal models.
