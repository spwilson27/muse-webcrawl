A leap year (also known as an intercalary year or bissextile year) is a calendar year that contains an additional day compared with a common year, inserted to keep the calendar synchronised with the astronomical year — the roughly 365.2422 days Earth takes to orbit the Sun. In the Gregorian calendar, the extra day is 29 February.

Gregorian rule

A year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4 — except that years divisible by 100 are not leap years unless they are also divisible by 400. Thus 2000 was a leap year, but 1700, 1800, and 1900 were not, and 2100 will not be. This gives an average year of 365.2425 days, close to the tropical year. The earlier Julian calendar used a simple four-year rule, which over-corrected and caused the seasonal drift the Gregorian reform of 1582 removed.

Leap day customs

People born on 29 February — "leaplings" — celebrate on 28 February or 1 March in common years; their legal birthday varies by jurisdiction. In British and Irish tradition, leap day was the one day on which women could propose marriage to men, a custom dramatised in folklore and film.

Other calendars

Lunisolar calendars such as the Hebrew and Chinese calendars instead insert a leap month in certain years (seven times in the 19-year Metonic cycle) to stay aligned with both the Moon and the seasons. The Solar Hijri calendar uses a 33-year cycle with eight leap years.
