Edict of Tolerance

An edict of tolerance is a declaration, made by a government or ruler, that members of a given religion or faith will not suffer persecution for engaging in their religious practices. Many such edicts have been issued in history, usually to end religious conflict or to secure the loyalty of minority communities, although they typically grant a limited toleration rather than full religious equality.

Notable examples

  • Edicts of Ashoka (3rd century BCE) — the Mauryan emperor Ashoka inscribed edicts urging respect among religious sects in India.
  • Edict of Milan (313 CE) — Constantine the Great and Licinius granted Christianity legal status and restored confiscated church property throughout the Roman Empire.
  • Warsaw Confederation (1573) — ensured religious freedom for nobles in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth.
  • Edict of Nantes (1598) — Henry IV of France granted substantial rights to the Huguenots; revoked by Louis XIV in 1685.
  • English Toleration Act (1689) — exempted Protestant dissenters from certain penal laws.
  • Patent of Toleration (1781) — Joseph II of Austria extended civil rights to Lutherans, Calvinists, and Orthodox Christians, followed by an Edict of Tolerance for Jews in 1782.
  • Edict of Versailles (1787) — restored civil status to French non-Catholics shortly before the Revolution.

Toleration edicts are generally distinguished from modern guarantees of freedom of religion in that they were acts of grace by a ruler — revocable, as the fate of the Edict of Nantes showed — rather than rights held against the state.
