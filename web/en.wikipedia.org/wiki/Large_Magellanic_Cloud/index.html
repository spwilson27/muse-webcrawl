The Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) is a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way. At a distance of around 50 kiloparsecs (163,000 light-years), the LMC is the third-closest galaxy to the Milky Way, after the Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal and the Canis Major Overdensity. It is visible as a faint cloud in the southern hemisphere night sky.

With a mass approximately 10 billion times that of the Sun, the LMC is roughly one-hundredth the mass of the Milky Way. It is the fourth-largest galaxy in the Local Group, after Andromeda, the Milky Way, and Triangulum.

Structure and contents

The LMC is classified as a Magellanic spiral: it has a single spiral arm and a central bar offset from the galaxy's centre. It contains the Tarantula Nebula (30 Doradus), the most active star-forming region in the Local Group, and was the host of SN 1987A, the nearest supernova observed in modern times. Together with the Small Magellanic Cloud, it is connected by the Magellanic Bridge and trailed by the Magellanic Stream of gas.

Observational history

The Clouds were described by Amerigo Vespucci and named for Ferdinand Magellan's expedition of 1519–1522. Henrietta Leavitt's study of Cepheid variables in the Clouds (see Harvard Computers) established the period–luminosity law that first allowed their distances to be measured, revealing them to lie far outside the Milky Way.
