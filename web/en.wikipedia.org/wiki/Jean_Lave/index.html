Jean Lave is an American social anthropologist and learning theorist, best known for her work on situated learning and for developing, together with Etienne Wenger, the concept of communities of practice. She is a professor emerita at the University of California, Berkeley, in the Department of Geography and the Graduate School of Education.

Career and research

Lave received her doctorate in social anthropology from Harvard University in 1968. Her early fieldwork examined apprenticeship among Vai and Gola tailors in Liberia, which led her to question the assumption that learning acquired in one context transfers readily to others. This research formed the basis for her influential book Cognition in Practice (1988), a critique of cognitive psychology's laboratory-based studies of learning and a foundational text in the study of everyday reasoning and numeracy.

With Etienne Wenger, Lave published Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation (1991), one of the most widely cited works in education and organizational studies. The book argues that learning is fundamentally a social process: newcomers become members of a community of practice by participating in its activities in ways that are at first peripheral but legitimate, gradually moving toward fuller participation as they acquire the community's knowledge, identity, and ways of speaking.

Key works

Lave's books include Cognition in Practice: Mind, Mathematics and Culture in Everyday Life (1988), Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation (1991, with Etienne Wenger), and Apprenticeship in Critical Ethnographic Practice (2011). Her work has been influential across anthropology, education, computer-supported collaborative learning, and organizational theory, where the notion of legitimate peripheral participation is used to describe how newcomers are integrated into workplaces and professional communities.
