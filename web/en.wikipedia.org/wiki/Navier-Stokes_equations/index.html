Navier–Stokes equations

The Navier–Stokes equations are a set of partial differential equations which describe the motion of viscous fluid substances. They were named after French engineer and physicist Claude-Louis Navier and Irish physicist and mathematician George Gabriel Stokes. They were developed over several decades of progressively building the theories, from 1822 (Navier) to 1842–1850 (Stokes).

The Navier–Stokes equations mathematically express momentum balance and conservation of mass for Newtonian fluids. They are sometimes accompanied by an equation of state relating pressure, temperature and density. They are a cornerstone of fluid mechanics and are used to model weather, ocean currents, water flow in pipes, airflow around aircraft wings, and many other phenomena.

Formulation

For an incompressible fluid with constant density, the momentum equation can be written as:

ρ (∂u/∂t + u · ∇u) = −∇p + μ∇²u + f

together with the incompressibility condition ∇ · u = 0, where u is the flow velocity, p is the pressure, ρ is the density, μ is the dynamic viscosity, and f represents external body forces (such as gravity).

Properties

The equations are nonlinear, owing to the convective term u · ∇u, which is responsible for many complex flow phenomena including turbulence. In three spatial dimensions it is not known whether smooth solutions always exist: the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute, each carrying a prize of one million US dollars.

Applications

Because no general analytical solutions are known, engineers rely on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) to solve the equations numerically. Simplified forms — such as the Euler equations (inviscid limit), the Stokes equations (creeping-flow limit), and boundary-layer theory — remain widely used. The Reynolds number determines whether a flow is laminar or turbulent in typical regimes.
