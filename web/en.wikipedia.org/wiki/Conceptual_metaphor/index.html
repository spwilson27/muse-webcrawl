Conceptual metaphor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In cognitive linguistics, a conceptual metaphor is a cognitive mechanism in which one conceptual domain (the target domain) is understood in terms of another (the source domain). Conceptual metaphors shape not just language but thought and action: the conventional expressions of everyday speech are taken as evidence of underlying mappings between domains.

The theory was introduced by George Lakoff and Mark Johnson in their 1980 book Metaphors We Live By, which argued that metaphor is not a decorative feature of literary language but a fundamental part of ordinary cognition.

Examples of mappings

Conceptual metaphors are conventionally written in small capitals as TARGET IS SOURCE:

  • ARGUMENT IS WAR — "He attacked every weak point in my argument"; "I demolished his argument."
  • TIME IS MONEY — "You're wasting my time"; "That gadget will save you hours."
  • LIFE IS A JOURNEY — "She's at a crossroads in her life"; "He went through a rough patch."
  • MORE IS UP — "Prices rose"; "His spirits fell."
  • THEORIES ARE BUILDINGS — "The theory needs support"; "The argument collapsed."

Each mapping is systematic: it licenses a family of related expressions and carries entailments — inferences from the source domain that can be applied to the target.

Primary metaphors and embodiment

Lakoff and Joseph Grady proposed primary metaphors — simple mappings arising directly from embodied experience, such as AFFECTION IS WARMTH (from the correlation of affection with being held in infancy) or KNOWING IS SEEING. Complex metaphors are built by combining primary ones. Related notions include image schemas and conceptual blending, developed by Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner, which generalises the two-domain model into networks of mental spaces.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether linguistic metaphors reliably reveal cognitive structure, whether experimental evidence supports the claimed direction of influence, and whether the inventories of metaphors proposed by analysts amount to post-hoc labelling. Psycholinguistic research using priming and gesture studies has produced mixed but often supportive results.
