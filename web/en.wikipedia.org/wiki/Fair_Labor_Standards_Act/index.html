Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938
Enacted25 June 1938
Signed byPresident Franklin D. Roosevelt
EstablishedFederal minimum wage, overtime pay, child labour restrictions
Administered byWage and Hour Division, US Department of Labor

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA) is a United States labor law that creates the right to a minimum wage, and "time-and-a-half" overtime pay when people work over forty hours a week. It also prohibits the employment of minors in "oppressive child labor." It was the last major statute of the New Deal, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on 25 June 1938, and applies to employees engaged in interstate commerce or employed by an enterprise engaged in commerce.

Provisions

As originally enacted, the Act set a minimum wage of 25 cents per hour (rising to 40 cents within seven years), a maximum workweek of 44 hours falling to 40, overtime at one-and-a-half times the regular rate beyond those hours, and a general ban on the labour of children under 16 (under 18 in hazardous occupations). It initially covered only about a fifth of the workforce, excluding agricultural, domestic and retail workers — exclusions that fell disproportionately on Black workers in the South and reflected the price of Southern Democratic support. The Supreme Court upheld the Act in United States v. Darby (1941), overruling the earlier Hammer v. Dagenhart child-labour precedent.

Later development

Amendments in 1949, 1955, 1961, 1966 and later extended coverage to retail, service, hospital, school and (in 1974) domestic workers, and raised the minimum wage in steps; the Equal Pay Act of 1963 amended the FLSA to prohibit sex-based wage discrimination. The federal minimum wage has stood at $7.25 per hour since 2009, though many states and cities set higher rates. The FLSA's "exempt" categories for executive, administrative and professional employees — governed by salary and duties tests — remain a recurring subject of regulation and litigation, as do questions about tipped wages, gig-economy classification and whether a job is covered by the Act at all. Together with the National Labor Relations Act, it forms the core of US federal labour standards law.
