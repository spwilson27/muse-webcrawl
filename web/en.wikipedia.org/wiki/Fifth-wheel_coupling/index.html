The fifth-wheel coupling is the device that connects a semi-trailer to the towing truck, tractor unit, leading trailer, or dolly. It consists of a large, flat, horseshoe-shaped coupling plate mounted on the tractor — the "fifth wheel" — and a downward-facing steel pin, the kingpin, fixed to the front of the trailer. The kingpin slides into the slot of the fifth wheel and is gripped by locking jaws, allowing the trailer to pivot horizontally for cornering while the plate carries a large share of the trailer's weight.

Origin of the name

The name derives from horse-drawn wagons and carriages, on which a horizontal wheel over the front axle — literally a fifth wheel — allowed the front axle assembly to pivot for steering. Early motor trucks adopted a similar pivoting plate over the rear axle, and the name transferred to the modern coupling.

Operation

To couple, the driver reverses the tractor so the kingpin rides up the sloped tail of the plate and into the jaws, which snap shut; a safety check ("tug test" and visual inspection of the jaw and release handle) confirms the lock. Air and electrical lines are then connected. To uncouple, the trailer's landing gear is lowered, lines are disconnected, the jaws are released, and the tractor drives out. The plate is kept lubricated with grease or fitted with low-friction pads so the trailer can pivot smoothly; some fifth wheels also oscillate fore-and-aft or side-to-side for off-road and tanker use. Standard kingpins are 2 inches in diameter, with 3.5-inch pins used for heavy haulage.

Smaller versions of the fifth wheel are used in the beds of pickup trucks to tow large travel trailers ("fifth-wheel trailers") and gooseneck-like conversions, giving greater stability and weight capacity than a bumper hitch. Major manufacturers include Jost, SAF-Holland, and Fontaine.
