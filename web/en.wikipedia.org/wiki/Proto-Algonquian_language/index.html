Proto-Algonquian language

Proto-Algonquian (commonly abbreviated PA) is the reconstructed proto-language from which all of the Algonquian languages are descended. It was spoken roughly 2,500 to 3,000 years ago, most likely in the region around the Great Lakes and the upper Midwest/Great Lakes borderlands of present-day Canada and the United States, though its precise homeland is debated.

Reconstruction

Proto-Algonquian was first systematically reconstructed by Leonard Bloomfield in the 1920s–40s, whose work on the language became a landmark demonstration of the comparative method. Bloomfield established the phonemic inventory (including the consonant clusters and the vowel system) using four Central Algonquian languages — Fox, Cree, Ojibwe, and Menominee — and his reconstructions were later confirmed by evidence from other branches, a famous vindication of the method.

Features

Reconstructions posit four short and four long vowels and a consonant system including the cluster series (e.g., *çk, *xk, *xθ). The language was highly polysynthetic, with complex verbal morphology encoding animate/inanimate gender, obviation, and direct–inverse alignment — features inherited by its descendants, which include Cree, Ojibwe, Blackfoot, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Miami-Illinois, Shawnee, and the Eastern Algonquian languages such as Massachusett and Powhatan. Proto-Algonquian is itself a member of the larger Algic family alongside Wiyot and Yurok of California, from the more ancient Proto-Algic.
