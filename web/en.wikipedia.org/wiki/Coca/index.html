Coca

Coca plant with leaves and berries

Kingdom: Plantae

Family: Erythroxylaceae

Genus: Erythroxylum

Species: E. coca, E. novogranatense

Coca is any of the four cultivated plants in the family Erythroxylaceae native to western and northwestern South America, most notably Erythroxylum coca and Erythroxylum novogranatense. The coca plant plays a central role in traditional Andean culture, where its leaves have been chewed and brewed as a mild stimulant for thousands of years. The plant is best known internationally as the raw material from which the drug cocaine is extracted.

Description and cultivation

Coca is a shrub growing 2–3 metres high, with straight branches and oval, tapering leaves of a green tint. Two species are commercially cultivated: E. coca (Huanuco or Bolivian coca), grown on the eastern slopes of the Andes in Peru and Bolivia, and E. novogranatense, cultivated in Colombia, the Caribbean coast and in Java historically. Leaves can be harvested several times a year. The leaf contains a number of alkaloids, of which cocaine constitutes roughly 0.25–0.77% by dry weight in most varieties.

Traditional use

Archaeological evidence suggests coca leaves have been chewed in the Andes for at least 8,000 years. In Inca civilisation, coca was considered sacred — the gift of Mama Coca — and used in religious offerings and by runners (chasquis) to combat altitude sickness, hunger, fatigue and cold. Today, millions of people in Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia and northern Argentina chew coca leaves with an alkaline substance (such as lime or ash) or drink coca tea (mate de coca), a practice that is legal in several countries and regarded as a cultural heritage.

Legal status and the cocaine trade

Under the 1961 United Nations Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, the coca leaf was listed as a Schedule I substance and signatory states undertook to abolish coca chewing — a classification long contested by Andean countries. Bolivia withdrew from and re-acceded to the Convention in 2011–2013 with a reservation protecting traditional coca use. Most of the world's illicit coca cultivation occurs in Colombia, Peru and Bolivia, feeding the international cocaine trade; eradication and alternative-development programmes have been major elements of drug policy in the region for decades. "Decocainised" coca leaf extract remains a legal flavouring ingredient in Coca-Cola, produced under licence in the United States.
