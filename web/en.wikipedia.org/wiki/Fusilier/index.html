Fusilier

Fusilier is a name given to various kinds of soldiers; its meaning depends on the historical context. While fusilier originated as the designation for a soldier armed with a light flintlock musket (fusil), over time the term has come to be used for different roles in different armies, and today survives mainly in regimental titles and ceremonial distinctions.

History

The word comes from the French fusil, a light flintlock musket that was safer and more reliable than the matchlock it replaced. In the mid-17th century, fusiliers were originally guards for artillery trains, chosen because their flintlocks had no glowing match that might ignite the gunpowder they protected. King Louis XIV of France raised an early regiment of fusiliers in 1670.

In the 18th-century Prussian and other armies, fusiliers served as light infantry, distinguished by shorter stature and mitre caps, used for skirmishing. In the British Army, fusilier regiments were originally armed with fusils and retained distinctive badges and hackles; famous examples include the Royal Fusiliers, the Royal Welch Fusiliers and the Lancashire Fusiliers.

Modern usage

By the mid-19th century, rifled muskets made the technical distinction obsolete, and "fusilier" became an honorary title for certain infantry regiments. It survives today in units such as the Royal Regiment of Fusiliers in the British Army, the Royal 22e Régiment's French-Canadian counterparts in Canada, and fusilier units in several other armies. In Germany during the Second World War, fusilier battalions served as divisional reconnaissance units.
