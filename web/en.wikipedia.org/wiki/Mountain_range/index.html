Mountain range

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mountain range is a geographic chain of mountains connected by high ground, formed by the same geological processes. Ranges are built mainly by plate tectonics — the collision of continental plates crumpling rock upward (orogenesis), as in the Himalayas, where the Indian plate drives into Asia and raises Mount Everest — or by subduction and volcanism, as in the Andes, the longest continental range at about 7,000 km (see also plate tectonics).

Major ranges include the Himalayas and Karakoram in Asia, the Alps, Pyrenees and Caucasus in Europe, the Rockies and Appalachians in North America, the Atlas in Africa and the Great Dividing Range in Australia. Between ranges and hills lies no strict boundary, only convention.

Mountain ranges shape climate — forcing air to rise, cool and drop rain or snow on windward slopes and deserts on leeward sides — and they are the sources of most great rivers, including the Ganges, Yangtze and (via the Andes) the Amazon's headwaters. They harbour disproportionate biodiversity, including the species catalogued in resources such as the IUCN Red List, and are especially vulnerable to climate change as glaciers retreat.
