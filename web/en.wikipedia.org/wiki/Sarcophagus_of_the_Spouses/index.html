The Sarcophagus of the Spouses (Italian: Sarcofago degli Sposi) is a late sixth-century BC Etruscan anthropoid sarcophagus from Caere (modern Cerveteri). It is nearly two metres long and is crafted from terracotta.

Description

The sarcophagus depicts a married couple reclining together at a banquet in the afterlife. The husband and wife lie on a couch, their bodies elongated in the Archaic style, with smiling faces, almond-shaped eyes, and braided hair characteristic of Ionian Greek influence. The couple's intimate pose — the man gesturing and with his arm around his wife — reflects the relatively high status of women in Etruscan society.

Discovery and counterparts

The sarcophagus was excavated in fragments in the Banditaccia necropolis at Cerveteri in 1881 and reassembled. An almost identical sarcophagus is preserved in the Villa Giulia in Rome, while the better-known example is held by the Louvre in Paris.

Significance

The work is considered a masterpiece of Etruscan art, illustrating both the civilisation's banqueting customs and its distinctive practice of housing the ashes of the dead in vessels modelled on the deceased.
