Taarab

Stylistic originsArabic, Indian, African and Western music of the Swahili coast
Cultural originsLate 19th–early 20th century, Zanzibar
InstrumentsOud, qanun, violin, accordion, tabla, riq, dumbek
LanguageSwahili (sung poetry)
Key figuresSiti binti Saad; Bi Kidude; Culture Musical Club; Ikhwani Safaa

Taarab is a popular music genre of the Swahili coast of East Africa, centred on Zanzibar and the coastal towns of Tanzania and Kenya such as Mombasa and Dar es Salaam. Sung in Swahili, taarab combines poetic song with an orchestra mixing Arab, Indian, African and Western instruments, and it remains the classic music of weddings and celebrations along the coast.

Origins

The genre grew from the court culture of the Sultan of Zanzibar in the late 19th century, when Sultan Barghash is said to have sent a musician to Cairo for training and imported Egyptian ensembles; the word taarab derives from the Arabic tarab, musical ecstasy. Early ensembles were elite and male. The decisive popularization came with the singer Siti binti Saad (1880–1950), the first East African to make commercial recordings (for HMV in Bombay from 1928): she switched the repertoire to Swahili, drew lyrics from everyday life and satire, and made taarab the music of ordinary people.

Style

Classic taarab (taarab ya asili, "original taarab") uses large orchestras with violins, cello, double bass, oud, qanun, accordion and hand percussion, playing long, ornamented songs built on sung Swahili poetry (mashairi) rich in metaphor and proverb — indirect language that allows pointed social comment. Rival clubs such as Zanzibar's Culture Musical Club (founded 1958) and Ikhwani Safaa Musical Club (founded 1905) turned performances into celebrated competitions. From the 1990s a faster, dance-oriented, keyboard-driven offshoot — taarab ya kisasa or "rusha roho" ("heart-throwing") — with direct, teasing lyrics transformed the genre, especially in Dar es Salaam.

Performers

Beyond Siti binti Saad, emblematic artists include the singer Bi Kidude (c. 1910s–2013), famed for her powerful voice and unyago drumming; Mzee Yusuf; and modern orchestras such as Jahazi Modern Taarab. Taarab is a living emblem of Swahili identity across the western Indian Ocean.
