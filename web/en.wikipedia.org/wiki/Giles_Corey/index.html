Giles Corey

Giles Corey (c. August 1611 – September 19, 1692) was an English-born American farmer who was accused of witchcraft during the Salem witch trials in colonial Massachusetts. Refusing to enter a plea to the indictment, he was subjected to peine forte et dure — pressing with heavy stones — in an attempt to force him to plead, and died after two days of pressing without ever pleading. He is the only person in American history known to have been executed by pressing, and his death became one of the most famous episodes of the trials; tradition holds that his only words under the stones were "More weight."

Background

Corey emigrated from England to Salem, Massachusetts, where he became a prosperous farmer and a full member of the church. His life was not without blemish: in 1676 he was tried for beating a farmhand, Jacob Goodale, who died of his injuries; Corey was fined after being found guilty of unreasonable force. In 1692 his third wife, Martha Corey, was arrested for witchcraft after she had publicly doubted the accusers' claims; Giles initially testified against her, apparently believing the charges, but was himself accused in April 1692 by the afflicted girls and others, who claimed his spectre tormented them.

Refusal to plead and pressing

Brought before the Court of Oyer and Terminer in September 1692, Corey "stood mute": under English common law a defendant who refused to plead could neither be tried nor acquitted, and the court's remedy was peine forte et dure — the prisoner was laid down and progressively heavier stones or weights placed on a board on his chest until he pleaded or died. A common interpretation is that Corey refused to plead so that his estate would pass to his heirs rather than being forfeited to the colony upon conviction. Sheriff George Corwin supervised the pressing, which took place over two days, from 17 to 19 September; according to Robert Calef's later account, Corey's tongue was forced out of his mouth and the sheriff pushed it back with his cane. Corey died on 19 September 1692; Martha Corey was hanged three days later.

Legacy

Corey was excluded from burial in consecrated ground and buried near the place of his death; his name was cleared when the Massachusetts legislature reversed the attainders of the trials' victims in 1711, with compensation to his heirs. The episode is dramatised in Arthur Miller's play The Crucible (1953), where Corey's stubborn defiance symbolises integrity under persecution, and a memorial in Salem marks the site; local legend long held that the apparition of an elderly man seen before disasters was Corey's ghost, and that he had cursed Sheriff Corwin and his successors.
