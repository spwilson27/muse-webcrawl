Czech National Revival

The Czech National Revival (Czech: České národní obrození) was a cultural movement which took place in the Czech lands during the 18th and 19th centuries. The purpose of the movement was to revive the Czech language, culture and national identity. The most prominent figures of the revival were Josef Dobrovský and Josef Jungmann.

Background

After the Battle of White Mountain in 1620, the Bohemian lands were subjected to re-Catholicization and Germanization. The Czech language retreated to the countryside, and German became the language of administration, higher education and polite society in the towns. Czech survived mainly as the spoken language of the peasantry.

Phases of the revival

The revival is conventionally divided into phases. The defensive phase, led by scholars such as Josef Dobrovský, focused on codifying the Czech language; Dobrovský wrote a systematic grammar of Czech and a history of Czech literature, though he was famously sceptical about the language's prospects. The offensive phase, associated with Josef Jungmann, aimed to make Czech a full literary language again: Jungmann translated works of world literature into Czech and compiled a monumental five-volume Czech–German dictionary (1834–1839). His collaborator František Palacký wrote The History of the Czech Nation in Bohemia and Moravia, which framed the Czech past as a defining national narrative.

Cultural achievements

The revival produced lasting institutions: the National Museum in Prague (founded 1818), the journal and publishing house Matice česká, and above all the National Theatre in Prague, opened in 1881 (and reopened in 1883 after a fire), built with funds collected from the Czech public under the slogan "The Nation for Itself" (Národ sobě). Composers such as Bedřich Smetana and Antonín Dvořák embodied the national spirit in music.

Legacy

The revival transformed Czech from a declining peasant vernacular into a fully functional modern language of literature, scholarship and politics, and laid the cultural foundations for the creation of Czechoslovakia in 1918. A famous episode of the period was the controversy over the forged Královédvorský and Zelenohorský manuscripts, purported medieval epics that were championed as evidence of an ancient Czech literary tradition before being exposed as forgeries.
