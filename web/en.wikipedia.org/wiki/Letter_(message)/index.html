Letter (message)

A letter is a written message conveyed from one person (or group) to another, traditionally through a postal system, courier, or by hand. Letters range from brief notes to elaborate literary works, and have served for millennia as a principal means of personal, commercial, diplomatic, and legal communication.

History

Written messages on clay tablets, papyrus, and parchment date to the ancient Near East and Egypt; the epistles of Cicero, Seneca, and St Paul shaped the literary letter in the classical and Christian traditions. Systems of organised post developed from Persian and Roman relay couriers through medieval merchant and university posts to the public postal services of the early modern era. Cheap postage and the adhesive stamp — beginning with Britain's Penny Post of 1840 — made personal correspondence a mass phenomenon in the 19th and 20th centuries, before telephone and electronic mail displaced the personal letter in everyday life.

Form

A formal letter conventionally comprises the sender's address and date, the recipient's address, a salutation or greeting ("Dear Sir or Madam"), the body, a complimentary close ("Yours faithfully," "Sincerely"), and a signature. Letters are broadly classed as personal (friendly, condolence, love letters), business (cover letters, letters of complaint or recommendation), and official or open letters — the last, such as Zola's J'accuse…!, addressed to an individual but intended for the public.

As literature and evidence

Collections of correspondence are a major literary and historical genre: the letters of Abelard and Héloïse, of John Keats, and of Vincent van Gogh to his brother Theo are read as works of literature, and epistolary novels — from Pamela to Les Liaisons dangereuses and Dracula — imitate the letter form. Historians and biographers rely heavily on letters as primary sources for private life and thought.
