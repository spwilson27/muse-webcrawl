Shanghainese, sometimes known as the Shanghai dialect, is a variety of Wu Chinese spoken in the central districts of Shanghai and its surroundings. It is not mutually intelligible with Mandarin Chinese or Cantonese. Once the prestige lingua franca of the Yangtze River Delta region, it has declined since the 1990s under the promotion of Standard Mandarin, though local campaigns to preserve it have grown since the 2010s.

History

Historically, the speech of Shanghai county was similar to that of neighbouring Songjiang and Jiading. After Shanghai opened as a treaty port in 1842, immigration from Suzhou and Ningbo reshaped the urban dialect into a koine that became the region's prestige variety, widely used in commerce, publishing and entertainment during the Republican era.

Phonology

Like other northern Wu varieties, Shanghainese preserves a voicing contrast in obstruents (a three-way distinction of voiceless unaspirated, voiceless aspirated and voiced consonants) and has a system of checked syllables ending in a glottal stop. It is notable for its extensive tone sandhi: the pitch contour of a polysyllabic word is largely determined by the tone of its first syllable, giving the dialect a stress-like rhythm unusual among Chinese varieties. Older speakers distinguish more tones and vowels than younger speakers, whose speech shows heavy Mandarin influence.

Vocabulary and grammar

Shanghainese pronouns include ngu (I), non (you) and yi (he/she). The plural marker -la and sentence-final particles are characteristic. During the treaty-port era the dialect absorbed loanwords from English, such as terms for butter, cement and "stick" (adhesive), some of which later passed into general Chinese usage.

Status

Mandarin-only policies in schools and broadcasting sharply reduced Shanghainese transmission to children from the 1990s. Since the 2010s, preservation efforts have included Shanghainese announcements on public transport, local television segments, dictionaries, and classes aimed at young speakers.
