Pilgrimage of Grace
DateOctober 1536 – February 1537
LocationYorkshire, Lincolnshire and northern England
LeaderRobert Aske
StrengthUp to c. 30,000–40,000 "pilgrims" at its height
OutcomeRebels dispersed on royal promises; leaders arrested and executed; dissolution of the monasteries continued

The Pilgrimage of Grace was a popular uprising in northern England in 1536–37 against Henry VIII's break with the Roman Catholic Church and the policies of his chief minister Thomas Cromwell. Sparked by the dissolution of the smaller monasteries, new taxes and rumoured attacks on parish churches, it became the largest and most serious rebellion of the Tudor period, drawing in commoners, clergy and gentry under the banner of the Five Wounds of Christ. Its leader, the York lawyer Robert Aske, insisted the movement was a "pilgrimage" of loyal subjects petitioning the king, not a rebellion.

Course of the rising

The unrest began in Lincolnshire in early October 1536 — the Lincolnshire Rising — and spread across the Humber into Yorkshire, where Aske took command. The pilgrims swore an oath to defend the Catholic faith, restore the monasteries, remove "low-born" counsellors such as Cromwell, and resist taxation and enclosures. They occupied York, Hull and Pontefract Castle, whose keeper Lord Darcy surrendered it. At Doncaster some 30,000 pilgrims faced a much smaller royal army under the Duke of Norfolk, who — unable to fight — negotiated. At Pontefract in December the rebels presented their twenty-four articles, and Norfolk promised a free pardon, a parliament at York, and a halt to the dissolution pending its decisions. Trusting the promises, Aske persuaded the host to disperse.

Repression

Henry never intended to honour the terms. Fresh disturbances in early 1537 — notably Sir Francis Bigod's ill-timed rising — gave the government its pretext: martial law was declared in the north, and about 178–216 people were executed, including Aske (hanged in chains at York), Lord Darcy, Lord Hussey, Sir Robert Constable, Bigod, and the abbots of several great houses. The greater monasteries, many implicated in the rising, were now dissolved in turn, and the Council of the North was strengthened to tighten royal control of the region.

Significance

Historians debate the rebels' motives — religious conservatism looms largest in the pilgrims' own articles, alongside economic grievances, hostility to Cromwell's centralising regime and the later prayer-book settlement's roots. The Pilgrimage remains the most famous of the Tudor popular revolts and a touchstone for the argument that the English Reformation was imposed from above upon a largely unwilling population.
