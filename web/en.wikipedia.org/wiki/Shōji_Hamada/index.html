Shōji Hamada

Born: 9 December 1894, Tokyo, Japan
Died: 5 January 1978, Mashiko, Japan
Known for: Studio pottery, mingei movement

Shōji Hamada (濱田 庄司, Hamada Shōji, 9 December 1894 – 5 January 1978) was a Japanese potter and one of the most influential figures in 20th-century studio ceramics. A central figure in the mingei (folk craft) movement alongside the philosopher Yanagi Sōetsu and the English potter Bernard Leach, Hamada helped establish Mashiko, in Tochigi Prefecture, as a major pottery town, and was designated a Living National Treasure by the Japanese government in 1955.

Life and work

Hamada studied ceramics at the Tokyo Technical College (now Tokyo Institute of Technology), where he experimented with glazes under Itaya Hazan. In 1920 he travelled to England with Bernard Leach, whom he had met in Japan, and helped build the Leach Pottery at St Ives, Cornwall, including its traditional climbing kiln. After three years in St Ives, he returned to Japan and, following a period in Okinawa studying local pottery, settled in 1924 in Mashiko, a village then known for plain utilitarian ware.

At Mashiko, Hamada built a workshop and large noborigama climbing kiln, using local clays and materials — ash glazes, iron-bearing pigments, and kaki (persimmon) and tenmoku glazes. His work combined robust wheel-thrown forms with decoration in wax-resist, brushwork, and poured glazes, and he famously preferred intuition and local tradition over individualistic "art" pottery, signing little of his output.

Influence

Hamada's 1952 tour of the United States with Yanagi and Leach, including demonstrations at Black Mountain College, profoundly influenced American studio pottery. He received the Order of Culture in 1968. His Mashiko workshop is preserved as part of the Mashiko Sankokan Museum, and the town remains a living pottery centre attracting thousands of visitors to its twice-yearly pottery fairs.
