Radium

Radium is a chemical element with symbol Ra and atomic number 88, an almost pure-white alkaline earth metal that blackens on exposure to air and glows faintly due to its intense radioactivity. It was discovered by Marie and Pierre Curie in 1898 in uranium ore (pitchblende).

Radium decays through a chain headed by radon gas, ultimately to lead; radium-226 has a half-life of about 1,600 years. Its glow-in-the-dark paint — used on watch and instrument dials — caused devastating poisoning among the 'Radium Girls' who painted dials in the 1910s–20s and licked their brushes. Radium was once also used in radiotherapy and in quack health products; modern uses are far more restricted and largely replaced by safer isotopes.

Properties and history

The Curies isolated radium chloride from pitchblende obtained from Bohemia; metallic radium was prepared in 1910 by Marie Curie and André-Louis Debierne by electrolysis. Because it sits in the same group as calcium, radium accumulates in bones, explaining much of its toxicity.
