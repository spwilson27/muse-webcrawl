The 1796 United States presidential election was the third quadrennial presidential election, held from Friday, November 4, to Wednesday, December 7, 1796. It was the first contested American presidential election, the first presidential election in which political parties played a dominant role, and the only presidential election in which a president and vice president were elected from opposing tickets.

Incumbent President George Washington declined to run for a third term. With Washington absent, Federalist John Adams of Massachusetts and Democratic-Republican Thomas Jefferson of Virginia each sought the presidency. The Federalists also backed Thomas Pinckney of South Carolina as their intended vice president, while Democratic-Republicans backed Aaron Burr.

Campaign

The campaign was bitter and marked by attacks from both parties. Federalists attacked Jefferson as a radical Francophile who would ruin the country on the model of the French Revolution, while Democratic-Republicans attacked Adams as a monarchist. Foreign interference also occurred: the French ambassador, Pierre Adet, openly attempted to sway voters toward Jefferson.

Results

Adams won the presidency with 71 electoral votes to Jefferson's 68. Under the original constitutional procedure, the runner-up became vice president, so Jefferson — Adams's political rival — became his vice president. Pinckney finished third with 59 votes, and Burr fourth with 30. The peculiar result, a Federalist president and a Democratic-Republican vice president, helped motivate the ratification of the Twelfth Amendment in 1804, which required separate electoral votes for president and vice president.

Significance

The election of 1796 was the first real test of the American party system and set the stage for the even more contentious election of 1800, the first peaceful transfer of power between opposing parties in the United States.
