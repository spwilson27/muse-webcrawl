Local anaesthetic

A local anaesthetic (American English: local anesthetic) is a drug that causes a reversible loss of sensation in a specific, limited region of the body without loss of consciousness. Local anaesthetics are widely used in dentistry, minor surgery, and pain management, and for regional techniques such as epidural and spinal anaesthesia.

Mechanism

Local anaesthetics work by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve cell membranes. By preventing sodium influx, the drug stops the initiation and propagation of action potentials, so pain signals from the affected area do not reach the central nervous system. Smaller, unmyelinated pain fibres are generally blocked before larger motor fibres, which is why sensation can be lost while movement is partly preserved.

Types

Chemically, most local anaesthetics consist of a lipophilic aromatic ring joined by an intermediate chain to a hydrophilic amine group; they are classified as esters (such as procaine and benzocaine) or amides (such as lidocaine, bupivacaine, and ropivacaine). Amide agents are longer-acting and less allergenic, and dominate modern practice. Cocaine, the first local anaesthetic, introduced in the 1880s by Karl Koller for eye surgery, has both anaesthetic and vasoconstrictor properties.

Administration and safety

The agents may be applied topically to skin or mucosa, infiltrated into tissue around a wound, injected beside specific nerves (nerve blocks), or given into the epidural or intrathecal space. Adrenaline is often added to prolong the block and reduce bleeding. Excessive doses can cause systemic toxicity affecting the central nervous system and heart, so maximum recommended doses must be respected.
