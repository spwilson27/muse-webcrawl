The Guatemalan Civil War was a civil war in Guatemala fought from 1960 to 1996 between the country's government — backed considerably by the United States — and various leftist rebel groups. The war grew out of the 1954 CIA-backed coup that overthrew the democratically elected president Jacobo Árbenz, ending the reformist "Ten Years of Spring" and installing a succession of authoritarian, military-dominated governments.

Course of the war

The conflict began with a failed officers' revolt on 13 November 1960; surviving officers formed the first guerrilla movements. Successive insurgencies — including the FAR, EGP, ORPA, and PGT — united in 1982 as the Unidad Revolucionaria Nacional Guatemalteca (URNG). The state's counterinsurgency was at its most brutal in the late 1970s and early 1980s under generals Romeo Lucas García and Efraín Ríos Montt, whose "scorched earth" campaigns in the Maya highlands destroyed hundreds of villages.

Human rights

The UN-backed Commission for Historical Clarification (CEH) concluded in 1999 that some 200,000 people were killed or disappeared, that 93 percent of violations were attributable to state forces and allied paramilitaries, and that state agents committed acts of genocide against Maya groups in 1981–1983. Ríos Montt was convicted of genocide by a Guatemalan court in 2013, though the verdict was annulled on procedural grounds and he died in 2018 during a retrial.

Peace process

Negotiations brokered by the United Nations produced the Firm and Lasting Peace Accord, signed on 29 December 1996. The accords addressed indigenous rights, land, and military reform, but many commitments were only partially implemented, and Guatemala continues to struggle with violence, impunity, and inequality rooted in the war.
