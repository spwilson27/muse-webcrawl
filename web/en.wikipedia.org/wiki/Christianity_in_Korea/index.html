Christianity in Korea is a major religious tradition in South Korea and a historically significant, though heavily restricted, presence in North Korea. Unlike most of Asia, where Christian missions arrived with Western colonial expansion, Korean Catholicism began through the initiative of Korean Confucian scholars who encountered Catholic writings in Beijing and brought the faith home themselves. Today roughly three in ten South Koreans identify as Christian, making South Korea one of the most Christian countries in East Asia, home to some of the largest congregations in the world.

History

Catholic ideas reached Korea in the late 18th century through diplomatic missions to Qing China. In 1784 the scholar Yi Seung-hun was baptised in Beijing and returned to baptise others, founding a lay-led church. The new faith's rejection of ancestor rites brought persecution: thousands of Korean Catholics were martyred during the 19th century, including in the great persecutions of 1801, 1839 and 1866, when French missionaries and Korean converts were executed. In 1984 Pope John Paul II canonised 103 Korean martyrs in Seoul.

Protestant missions began in earnest in 1884–1885 with the arrival of Horace Newton Allen, Horace Grant Underwood and Henry Appenzeller. Missionaries founded schools and hospitals and translated the Bible into Hangul, contributing to literacy and nationalism. Christianity became associated with the independence movement under Japanese rule (1910–1945); many leaders of the March First Movement of 1919 were Christians.

Growth after 1945

After division, Christians fled persecution in the communist North, swelling churches in the South. Explosive growth followed in the 1960s–1980s alongside industrialisation and urbanisation. The Yoido Full Gospel Church in Seoul, founded by David Yonggi Cho, became the world's largest congregation. Korean churches also became major senders of missionaries abroad. Growth levelled off in the 21st century amid generational change and public controversies, though megachurches, house churches and a large Catholic minority remain prominent features of South Korean society.

North Korea

Before 1945 Pyongyang was a centre of Korean Protestantism, nicknamed the "Jerusalem of the East." Since the founding of the DPRK, independent religious practice has been suppressed; a small number of state-controlled churches exist in Pyongyang, and underground believers face severe punishment according to defector and human-rights reports.
