Carl Peter Henrik Dam (21 February 1895 – 17 April 1976) was a Danish biochemist and physiologist. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1943 for his discovery of vitamin K, the blood-clotting vitamin. He shared the prize with the American biochemist Edward A. Doisy, who had determined the chemical structure of the vitamin.

Early life and education

Dam was born in Copenhagen, the son of a pharmacist. He graduated in chemistry from the Polytechnic Institute in Copenhagen in 1920 and held teaching posts in chemistry and physiology before becoming an instructor at the University of Copenhagen's Physiological Laboratory in 1928. In 1934 he received his doctorate in biochemistry with a thesis on the biological significance of the sterols.

Discovery of vitamin K

In the late 1920s, Dam was studying cholesterol metabolism in chicks fed a diet from which the fats had been extracted. After several weeks the chicks developed haemorrhages and their blood failed to clot normally — a syndrome that could not be cured by adding pure cholesterol back to the diet. Dam concluded that the food must contain another, previously unrecognised protective factor. In a 1934 paper he proposed the name vitamin K, from the Danish and German word Koagulationsvitamin. He went on to isolate the vitamin from green leaves and lucerne (alfalfa) and showed it was fat-soluble and distinct from all previously known vitamins.

In 1940 Dam moved to the United States to escape the disruption of the war years, working at the Marine Biological Laboratory at Woods Hole and at the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research in New York, where he learned in 1943 that he had won the Nobel Prize. He returned to Denmark in 1946 as professor of biochemistry and nutrition at the Polytechnic Institute in Copenhagen, later moving to the Danish Fat Research Institute. His later research dealt with vitamin E and the role of nutrition in gallstone formation.

Legacy

Vitamin K proved essential for the synthesis of several blood-clotting factors, including prothrombin, and its discovery led quickly to clinical uses: treating haemorrhagic disease of the newborn (now routinely prevented by a vitamin K injection at birth) and counteracting overdoses of anticoagulant drugs such as warfarin, which act by antagonising vitamin K.
