10 Gigabit Ethernet

10 Gigabit Ethernet (10GE, 10GbE, or 10 GigE) is a group of computer networking technologies for transmitting Ethernet frames at a rate of 10 gigabits per second. It was first defined by the IEEE 802.3ae-2002 standard. Unlike earlier Ethernet standards, 10 Gigabit Ethernet defines only full-duplex point-to-point links, which are generally connected by network switches.

Standards

The IEEE 802.3 working group has standardized several physical layers for 10 GbE, including fiber-optic variants (10GBASE-SR, -LR, -ER, and -ZR), the 10GBASE-CX4 copper standard, backplane Ethernet (10GBASE-KR), and 10GBASE-T for twisted-pair cabling, ratified in 2006, which allows 10 Gbit/s over unshielded twisted pair for distances up to 100 metres.

Encoding

Most 10-gigabit physical layers use 64b/66b encoding, which scrambles data into 66-bit blocks to maintain DC balance and clock recovery with only about 3% overhead — a major improvement over the 8b/10b encoding used by earlier Gigabit Ethernet, which imposed 25% overhead.

Adoption

10 Gigabit Ethernet became common in data centers, enterprise backbones, and metropolitan area networks during the 2000s and 2010s, and it laid the groundwork for 25, 40, 100, and 400 Gigabit Ethernet.
