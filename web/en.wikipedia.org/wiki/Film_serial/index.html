Film serial

A film serial, also known as a movie serial or chapter play, is a motion picture form popular during the first half of the 20th century, consisting of a series of short films exhibited in consecutive order at one theatre, generally advancing a single, multipart story over the course of weeks. Each chapter was screened at the same theatre on the same day of the week and usually ended with a cliffhanger, in which characters found themselves in seemingly inescapable peril, to be resolved at the beginning of the next instalment.

History

The serial emerged in the 1910s, following serialised magazine fiction. Edison's What Happened to Mary (1912) is often cited as an early example, and The Perils of Pauline (1914) made the "serial queen" melodramas famous. Serials flourished in the silent era in the United States and France, where Louis Feuillade's Fantômas (1913) and Les Vampires (1915) became classics.

With the coming of sound, the serial became a staple of Saturday-afternoon matinees aimed chiefly at children. Studios such as Universal, Republic Pictures, and Columbia produced serials based on comic strips, radio shows, pulp magazines and comic books: Flash Gordon (1936), The Lone Ranger (1938), Adventures of Captain Marvel (1941), and Batman (1943) among them. Republic became especially noted for its action choreography, stunt work, and special effects. Production costs and the rise of television ended the form; the last American serial was Columbia's Blazing the Overland Trail (1956).

Characteristics

Sound-era serials typically had 12 to 15 chapters of about 15–20 minutes each (the first chapter often longer). Cliffhanger endings — cars plunging over cliffs, buildings exploding with the hero inside — were sometimes resolved by "cheats" that rewound or altered the previous week's scene. Serials strongly influenced later adventure filmmaking; George Lucas cited them as an inspiration for Star Wars and Raiders of the Lost Ark, with its hero Indiana Jones modelled on serial adventurers.
