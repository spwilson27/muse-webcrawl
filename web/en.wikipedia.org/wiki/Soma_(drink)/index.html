Soma (drink)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the Vedic tradition of ancient India, soma (Sanskrit: सोम, sóma) was a ritual drink of great religious significance. The drink was prepared by extracting the juice of a plant with the same name, pressing the stalks between stones or pounding them in a mortar, filtering the juice through sheep's wool, and mixing it with water and milk. Soma is praised throughout the Rigveda — the entire ninth book (the Soma Mandala, 114 hymns) is devoted to Soma Pavamana, "soma being purified" — and the drink, the plant, and the god Soma were regarded as aspects of a single divine reality.

The ritual

Soma was the centre of the great soma sacrifices of Vedic religion, in which the pressed juice was offered to the gods, especially Indra, who was said to drink vast quantities before battle, and consumed by the priests. The hymns describe soma as exhilarating and inspirative: it grants strength, poetic insight, and a foretaste of immortality. The Avestan tradition of Zoroastrianism preserves a close cognate, haoma, likewise a sacred pressed plant juice with its own deity.

Identity of the plant

The identity of the original soma plant was lost as the Indo-Aryans moved away from its habitat, and later Indian tradition substituted other plants. Modern candidates proposed by scholars include species of Ephedra, the fly agaric mushroom Amanita muscaria (argued by R. Gordon Wasson in 1968), Syrian rue (Peganum harmala), cannabis, and Sarcostemma. No consensus has been reached; the question remains one of the most debated in Indo-Iranian studies.

Legacy

The name soma was borrowed by Aldous Huxley for the state-sanctioned pleasure drug in his 1932 dystopian novel Brave New World, a fictional drug rather than the Vedic drink.
