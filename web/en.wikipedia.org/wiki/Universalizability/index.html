Universalizability

Universalizability is a principle in moral philosophy according to which moral judgments must be capable of being applied universally: if an action is right (or obligatory) for one person in a given situation, it must be right for every relevantly similar person in a relevantly similar situation. The concept is most famously associated with Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative, whose first formulation commands: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it become a universal law."

Kant

In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), Kant tested maxims by whether their universal adoption generates a contradiction — either a contradiction in conception (the universalized maxim cannot even be coherently conceived, as with universal false promising, which would destroy the practice of promising) or a contradiction in the will (the agent cannot rationally will the universalized maxim).

Hare and later developments

R. M. Hare made universalizability (together with prescriptivity) a defining feature of moral language in The Language of Morals (1952): to say "you ought to do X" logically commits one to prescribing X for anyone in identical circumstances, including oneself in reversed roles. The principle also underlies related tests such as the Golden Rule, veil-of-ignorance reasoning, and "what if everyone did that?" arguments. Critics have objected that the principle is purely formal and empty without substantive premises, that results depend on how maxims are described, and that it struggles with fanatical or gerrymandered maxims — objections debated extensively in Kantian scholarship.
