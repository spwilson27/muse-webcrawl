Kunstformen der Natur (known in English as Art Forms in Nature) is a book of lithographic and halftone prints by the German naturalist Ernst Haeckel. Originally published in sets of ten between 1899 and 1904 and collectively in two volumes in 1904, it consists of 100 prints of various organisms, many of which were first described by Haeckel himself.

Content

The plates depict marine and other organisms — radiolarians, jellyfish (medusae), sea anemones, siphonophores, cephalopods, bats, frogs, lizards, hummingbirds, orchids and antelope among them — arranged with striking symmetry and decorative composition. Haeckel's master lithographer Adolf Giltsch translated his drawings into the published prints. The radiolarian plates drew on specimens from the Challenger expedition, on which Haeckel was the leading authority. Many taxonomic names coined by Haeckel in his scientific monographs survive today.

Influence

The book is regarded as a bridge between science and art. Its organic, symmetrical motifs strongly influenced the Art Nouveau movement — the architect René Binet's monumental gate for the 1900 Paris Exposition was modelled on a Haeckel radiolarian — and it remains a touchstone for scientific illustrators, designers and artists exploring biomorphic form. Scholarly reassessments note that Haeckel sometimes idealized and regularized his specimens, arranging them for aesthetic effect beyond strict accuracy.
