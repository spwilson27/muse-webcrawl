Kīlauea is an active shield volcano in the Hawaiian Islands. Located along the southeastern shore of the island of Hawaii, it is between 210,000 and 280,000 years old and emerged above sea level about 100,000 years ago. It is the second-youngest volcano of the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain and the current eruptive centre of the Hawaiian hotspot.

Geology

Kīlauea has a large summit caldera containing Halemaʻumaʻu crater. Two rift zones extend from the summit — the East Rift Zone and the Southwest Rift Zone — along which most eruptions occur. In Hawaiian religion the volcano is the home of the goddess Pele.

Eruptive history

Kīlauea is one of the world's most active volcanoes. Its eruption from 3 January 1983 at Puʻu ʻŌʻō on the East Rift Zone continued for 35 years, adding land, destroying the communities of Kalapana and Royal Gardens, and burying the village of Kapoho's outskirts. In May 2018 the eruption shifted to the lower East Rift Zone in Leilani Estates: fissures destroyed more than 700 structures and reshaped the coast, while the summit caldera collapsed in a series of explosions. Summit eruptions resumed in December 2020 and have continued episodically, with lava lakes and fountaining within Halemaʻumaʻu.

Monitoring and tourism

The volcano is monitored by the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (founded 1912) and forms the core of Hawaii Volcanoes National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
