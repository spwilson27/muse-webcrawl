Psychosomatic medicine

Psychosomatic medicine is an interdisciplinary medical field exploring the relationships among social, psychological and behavioural factors on bodily processes and quality of life in humans and animals. It studies how mental states influence physical health and how physical illness affects the mind — in the modern view, most diseases are considered psychosomatic in the sense that mental and physical elements interact throughout their course.

History

The term was coined by Johann Christian August Heinroth in 1818. The field grew in the early 20th century through the work of Franz Alexander and the Chicago Institute for Psychoanalysis, which proposed that specific emotional conflicts contributed to specific diseases — the "holy seven" psychosomatic disorders, including peptic ulcer, ulcerative colitis, asthma, hypertension and hyperthyroidism. Modern psychosomatic medicine has moved away from simple linear causation toward biopsychosocial models, as articulated by George Engel in 1977.

Scope

The specialty, also called consultation-liaison psychiatry in hospital practice, addresses conditions such as somatic symptom disorder, functional disorders (irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue), stress-related exacerbations of physical disease, and the psychiatric complications of medical illness and transplantation. Psychoneuroimmunology is a closely related research field.

Treatment

Management combines medical care with psychological interventions — cognitive behavioural therapy, stress management, relaxation techniques and, where indicated, psychotropic medication.
