Inverse (logic)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic and mathematics, the inverse of a conditional statement "If P, then Q" is the statement "If not P, then not Q" (¬P → ¬Q). Together with the converse (Q → P) and the contrapositive (¬Q → ¬P), the inverse is one of the three standard transformations of a conditional.

Logical equivalence

StatementFormEquivalent to
ConditionalP → QContrapositive
ConverseQ → PInverse
Inverse¬P → ¬QConverse
Contrapositive¬Q → ¬PConditional

The inverse is not logically equivalent to the original conditional: from "If it is raining, the ground is wet" one cannot infer "If it is not raining, the ground is not wet", since sprinklers may also wet the ground. The inverse is, however, the contrapositive of the converse, so the inverse and the converse are equivalent to each other. Inferring the inverse from a conditional is a common logical error related to denying the antecedent.

Example

For the true statement "If a figure is a square, then it is a rectangle", the inverse is "If a figure is not a square, then it is not a rectangle", which is false (a non-square may still be a rectangle) — showing that a conditional and its inverse can differ in truth value.
