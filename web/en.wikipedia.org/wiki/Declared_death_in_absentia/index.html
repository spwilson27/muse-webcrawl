Declared death in absentia

Memorials to the missing: in law, a court may declare a missing person dead without recovery of a body.

A declaration of death in absentia (or presumed death) is a legal determination, made by a court, that a missing person is dead, even though no body has been found and the death cannot be proved directly. Such declarations allow the person's estate to be administered, life insurance to be paid, and a spouse to remarry. In common-law jurisdictions the underlying concept is the presumption of death.

Legal basis

Traditionally, the common law presumed a person dead after an unexplained absence of seven years during which they had not been heard from by those who would naturally have heard from them. Modern statutes have codified and often shortened this rule. In England and Wales, the Presumption of Death Act 2013 allows the High Court to make a declaration that a missing person is presumed dead, with effect equivalent to a death certificate, either after seven years' absence or sooner if the evidence suggests the person has died. Similar procedures exist across the United States, generally with five-to-seven-year waiting periods, and courts may dispense with the wait where the person disappeared in circumstances of specific mortal peril, such as a shipwreck, air crash or natural disaster.

Mass disasters and expulsions of doubt

Special procedures have repeatedly been created after catastrophes. Following the September 11 attacks, New York expedited death certificates for victims whose remains were never recovered. After wars and enforced disappearances, states have issued declarations for missing soldiers and victims of regimes, a step of great symbolic as well as legal significance for their families.

If the person returns

A declaration of presumed death can be varied or revoked if the person proves to be alive. Legislation typically protects good-faith transactions made in reliance on the declaration, such as distributions of the estate, while allowing the returned person to recover property that remains; marriages dissolved by the declaration are not automatically revived. Famous historical cases include those of the Australian prime minister Harold Holt, who vanished while swimming in 1967, and the numerous legal disputes over people declared dead who reappeared — sometimes because of fraud or mistaken identity.
