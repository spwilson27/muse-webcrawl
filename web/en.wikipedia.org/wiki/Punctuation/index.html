Punctuation

Punctuation is the use of spacing, conventional signs (called punctuation marks), and certain typographical devices as aids to the understanding and correct reading of written text, either silent or aloud. Common marks in English include the full stop (period), comma, colon, semicolon, question mark, exclamation mark, quotation marks, brackets, apostrophe, hyphen, and dash.

History

Ancient Greek and Latin texts were often written in scriptio continua, without word spacing or punctuation. The librarian Aristophanes of Byzantium is credited in the 3rd century BCE with a system of single dots at different heights to mark pauses. Medieval scribes added a variety of marks to aid reading aloud in monasteries and churches. The invention of printing in the 15th century gradually standardized punctuation; the Venetian printer Aldus Manutius is credited with early systematic use of the comma and semicolon. Punctuation grew heavier in the 18th and 19th centuries and lighter again in the 20th, as style shifted from marking rhetorical pauses to marking grammatical structure.

Functions

Punctuation principally serves to clarify syntax — showing where sentences end, how clauses relate, and which words group together — but it also conveys tone and emphasis. Misplaced punctuation can change meaning, as in the contrast between "Let's eat, Grandma" and "Let's eat Grandma." Different languages use different conventions: Spanish uses inverted opening question and exclamation marks, French places spaces before some marks, and Chinese and Japanese use full-width marks such as 、and 。.

In the digital age

Computing introduced new punctuation practices: emoticons and emoji express tone in text messages, hashtags and @-mentions function as markers, and marks such as the interrobang (‽) and irony punctuation have been proposed, though few have entered general use.
