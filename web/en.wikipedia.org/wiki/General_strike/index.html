General strike

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A general strike is a strike action in which participants cease all work across a community, city or country — a substantial proportion of the total labour force — rather than at a single employer or in a single trade. It is the trade-union movement's ultimate weapon and, in syndicalist theory (Sorel's myth of the general strike), the revolutionary act itself: the working class withdrawing its labour stops the world and starts another.

History

Practice has been more political than revolutionary: general strikes as national protest and defence of democracy — Belgium's suffrage strikes, Sweden 1909, Germany's strike that defeated the Kapp Putsch (1920), Britain's nine-day General Strike of 1926 (a solidarity action for miners, defeated and followed by restrictive legislation), Winnipeg 1919, San Francisco and Oakland 1934/1946, France in 1936 and May 1968 (the largest stoppage in an advanced economy, ending in wage settlements and Gaullist electoral victory), Poland's Solidarity strikes (1980, mid-wifing the union's legalisation), and recurring one-day general strikes in India, Greece, Spain and Italy against austerity and labour reforms. Legality varies (political strikes unprotected in many jurisdictions, including the US and UK), as does effect: general strikes reliably demonstrate labour's social weight and disrupt; their record at extracting durable political concessions depends on whether they open negotiations or dare governments to outlast them.
