In mathematics, particularly in complex analysis, a Riemann surface is a one-dimensional complex manifold — a surface that locally looks like the complex plane, glued together by holomorphic transition maps. Introduced in Bernhard Riemann's 1851 doctoral dissertation, Riemann surfaces turn multi-valued complex functions such as √z and log z into ordinary single-valued functions defined on the surface.

Definitions and examples

Riemann surfaces can be studied as complex manifolds, as algebraic curves, or as real surfaces with a conformal structure. The simplest examples are the complex plane itself and the Riemann sphere (the plane plus a point at infinity). Every simply connected Riemann surface is conformally equivalent to the plane, the sphere, or the open unit disc — the content of the uniformization theorem. Compact Riemann surfaces of genus g are classified by their genus: genus 0 is the sphere, genus 1 gives complex tori (elliptic curves), and higher genera give surfaces with richer moduli.

Functions and significance

Meromorphic functions on a Riemann surface form a function field; on a compact surface the Riemann–Roch theorem counts the dimensions of spaces of such functions with prescribed poles. The theory underlies modern algebraic geometry, number theory (via modular curves) and mathematical physics, including string theory, where Riemann surfaces are the worldsheets of strings.
