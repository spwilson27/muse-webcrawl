Shiraz (Persian: شیراز) is the fifth-most-populous city of Iran and the capital of Fars Province, in the southwest of the country. Celebrated in Persian culture as a city of poets, gardens, nightingales and — before 1979 — wine, it was a major medieval centre of Islamic learning and served as Iran's capital under several dynasties, most splendidly under the Zand dynasty in the 18th century.

History

The modern city was founded or refounded in the early Islamic period, around 693 CE, near the Achaemenid ceremonial sites whose ruins lie at Persepolis and Pasargadae nearby. Shiraz flourished under the Buyids in the 10th–11th centuries and, having negotiated surrender to the Mongols, escaped destruction and became a haven for scholars and poets. It is the birthplace and burial place of the poets Saadi (d. 1291) and Hafez (d. 1390), whose tombs in garden settings remain places of pilgrimage. Under Karim Khan Zand, who made Shiraz his capital in the mid-18th century, the city gained its citadel (Arg), the Vakil mosque, bazaar and bathhouse, and many of its famous gardens.

Culture

Shiraz is famed for its gardens — Eram, Narenjestan, Delgosha and the tomb gardens of Hafez and Saadi — designed around cypresses, orange trees, roses and water channels. The region's name was lent, by associations now debated by historians, to the Shiraz (Syrah) wine grape tradition; the city was renowned for wine for centuries before the Islamic Revolution ended production. Landmarks include the pink-tiled Nasir al-Mulk Mosque, famed for its stained-glass light, the Shah Cheragh shrine, and the Qur'an Gate.

Economy and transport

Today Shiraz is a centre for electronics and cement industries, agriculture and tourism, with an international airport and a metro system opened in 2014. Its universities include Shiraz University and the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences.
