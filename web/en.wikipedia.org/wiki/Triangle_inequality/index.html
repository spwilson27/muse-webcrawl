Triangle inequality

In mathematics, the triangle inequality states that for any triangle, the sum of the lengths of any two sides must be greater than or equal to the length of the remaining side. For sides of length a, b and c:

a + b ≥ c,   b + c ≥ a,   c + a ≥ b

Equality holds only in the degenerate case, where the three vertices are collinear and the "triangle" collapses into a line segment. The inequality is intuitively obvious — the shortest path between two points is a straight line — but it is a fundamental axiom of geometry and analysis.

Normed vector spaces

In any normed vector space, the triangle inequality generalises to ||x + y|| ≤ ||x|| + ||y|| for all vectors x and y. It is one of the defining properties of a norm. Metric spaces likewise require the axiom d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) for every distance function.

Reverse triangle inequality

A consequence of the triangle inequality is the reverse form, ||x|| − ||y|| ≤ ||x − y||, which bounds the difference of the norms by the norm of the difference.

History

The inequality appears in Euclid's Elements, Book I, Proposition 20: "In any triangle two sides taken together in any manner are greater than the remaining one." Euclid's proof constructs an isosceles triangle to compare the relevant lengths.
