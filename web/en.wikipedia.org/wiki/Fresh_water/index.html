Fresh water

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fresh water is water with low concentrations of dissolved salts — conventionally less than about 0.05% salts — found in lakes, rivers, streams, glaciers, ice caps and groundwater. Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh; of that, roughly two-thirds is locked in glaciers and ice caps (chiefly Antarctica and Greenland), about a third is groundwater, and a tiny fraction — around 1% of fresh water — is the surface water in lakes, rivers and swamps on which ecosystems and most human use visibly depend. Lake Baikal alone holds about 20% of the world's unfrozen surface fresh water.

Distribution and scarcity

Fresh water is distributed very unevenly: the Amazon carries about a fifth of global river discharge, while arid regions from the Arabian Peninsula to the Caspian basin face chronic scarcity. The World Health Organization and UN estimate that over two billion people live in countries under high water stress, and billions lack safely managed drinking water — with waterborne disease the deadly consequence. Agriculture consumes about 70% of withdrawals. Aquifers such as the Ogallala (US) and those under the North China Plain are being depleted faster than recharge, while climate change shifts rainfall, melts glaciers that act as seasonal reservoirs, and intensifies both droughts and floods.

Ecosystems

Freshwater habitats — less than 1% of Earth's surface — host a disproportionate share of species (about 10% of known animals), and freshwater populations have declined faster than terrestrial or marine ones since 1970 (by over 80% on average in monitored populations, per WWF's Living Planet Index). Conservation of rivers and wetlands is therefore among the highest-leverage fronts in conservation biology.
