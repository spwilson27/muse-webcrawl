Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem

In transcendental number theory, the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem is a result concerning the algebraic independence of exponentials of algebraic numbers. It is a powerful tool for establishing that many familiar numbers are transcendental.

Theorem. If α1, …, αn are algebraic numbers that are linearly independent over the rational numbers ℚ, then eα1, …, eαn are algebraically independent over ℚ.

History

The theorem was proved in stages. Charles Hermite proved in 1873 that e is transcendental. Ferdinand von Lindemann extended the methods in 1882 to prove that π is transcendental, and stated the general result; Karl Weierstrass gave the first complete proof in 1885. A closely related consequence, sometimes called Lindemann's theorem, states that if α is a non-zero algebraic number, then eα is transcendental.

Consequences

Because π must be irrational in a strong sense for products with algebraic numbers to behave correctly, the transcendence of π implies the impossibility of squaring the circle with compass and straightedge. The theorem also yields the transcendence of the natural logarithm of any algebraic number other than 0 and 1, and of values such as sin(1) and other trigonometric values at non-zero algebraic points.

Related results

The Gelfond–Schneider theorem (1934) is a related partial result solving Hilbert's seventh problem. Schanuel's conjecture, still unproved, would imply both the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem and the Gelfond–Schneider theorem.
