An archbishop is a bishop of higher rank or office in several Christian traditions, including Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Anglicanism, and some Lutheran and other Protestant churches. The word comes from the Greek archiepiskopos, "chief overseer".

Roles

In the Roman Catholic Church, an archbishop ordinarily governs an archdiocese, usually the principal see of an ecclesiastical province; a metropolitan archbishop has limited supervisory authority over the suffragan dioceses of the province and receives the woollen pallium from the pope as a sign of communion and jurisdiction. Other archbishops hold the title personally (ad personam), serve in the diplomatic corps as nuncios, or head offices of the Roman Curia. In canon law, all archbishops are bishops; the title confers precedence and certain functions rather than a separate sacramental order.

In Eastern Orthodoxy, usage varies: in the Greek tradition an archbishop ranks above a metropolitan, while in the Slavic tradition the reverse holds. In the Anglican Communion, archbishops head provinces: the Archbishop of Canterbury is the senior bishop of the Church of England and the symbolic head of the worldwide Communion, alongside the Archbishop of York and archbishops of other provinces such as Wales, Ireland, Canada, Australia, and Southern Africa.

History

The title emerged in the 4th–5th centuries for the bishops of great sees — Alexandria, Antioch, Constantinople, and others — as the Church organised itself into provinces headed by metropolitans. Over time "archbishop" became the standard title of a metropolitan in the Latin West, formalised in medieval canon law.

Notable offices

  • Archbishop of Canterbury (Church of England, est. 597)
  • Archbishop of Constantinople — the Ecumenical Patriarch
  • Metropolitan archbishops of major Catholic sees such as New York, Paris, Munich, and São Paulo
