A mental representation (or cognitive representation) is a hypothetical internal cognitive symbol that represents external reality, or a mental process that makes use of such a symbol. Mental representations are the objects of beliefs, desires, perceptions, and other intentional states: to believe that it is raining is, on representational theories, to stand in a certain relation to a representation with the content it is raining.

The representational theory of mind

The representational theory of mind holds that propositional attitudes are relations between a thinker and mental representations, typically conceived as sentences in an internal "language of thought". Jerry Fodor's language of thought hypothesis proposes that thinking occurs in a compositional symbolic system, sometimes called Mentalese, whose structure explains the productivity and systematicity of thought.

Theories of content

A central problem is explaining how a mental representation gets its content — what makes it represent one thing rather than another. Major approaches include causal and informational theories (Fred Dretske), teleosemantics, which grounds content in biological function (Ruth Millikan), and conceptual-role semantics, which fixes content by a representation's inferential relations to other representations.

Formats of representation

Beyond sentence-like representations, researchers posit pictorial or map-like representations (mental imagery, cognitive maps), distributed representations in connectionist networks, and embodied or enactive accounts that challenge the need for internal symbols altogether. The debate between classical, connectionist, and embodied approaches remains central to cognitive science.
