Distributed cognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Distributed cognition is an approach in cognitive science that holds that cognitive processes are not confined to the mind of an individual, but are distributed across people, artifacts, and the environment. Knowledge lies not only in the individual but in their social and physical surroundings: a group of people collaborating with tools forms a cognitive system in its own right.

Origins

The framework was developed in the 1980s and 1990s by Edwin Hutchins and colleagues at the University of California, San Diego. Hutchins's classic study Cognition in the Wild (1995) analysed navigation aboard a US Navy ship, showing that the task of fixing the ship's position was accomplished by a system of crew members, instruments, charts, and procedures, no one of whom performed the whole computation alone. Earlier related ideas include Vygotsky's cultural-historical psychology and the notion that tools mediate thought.

Principles

  • Cognition is distributed among members of a social group, through communication and coordination.
  • Cognition is distributed between people and artifacts, such as checklists, abacuses, cockpit instruments, and software, which store and transform information.
  • Cognition is distributed through time, so that earlier events and cultural products shape later ones.

The unit of analysis is therefore the whole socio-technical system, studied with methods from ethnography as well as cognitive psychology.

Applications

Distributed cognition has influenced human–computer interaction, education, organisational studies, and the design of safety-critical workplaces such as aircraft cockpits and hospital wards, where understanding how information flows through a system helps prevent error. It is closely related to situated cognition, extended cognition, and activity theory.
