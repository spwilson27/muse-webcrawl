Macedonia is a geographical and historical region of the Balkan Peninsula in southeastern Europe. Its boundaries have changed considerably over time; today it is generally defined as a region of about 67,000 square kilometres (26,000 sq mi) divided among several countries: the largest share lies in Greece (Greek Macedonia, roughly half the region), followed by North Macedonia, and Bulgaria (Blagoevgrad Province, often called Pirin Macedonia), with smaller portions in Albania, Kosovo, and Serbia.

The name derives from the ancient kingdom of Macedon, which under Philip II and Alexander the Great in the 4th century BC expanded from its heartland in the northeastern Greek peninsula to rule an empire stretching to Egypt and India. The ancient capital was Pella, and the region's second city, Thessalonica (modern Thessaloniki), founded around 315 BC, remains its largest urban centre.

History

After Rome conquered Macedon in 168 BC, Macedonia became a Roman province and later passed to the Byzantine Empire. Slavic peoples settled the region from the 6th century AD. It was contested by the Bulgarian, Serbian, and Byzantine empires in the Middle Ages, then ruled by the Ottoman Empire for roughly five centuries. The "Macedonian Question" — rivalry among Greece, Serbia, and Bulgaria over the region — led to the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, in which Ottoman Macedonia was partitioned among the three victors, roughly along the lines that persist today. Vardar Macedonia became part of Serbia and then Yugoslavia, emerging as the independent Republic of Macedonia in 1991 — renamed North Macedonia in 2019 after the Prespa agreement resolved its naming dispute with Greece.

Geography

The region is largely mountainous, drained by the Vardar (Axios) and Struma (Strymon) rivers, and includes lakes Ohrid and Prespa in the west. Its principal cities are Thessaloniki and Skopje, followed by Bitola, Prilep, Blagoevgrad, Kavala, and Serres.
