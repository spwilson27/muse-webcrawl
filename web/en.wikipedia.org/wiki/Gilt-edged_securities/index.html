Gilt-edged securities

Gilt-edged securities, commonly called gilts, are bonds issued by the UK government. The term is also used in some Commonwealth countries for similar government securities, particularly in India, where "gilt-edged securities" (G-Secs) are issued by the Reserve Bank of India on behalf of the Government of India. The name derives from the original certificates, which had gilded edges, and by extension from the securities' reputation as among the safest investments available — "gilt-edged" became a general expression for high quality and low risk.

Types

Conventional gilts are nominal bonds paying a fixed coupon twice a year and redeeming at par (100) on a set maturity date; they are named by coupon and year, e.g. "4¼% Treasury Gilt 2034". Index-linked gilts, first issued in 1981 — making the UK a pioneer of inflation-linked bonds — have coupon and principal payments adjusted in line with the Retail Prices Index, providing investors with protection against inflation. A small number of undated gilts (successors to the famous Consols and War Loan) existed until the government redeemed the last of them in 2015.

Issuance and holders

Since 1998 gilts have been issued by the Debt Management Office (DMO), an executive agency of HM Treasury, mostly by auction to gilt-edged market makers (GEMMs). Major holders include pension funds and insurance companies (which favour long-dated and index-linked gilts to match liabilities), overseas investors, and the Bank of England, whose Asset Purchase Facility accumulated roughly a third of the market through quantitative easing after 2009. Because the UK borrows in its own currency, gilts have historically carried top credit ratings and serve as the benchmark "risk-free" asset for sterling interest rates.

Market events

Gilt yields move inversely to prices and anchor UK borrowing costs. In September–October 2022, following the government's "mini-budget", gilt yields spiked violently and prices collapsed, forcing the Bank of England to intervene with emergency purchases to protect pension funds using liability-driven investment strategies — an episode that demonstrated the market's systemic importance.
