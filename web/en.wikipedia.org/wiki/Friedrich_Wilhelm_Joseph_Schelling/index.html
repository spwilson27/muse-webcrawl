Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling

Born: 27 January 1775, Leonberg, Duchy of Württemberg
Died: 20 August 1854 (aged 79), Bad Ragaz, Switzerland
Era: 19th-century philosophy
School: German idealism, Naturphilosophie
Main interests: Metaphysics, philosophy of nature, philosophy of art, philosophy of religion

Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling (27 January 1775 – 20 August 1854), later von Schelling, was a German philosopher. Standard histories of philosophy mark him as a central figure in the development of German idealism between Fichte and Hegel, his former roommate at the Tübinger Stift alongside the poet Hölderlin. His philosophy changed remarkably over his long career, moving through a philosophy of nature (Naturphilosophie), a philosophy of identity, and a later "positive philosophy" of mythology and revelation.

Life

Schelling entered the Tübinger Stift at fifteen, where he shared a room with Hegel and Hölderlin. He studied Fichte's philosophy, published his first philosophical works as a teenager, and in 1798, at only twenty-three, became a professor at the University of Jena, a centre of early German Romanticism. There he associated with the Schlegel brothers, Novalis, Tieck and Caroline Schlegel, whom he later married. He subsequently held posts at Würzburg, Erlangen and Munich, and in 1841 was called to Berlin — in part to counter the influence of the Young Hegelians — where his lectures were attended by Kierkegaard, Engels and Bakunin.

Philosophy

In works such as Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature (1797) and System of Transcendental Idealism (1800), Schelling argued that nature and mind are expressions of a single absolute reality, and that art is the highest manifestation of their unity. His "identity philosophy" proposed the Absolute as the point of indifference of subject and object. In the 1809 essay on human freedom, he explored the problem of evil and the ground of God's existence, influencing later existentialist thought. His late distinction between "negative" and "positive" philosophy — between what things are and that they exist — anticipated themes in Kierkegaard and later philosophy.

Influence

Schelling's Naturphilosophie influenced Romantic science and thinkers including Coleridge; his later work was largely overshadowed by Hegel during the 19th century, but the 20th century saw a revival of interest in his writings on freedom, mythology and the philosophy of religion.
