Characteristic energy

In astrodynamics, the characteristic energy, usually written C3, is a measure of the energy required for an interplanetary mission: it is the specific orbital energy of a spacecraft's departure hyperbola, equal to the square of the hyperbolic excess velocity, C3 = v∞². It is expressed in units of km²/s² and is commonly used by launch-vehicle providers and mission designers to state how much payload a rocket can send toward a given target such as Mars or Venus.

C3 is the square of the hyperbolic excess speed at departure

Definition

For a spacecraft departing a planet on a hyperbolic escape trajectory, the orbital energy per unit mass is ε = v²/2 − μ/r. At infinity the gravitational term vanishes, so the characteristic energy equals twice the specific energy at departure: C3 = v∞² = v² − vesc², where v is the speed at burnout and vesc the local escape speed. A C3 of zero corresponds to a parabolic, minimum-energy escape; positive values indicate faster departures, and negative values describe closed (elliptical) Earth orbits by the same energy convention.

Use in mission design

Launch vehicle performance charts plot deliverable payload mass against C3 for each target and launch window. Typical values for robotic missions are roughly 10–30 km²/s² for Mars and Venus launches, around 100 km²/s² or more for fast outer-planet trajectories (New Horizons left Earth with a C3 of about 158 km²/s², the highest of any launch at the time), and much lower values for lunar missions. Because C3 depends only on the required departure energy, it lets mission planners separate the launch vehicle's job from the interplanetary trajectory design.
