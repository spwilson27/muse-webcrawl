Convenience food

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A tray of instant noodles, a frozen ready meal and a TV dinner]

Convenience foods are commercially prepared foods designed to need little labour from the eater: canned soups and beans, frozen ready meals, instant noodles, boxed mixes, rotisserie chickens, bagged salads. They sell time — the industry's real product — and rose with urbanisation, refrigeration, and women's entry into the paid workforce through the 20th century. The category's landmarks are the TV dinner (1954), Momofuku Ando's instant ramen (1958) and Cup Noodles (1971), microwave popcorn, and the Japanese convenience store, whose onigiri and bento set the world's standard for fresh convenience.

History

Canning made the first convenience foods — armies and navies were the early adopters, sailors and soldiers the captive tasters — and industrial cities built diets around baker's bread, canned fish and condensed milk. Military rations drove technique: retort pouches, freeze-drying and the MRE all fed back into civilian markets. Postwar suburbs, home freezers and television created the TV dinner moment; the microwave (mainstream by the 1980s) redrew the category around the three-minute meal. Ruth Schwartz Cowan's paradox applies: appliances and convenience foods promised liberation yet standards rose to consume the saved time — though few would trade back the hours.

Balance sheet

Convenience food feeds shift workers, students, the elderly and the exhausted — a genuine social service — while drawing criticism for salt, sugar, processing and packaging. The frontier now is "convenience without compromise": chilled (never frozen) ready meals, meal kits that outsource only the shopping and chopping, and convenience-store fresh food of the Japanese kind. The category keeps winning because its competition — cooking from scratch — must be paid for in the scarcest currency there is.
