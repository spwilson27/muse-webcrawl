Frost
FormationDeposition of water vapour as ice on surfaces below 0 °C
TypesHoar frost, rime, black frost, ground frost

Frost is a thin layer of ice on a solid surface, which forms from atmospheric water vapour in an atmosphere that is freezing or below freezing temperature, coming in contact with a solid surface whose temperature is freezing or below freezing. On Earth, it typically occurs when the temperature of the surface falls below the frost point of the surrounding air.

Frost is most likely to form on clear, calm nights, when surfaces lose heat by radiation to the clear sky. Because cold air is dense, it pools in low-lying areas, so frost hollows and valley bottoms are especially susceptible — a phenomenon known as a frost pocket.

Types

Hoar frost (also radiation frost) consists of delicate white ice crystals deposited directly on surfaces. Rime ice forms when supercooled droplets of fog or cloud freeze on impact with a surface, often on windward edges. Black frost refers to freezing conditions without visible ice deposition, in which vegetation is damaged and blackened. Ground frost refers to the freezing of the soil surface.

Effect on plants

Frost can severely damage crops and garden plants: freezing causes water inside plant cells to expand and rupture cell walls. Farmers protect vulnerable crops with covers, heaters, wind machines that mix warmer air from above, and water sprinkling, which releases latent heat as it freezes. The length of the frost-free season, bounded by the last spring frost and the first autumn frost, is a key constraint on agriculture in temperate climates.
