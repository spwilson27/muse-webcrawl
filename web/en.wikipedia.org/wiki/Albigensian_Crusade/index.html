The Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), also known as the Cathar Crusade, was a twenty-year military campaign initiated by Pope Innocent III to eliminate Catharism, a dualist Christian movement condemned as heresy, in Languedoc in southern France. It was the first crusade directed against Christians within Europe, and it permanently extended the power of the French monarchy into the south.

Background

By 1200 the Cathars — called Albigensians after the city of Albi — were deeply rooted in Languedoc, protected or tolerated by local nobles including Raymond VI, Count of Toulouse. Their "perfects" preached a dualism of good spirit and evil matter, rejecting the Catholic sacraments, oaths, and church hierarchy; missions by Cistercians and by St Dominic made little headway. When the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau was murdered in January 1208, Innocent III proclaimed a crusade, offering heretics' lands to Catholic conquerors.

Course of the war

The crusading army that mustered in 1209 stormed Béziers, where much of the population was massacred — the origin of the (probably apocryphal) order attributed to the legate Arnaud Amalric, "Kill them all; God will know His own" — and took Carcassonne, expelling the young Viscount Raymond-Roger Trencavel, who soon died in captivity. Simon de Montfort emerged as the crusade's leader and viscount of the conquered lands, winning a decisive victory at Muret in 1213, where King Peter II of Aragon, intervening for his southern vassals, was killed.

Montfort was killed besieging Toulouse in 1218, and much of the south was recovered by Raymond VI and his son. The French Crown then intervened directly: King Louis VIII campaigned in 1226, and by the Treaty of Paris (Meaux) of 1229 Raymond VII submitted, ceded territory, and accepted the marriage of his heiress Joan to the king's brother Alphonse of Poitiers — the path by which Languedoc passed to the Crown. A papal Inquisition, staffed largely by Dominicans, hunted the surviving Cathars for decades; the last great stronghold, Montségur, fell in 1244, when over two hundred perfects were burned.

Legacy

The crusade devastated the prosperous culture of the troubadours, replaced the southern nobility with northern French lords, and stands as a landmark in the history of religious persecution and of French state formation.
