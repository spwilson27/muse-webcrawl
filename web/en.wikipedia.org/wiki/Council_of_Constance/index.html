Council of Constance

The Council of Constance (Latin: Concilium Constantiense) was an ecumenical council of the Catholic Church held from 1414 to 1418 in Constance, in the Holy Roman Empire. Its main purpose was to end the Western Schism, in which three rival men claimed to be pope. The council deposed or accepted the resignations of the rival claimants and elected Pope Martin V in 1417, restoring a single line of popes.

Background

The Western Schism began in 1378 with rival papal lines at Rome and Avignon; the Council of Pisa (1409) added a third claimant. The Emperor Sigismund pressured the Pisan pope John XXIII to summon a new council at Constance.

Proceedings

The council asserted its authority in the decree Haec sancta (1415), declaring that a general council derives its power directly from Christ and that even the pope must obey it in matters of faith and ending the schism — a landmark statement of conciliarism. The Roman pope Gregory XII resigned in 1415; John XXIII fled and was deposed; the Avignon claimant Benedict XIII refused to resign and was deposed in 1417. The conclave of 1417 elected Oddone Colonna as Martin V.

The council also condemned the teachings of John Wycliffe and Jan Hus. Hus, who had come under a promise of safe conduct from Sigismund, was arrested, tried for heresy, and burned at the stake on 6 July 1415; his follower Jerome of Prague was burned in 1416. These executions helped provoke the Hussite Wars in Bohemia.

Legacy

The council ended the Western Schism but the status of its conciliarist decrees, especially Haec sancta and Frequens, remained contested in later debates over papal supremacy. Ulrich von Richental's illustrated chronicle provides a vivid record of the council, one of the largest assemblies of medieval Europe.
