Mitochondrial DNA

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA or mDNA) is the DNA located in mitochondria, cellular organelles within eukaryotic cells that convert chemical energy from food into a form that cells can use, adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Mitochondrial DNA is only a small portion of the DNA in a eukaryotic cell; most of the DNA can be found in the cell nucleus and, in plants and algae, also in plastids such as chloroplasts.

Structure

Human mitochondrial DNA was the first significant part of the human genome to be sequenced. It is a circular, double-stranded molecule of 16,569 base pairs containing 37 genes: 13 encoding subunits of the respiratory chain complexes, 22 encoding transfer RNAs, and 2 encoding ribosomal RNAs. Unlike nuclear DNA, mtDNA is not wrapped around histones and is replicated independently of the cell cycle.

Inheritance

In most multicellular organisms, mtDNA is inherited from the mother (egg) only, a pattern known as maternal inheritance. The sperm's mitochondria, located in the midpiece, are typically destroyed by the egg cell after fertilisation. Because of this uniparental inheritance and its relatively rapid mutation rate, mtDNA is a powerful tool for tracing ancestry through the female line. Analysis of mtDNA has been used to estimate the time of the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all living humans, popularly known as "Mitochondrial Eve," who lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.

Replication and repair

mtDNA is replicated by the DNA polymerase gamma complex. Mitochondria have limited DNA repair capacity compared with the nucleus, and mtDNA sits close to the electron transport chain, a source of reactive oxygen species. As a result, mtDNA accumulates mutations at a higher rate than nuclear DNA; somatic mtDNA mutations accumulate with age and are implicated in ageing and degenerative disease.

Mutations and disease

Mutations in mitochondrial DNA cause a range of disorders, typically affecting tissues with high energy demands such as muscle, brain, heart and retina. Examples include Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), MELAS syndrome (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes), MERRF, and chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia. Because each cell contains many mitochondria and many mtDNA copies, mutant and normal molecules often coexist — a state called heteroplasmy — and disease severity depends on the proportion of mutant copies.

Uses in science

mtDNA is widely used in evolutionary biology, population genetics, forensic science and genealogy. Its lack of recombination and high copy number make it easier to recover from degraded samples than nuclear DNA, so it has been used to identify human remains and to sequence the genomes of extinct species such as Neanderthals.
