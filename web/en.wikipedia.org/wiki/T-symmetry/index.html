T-symmetry

T-symmetry
Also calledTime reversal invariance
Operationt → −t
Related symmetriesC (charge conjugation), P (parity)
Violated byWeak interaction (indirectly observed 2012)

T-symmetry or time reversal invariance is the theoretical symmetry of physical laws under the transformation of time reversal, in which the time coordinate t is replaced by −t. A system is said to possess T-symmetry if its dynamics are unchanged when the direction of the flow of time is reversed — that is, if a film of any allowed process, played backwards, also depicts an allowed process.

Macroscopic processes

At the macroscopic scale, everyday experience displays a strong arrow of time: eggs break but do not unbreak, and heat flows from hot bodies to cold ones. This asymmetry is explained statistically by the second law of thermodynamics, since entropy-increasing sequences of microstates vastly outnumber entropy-decreasing ones, even though the underlying microscopic laws are (nearly) T-symmetric. Systems described by Newton's laws, Maxwell's equations (with appropriate sign changes for magnetic fields) and general relativity are time-reversal invariant.

Quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics, time reversal is represented by an antiunitary operator, first analysed by Eugene Wigner. Under time reversal, momenta and spins change sign while positions and charges do not. Spin-½ particles acquire a twofold degeneracy in T-symmetric systems, known as Kramers' degeneracy.

T violation

The weak interaction violates T-symmetry. Following the discovery of parity violation in 1956–1957 and of CP violation in neutral kaon decay in 1964, the CPT theorem — which states that any local, Lorentz-invariant quantum field theory must be invariant under the combined CPT transformation — implied that T must also be violated. Direct observation of T violation, independent of CP measurements, was reported by the BaBar experiment in 2012 using entangled B mesons, confirming the effect at high statistical significance.
