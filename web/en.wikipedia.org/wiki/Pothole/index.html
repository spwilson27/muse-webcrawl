Pothole

A pothole in an asphalt road surface.

A pothole is a depression in a road surface, usually an asphalt pavement, where traffic has removed broken pieces of the surface. Potholes range from a few centimetres to over a metre across and can be deep enough to damage vehicle tyres, wheels and suspension, and to endanger cyclists and motorcyclists.

Formation

Potholes form when water penetrates cracks in the surface and weakens or washes out the underlying soil and aggregate base. In cold climates, freeze–thaw cycles accelerate the process: water in the pavement freezes, expands and heaves the surface, then thaws and leaves voids. Repeated traffic loading, especially from heavy vehicles, then breaks the unsupported surface, and loose fragments are displaced, enlarging the hole. Poor drainage, utility trenches and aged, brittle asphalt are contributing factors.

Repair

Repairs range from temporary "throw-and-go" cold patching, in which asphalt mix is simply placed in the hole, to semi-permanent repairs in which the cavity is squared, cleaned and filled in compacted layers, and durable infrared or full-depth reconstruction. Because patch repairs often fail where the underlying cause — water in the base — is not addressed, resurfacing or reconstruction of the pavement structure may be needed. Delayed maintenance greatly increases cost: industry studies commonly estimate that every dollar of timely pavement preservation saves several dollars in later rehabilitation.

Costs and liability

Potholes are a persistent public complaint in many countries, and highway authorities may be liable for vehicle damage where defects were known and not repaired within a reasonable time. Reporting systems, including mobile apps, are used by many authorities to log defects. In geology, the same word describes cylindrical pits worn in bedrock by swirling water and sediment, as in glacial potholes.
