The second (symbol: s) is the base unit of time in the International System of Units (SI). Historically it was defined as 1/86400 of the mean solar day — the product of dividing an hour into sixty minutes and each minute into sixty seconds, a scheme inherited from Babylonian sexagesimal arithmetic via Greek astronomy. Since 1967 the second has been defined by the unperturbed ground-state hyperfine transition of the caesium-133 atom.

Definition

The SI definition, reaffirmed in the 2019 revision of the SI, states: "The second, symbol s, is the SI unit of time. It is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the caesium frequency ΔνCs, the unperturbed ground-state hyperfine transition frequency of the caesium-133 atom, to be 9 192 631 770 when expressed in the unit Hz." In practice, one second is the duration of 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation corresponding to that transition. Atomic clocks based on this definition keep Coordinated Universal Time (UTC); occasional leap seconds have been added to keep UTC aligned with Earth's irregular rotation.

Historical origins

The division of the day into 24 hours, each into 60 minutes and each minute into 60 seconds, derives from Babylonian mathematics transmitted through Hellenistic astronomy. The second ("secunda minuta", the second small division) became measurable only with the pendulum clocks of the 17th century. In 1832 Gauss proposed the second as a base unit; the British Association adopted it in the CGS system in 1874. From 1956 to 1967 the second was briefly defined astronomically as a fraction of the tropical year 1900 (the "ephemeris second") before the atomic definition replaced it.

Multiples and submultiples

Common submultiples include the millisecond (10−3 s), microsecond (10−6 s), nanosecond (10−9 s) and picosecond (10−12 s); attosecond clocks reach 10−18 s. Larger intervals use the minute (60 s), hour (3,600 s) and day (86,400 s).

Second of arc

The same word denotes a unit of angle: a second of arc (arcsecond) is 1/60 of a minute of arc and 1/3600 of a degree — another inheritance of sexagesimal division.
