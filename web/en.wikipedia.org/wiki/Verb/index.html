Verb

Verb
The verb as the core of the clause

A verb (from Latin verbum, "word") is a word (part of speech) that in syntax generally conveys an action (bring, read, walk, run, learn), an occurrence (happen, become), or a state of being (be, exist, stand). In the usual description of English, the basic form, with or without the particle to, is the infinitive. In many languages verbs are inflected (modified in form) to encode tense, aspect, mood, modality and voice, as well as agreement with the person, gender or number of some of their arguments.

Agreement and valency

In languages with subject–verb agreement, the verb changes form according to the person and number of its subject (English I am, she is). Valency describes the number and type of arguments a verb takes: intransitive verbs take only a subject (she sleeps); transitive verbs also take an object (she reads a book); ditransitive verbs take two objects. Valency patterns are a central topic in syntax and lexicography.

Tense, aspect and mood

Verbs typically encode when the action occurs (tense: past, present, future), how it unfolds in time (aspect: perfective, progressive, habitual), and the speaker's attitude toward it (mood: indicative, subjunctive, imperative). Languages differ greatly in which of these categories are grammaticalised in the verb.

Classes

  • Auxiliary verbs combine with a main verb to express tense, mood or voice (have finished, was seen).
  • Modal verbs express possibility, necessity or permission (can, must, may).
  • Copular verbs, such as be, link subject and complement rather than expressing an action.
  • Phrasal verbs combine a verb with a particle (give up, look after).
  • Regular and irregular verbs differ in whether their past forms follow the standard pattern or are formed irregularly.
