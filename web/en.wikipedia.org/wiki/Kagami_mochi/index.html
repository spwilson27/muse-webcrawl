Kagami mochi

Kagami mochi (鏡餅, "mirror rice cake") is a traditional Japanese New Year decoration. It consists of two round mochi rice cakes of different sizes, the smaller stacked on top of the larger, crowned with a daidai (Japanese bitter orange). The arrangement is placed on a small stand (sanpō) over a sheet of paper, and is displayed in homes and businesses to welcome the New Year deity (toshigami).

Symbolism

The name refers to the resemblance of the round cakes to the bronze mirrors of ancient Japan. The two stacked cakes are said to symbolise the passing and coming years, the sun and moon, or yin and yang; the daidai, whose name puns on "generation after generation", represents the continuity of the family. Other decorations, such as dried persimmons, konbu kelp and a fern leaf, may accompany the arrangement.

Kagami biraki

The decoration is traditionally broken and eaten on 11 January (with regional variations) in a ceremony called kagami biraki ("mirror opening"). Because cutting with a knife is considered inauspicious, the hardened cakes are broken with a wooden mallet or by hand, and the pieces are cooked in dishes such as shiruko (sweet red-bean soup) or zoni.

Modern practice

Today many households buy mass-produced plastic-encased kagami mochi containing small individual portions of mochi inside the moulded shell, which are easier to prepare and keep.
