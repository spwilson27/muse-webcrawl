Price-to-earnings ratio

The price-to-earnings ratio (P/E ratio, or price multiple) is a valuation measure for a company, defined as its share price divided by its earnings per share (EPS). Equivalently, it is the market capitalisation divided by total net income. A P/E of 20 means investors pay twenty units of currency for each unit of annual earnings. It is among the most widely quoted statistics in financial journalism and equity analysis.

Variants

The trailing P/E uses earnings from the most recent twelve months, while the forward P/E uses analysts' forecast earnings for the coming year, and is therefore lower when earnings are expected to grow. The cyclically adjusted P/E (CAPE or Shiller P/E), popularised by economist Robert Shiller, divides price by the average of ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings, smoothing business-cycle swings; it is mainly used to judge whole market indices. "PEG ratio" divides the P/E by the expected earnings growth rate to penalise expensive shares whose growth does not justify the multiple.

Interpretation

A high P/E can signal that investors expect rapid future growth, that the company is perceived as low-risk, or that the stock is overvalued; a low P/E may indicate undervaluation, weak prospects, or temporarily depressed prices against temporarily inflated earnings. The reciprocal of the P/E, the earnings yield, allows rough comparison with bond yields. Because the ratio is meaningless for loss-making companies (a negative P/E is not usually quoted), analysts turn to other multiples such as price-to-sales for them.

Determinants

From the Gordon growth model, the justified P/E depends on the dividend payout ratio, the required rate of return and the expected growth rate: higher growth and lower perceived risk raise the multiple. Interest rates matter — when bond yields are low, investors tolerate higher P/Es, which is one reason market multiples expanded during the long era of cheap money and compressed as rates rose. Sector norms differ greatly: mature utilities often trade in the low teens, while fast-growing technology firms may trade at thirty or more times earnings.

Limitations

Earnings are an accounting figure, affected by depreciation policy, one-off items and share buybacks, so P/Es are not perfectly comparable between firms or countries. The ratio also ignores debt — two companies with identical P/Es can have very different enterprise values — and it becomes distorted at cyclical peaks, when swollen earnings make shares look deceptively cheap.
