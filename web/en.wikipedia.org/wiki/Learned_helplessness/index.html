Learned helplessness

Learned helplessness is a mental state in which an organism that has been forced to bear aversive stimuli — painful or otherwise unpleasant events it cannot escape or avoid — comes to accept and endure them, ceasing attempts to escape even when opportunities arise. The theory holds that when an animal or person perceives that outcomes are independent of their responses, they learn that they are helpless, and this learning generalises to new situations. In humans, learned helplessness has been linked to depression and other mental-health problems, and an associated concept, explanatory style, concerns how people habitually explain bad events to themselves.

Original experiments

The phenomenon was discovered accidentally in 1964–65 by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier at the University of Pennsylvania, when dogs that had previously received inescapable electric shocks in a harness later failed to escape shocks in a shuttle box where escape was easy — they simply lay down and whined, enduring the shock. In the standard triadic design, one group of dogs could terminate shock by pressing a panel ("escape" group), a yoked group received identical shocks but had no control over them, and a third received no shock. Only the yoked group later showed helplessness, demonstrating that it was the uncontrollability of the shock, not the shock itself, that produced the deficit. Similar effects were demonstrated in rats, mice, cats, fish and humans (for example with inescapable noise).

Reformulated theory

In 1978, Abramson, Seligman and Teasdale reformulated the theory for humans in terms of causal attributions. When people face bad events, they ask why; explanations that are stable ("it will always be this way"), global ("it affects everything") and internal ("it's my fault") tend to produce longer-lasting, generalised helplessness and depressed mood. This "pessimistic explanatory style" is a risk factor for depression, whereas an optimistic style buffers against it. Seligman's later work founded positive psychology and programmes such as the Penn Resiliency Program to teach more resilient explanatory styles.

Neurobiology and revision

Later research by Maier and Seligman (2016) revised the original interpretation: neuroscience evidence suggests passivity in the face of prolonged aversion is a default response mediated by the dorsal raphe nucleus, and what is actually learned is control — animals that experience escapable shock develop cortical (ventromedial prefrontal) circuitry that inhibits the passivity response. Helplessness, on this view, is not learned at all; mastery is.

Applications

The concept has been applied to clinical depression, post-traumatic stress, classroom underachievement, institutionalisation, poverty, chronic illness, and relationships involving abuse, in which victims may come to believe escape or change is impossible. Critics note that animal models cannot capture the full cognitive complexity of human depression and that the construct overlaps considerably with low self-efficacy and external locus of control.
