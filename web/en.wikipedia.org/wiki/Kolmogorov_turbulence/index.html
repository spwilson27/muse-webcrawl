Kolmogorov turbulence

Kolmogorov's theory of turbulence
Proposed byAndrey Kolmogorov (1941)
FieldFluid dynamics, statistical mechanics
Key resultsEnergy cascade; −5/3 spectrum; Kolmogorov microscales

Kolmogorov turbulence refers to the statistical theory of turbulence developed by Andrey Kolmogorov in 1941 (often called K41 theory), which describes how kinetic energy in a turbulent flow cascades from large eddies to small ones, where it is dissipated as heat by viscosity. The theory remains the foundational framework for understanding the small-scale structure of high-Reynolds-number turbulence.

The energy cascade

Building on Lewis Fry Richardson's earlier picture—"big whirls have little whirls that feed on their velocity"—Kolmogorov proposed that energy injected at the largest scales of motion is transferred through an inertial range of intermediate scales, essentially without loss, until it reaches scales small enough for viscosity to dissipate it. In statistically stationary turbulence the rate of energy transfer equals the mean dissipation rate ε.

Kolmogorov's hypotheses

Kolmogorov's first similarity hypothesis states that at sufficiently high Reynolds number, the statistics of the smallest scales depend only on the kinematic viscosity ν and the dissipation rate ε. From these two quantities one can form the Kolmogorov microscales:

η = (ν³/ε)1/4,   τη = (ν/ε)1/2,   uη = (νε)1/4

where η is the Kolmogorov length scale, τη the time scale, and uη the velocity scale of the smallest eddies.

The −5/3 law

Kolmogorov's second hypothesis implies that in the inertial range the energy spectrum depends only on ε and the wavenumber k:

E(k) = C ε2/3 k−5/3

This famous −5/3 spectrum, with C ≈ 1.5 the Kolmogorov constant, has been confirmed in a wide range of laboratory, atmospheric, and oceanic measurements. A related exact result, Kolmogorov's 4/5 law for third-order structure functions, is one of the few exact results in turbulence theory.

Limitations

Later work, including Kolmogorov's own 1962 refinement, showed that dissipation is not uniform but intermittent, so real flows exhibit small corrections (anomalous scaling) to K41 predictions. Nevertheless, the cascade picture and the −5/3 law remain central to turbulence modelling, large-eddy simulation, and geophysical fluid dynamics.
