Irish republicanism

Irish republicanism is the political movement for the unity and independence of Ireland in the form of a republic. Republicans seek an end to British rule in Northern Ireland and the establishment of an all-Ireland republic, drawing on a tradition stretching back to the Society of United Irishmen of the 1790s.

Origins

Inspired by the American and French revolutions, Theobald Wolfe Tone and the United Irishmen sought an independent Irish republic uniting Catholic, Protestant and Dissenter. The Irish Rebellion of 1798 was suppressed, and the Acts of Union 1800 incorporated Ireland into the United Kingdom.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

The Fenian movement and the Irish Republican Brotherhood carried the republican ideal through the 19th century. The Easter Rising of 1916 proclaimed an Irish Republic; the First Dáil of 1919 asserted its legitimacy, and the Irish War of Independence followed. The Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921 partitioned Ireland, leading to the Irish Civil War. The Republic of Ireland was formally declared in 1949, while republicans who rejected partition continued armed campaigns, most notably during the Troubles (late 1960s–1998).

Modern republicanism

Following the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, the largest republican party, Sinn Féin, participates in government in both jurisdictions while advocating a referendum on Irish unity. Smaller "dissident republican" groups reject the settlement.
