The Japanese calendar designates dates in several complementary ways. Japan used a lunisolar calendar adapted from the Chinese calendar for over a millennium, then adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1873 during the Meiji Restoration, while retaining the distinctive system of imperial era names (nengō).

Traditional lunisolar calendar

Introduced from China via Korea around the 6th–7th centuries, the traditional calendar divided the year into months beginning at the new moon, inserting leap months to track the seasons, and used refinements such as the various Chinese-derived computations (the Senmyō and later Jōkyō calendars). Years were also counted in the sixty-year cycle of heavenly stems and earthly branches.

Era names

Since 645 (the Taika era), years have been grouped under era names proclaimed by the imperial court; historically eras changed for auspicious or inauspicious events, but since Meiji each emperor has had a single era: Meiji, Taishō, Shōwa, Heisei, and — from the accession of Emperor Naruhito on 1 May 2019 — Reiwa, drawn from the Man'yōshū poetry anthology. Official documents still commonly date by era and year (e.g., Reiwa 7 = 2025).

Modern usage

Everyday life uses the Gregorian calendar and its month and weekday names, while traditional festivals often follow old lunisolar dates or fixed modern equivalents. The rokuyō cycle of lucky and unlucky days still influences the scheduling of weddings and funerals.
