Man Jiang Hong

Man Jiang Hong (Chinese: 滿江紅; "The Whole River Red") is a famous Chinese ci lyric poem traditionally attributed to the Song-dynasty general Yue Fei (1103–1142), the patriotic hero who fought against the Jurchen Jin dynasty. Set to a pre-existing tune pattern of the same name, the poem expresses furious resolve to recover the lost northern territories of the Song empire.

Text and themes

The poem opens with the poet standing in a raging downpour, his hair bristling beneath his helmet with anger. It laments the Jingkang Incident of 1127, when the Jurchens captured the Song capital and two emperors, and vows that the shame will only be washed away when the enemy is destroyed. Its most famous lines declare: "Do not say lightly that one has reached the summit in vain — at thirty, fame and dust are but dirt and earth; eight thousand li travelled beneath cloud and moon," and "Hungry, I would eat the flesh of the barbarians; thirsty, I would drink their blood."

Authorship debate

The attribution to Yue Fei is not recorded until the Ming dynasty, centuries after his death, and some scholars have argued the poem's tone and references fit a later, Ming-era author writing during campaigns against Mongols and coastal pirates. Nevertheless, the poem is inseparably linked with Yue Fei in Chinese culture and is inscribed at his tomb and temple in Hangzhou.

Legacy

Man Jiang Hong is among the most widely memorised classical poems in China, taught in schools and repeatedly set to music. It has become a byword for patriotism, quoted in political speeches and popular culture, and its tune pattern remains a standard cipai used by later poets.
