Miscegenation is a term historically used — and now widely regarded as offensive and pseudoscientific — to describe sexual relations or marriage between people of different "races". The word was coined in 1863 in an anonymous pamphlet, Miscegenation: The Theory of the Blending of the Races, later revealed to have been a hoax written by two Democratic journalists, David Goodman Croly and George Wakeman, during the American Civil War, intended to embarrass Abraham Lincoln's Republican Party by attributing to it a program of racial mixing.

Historical usage

Although the word was invented in the United States, it came into general use in discussions of laws prohibiting interracial relationships — the anti-miscegenation laws enacted in many U.S. states, as well as comparable prohibitions in Nazi Germany (the Nuremberg Laws of 1935), apartheid South Africa (the Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act of 1949), and other racial regimes. The term carried a pseudoscientific implication that races were distinct biological kinds whose "mixing" was unnatural.

Anti-miscegenation laws in the United States

Laws against interracial marriage existed in some American colonies from the 17th century and eventually in a majority of states. They were upheld by the Supreme Court in Pace v. Alabama (1883) but began falling after the Second World War; California's law was struck down in Perez v. Sharp (1948). In Loving v. Virginia (1967), the Supreme Court unanimously invalidated the remaining state bans as violations of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Modern usage

Scholars and journalists now generally avoid "miscegenation" except when discussing the historical ideology itself, preferring neutral terms such as interracial marriage or "mixed-race". The concept remains a subject of study in the history of racism and scientific racism.
