The Sixties Scoop is a name given to the Canadian practice, prevalent from the 1960s through the 1980s, of taking ("scooping up") Indigenous children from their families and communities for placement in foster homes or adoption, largely into non-Indigenous middle-class families across Canada, the United States and overseas. The term was coined by researcher Patrick Johnston in his 1983 report Native Children and the Child Welfare System.

Background

A 1951 amendment (section 88) to the Indian Act made provincial laws of general application apply on reserves, and bilateral funding agreements in the 1960s extended provincial child-welfare services to First Nations communities. Social workers, applying Euro-Canadian middle-class standards of neglect to families impoverished by colonial policy, removed children at extraordinary rates. By the late 1970s, Indigenous children made up a grossly disproportionate share of children in care — in some western provinces the majority of children in care, despite Indigenous people being a small percentage of the population. Programmes such as Saskatchewan's Adopt Indian Métis (AIM) project (1967–1969) actively advertised Indigenous children in newspapers for adoption.

Effects

Many adoptees lost their languages, cultures, status rights and contact with their families, and a significant number experienced abuse or neglect in their adoptive placements. Survivors describe lasting identity crises and intergenerational trauma; many have struggled to reconnect with their birth communities or to regain Indian status under the Indian Act.

Responses and redress

Manitoba's 1985 Kimelman Report (No Quiet Place) concluded that "cultural genocide has been taking place in a systematic, routine manner." In 2015, Manitoba premier Greg Selinger issued the first provincial apology. A national class-action settlement followed in 2017 (approved 2018), providing roughly $875 million, with individual compensation of $25,000–$50,000 for status First Nations and Inuit survivors removed between 1951 and 1991, and funding for the Sixties Scoop Healing Foundation. Ontario's 2017 court ruling in Brown v. Canada found the federal government had breached its duty to preserve Indigenous children's cultural identity. Legislation in 2019 (Bill C-92) affirmed Indigenous jurisdiction over child and family services.
