Individual retirement account

An individual retirement account (IRA) in the United States is a form of pension provided by many financial institutions that provides tax advantages for retirement savings. It is a trust that holds investment assets purchased with a taxpayer's earned income for the taxpayer's eventual benefit in old age.

IRAs are a major vehicle for retirement saving in the US.

Types

TypeTax treatment
Traditional IRAContributions may be tax-deductible; withdrawals taxed as income
Roth IRAContributions made after tax; qualified withdrawals tax-free
SEP IRAEmployer-funded plan for self-employed people and small businesses
SIMPLE IRAEmployer-sponsored plan with mandatory employer contributions

History

IRAs were established by the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA), initially for workers not covered by employer pension plans. Eligibility was expanded in 1981, restricted again in 1986 for higher earners with workplace plans, and the Roth IRA was created by the Taxpayer Relief Act of 1997, named after Senator William Roth.

Rules

Contributions are subject to annual limits that are adjusted periodically and are higher for savers aged 50 and over ("catch-up" contributions). Withdrawals from a traditional IRA before age 59½ generally incur a 10% early-withdrawal penalty in addition to income tax, with exceptions for items such as first-home purchases and certain medical expenses. Required minimum distributions (RMDs) from traditional IRAs must begin in the account holder's seventies. Assets may be held in a wide range of investments, although collectibles and life insurance are prohibited, and the account must be held by a custodian such as a bank or brokerage.

Statistics

IRAs hold several trillion dollars of assets; a large share of IRA assets originated as rollovers from employer-sponsored plans such as 401(k) accounts rather than from direct contributions.
