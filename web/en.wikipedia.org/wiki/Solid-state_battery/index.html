Solid-state battery

Schematic of a solid-state lithium-metal battery cell

A solid-state battery is an electrical battery that uses a solid electrolyte to conduct ions between its electrodes, instead of the liquid or polymer-gel electrolytes found in conventional lithium-ion batteries. Solid-state designs are pursued for higher energy density, improved safety (solid electrolytes are not flammable), and potential use of lithium-metal anodes.

Technology

Candidate solid electrolytes fall into three families: inorganic ceramics such as garnets (LLZO) and perovskites; sulfides such as LGPS, which offer high ionic conductivity but are moisture-sensitive; and solid polymers such as PEO, used in some commercial designs. Key challenges include maintaining low-resistance contact at electrode–electrolyte interfaces, suppressing lithium dendrites that can penetrate ceramic electrolytes and short the cell, managing volume changes during cycling, and manufacturing thin electrolyte layers at scale and at acceptable cost.

History and commercialization

Solid electrolytes have been known since the 19th century (Michael Faraday's work on solid ionic conductors), and thin-film solid-state batteries were developed in the late 20th century. The French Bolloré Bluecar used lithium-metal-polymer batteries from 2011. Major manufacturers including Toyota, Samsung, QuantumScape, Solid Power, and CATL have invested heavily in the technology, targeting electric vehicles and consumer electronics; semi-solid-state batteries from makers such as WeLion reached production in NIO vehicles in the early 2020s, while fully solid automotive cells were generally expected in limited production later in the decade.
