Revenue

Revenue
Also calledSales, turnover (British English)
Income statement positionTop line
Related conceptsProfit, gross income, operating income

In accounting, revenue is the income generated from normal business operations and includes discounts and deductions for returned merchandise. It is the "top line" or "gross income" figure from which costs are subtracted to determine net income. Revenue is also referred to as sales or turnover. Some companies receive revenue from interest, royalties, or other fees; for the purposes of this article, revenue generally refers to business revenue—the money a company receives from selling goods and services to its customers.

Overview

Revenue is a crucial measure of a company's performance. It is often used to measure and predict growth, and investors frequently compare a company's revenue growth from quarter to quarter and year to year. If revenue declines while costs remain constant, profitability erodes quickly; conversely, revenue growth does not guarantee profit if expenses grow faster.

In the income statement, revenue is the first line item. From it are subtracted the cost of goods sold to arrive at gross profit; operating expenses then yield operating income, and after interest and taxes, net income—the "bottom line."

Recognition

Revenue recognition standards govern when revenue may be recorded. Under the accrual basis of accounting, revenue is recognised when it is earned—typically when goods are delivered or services are rendered—rather than when cash is received. The joint standard IFRS 15 / ASC 606, adopted by the IASB and FASB, sets out a five-step model: identify the contract, identify performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate the price to obligations, and recognise revenue as obligations are satisfied.

Types of revenue

Operating revenue

Revenue generated from a company's primary activities—for a manufacturer, selling its products; for a retailer, merchandising; for a service firm, fees for services.

Non-operating revenue

Income from secondary sources such as interest on deposits, dividends from investments, rent, and gains on asset sales.

Government revenue

For governments, revenue comprises taxes, fees, fines, intergovernmental grants, and proceeds from the sale of assets. Government revenue is the counterpart of government spending in fiscal policy.

Analysis

Common analyses include revenue per employee, revenue growth rates, and segment reporting. A company's price-to-sales ratio compares market capitalisation to total revenue and is widely used to value growth companies that are not yet profitable.
