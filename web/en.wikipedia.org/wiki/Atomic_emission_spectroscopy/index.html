Atomic emission spectroscopy

Atomic emission spectroscopy (AES) is a method of chemical analysis that uses the intensity of light emitted from a flame, plasma, arc, or spark at a particular wavelength to determine the quantity of an element in a sample. The wavelength of the atomic spectral line in the emission spectrum gives the identity of the element while the intensity of the emitted light is proportional to the number of atoms of the element in the sample.

Principle

The sample is introduced into a high-temperature excitation source, which vaporizes and atomizes it and promotes electrons in the atoms to higher energy levels. As the excited atoms relax back to lower states, they emit photons at characteristic wavelengths. A spectrometer disperses the light and detectors measure the intensities of the elemental emission lines, which are compared against standards for quantitative analysis.

Sources

Classical sources include flames (flame emission spectroscopy or flame photometry) and electrical arcs and sparks. The most widely used modern source is the inductively coupled plasma (ICP-AES, also called ICP-OES, optical emission spectrometry), in which an argon plasma reaching 6,000–10,000 K excites the sample; microwave-induced plasmas are also used. ICP-AES permits simultaneous multi-element analysis with low detection limits and a wide linear range.

Applications

Atomic emission spectroscopy is used in environmental analysis (water and soils), metallurgy and mining, food and agriculture, clinical analysis, petrochemicals, and quality control. It complements atomic absorption spectroscopy and ICP mass spectrometry; compared with ICP-MS it is generally less sensitive but simpler and more tolerant of high dissolved solids.

History

The method grew out of the nineteenth-century spectral work of Bunsen and Kirchhoff, whose flame spectra founded elemental spectral analysis and led to the discovery of new elements such as caesium and rubidium.
