Profit (accounting)

Profit, in accounting, is an income distributed to the owner in a profitable market production process (business). Profit is a measure of profitability which is a major determinant of the investor's return and the principal item of concern to a firm's stakeholders. In simple terms:

Profit = Total Revenue − Total Expenses

Profitability is a key element of measuring a firm's "going concern" status and is reflected on the income statement.

Types of profit

Gross profit

Gross profit is the profit a company makes after deducting the costs associated with making and selling its products, or the costs associated with providing its services: revenue minus the cost of goods sold (COGS). Gross profit appears on a company's income statement and is often expressed as a percentage (gross margin).

Operating profit

Operating profit (or operating income) subtracts operating expenses — wages, depreciation, and day-to-day costs of running the business — from gross profit, but excludes interest and taxes. The related measure EBIT (earnings before interest and taxes) is widely used for comparing operating performance.

Net profit

Net profit (net income, "the bottom line") is the residual after all expenses, including interest, taxes, and extraordinary items, have been deducted from total revenue. Net profit may be distributed as dividends to shareholders or retained in the business.

Accounting vs economic profit

Accounting profit is calculated using explicit costs recorded under generally accepted accounting principles. Economic profit additionally subtracts implicit costs — the opportunity costs of resources supplied by the owners, such as the owner's labour and capital — so economic profit is normally lower than accounting profit. A firm earning zero economic profit is said to earn a "normal profit", covering all its opportunity costs.
