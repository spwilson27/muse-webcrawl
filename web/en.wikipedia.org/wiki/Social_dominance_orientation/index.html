Social dominance orientation

Social dominance orientation (SDO) is a personality trait and individual-differences measure of a person's preference for hierarchy and inequality among social groups. It was developed by psychologists Felicia Pratto, Jim Sidanius and colleagues as part of social dominance theory, and it is typically assessed with the SDO scale, a set of statements about group equality with which respondents agree or disagree.

Findings

People scoring high in SDO tend to favour hierarchy-enhancing policies and occupations and to oppose redistributive and civil-rights measures; SDO scores correlate with prejudice toward low-status outgroups, nationalism, militarism and support for punitive policies. On average, men score higher than women — a pattern the theory links to the "invariance hypothesis" — and members of high-status groups tend to score higher than members of low-status groups. Later versions of the scale distinguish two subdimensions: SDO-Dominance (support for active oppression of outgroups) and SDO-Egalitarianism reversed (opposition to equality).

Relation to other constructs

SDO is studied alongside right-wing authoritarianism; together they form the dual-process model of prejudice developed by John Duckitt, in which authoritarianism predicts prejudice toward threatening groups and SDO predicts prejudice toward subordinate groups.
