Externalism (philosophy of mind)

Externalism is a group of positions in the philosophy of mind and language which argue that the meaning or content of a person's mental states is determined, at least in part, by features of the external environment, rather than solely by what is inside the person's head. The view contrasts with internalism, according to which mental content supervenes on the internal physical state of the subject.

Semantic externalism

Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment (1975) argued that two molecule-for-molecule identical speakers, one on Earth where "water" refers to H₂O and one on Twin Earth where it refers to a different substance XYZ, nevertheless mean different things by "water" — "meanings just ain't in the head." Tyler Burge extended externalism to social factors: what a person believes can depend on how their linguistic community uses words, as in his arthritis thought experiment.

Active externalism

Andy Clark and David Chalmers defended an "active externalism" in their 1998 paper "The Extended Mind", arguing that external objects such as notebooks can literally constitute part of a person's cognitive processes when they play the right functional role, as in their example of Otto, a man with Alzheimer's disease who relies on a notebook as memory.

Criticism

Internalists object that externalism conflicts with the privileged access we seem to have to our own thoughts, and with causal explanations of behaviour. Responses appeal to distinctions between concepts of content used for psychological explanation and those used for semantic evaluation.
