Empirical risk minimization

Empirical risk minimization (ERM) is a principle in statistical learning theory which defines a family of learning algorithms based on evaluating performance over a known and fixed dataset. The core idea is based on the empirical risk, which cannot be computed because the distribution of the data is unknown, being approximated by evaluating a model against a finite training set.

Background

In a supervised learning setting, one has inputs and corresponding responses, and wishes to learn a function that predicts responses for new inputs. The true risk of a hypothesis is the expected loss over the underlying data distribution; since this distribution is unknown, the risk is instead estimated by the empirical risk, the average loss over the available sample:

Remp(h) = (1/n) Σi L(h(xi), yi)

The ERM principle selects the hypothesis within a hypothesis class that minimises this empirical risk.

Theory

The theoretical justification of ERM rests on uniform convergence results: with enough samples, the empirical risk of all hypotheses in a class is simultaneously close to their true risk, so the empirical risk minimiser is nearly as good as the best hypothesis in the class. The capacity of the class is commonly measured by its VC dimension. A central risk of ERM is overfitting, which can be mitigated by regularization, leading to structural risk minimization. Many standard algorithms — including least squares regression, logistic regression, and support vector machines — can be viewed as instances or approximations of ERM.
