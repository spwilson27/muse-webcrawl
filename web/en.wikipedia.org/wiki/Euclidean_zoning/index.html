Euclidean zoning

Euclidean zoning is the most common form of zoning in the United States. It is named not after the Greek mathematician Euclid but after the village of Euclid, Ohio, whose zoning ordinance was upheld as constitutional by the U.S. Supreme Court in the landmark case Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (1926).

Characteristics

Euclidean zoning divides a municipality into districts (zones) — typically residential, commercial, and industrial — and within each district permits only a fixed list of uses, separating incompatible activities from one another by law. It is typically "cumulative": districts are arranged in a hierarchy in which a "higher" zone (such as single-family residential) excludes all uses allowed in "lower" zones (such as industry), while lower zones may admit the uses of higher ones. Regulations also prescribe dimensional standards: lot size, setbacks, building height, and density.

History and criticism

Following the 1926 decision, Euclidean zoning spread rapidly across the United States and became the default template for suburban development. It is credited with protecting homeowners from adjacent nuisance industry, but it has also been widely criticised: by mandating the separation of homes from shops and workplaces it encourages car-dependent sprawl, long commutes, and — through large minimum lot sizes and single-family-only districts — housing unaffordability and racial and economic segregation. Alternatives and reforms include form-based codes, mixed-use zoning, inclusionary zoning, and "Euclidean zoning reform" ordinances that legalise gentle density such as duplexes and accessory dwelling units in residential districts.
