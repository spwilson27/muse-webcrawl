Samuel James Ervin Jr. (September 27, 1896 – April 23, 1985) was an American politician and lawyer who served as a United States Senator from North Carolina from 1954 to 1974. A Democrat and a renowned constitutional scholar, he is best remembered as chairman of the Senate Select Committee on Presidential Campaign Activities — the Senate Watergate Committee — whose televised hearings in 1973 exposed the Watergate scandal.

Early life and career

Ervin was born in Morganton, North Carolina, the son of a lawyer. He served as an infantry officer in the First World War, was wounded twice, and was decorated for bravery. He graduated from Harvard Law School in 1922 — remarking that he was the only student who had completed the course "backwards", having taken the third-year examinations first — and served in the U.S. House of Representatives (1946–1947), as a state judge and on the North Carolina Supreme Court before his appointment to the Senate in 1954.

Senate career

Ervin cultivated the persona of a plain-spoken "country lawyer", prefaced constitutional arguments with "I'm just a country lawyer, but…", and kept a copy of the Constitution in his pocket. He had served on the committee that censured Senator Joseph McCarthy and on the Army–McCarthy hearings panel. Although a fierce defender of civil liberties against government surveillance and executive power, he was also a segregationist who signed the Southern Manifesto and opposed the civil rights legislation of the 1960s — a contradiction historians continue to weigh.

Watergate Committee

Chosen as a respected, non-partisan-seeming elder statesman, Ervin chaired the Watergate Committee from February 1973. Its hearings produced the revelation of the Nixon taping system by Alexander Butterfield and John Dean's devastating testimony. Ervin's refrain — "What did the President know, and when did he know it?" was actually asked by Senator Howard Baker — but Ervin's own dictum that the hearings concerned whether the nation was "a government of laws or a government of men" defined the inquiry's constitutional stakes. He retired at the end of 1974.
