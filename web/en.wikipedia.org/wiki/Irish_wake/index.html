Irish wake

Irish wake
A traditional wake: mourners keep vigil with the body at home.
TypeFuneral custom
CultureIrish
DurationTraditionally two to three days
FeaturesVigil, keening, prayers, refreshments, storytelling

An Irish wake is a traditional Irish funeral custom in which family, friends and neighbours keep vigil with the body of the deceased in the home before burial. The wake typically lasts two or three days, with mourners gathering to pray, grieve, console the bereaved, and — famously — to share stories, drink and even play games, blending sorrow with a celebration of the departed person's life.

Customs

Historically the body was laid out on a bed or table in the home, surrounded by candles, with rosary beads placed in the hands. Neighbours took turns "sitting up" through the night so the dead would never be left alone. Women keeners (bean chaointe) performed ritual laments, or caoineadh, a custom that survived into the nineteenth century.

The wake served practical as well as spiritual purposes: it confirmed death before burial, allowed the community to pay respects, and gave the family support. Food, whiskey and porter were traditionally provided, and the gathering often took on a convivial character — storytelling, music and wake games were common. Priests and bishops in the nineteenth century sometimes criticised the drinking associated with wakes.

Modern practice

Wakes remain widespread in Ireland, though the body may now repose in a funeral home rather than the family home. The custom has been carried to the Irish diaspora, particularly in the United States. Irish wakes have entered popular culture as a symbol of Irish attitudes towards death — exemplified by plays, films and literature, and by the famous scene in the film The Quiet Man.
