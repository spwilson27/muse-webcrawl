Dunkleosteus is an extinct genus of large arthrodire placoderm fish that lived during the Late Devonian period, about 382–358 million years ago. It was a pelagic apex predator in the seas of what are now North America, Europe, and North Africa. The largest species, D. terrelli, was once estimated at up to 8.8–10 m (29–33 ft) long, though more recent studies suggest a shorter, stockier body of roughly 4–5 m. It is one of the most famous prehistoric fish, recognisable by its heavily armoured head and self-sharpening bony jaw plates instead of teeth.

Description

The head and thorax of Dunkleosteus were covered in thick bony plates. Instead of true teeth it had paired bony blades in the upper and lower jaws that ground against one another, staying sharp. Biomechanical modelling suggests it had one of the most powerful bites of any fish, with rapid jaw opening powered by a four-bar linkage mechanism, creating both a fast snap and a suction effect that drew prey in.

History of discovery

Fossils were first found near Lake Erie and described by John Strong Newberry; the genus was originally named Dinichthys ("terrible fish"). In 1956 it was renamed Dunkleosteus in honour of David Dunkle, a curator at the Cleveland Museum of Natural History. Specimens come prominently from the Cleveland Shale of Ohio.

Palaeobiology

Dunkleosteus preyed on other placoderms, sharks, and ammonites, and evidence of regurgitated fossil remains suggests it could not digest bone. It likely lived in both coastal and open-ocean waters. Juveniles probably occupied different niches from adults.
