Spätzle

Spätzle
TypeEgg noodle / dumpling
RegionSwabia (southwest Germany), Austria, Switzerland, Alsace, South Tyrol
Main ingredientsFlour, eggs, salt, water or milk
VariationsKnöpfle, Kässpätzle, Krautschupfnudeln accompaniments

Spätzle (Swabian diminutive of Spatz, "sparrow") are soft egg noodles or dumplings made from a batter of flour, eggs, salt, and water. They are a staple of Swabian cuisine in southwest Germany and are also traditional in Austria, Switzerland, Alsace, Hungary, and South Tyrol. The irregular, rustic shapes are produced fresh and boiled immediately.

Preparation

The batter is thicker than pancake batter but thinner than bread dough. Traditionally it was spread on a wet wooden board (Spätzlebrett) and thin strips scraped into boiling salted water with a knife. Modern tools include the Spätzlehobel (a flat grater-like slider), a perforated press resembling a potato ricer, and colander-like sieves. The noodles are done when they float; they are skimmed out and usually tossed with butter.

Shorter, rounder shapes are called Knöpfle ("little buttons"), while long, thin ones are classic Spätzle. Estimates of the canonical form vary, but in Swabia a favourite saying holds that a girl should master spätzle-making before marriage.

Dishes

  • Kässpätzle (cheese spätzle) – layered hot with grated mountain cheese (Emmentaler, Bergkäse) and topped with crispy fried onions; the signature dish of Swabia and the Austrian Alps, comparable to macaroni and cheese.
  • Linsen mit Spätzle – lentils in a tangy sauce served over spätzle with stringy sausage (Saitenwurst).
  • Krautspätzle / Krautschupfnudeln style – pan-fried with sauerkraut and bacon.
  • Spinach and herb spätzle – the batter coloured and flavoured with puréed spinach, nettle, or wild garlic.

Commercial production and status

Fresh and dried spätzle are produced commercially across Germany, and "Swabian spätzle" (Schwäbische Spätzle) enjoy protected geographical indication (PGI) status in the European Union since 2012. Annual production in Baden-Württemberg alone runs to tens of thousands of tonnes.
