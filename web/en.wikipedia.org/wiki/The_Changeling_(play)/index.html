The Changeling (play)

Written byThomas Middleton and William Rowley
CharactersBeatrice-Joanna, De Flores, Alsemero, Isabella
Date premiered1622
Place premieredPhoenix Theatre (Cockpit), London
Original languageEarly Modern English
GenreJacobean tragedy / tragicomedy subplot
SettingAlicante, Spain; a madhouse

The Changeling is a Jacobean tragedy written by Thomas Middleton and William Rowley, first performed in 1622 by the Lady Elizabeth's Men at the Phoenix Theatre in Drury Lane and first published in 1653. It is regularly ranked among the finest tragedies of the English Renaissance stage, and is Middleton's best-known play, often mentioned alongside August Strindberg's much later chamber tragedies such as The Father for their psychological intensity.

Plot

In Alicante, Beatrice-Joanna, daughter of Vermandero, is betrothed to Alonzo de Piracquo but has fallen in love with Alsemero. She persuades her father's servant De Flores — a man she outwardly loathes but who secretly desires her — to murder Alonzo. When De Flores demands her virginity as his payment, she is trapped: the murderer and the lady are bound together, and Beatrice sinks into further deception, including the substitution of her maid Diaphanta in Alsemero's bed on the wedding night, and a second murder to conceal the first. A parallel subplot, generally attributed to Rowley, is set in a madhouse run by Alibius, whose young wife Isabella is courted by two counterfeit madmen. The play ends with Beatrice and De Flores, exposed, dying together.

Authorship and sources

The main plot is generally assigned to Middleton and the madhouse subplot largely to Rowley, who also played a comic role in the original production. The story derives from John Reynolds's moral prose collection The Triumphs of God's Revenge (1621).

Reception

Long neglected, the play was revived in the twentieth century and is now a repertory staple; its study of obsession, complicity, and self-deception — encapsulated in Beatrice's cry "I am in a labyrinth" — has attracted major productions by the Royal Shakespeare Company and at the Barbican, and screen and stage adaptations.
