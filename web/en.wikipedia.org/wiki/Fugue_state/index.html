Fugue state

SpecialtyPsychiatry
ClassificationSpecifiers of dissociative amnesia (DSM-5)

A fugue state, formally dissociative fugue, is a rare psychiatric phenomenon characterised by reversible amnesia for one's own personal identity, usually combined with unexpected, wandering travel away from home, and sometimes the assumption of an entirely new identity. In the DSM-5 it is classified not as a separate disorder but as a specifier of dissociative amnesia; in ICD-11 it is likewise a qualifier of dissociative amnesia.

Features

During a fugue, the individual loses awareness of their identity or other important autobiographical information, and may travel far from home, take up new work, and behave in an outwardly normal and unremarkable way. Episodes are usually precipitated by severe psychosocial stress, trauma, or conflict, and they typically end abruptly, with the person "coming to" in an unfamiliar place and recovering memory of their former identity — while remaining amnesic for events during the fugue itself.

History

The condition was described in the 19th century; the term derives from the Latin fuga ("flight"). A famous historical case was Ansel Bourne, a Rhode Island preacher who in 1887 disappeared and lived for two months as a shopkeeper named A. J. Brown before recovering his original identity. The writer Agatha Christie was famously found at a hotel in Harrogate in 1926 after an 11-day disappearance, an episode sometimes retrospectively associated with fugue, though its precise nature remains debated.
