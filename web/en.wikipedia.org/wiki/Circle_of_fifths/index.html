Circle of fifths

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In music theory, the circle of fifths is a way of organising the twelve tones of the chromatic scale, their corresponding key signatures, and the associated major and minor keys. Moving clockwise by perfect fifths adds one sharp (or removes one flat) from the key signature; twelve steps return to the starting pitch class, closing the circle.

Structure

Beginning at C major (no sharps or flats) at the top, the clockwise sequence runs G (1 sharp), D (2), A (3), E (4), B (5), F♯ (6), then through the enharmonic equivalents C♭–G♭ region to F (1 flat), B♭ (2), E♭ (3), A♭ (4) and D♭ (5) back to C. Each major key's relative minor is shown on the inner ring, a minor third below. Keys adjacent on the circle are closely related — sharing most of their notes and differing by a single accidental — while opposite keys (a tritone apart) are maximally distant.

Uses

The circle is a standard tool for learning key signatures, planning modulations, and analysing chord progressions: much tonal and popular music moves around small arcs of the circle (for example, the ii–V–I progression follows descending fifths). In equal temperament the circle closes exactly; in just intonation, stacking pure fifths produces the Pythagorean comma, which the diagram conventionally ignores.

History

The concept emerged in late-17th-century theory (notably in Russian theorist Nikolay Diletsky's treatise of 1679 and Heinichen's of 1728) and became a staple of harmony pedagogy.
