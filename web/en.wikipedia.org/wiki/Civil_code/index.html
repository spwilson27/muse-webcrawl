Civil code

A civil code is a systematic codification of private law, typically covering persons, property, obligations, and family law in civil-law jurisdictions.

A civil code is a codification of private law relating to property, family, and obligations. A jurisdiction that has a civil code generally also has a code of civil procedure. In some jurisdictions, such as France, Spain and some US states, the civil code also includes elements of commercial and criminal law, although these are more often codified separately.

Civil codes are characteristic of civil law legal systems, in which legislation is the primary source of law and codes aim to state the law comprehensively and systematically, in contrast to common law systems where case law plays a larger role.

History

The idea of codifying private law goes back to ancient collections such as the Code of Hammurabi and Roman law; the great modern codification was Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis (6th century AD), which was revived in medieval European universities. The modern era of codes began with the Bavarian Code (1756), the Prussian Allgemeines Landrecht (1794), and above all the French Napoleonic Code of 1804 (Code civil des Français), which spread through Europe and Latin America by conquest, imitation, and colonial transplantation. Germany's Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (BGB), effective 1900, influenced codes in Japan, China, Brazil, and elsewhere. Major codes were (re)enacted in the 20th and 21st centuries in, among others, Italy (1942), Quebec (1994), the Netherlands (1992), Russia (1994–2006), and China (2021).

Contents

A typical civil code is organised into books dealing with: persons (natural and legal); family law (marriage, divorce, parentage, guardianship); property (ownership, possession, servitudes); obligations (contracts, torts/delicts, unjust enrichment); and succession (wills and intestacy). Some codes start with a general part stating principles such as good faith and the prohibition of abuse of rights.
