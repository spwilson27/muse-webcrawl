Train driver

A train driver, also called a locomotive engineer in North America, is the person who operates a train. The driver is responsible for driving safely according to signals and speed restrictions, observing the route, stopping accurately at stations, and responding to faults and emergencies. On most modern railways the driver works alone in the cab, a practice known as driver-only operation, though freight and some passenger services may carry additional crew.

Duties

Before a journey the driver prepares the train, checks the locomotive or multiple unit, and reviews notices about temporary speed restrictions and engineering work. During the journey the driver controls acceleration and braking, sounds the horn where required, communicates with signallers and control, and manages passenger information and doors on driver-only trains. Drivers must learn each route in detail — gradients, junctions, signal positions and station layouts — a body of knowledge assessed before they may drive unsupervised.

Training and licensing

Training typically takes one to two years and combines classroom instruction in rules and traction with supervised driving hours. In the European Union, drivers are licensed under a common directive requiring medical fitness, including good vision and hearing, and periodic re-assessment. Strict rules limit working hours and alcohol consumption because of the safety-critical nature of the job.

Terminology

"Engineer" in American usage descends from the era when the driver was responsible for the locomotive as a machine, while the conductor was in charge of the train. In Britain, "driver" is standard; the historic footplate crew of steam locomotives paired the driver with a fireman who managed the boiler.
