The "Letter from Birmingham Jail", also known as the "Letter from Birmingham City Jail" and "The Negro Is Your Brother", is an open letter written on 16 April 1963 by Martin Luther King Jr. from his cell in the Birmingham, Alabama city jail, where he had been imprisoned for participating in nonviolent demonstrations against segregation. It is one of the most celebrated documents of the American civil rights movement and a classic of American political rhetoric.

Background

King wrote the letter in response to "A Call for Unity," a statement by eight white Alabama clergymen who had criticized the Birmingham campaign's demonstrations as "unwise and untimely" and appealed for negotiation and patience. King began composing his reply on the margins of the newspaper in which the statement appeared, continued on scraps of paper supplied by a jail trustee, and completed it on a legal pad provided by his attorneys.

Contents

The letter defends the strategy of nonviolent direct action. King argues that he is in Birmingham because "injustice is here," and that he cannot sit idly by because he is tied in "a single garment of destiny" with all Americans: "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere." He outlines the four steps of a nonviolent campaign — collection of facts, negotiation, self-purification, and direct action — and explains that direct action seeks to create a crisis that forces a community that has refused to negotiate to confront the issue.

King distinguishes between just and unjust laws, drawing on Augustine and Aquinas: an unjust law is one that degrades human personality, and a person who breaks such a law openly and accepts the penalty expresses the highest respect for law. Perhaps most famously, he expresses his disappointment with the "white moderate," whom he describes as more devoted to "order" than to justice and who paternalistically believes he can set the timetable for another man's freedom. His statement that "the time is always right to do right" and his warning that the church risked becoming an irrelevant social club have echoed through later debates.

Publication and legacy

The letter was published in mid-1963 in several venues, including The Christian Century and The Atlantic Monthly (under the title "The Negro Is Your Brother"). It was later included in King's 1964 book Why We Can't Wait. It is now read annually in many schools and is commemorated each Martin Luther King Jr. Day by public readings, and excerpts are inscribed at memorials including the Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial in Washington, D.C.
