Scorched earth

A scorched-earth policy is a military strategy of burning or destroying buildings, crops, and other resources that might be useful to an invading enemy force. The destruction could include food supplies, transportation infrastructure, industrial resources and even the local population's shelter — anything that could be used by the enemy is targeted, and bodies of water may be deliberately contaminated. The word is derived from the ancient Chinese concept of "clearing the fields".

Historical examples

The Scythians used scorched earth against Darius the Great of Persia, retreating into the steppe and destroying sustenance along the way. In the Napoleonic Wars, the Portuguese and Spanish populations destroyed supplies ahead of French armies during the Peninsular War, and the strategy is famously associated with Russia's retreat before Napoleon's 1812 invasion of Russia, in which Moscow itself was burned.

In the American Civil War, Union general William Tecumseh Sherman's March to the Sea destroyed farms, railroads and industry across Georgia in 1864, deliberately targeting the Confederate war economy and civilian morale. During the Second Boer War, British forces under Lord Kitchener burned farms and interned civilians in concentration camps to deny support to Boer commandos.

Twentieth century and beyond

In the Second World War, the Soviet Union applied scorched earth on a massive scale during the German invasion of 1941, evacuating industry eastward and destroying stocks in retreat; Nazi Germany reciprocated with orders to devastate territory it abandoned, culminating in Hitler's "Nero Decree" of March 1945 (largely sabotaged by Albert Speer and local commanders). Saddam Hussein's forces set Kuwaiti oil wells ablaze while retreating in 1991, one of the most notorious modern examples, causing a major environmental disaster.

Legality

Scorched-earth tactics against an occupying force on one's own territory have generally been treated differently from destruction in occupied territory. The 1977 Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions prohibits destroying objects indispensable to civilian survival except under narrow conditions where territory is defended against invasion, and extensive destruction not justified by military necessity constitutes a grave breach. The Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court similarly criminalises extensive, wanton destruction of property.
