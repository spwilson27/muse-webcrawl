Enterprise architecture

Enterprise architecture (EA) is the practice of analysing, designing, planning and implementing the structures and processes of an organisation so that its business strategy, information and technology work together coherently. An enterprise architecture describes the current state of the organisation's business processes, data, applications and technology infrastructure, a desired future ("target") state, and a roadmap for getting from one to the other.

Domains

EA is conventionally divided into four interlocking domains:

  • Business architecture — the organisation's strategy, governance, structure and key business processes.
  • Data architecture — the structure of the organisation's logical and physical data assets and how they flow.
  • Application architecture — the portfolio of software systems, their responsibilities and interactions.
  • Technology architecture — the hardware, platforms, networks and standards underpinning the rest.

History and purpose

The discipline grew out of information-systems planning in the 1980s, notably John Zachman's framework paper of 1987. It gained prominence as large organisations struggled with duplicated systems, incompatible data and IT investment that poorly matched business goals. A well-run EA function aims to reduce complexity and cost, improve agility, manage risk and ensure that technology decisions support long-term strategy rather than short-term convenience. Practitioners use enterprise architecture frameworks such as TOGAF®, the Zachman Framework, FEAF (in US government) and DoDAF (in defence) to organise their work.

Criticism

Enterprise architecture is sometimes criticised for producing elaborate models that quickly fall out of date, or for becoming an "ivory tower" function detached from delivery. Modern practice stresses lightweight, continuously updated models, close collaboration with agile product teams, and measurable business outcomes.
