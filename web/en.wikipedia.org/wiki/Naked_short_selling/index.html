Naked short selling

Naked short selling
TypeSecurities trading practice
Key riskFailure to deliver (FTD)
Regulation (US)Regulation SHO (2005); Rule 105; 2008 emergency orders
StatusGenerally prohibited in the US, EU and other markets

Naked short selling, or naked shorting, is the practice of selling shares of a stock short without first borrowing the shares or arranging to borrow them, and without delivering them by the settlement date. In a conventional ("covered") short sale the seller borrows the stock before selling; in a naked short sale the shares may never enter the buyer's account, producing a failure to deliver (FTD).

Regulation

In the United States, the SEC adopted Regulation SHO in 2005, imposing locate and close-out requirements for fails. During the 2008 financial crisis the SEC issued emergency orders temporarily banning short selling (naked and covered) in financial stocks, and permanently tightened close-out rules (Rule 204T, later Rule 204). The European Union's Short Selling Regulation (2012) largely bans uncovered short sales of shares and sovereign debt. Australia, Japan, Hong Kong and Canada impose similar restrictions.

Debate

Critics argue naked shorting lets sellers create artificial supply, depress prices and even drive small companies into bankruptcy — complaints that featured prominently around stocks such as Overstock.com and, in 2021, GameStop. Defenders and regulators note that some fails arise innocently from market-making and settlement mechanics, and empirical studies have found the scale of abusive naked shorting smaller than its public reputation. Market makers are commonly granted exemptions for bona fide hedging and liquidity provision.
