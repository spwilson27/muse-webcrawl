Sanskritization

Sanskritization is a term in sociology and anthropology describing the process by which castes or communities placed lower in the Indian caste hierarchy seek upward mobility by emulating the rituals, customs, ideology, and way of life of higher, especially Brahminical, castes. The concept was introduced by the Indian sociologist M. N. Srinivas in his study of the Coorgs of Mysore, published in Religion and Society Among the Coorgs of South India (1952).

The process

According to Srinivas, when a lower caste group gains economic or political power, it typically adopts the practices of a higher caste over a generation or two: vegetarianism, teetotalism, Sanskritic deities and rituals, Brahmin priests, and customs such as dowry in place of bride-price. The group then claims a higher varna status, historically often Kshatriya. Sanskritization thus generally produces positional change within the caste system rather than structural change to the system itself.

Criticism and related concepts

Scholars have debated the concept's reach: it has been contrasted with "Westernization" and "modernization", criticised for overstating Brahmin models where Kshatriya, Vaishya, or regional dominant-caste models predominate, and supplemented by notions of de-Sanskritization and Dalit assertion, in which groups instead reject Sanskritic norms.
