A dirndl is a traditional dress worn in Bavaria, Austria, South Tyrol and other Alpine regions of German-speaking Europe. It consists of a close-fitting bodice, a low-cut blouse, a full skirt gathered at the waist, and an apron. The word derives from the Bavarian and Austrian dialect Dirndl, meaning a young girl or maidservant, whose work clothes the garment originally was.

History

The dirndl developed in the nineteenth century from the clothing of Alpine peasant women and servants. In the late 1800s it was adopted as fashionable summer dress by wealthy townspeople holidaying in the countryside, becoming more decorative and varied in fabric and trimming. Regional styles (Tracht) evolved, with distinctive colours, embroidery, hats and jewellery marking local identity.

Components and customs

The complete outfit typically includes the dress itself, a white or coloured blouse, an apron tied with a bow, and accessories such as a shawl, choker or silver jewellery. A widely repeated tradition holds that the position of the apron bow signals the wearer's marital status — tied on the left for single, on the right for married — though the custom is largely a modern festival convention.

Modern use

Today dirndls are worn at folk festivals such as Munich's Oktoberfest, at weddings and church celebrations, and as a statement of regional identity. They range from inexpensive festival versions to hand-tailored garments costing many thousands of euros. The male counterpart is lederhosen.
