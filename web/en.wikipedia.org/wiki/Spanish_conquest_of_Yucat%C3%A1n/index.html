The Spanish conquest of Yucatán was the campaign undertaken by Spanish conquistadores against the Late Postclassic Maya states of the Yucatán Peninsula, in present-day Mexico, Guatemala, and Belize. Unlike the swift fall of the Aztec and Inca empires, the conquest of the politically fragmented Maya region took about 170 years, ending with the fall of the Itza kingdom of Nojpetén in 1697.

Early expeditions

Spanish contact began with the shipwrecked sailors Jerónimo de Aguilar and Gonzalo Guerrero in 1511, and the expeditions of Hernández de Córdoba (1517), Grijalva (1518), and Cortés (1519). Francisco de Montejo received the title of adelantado of Yucatán in 1526 and launched the first campaigns in 1527–28 and 1531–35, both failures in the face of Maya resistance, disease, and the lure of richer conquests elsewhere.

Conquest of the peninsula

Montejo's son, Francisco de Montejo the Younger, renewed the campaign from 1540, founding Mérida in 1542 on the site of T'ho. A great Maya revolt in 1546–47 was crushed, and Franciscan missions and encomiendas extended Spanish control. The southern lowlands, however, remained unconquered: the Itza of Lake Petén Itzá maintained independence until Martín de Ursúa's expedition took Nojpetén on 13 March 1697, the last independent indigenous kingdom in the Americas to fall.

Legacy

The conquest brought epidemic disease, forced labour, and the destruction of Maya books — most notoriously Bishop Diego de Landa's auto-da-fé at Maní in 1562 — though Landa's Relación also preserved invaluable information about Maya culture. Maya resistance continued into the colonial period and beyond, culminating in the Caste War of the 19th century.
