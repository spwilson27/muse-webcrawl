Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln

Little Saint Hugh of Lincoln (1246 – 27 August 1255) was an English boy whose death in Lincoln was falsely blamed on Jews, in one of the most notorious episodes of the medieval blood libel in England. After Hugh's body was found in a well, a Jew named Copin was tortured into a confession and executed, and many other Jews were arrested; eighteen were hanged in London. Hugh was never formally canonised by the Roman Catholic Church, but he was venerated locally as a saint and martyr, and his story entered ballad and folklore.

Background

Accusations that Jews ritually murdered Christian children had appeared in England since the case of William of Norwich in 1144. By the thirteenth century such charges were a recurrent feature of anti-Jewish persecution in Europe, despite repeated papal condemnations. Lincoln had a significant Jewish community, one of the most important in medieval England, engaged largely in moneylending.

The events of 1255

Hugh, the son of a Lincoln woman named Beatrice, disappeared on 31 July 1255. His body was discovered about a month later, on 29 August, in a well (or, in some accounts, a pit) in the Jewish quarter of the city, in the house or vicinity of a Jew named Copin (also given as Koppin or Jopin). According to the chronicler Matthew Paris, the Jews of Lincoln had gathered for a wedding, and suspicion fell on them.

Copin was arrested and, under torture and the promise of immunity from John of Lexington, a servant of King Henry III, confessed that the boy had been crucified by Jews assembled from across England. The alleged motive, in the standard blood-libel narrative, was ritual mockery of the Passion of Christ. Copin was executed by being dragged by a horse and hanged, despite the promised immunity.

Arrests and executions

Some ninety Jews of Lincoln were seized and sent to the Tower of London. Eighteen were hanged for refusing to plead before a jury that included Jews, or for the alleged crime itself, and the remainder were imprisoned; they were eventually released after the intervention of Richard of Cornwall, the king's brother, who held rights over English Jewry, and of the friars. Henry III, who had sold his rights in the Jews to Richard for a term, initially pressed the prosecution, making this the first blood-libel case in England to receive direct royal endorsement.

Veneration

Hugh's body was buried in Lincoln Cathedral, where a shrine was erected and pilgrims reported miracles. He was known as "Little Saint Hugh" to distinguish him from the adult Saint Hugh of Lincoln, the twelfth-century bishop of the city. The shrine was destroyed during the Reformation, though a fragment, sometimes said to be part of it, survives in the cathedral. The site of the former shrine now bears a plaque explaining that the accusations were false and that the story promoted anti-Jewish prejudice.

Legacy

The story was retold in chronicles, in the ballad "Sir Hugh, or the Jew's Daughter" (Child Ballad 155), and in later literature; Chaucer's Prioress's Tale is often linked to the same tradition of the child martyr. Modern historians regard the case as a landmark in medieval English antisemitism and a prelude to the expulsion of the Jews from England in 1290. In 1955, the Church of England placed a plaque at the site of the shrine acknowledging the injustice, and Lincoln Cathedral has since used the episode in education about prejudice.
