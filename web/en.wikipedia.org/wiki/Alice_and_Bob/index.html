Alice and Bob

Alice and Bob are fictional characters commonly used as placeholders in discussions of cryptographic protocols and systems. The names were introduced by Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman in their 1978 paper describing the RSA cryptosystem, and have since become a standard convention in cryptography, computer security, physics, and game theory.

Rather than describing a protocol in terms of "party A sends a message to party B", authors personify the participants: Alice generally wants to send a message to Bob. The cast expanded over time to include other characters with mnemonic roles.

List of characters

  • Alice and Bob — the two original parties who wish to communicate.
  • Carol (or Charlie) — a third participant.
  • Dave — a fourth participant.
  • Eve — an eavesdropper; usually a passive attacker who listens but does not alter messages.
  • Mallory — a malicious active attacker who can intercept, modify, delete, or substitute messages.
  • Trent — a trusted third party or arbitrator.
  • Peggy and Victor — the prover and verifier in zero-knowledge proofs.
  • Oscar — an opponent, sometimes equivalent to Mallory.
  • Wendy — a whistleblower.

History

The names first appeared in the 1978 Communications of the ACM paper "A Method for Obtaining Digital Signatures and Public-key Cryptosystems". Rivest later explained that the names were chosen simply because "A" and "B" needed memorable counterparts, and the characters were reportedly influenced by the 1969 film Bob & Carol & Ted & Alice. Bruce Schneier popularised the extended cast in his 1994 book Applied Cryptography.

Use in other fields

In quantum mechanics and quantum information, Alice and Bob are typically separated observers who perform measurements on shared entangled particles, as in discussions of the EPR paradox and Bell tests. In game theory and economics they may stand for two players or agents. The convention has also spread into examples about network protocols, distributed systems, and even linguistics.
