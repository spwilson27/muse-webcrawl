Osiris myth

Isis mourns over the body of Osiris.

The Osiris myth is the most elaborate and influential story in ancient Egyptian mythology. It concerns the murder of the god Osiris, a primeval king of Egypt, and its consequences. Osiris's murderer, his brother Set, usurps his throne. Meanwhile, Osiris's wife Isis restores her husband's body, allowing him to posthumously conceive a son, Horus. The remainder of the story focuses on Horus, once a vulnerable child protected by his mother, then, as he comes of age, a rival for the throne. Their often violent conflict ends with Horus's triumph, which restores maat (cosmic and social order) in Egypt, while Osiris reigns as king of the dead in the afterlife.

The story

No single Egyptian text tells the complete myth; episodes are woven through the Pyramid Texts (c. 2400–2300 BCE), Coffin Texts, hymns, and ritual texts across three millennia. The most complete continuous account is the Greek writer Plutarch's treatise On Isis and Osiris (c. 100 CE). In Plutarch's version, Set tricks Osiris into lying in a decorated chest, seals it, and throws it into the Nile; it drifts to Byblos, where a tree grows around it. Isis recovers the body, but Set discovers it and dismembers it — in Plutarch's account into fourteen pieces — scattering them across Egypt. Isis, aided by her sister Nephthys and the jackal god Anubis, gathers the pieces, reassembles and embalms the body (the origin of mummification), and briefly revives Osiris, conceiving Horus.

Significance

The myth underlay the Egyptian ideology of kingship: the living pharaoh was identified with Horus, the deceased pharaoh with Osiris, so each royal succession re-enacted Horus's triumph. Because Osiris died and was restored, he became the paradigmatic god of the afterlife, presiding over the judgment of the dead, and ordinary Egyptians hoped to share his resurrection. Festivals re-enacting the myth, notably the Khoiak festival with its "corn mummies" of sprouting grain, linked Osiris to the annual renewal of vegetation and the Nile flood.

Influence

The Osiris cult spread through the Greco-Roman world, and the myth's themes of death and rebirth have been compared — controversially — with other dying-and-rising deities of the ancient Near East and Mediterranean. It remains the best-known narrative of Egyptian religion.
