Land power

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A land power is a state whose strength is based primarily on the control of land territory and on military forces — principally armies — able to dominate a contiguous landmass. The concept is most often contrasted with sea power, the strength derived from command of the seas by a navy. The terms form a classic pairing in geopolitics and grand strategy.

Concept

The land power–sea power distinction underlies much of classical geopolitical theory. Halford Mackinder's "Heartland" thesis (1904, developed in Democratic Ideals and Reality, 1919) argued that the state controlling the interior of Eurasia — the "Heartland", largely inaccessible to sea power — could command the resources to dominate the "World-Island" of Europe, Asia, and Africa. Nicholas Spykman later emphasised the "Rimland", the coastal fringes of Eurasia, as the decisive zone contested between land and sea powers.

Land powers typically enjoy interior lines of communication: railways and roads allow them to shift forces across a continental interior more cheaply, in the modern era, than rivals can move them by sea. Their vulnerability lies in the cost of defending long land frontiers and in dependence on overland logistics.

Historical examples

Classic land powers include the continental empires of continental Europe and Asia: the Roman Empire in its later continental form, the Ottoman Empire, the Russian Empire and its successor the Soviet Union, Napoleonic France, and Imperial and Nazi Germany. In Asia, imperial China is often described as a predominantly land power, though it periodically maintained substantial fleets. Modern examples frequently cited include Russia and China, while the United States is usually classed as a sea power (with strong land and air components) because of its oceanic position and naval reach.

Strategy

Land powers tend to prioritise large standing armies, fortified frontiers, and buffer states or client territories on their periphery. Because continental states frequently border several potential rivals, their strategies often stress depth — trading space for time — and the secure control of land corridors. The rise of air power, missiles, and nuclear weapons in the twentieth century complicated the classical dichotomy, leading theorists to add air power and space power to the taxonomy.
