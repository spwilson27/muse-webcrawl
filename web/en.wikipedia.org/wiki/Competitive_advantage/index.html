Competitive advantage

In business, a competitive advantage is an attribute that allows an organization to outperform its competitors. A competitive advantage may include access to natural resources, such as high-grade ores or a low-cost power source, highly skilled labor, geographic location, high entry barriers, and access to new technology and information. The term competitive advantage also applies to strategies and people: an employee may be described as having a competitive advantage in the marketplace based on their skills, experience, or reputation.

The concept is central to strategic management and was popularized by Harvard Business School professor Michael Porter, particularly in his books Competitive Strategy (1980) and Competitive Advantage (1985). Porter identified two basic types of competitive advantage a firm can possess: cost leadership and differentiation, which, combined with the scope of activities (broad or narrow market target), yield three generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation, and focus.

Porter's generic strategies

  • Cost leadership – the firm becomes the lowest-cost producer in its industry, exploiting economies of scale, proprietary technology, or preferential access to raw materials.
  • Differentiation – the firm offers a product or service perceived industry-wide as unique, allowing it to charge a premium price.
  • Focus – the firm concentrates on a narrow segment of the market, either through cost focus or differentiation focus.

Porter argued that a firm that engages in both strategies without achieving either may become "stuck in the middle" and earn below-average profitability.

Resource-based view

The resource-based view (RBV) of the firm, associated with scholars such as Jay Barney, holds that sustainable competitive advantage arises from resources and capabilities that are valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, and non-substitutable (the VRIN criteria). A related framework, VRIO, adds the question of whether the firm is organized to exploit the resource.

Sustainability

A competitive advantage is sustainable if competitors cannot easily duplicate or neutralize it. Barriers to imitation include patents and other intellectual property, brand reputation, network effects, switching costs, and causally ambiguous organizational capabilities. In rapidly changing industries, the related concept of dynamic capabilities – the firm's capacity to reconfigure its resource base – is used to explain how advantages are renewed over time.
