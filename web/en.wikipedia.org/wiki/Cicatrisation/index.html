Cicatrisation

Cicatrisation is the process by which a wound heals and a scar (a cicatrix) forms. It is the final phase of secondary wound healing, in which granulation tissue matures, collagen is reorganised and remodelled, and the defect is closed by fibrous connective tissue rather than by regeneration of the original tissue.

Process

Following injury, healing proceeds through overlapping phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation (with formation of granulation tissue, angiogenesis and deposition of type III collagen), and finally maturation or cicatrisation. During cicatrisation, type III collagen is progressively replaced by stronger type I collagen, cross-linking increases tensile strength, and myofibroblasts contract the wound. A mature scar typically reaches only about 70–80% of the tensile strength of uninjured skin.

Abnormal cicatrisation

Disorders of scar formation include hypertrophic scars, which remain within the boundaries of the original wound, and keloids, which extend beyond it. Delayed or excessive cicatrisation can lead to contractures, particularly over joints and after burns. In some cultures, deliberate scarification (also called cicatrisation) is practised as a form of body modification or tribal identification.
