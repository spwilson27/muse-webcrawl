Mop fair

Mop fairs are a traditional feature of English towns, held annually — traditionally around Michaelmas (29 September) or the Old Michaelmas Day (11 October) — as hiring fairs at which agricultural labourers and domestic servants sought employment for the coming year. Many survive today as funfairs, having lost their employment function in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

History and customs

Hiring or "statute" fairs arose from statutes of Edward III and Elizabeth I requiring servants to be hired before justices; by the 17th and 18th centuries they had become the main labour exchanges of rural England. Job-seekers carried a token of their trade: a maid carried a mop head (hence the name), a shepherd a crook, a carter a whip, a waggoner a piece of whipcord, and so on. Employers paid a shilling "fastening penny" to seal a bargain, and the newly hired servant could then spend the fair day at the stalls, shows, and ale booths.

If a servant was dissatisfied or not hired at the first fair, a second "runaway mop" a week or so later gave another chance to find a place. Fairs were commonly accompanied by roast oxen, gingerbread stalls, boxing booths, and theatrical shows, and were periodically criticised by reformers for drunkenness and disorder.

Surviving fairs

Well-known surviving mop fairs include those at Stratford-upon-Avon, Warwick, Tewkesbury, Cirencester, King's Norton (Birmingham), Ashby-de-la-Zouch (the Statutes Fair), and the Michaelmas fair at Sherborne. Most are now pleasure fairs operated by showmen, but several retain ceremonial elements such as the roasting of an ox or the reading of the fair's charter.
