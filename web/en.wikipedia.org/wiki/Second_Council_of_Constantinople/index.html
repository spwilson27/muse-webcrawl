The Second Council of Constantinople is the fifth of the first seven ecumenical councils recognised by both the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Catholic Church. It was convoked by the Byzantine emperor Justinian I and met in Constantinople from 5 May to 2 June 553. Its principal work was to condemn the Three Chapters — the writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia, the anti-Cyrillian writings of Theodoret of Cyrus, and the letter of Ibas of Edessa to Maris — in an attempt to reconcile Miaphysite Christians to the Council of Chalcedon (451).

Background

Although Chalcedon had defined Christ as one person in two natures, many Christians in Egypt and Syria (Miaphysites) regarded it as Nestorian. Justinian sought their reconciliation by condemning writings they associated with Nestorianism. His edict against the Three Chapters (544) was resisted in the West, and Pope Vigilius, brought to Constantinople, vacillated under imperial pressure, alternately condemning and defending the chapters.

Proceedings and decrees

The council, dominated by Eastern bishops, issued fourteen anathemas against the Three Chapters and reaffirmed the orthodoxy of Cyril of Alexandria's Christology as the correct interpretation of Chalcedon. It also approved the theopaschite formula "One of the Holy Trinity suffered in the flesh." Vigilius, after refusing to attend, finally accepted the council's decrees in 554. A traditional association of the council with the condemnation of Origenism and the doctrine of the pre-existence of souls is debated by scholars, since the fifteen anti-Origenist anathemas may stem from a separate synod of 543.

Aftermath

The council failed in its main aim: Miaphysites were not reconciled, and separate (Oriental Orthodox) churches endured. In the West, the condemnation of the Three Chapters provoked the schism of Aquileia, which lasted over a century. The council is nonetheless recognised as ecumenical by both East and West.
