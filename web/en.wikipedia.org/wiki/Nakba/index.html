The Nakba (Arabic: النكبة, "the catastrophe") refers to the displacement, dispossession, and flight or expulsion of the Palestinian Arab population during the 1948 Palestine war and the surrounding period of the 1947–1949 war that accompanied the establishment of the State of Israel. More than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs — over half the pre-war Arab population of Mandatory Palestine — fled or were expelled from their homes, and more than 400 Arab villages and towns were depopulated and in many cases destroyed.

Causes and events

The exodus unfolded in several waves during the civil war phase following the 1947 UN Partition Plan and after Israel's declaration of independence on 14 May 1948 and the invasion by Arab armies. Causes included direct expulsions by Zionist and later Israeli forces — notably from Lydda and Ramle in July 1948 — flight from fighting, fear of atrocities amplified by events such as the Deir Yassin massacre of April 1948, and the collapse of Palestinian urban leadership and society. Historians continue to debate the relative weight of expulsion, fear, and encouragement by Arab leaders, a historiographical controversy sharpened by the work of Israel's "New Historians".

Aftermath

The refugees and their descendants settled mainly in the West Bank, the Gaza Strip, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria, where many remain in refugee camps administered with the support of UNRWA. Israel barred the refugees' return and, through laws such as the Absentees' Property Law, transferred their land and property to the state and new Jewish immigrants. The Palestinian right of return remains a core issue of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict.

Commemoration

Palestinians commemorate the Nakba annually on 15 May, Nakba Day. The term and its commemoration are politically contested in Israel, where legislation has restricted state funding for institutions that mark Israel's Independence Day as a day of mourning.
