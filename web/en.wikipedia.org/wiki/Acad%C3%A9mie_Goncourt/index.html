The Académie Goncourt is a French literary organisation based in Paris. It was founded by the will of the writer Edmond de Goncourt (1822–1896), who directed that an academy be created to award an annual prize for prose fiction in French. The academy was officially constituted in 1900, and the first Prix Goncourt was awarded in 1903.

Organisation

The academy consists of ten members, known as les Dix, who are elected for life by co-option. Members traditionally meet on the first Tuesday of each month at the Restaurant Drouant in Paris, at a table reserved for them, and deliberate over lunch. Each member is identified by a numbered couvert (place setting).

The Prix Goncourt

The Prix Goncourt is France's most prestigious literary award, given each year to the author of "the best and most imaginative prose work of the year". The prize money is nominal — traditionally a symbolic cheque of €10 — but winning greatly increases a book's sales and the author's reputation. Winners are announced in early November after voting at Drouant.

History

Edmond de Goncourt and his brother Jules were prominent figures of nineteenth-century French letters. After Jules's death, Edmond's testament funded the academy in both brothers' memory, in conscious rivalry with the Académie française, whose taste the Goncourts considered too conservative.
