The Styx (Ancient Greek: Στύξ, "shuddering" or "hateful") is a river in Greek mythology that forms the boundary between Earth (Gaia) and the Underworld (Hades). The rivers Acheron, Cocutus, Lethe, Phlegethon, and Styx all converge at the centre of the underworld on a great marsh, which is sometimes also called the Styx. The ferryman Charon carried the souls of the newly dead across the river in return for a coin (an obol) placed in the mouth of the deceased.

The goddess Styx

Styx is also the name of a goddess, the eldest of the Oceanids, daughter of Oceanus and Tethys. Because she was the first to rally to Zeus's side in the Titanomachy, Zeus honoured her children—Zelos (Rivalry), Nike (Victory), Kratos (Strength) and Bia (Force)—and decreed that the gods' most binding oath would be sworn by the water of Styx. In Hesiod's Theogony, a god who swears falsely by Styx lies breathless for a year and is exiled from the divine assemblies for nine more.

Properties and myths

The waters of the Styx were said to have special properties. In a later tradition, the infant Achilles was dipped into the river by his mother Thetis, making him invulnerable except for the heel by which she held him—hence the expression "Achilles' heel". In some accounts the river's water was corrosive or poisonous, able to dissolve any vessel except one made of a horse's hoof; a related legend linked the real Styx waterfall in Arcadia (the Mavroneri) with poisoning lore, including a story that Alexander the Great was poisoned with its water.

Later influence

The Styx appears throughout classical literature—in Homer, Virgil's Aeneid, and Dante's Inferno, where it forms a marsh in the fifth circle, home to the wrathful. It remains a potent symbol of the boundary between life and death, giving its name to works in literature, music (including the rock band Styx) and astronomy.
