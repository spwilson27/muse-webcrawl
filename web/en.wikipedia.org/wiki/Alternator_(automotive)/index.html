Alternator (automotive)

An alternator is a type of electric generator used in modern automobiles to charge the battery and to power the electrical system while the engine is running. It replaced the DC generator (dynamo) in the 1960s because it is lighter, simpler, more durable, and able to deliver high current even at idle speeds. Although the car's electrical system is direct current, the alternator produces alternating current internally and rectifies it with diodes.

Construction and operation

The alternator consists of a rotating magnetic field (the rotor, usually a claw-pole electromagnet fed through slip rings and brushes) inside fixed three-phase windings (the stator). Driven by a serpentine or V-belt from the crankshaft at roughly two to three times engine speed, the rotor induces three-phase AC in the stator. A bridge of silicon diodes rectifies this to DC, and a voltage regulator — usually built in — adjusts the rotor field current to hold system voltage near 13.8–14.4 volts regardless of speed and load.

Typical passenger-car alternators deliver 70–150 amperes; vehicles with heavy electrical loads may use units rated above 200 A. Air cooling by a shaft-mounted fan is standard, though some high-output designs are water-cooled.

History

Alternators with solid-state rectifiers appeared in production cars around 1960 — Chrysler offered one as standard equipment that year — and by the mid-1970s the alternator had universally displaced the dynamo in new cars. Later developments include computer-controlled "smart charging", in which the engine management system varies the regulated voltage to reduce fuel consumption, and 48-volt belt-driven starter-generators in mild hybrid vehicles, which combine the alternator with a starter motor and can recuperate braking energy.

Failure modes and symptoms

Common failures include worn brushes, failed rectifier diodes (causing battery drain and AC ripple), a defective regulator (over- or undercharging) and bearing wear. Symptoms include a dashboard charging warning light, dim headlights, a discharged battery and whining noises. Output is readily tested with a voltmeter across the battery: a healthy charging system shows roughly 13.8–14.4 V with the engine running.
