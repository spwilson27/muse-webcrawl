1 Maccabees, also known as the First Book of Maccabees, is a historical narrative written in Hebrew by an anonymous Jewish author after the restoration of an independent Jewish kingdom by the Hasmoneans in the late 2nd century BC. The original Hebrew is lost; the book survives in Greek in the Septuagint. It recounts the history of the Jews from 175 BC to 134 BC, centred on the Maccabean Revolt against the Seleucid Empire and its aftermath.

Contents

The book opens with Alexander the Great, the division of his empire, and the accession of the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, whose decrees suppressed Jewish worship and desecrated the Temple in Jerusalem (the "abomination of desolation," 167 BC). The priest Mattathias of Modein sparks the revolt by killing a royal official and a Jew offering a pagan sacrifice. After his death, leadership passes to his sons — above all Judah Maccabee ("the Hammer"), whose victories culminate in the recapture and rededication of the Temple, the event commemorated by Hanukkah. The narrative continues through the leadership of Jonathan Apphus and Simon Thassi, whose confirmation as high priest and ethnarch in 140 BC effectively establishes the independent Hasmonean dynasty.

Historical reliability

Modern scholars regard 1 Maccabees as one of the most reliable historical sources for the period, broadly corroborated by 2 Maccabees (an independent, more theological account focused on Judah) and by Josephus. The work is written in a sober, biblical style consciously modelled on the historical books of the Hebrew Bible, though God is never mentioned directly in the narrative.

Canonical status

The book is part of the deuterocanon accepted by the Catholic Church and the Eastern Orthodox Church. It is not part of the Jewish canon or the Protestant Old Testament, where it is classed among the Apocrypha. Along with 2, 3, and 4 Maccabees, it preserves the principal ancient accounts of the revolt.
