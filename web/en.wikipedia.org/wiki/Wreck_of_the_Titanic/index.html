The wreck of the Titanic lies on the seabed of the North Atlantic Ocean, about 3.8 kilometres below the surface, where the ocean liner RMS Titanic came to rest after striking an iceberg and sinking on 15 April 1912 with the loss of more than 1,500 lives.

Discovery

The wreck eluded searchers for 73 years. It was located on 1 September 1985 by a joint French-American expedition led by Jean-Louis Michel of IFREMER and Robert Ballard of the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, using the deep-towed sonar and camera system Argo. The search was partly funded by a U.S. Navy mission to survey two lost nuclear submarines. Ballard returned in 1986 aboard DSV Alvin to make the first crewed dives on the wreck.

Condition

The ship broke in two as it sank: the bow section is comparatively intact, while the stern is a mangled wreck, the two parts lying about 600 metres apart amid a large debris field. The wreck is being steadily consumed by iron-eating bacteria, notably Halomonas titanicae, which produce rusticles; scientists estimate the structure will largely collapse within decades.

Exploration and salvage

Expeditions by RMS Titanic Inc., the wreck's salvor-in-possession, recovered thousands of artifacts, a practice that remains controversial. In 2012, on the centenary of the sinking, the wreck came under the protection of UNESCO's convention on underwater cultural heritage. In June 2023 the submersible Titan imploded while descending to the wreck, killing all five occupants and intensifying debate about tourist dives.
