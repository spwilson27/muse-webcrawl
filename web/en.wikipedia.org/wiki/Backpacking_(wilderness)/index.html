Backpacking (wilderness)

Backpackers on a mountain trail
ActivityOutdoor recreation
Related activitiesHiking, camping, thru-hiking, ultralight backpacking

Backpacking is the outdoor recreation of carrying gear on one's back while hiking for more than one day. It is often an extended journey and commonly includes camping overnight in the wilderness. Backpacking combines hiking and camping: everything needed — food, water treatment, shelter, and clothing — is carried in a backpack, allowing travellers to reach remote areas inaccessible to vehicles.

Equipment

Essential equipment includes a backpack with a hip belt, a tent or other shelter, a sleeping bag and pad, a portable stove and cookware, water filtration or purification, food (often freeze-dried or dehydrated), layered clothing, rain gear, navigation tools, and a first-aid kit. The traditional checklist known as the "Ten Essentials" covers navigation, illumination, sun protection, first aid, repair tools, fire, emergency shelter, extra food, extra water, and extra clothing. Ultralight backpackers seek to reduce base weight — all gear excluding consumables — to under 4.5 kg (10 lb) using lightweight materials and multi-use items.

Long-distance trails

Backpacking is central to long-distance trails such as the Appalachian Trail, Pacific Crest Trail, and Continental Divide Trail in the United States. Hikers who walk an entire long trail in one season are called thru-hikers; resupply is arranged through mail drops or trail towns. In Europe, hut-to-hut routes in the Alps and long-distance paths such as the Camino de Santiago offer alternatives to tent camping.

Environmental ethics

The Leave No Trace principles guide backpackers to minimize impact: plan ahead, travel and camp on durable surfaces, dispose of waste properly, leave natural objects, minimize campfire impacts, respect wildlife, and be considerate of other visitors. Many wilderness areas require permits and bear-resistant food storage.

History

Recreational backpacking grew in the late 19th and early 20th centuries alongside conservation movements and organizations such as the Sierra Club. Modern equipment — aluminium pack frames, nylon tents, and freeze-dried food — developed after the Second World War, and authors such as Colin Fletcher, whose The Complete Walker appeared in 1968, popularized the activity.
