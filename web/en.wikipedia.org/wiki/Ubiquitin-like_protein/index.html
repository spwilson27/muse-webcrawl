Ubiquitin-like protein

Ubiquitin-like proteins (UBLs or UBLP) are a family of small proteins involved in post-translational modification of other protein targets, similar to ubiquitin itself. These proteins share amino acid sequence similarity with ubiquitin and adopt the same characteristic β-grasp fold, typically ending in a C-terminal glycine motif that can be conjugated to lysine residues on substrate proteins.

Conjugation machinery

Like ubiquitin, most UBLs are attached to substrates by a three-enzyme cascade: an activating enzyme (E1), a conjugating enzyme (E2) and a ligase (E3), and can be removed by specific proteases (deubiquitinase-like enzymes). Each UBL system has its own dedicated E1/E2/E3 enzymes and proteases, though some components are shared (for example, the ATG8 and ATG12 systems in autophagy).

Major members

  • SUMO (small ubiquitin-like modifier) — regulates nuclear transport, transcription, stress responses and genome stability; unlike ubiquitin it usually does not target proteins for degradation.
  • NEDD8 — modifies cullin scaffolds of cullin-RING ubiquitin ligases, activating them.
  • ATG8 and ATG12 — essential for autophagosome formation in autophagy.
  • ISG15 — interferon-induced modifier active in antiviral defense.
  • UFM1, URM1, FAT10 and others — roles in ER homeostasis, tRNA modification and immune function.

Evolution

UBL systems are ancient: prokaryotic sulfur-transfer proteins such as ThiS and MoaD share the β-grasp fold and adenylation chemistry, suggesting that ubiquitin-like conjugation evolved from metabolic pathways. UBL domains are also found inserted within larger proteins, where they act as protein-interaction modules.
