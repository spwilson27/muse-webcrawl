Star polygon

In geometry, a star polygon is a non-convex polygon that resembles a star. Regular star polygons are denoted by the Schläfli symbol {p/q}, where p is the number of vertices and q is the step: the figure is drawn by joining every q-th vertex of a regular p-gon until returning to the start.

Regular star polygons

The best-known example is the pentagram, {5/2}, formed by extending the diagonals of a regular pentagon; it has been a religious and magical symbol since antiquity and appears on the flags of Morocco and Ethiopia. Other examples include the heptagrams {7/2} and {7/3}, the octagram {8/3}, and the nonagram {9/4}. When p and q share a common factor, the figure decomposes into a compound of simpler polygons — for example, the hexagram {6/2} is a compound of two triangles rather than a single continuous path. Johannes Kepler studied star polygons and polyhedra in his Harmonices Mundi (1619), and Louis Poinsot later gave the theory a rigorous form.

Star figures and compounds

Related constructions include star figures built from several polygons (such as the Star of David, two overlapping triangles) and stellated polyhedra in three dimensions, where faces are extended until they meet to form star-like solids. Star polygons also occur in art, architecture, heraldry, and national symbols worldwide.

Mathematical properties

A regular star polygon {p/q} exists as a single closed path whenever p and q are coprime. The density of the figure — the number of times its interior winds around the center — equals q. The sum of the point angles of the pentagram is 180°, illustrating general formulas relating vertex angles to p and q.
