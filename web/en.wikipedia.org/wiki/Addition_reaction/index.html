Addition reaction

An addition reaction, in organic chemistry, is a chemical reaction in which two or more molecules combine to form a larger one (the adduct). Addition reactions are typically the reverse of elimination reactions. They are characteristic of unsaturated compounds — alkenes, alkynes, carbonyl compounds, and imines — in which a multiple bond is partially or fully broken as new bonds form at the same atoms.

Types

Addition reactions are classified by the nature of the attacking species. In electrophilic addition, an electrophile adds to a pi bond, as in the addition of hydrogen halides and halogens to alkenes; the regiochemistry with unsymmetrical reagents often follows Markovnikov's rule. In nucleophilic addition, a nucleophile attacks an electrophilic carbon, classically the carbonyl carbon of aldehydes and ketones, as in Grignard additions and cyanohydrin formation. Free-radical addition proceeds by radical chain mechanisms, such as the peroxide-initiated anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr to alkenes. Cycloadditions such as the Diels–Alder reaction combine addition with ring formation.

Hydrogenation and hydration

Among the most industrially important additions are catalytic hydrogenation, in which hydrogen adds across double bonds over metal catalysts (used in the hardening of vegetable oils and throughout petrochemical processing), and acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes to alcohols, a route to industrial ethanol. Hydrohalogenation, halogenation, and hydroboration–oxidation complete the standard repertoire taught in introductory organic chemistry.

Addition polymerisation

In polymer chemistry, chain-growth polymerisation is a series of addition reactions in which unsaturated monomers add to a growing active centre without the loss of any small molecule, producing polymers such as polyethylene and polystyrene.
