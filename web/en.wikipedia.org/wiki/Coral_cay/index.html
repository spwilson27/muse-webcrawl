Coral cay

A coral cay is a small, low island on the surface of a coral reef that is built up from deposits of coral sand, rubble and shingle produced by the breakdown of reef organisms and swept together by waves and currents. Coral cays usually rise only a few metres above sea level and may support vegetation once soil and a freshwater lens develop. The word "cay" (also "key", as in the Florida Keys) comes from the Spanish cayo.

Formation

Cays form where wave refraction and converging currents cause sediment to accumulate on a reef flat, often on the leeward side of the reef. Storms can rapidly build, move or destroy cays by depositing coral rubble ridges. Over time, guano from nesting seabirds and accumulating plant litter create soil, allowing grasses, shrubs and eventually trees such as coconut palms and Pisonia to colonise.

Distribution and significance

Coral cays are common on Australia's Great Barrier Reef (e.g. Heron Island, Green Island), in the Caribbean, and across the Pacific atolls. They provide important nesting habitat for seabirds and turtles but are highly vulnerable to sea-level rise and changes in storm patterns, being among the landforms most dynamic on geological timescales — many are only a few thousand years old.
