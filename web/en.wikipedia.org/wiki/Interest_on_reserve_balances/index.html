Interest on reserve balances

Interest on reserve balances (IORB) is interest paid by a central bank on the reserves that commercial banks hold in accounts at the central bank. In the United States, the Federal Reserve began paying interest on reserves in October 2008, under authority granted by the Financial Services Regulatory Relief Act of 2006 and accelerated by the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act of 2008. The rate, administered by the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), is one of the Fed's principal tools for implementing monetary policy in an "ample reserves" framework.

Function

Paying interest on reserves puts a floor under short-term market interest rates: banks will not lend reserves overnight at rates materially below what the central bank pays risk-free, so the effective federal funds rate trades near (usually slightly below) the IORB rate. Before 2008 reserves paid no interest, and the Fed steered the funds rate by making small open-market adjustments to the scarce supply of reserves. After large-scale asset purchases (quantitative easing) flooded the banking system with reserves, controlling rates instead required administered rates — IORB, together with the overnight reverse repurchase agreement (ON RRP) rate, which serves institutions without reserve accounts. In March 2020 the Fed reduced reserve requirement ratios to zero, and in July 2021 it merged the former separate rates on required and excess reserves into a single IORB rate.

Scale and debate

Because reserve balances swelled to several trillion dollars after 2008 and again during the COVID-19 pandemic, interest payments to banks became large — exceeding $100 billion per year when policy rates peaked in 2023 — and combined with losses on the Fed's securities portfolio to suspend its remittances to the US Treasury. Critics argue the payments are a subsidy to banks or distort lending incentives; the Fed responds that IORB is essential for controlling inflation-fighting policy rates and that banks' earnings must be weighed against the interest the Fed earns on its assets.

Elsewhere

Other central banks similarly remunerate reserves or operate deposit facilities: the European Central Bank's deposit facility rate long served as the floor of its interest-rate corridor, and the Bank of England pays Bank Rate on reserves under its sterling monetary framework.
