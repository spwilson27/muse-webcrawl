The Torres Strait Islander languages are the languages spoken by the Torres Strait Islanders, the Indigenous peoples of the islands of the Torres Strait between Cape York Peninsula in Queensland, Australia, and the southern coast of Papua New Guinea. Two unrelated traditional languages are spoken, together with an English-based creole that now serves as a lingua franca across the strait.

The traditional languages

Kalaw Lagaw Ya

Kalaw Lagaw Ya (also rendered Kala Lagaw Ya) is the language of the western and central islands of the Torres Strait. It is an Australian Aboriginal language, most closely related to the Paman languages of Cape York, and it has several dialects — including Kalaw Kawaw Ya (northern), Mabuiag (central/western), and Kalau Lagau Ya (southern/western) varieties historically recognised by speakers. It had a few thousand speakers at the turn of the twenty-first century, though intergenerational transmission has weakened in many communities.

Meriam Mir

Meriam Mir (Meriam Mìr) is the language of the eastern islands, including Mer (Murray Island), Dauar, and Waier. Unlike Kalaw Lagaw Ya, it is a Papuan language, belonging to the Eastern Trans-Fly family of southern New Guinea. Meriam Mir is notable as the language of the plaintiffs in the landmark Mabo v Queensland native title case, which drew attention to Meriam culture and law.

Torres Strait Creole

Torres Strait Creole (also called Torres Strait Pidgin, Cape York Creole, or by names such as Yumplatok) developed during the nineteenth century through contact between Islanders, Europeans, and Pacific Islander labourers in the maritime industries. It is now the most widely spoken language of the strait, used as a first language by many younger Islanders and as a common medium between speakers of the two traditional languages and English. Its vocabulary is largely English-derived, with influences from Kalaw Lagaw Ya, Meriam Mir, and Pacific pidgins.

Language endangerment and revival

Both traditional languages are considered endangered, with English and Torres Strait Creole dominant in education, media, and public life. Community-led efforts — including language teaching in schools, dictionaries and grammars, radio broadcasting, and cultural festivals — aim to maintain and revitalise Kalaw Lagaw Ya and Meriam Mir. A standard orthography and literacy materials have been developed for both languages, and they hold a central place in Torres Strait Islander cultural identity.
