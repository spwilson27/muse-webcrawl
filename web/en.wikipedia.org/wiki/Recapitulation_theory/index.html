Recapitulation theory

The theory of recapitulation, also called the biogenetic law or embryological parallelism, is a discredited biological hypothesis that the development of an individual organism (its ontogeny) follows the same phases as the evolution of its species (its phylogeny). It is summarized by Ernst Haeckel's famous phrase: "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny."

History

Ideas resembling recapitulation appear in the work of Johann Friedrich Meckel and Étienne Serres in the early 19th century (the Meckel–Serres law). Haeckel formulated his biogenetic law in 1866, proposing that embryos pass through stages resembling the adult forms of their evolutionary ancestors, and illustrated it with his widely reproduced — and criticized — embryo drawings. Karl Ernst von Baer's earlier laws of development, which emphasized that embryos of different species diverge from shared early forms rather than passing through adult stages of other species, already contradicted strict recapitulation.

Rejection and legacy

By the early 20th century, experimental embryology and genetics had undermined the theory: development is influenced by heterochrony (shifts in developmental timing) and other processes that do not replay ancestral adult stages. Although rejected as a literal law, recapitulation influenced fields from evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo) to psychology and education theory, where analogous ideas about child development appeared in the work of G. Stanley Hall and others.
