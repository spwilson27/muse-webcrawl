Council of Jerusalem

[Image: Council of Jerusalem]

The Council of Jerusalem or Apostolic Council is a council described in chapter 15 of the Acts of the Apostles, held in Jerusalem around AD 48–50. It decided a pivotal question in early Christianity: whether Gentile converts were required to be circumcised and to observe the Mosaic Law.

Background

The question arose in Antioch, where Paul and Barnabas preached to Gentiles, and some Jewish Christians taught that circumcision was necessary for salvation. Paul and Barnabas were sent to Jerusalem to consult the apostles and elders, including James the Just and Peter.

The decree

After debate, James proposed the Apostolic Decree: Gentile believers should abstain from food sacrificed to idols, from blood, from the meat of strangled animals, and from sexual immorality, but would not be required to undergo circumcision or keep the whole Law of Moses. The decision was conveyed by letter to the churches by Paul, Barnabas, Judas Barsabbas and Silas.

Significance

The council is regarded as a defining moment in the separation of Christianity from Judaism and in the opening of the early church to the Gentile world. Its interpretation remains important in debates about the relationship between Christianity and Jewish law.
