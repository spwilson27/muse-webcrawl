The Mythical Man-Month

AuthorFrederick P. Brooks Jr.
SubjectSoftware project management
PublisherAddison-Wesley
Published1975; 20th anniversary edition 1995

The Mythical Man-Month: Essays on Software Engineering is a book on software engineering and project management by Fred Brooks first published in 1975, with a 20th anniversary edition in 1995 including four additional chapters. Its central theme, expressed in Brooks's law, is that "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later," because of the time new staff need to become productive and the combinatorial growth of communication paths among team members.

Background

Brooks drew on his experience managing the development of IBM's OS/360 operating system in the 1960s, one of the largest and most troubled software projects of its era. The book's blend of case-study anecdotes and clear principles — illustrated memorably by animals trapped in the La Brea Tar Pits on its cover — made it one of the most influential books in the field, often described as "the Bible" of software engineering because, Brooks quipped, "everybody quotes it, some people read it, and a few people go by it."

Key ideas

Besides Brooks's law, the book argues that the "man-month" is a mythical unit of effort because progress and personnel are not interchangeable for tasks requiring sequential work and communication; that large systems need "conceptual integrity" enforced by a small chief-architect team; that the second version of a successful system is the most dangerous ("second-system effect"); that "plan to throw one away" because the first build is a learning prototype; and, in the 1986 paper added to later editions, that there is "no silver bullet" that will by itself deliver an order-of-magnitude improvement in software productivity.

Influence

The anniversary edition (1995) added the essays "No Silver Bullet," a defense of it, "The Mythical Man-Month After 20 Years," in which Brooks revisited his propositions, and a chapter of categorical propositions distilled from the book. It remains a standard text on software project management.
