Poison oak

Poison oak is a common name applied to several species of the genus Toxicodendron in the sumac family (Anacardiaceae), none of which are true oaks. The two species most often meant are Pacific poison oak (Toxicodendron diversilobum), abundant along the Pacific coast of North America from British Columbia to Baja California, and Atlantic poison oak (Toxicodendron pubescens), native to the southeastern United States. Their leaves are typically divided into three leaflets, resembling those of an oak, following the warning "leaves of three, let it be".

Description

Pacific poison oak grows as a dense shrub in sunny sites or as a climbing vine in shaded woodland, with leaves that turn brilliant red or pink in autumn. Atlantic poison oak is a low, upright shrub of sandy soils. Both produce small whitish berries and are important wildlife plants despite their reputation: birds eat the berries, and the foliage shelters small animals.

Toxicity

All parts of the plants contain urushiol, an oily mixture of catechols that causes allergic contact dermatitis in most people. Contact — including indirect contact with contaminated clothing, tools, or pet fur, and even smoke from burning plants — can produce an itchy, blistering rash appearing one to several days later. The reaction is a type IV hypersensitivity; sensitivity varies between individuals and can change over a lifetime. Washing skin promptly with soap and water, and applying calamine or corticosteroid creams, are standard treatments; severe cases may require oral steroids.
