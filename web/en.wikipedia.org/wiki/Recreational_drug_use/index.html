Recreational drug use

Recreational drug use
DefinitionUse of psychoactive substances for pleasure rather than medical purposes
Common substancesCaffeine, alcohol, nicotine, cannabis, MDMA, cocaine, psychedelics, opioids
Legal frameworksProhibition, decriminalisation, legalisation and regulation
Related approachesHarm reduction

Recreational drug use is the use of one or more psychoactive drugs to induce an altered state of consciousness, for pleasure or leisure, rather than for medical or spiritual purposes (though the boundaries overlap). It is an ancient and near-universal human practice: the most widely used recreational substances worldwide are caffeine, alcohol, nicotine and cannabis, alongside stimulants such as cocaine and MDMA, depressants, opioids and psychedelics.

Prevalence

The United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime estimates that around 5–6 percent of the world's population aged 15–64 used an illicit drug in the past year (roughly 275–296 million people in recent World Drug Reports), with cannabis by far the most common. Alcohol and tobacco, legal in most countries, are used by far larger numbers and account for a major share of the global burden of disease attributable to substance use.

Motives and patterns

People report using drugs for euphoria, relaxation, social bonding, enhanced perception or performance, curiosity and escape. Patterns range from one-off experimentation to regular use and dependence; only a minority of users develop substance use disorders, with risk influenced by the drug, dose, genetics, mental health and social environment.

Law and policy

Most recreational drugs other than alcohol, tobacco and caffeine are controlled under the international drug conventions (1961, 1971, 1988), implemented through national prohibition regimes, though policies are diversifying: cannabis has been legalised for recreational use in countries including Canada and Uruguay and in many US states; Portugal decriminalised possession of small amounts of all drugs in 2001; and several jurisdictions have adopted regulated or harm-reduction models — needle exchanges, drug checking and supervised consumption sites — aimed at reducing health harms without endorsing use.

Health effects

Risks include acute toxicity and overdose, accidents while intoxicated, cardiovascular and liver damage, psychosis in susceptible individuals, and dependence. Comparative harm assessments, such as the 2010 Lancet study led by David Nutt, ranked alcohol as the most harmful drug overall when harms to users and to others were combined.
