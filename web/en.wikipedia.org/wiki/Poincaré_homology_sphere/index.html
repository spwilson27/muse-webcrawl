Poincaré homology sphere

The Poincaré homology sphere (also called the Poincaré dodecahedral space) is a particular example of a homology sphere: a closed 3-manifold whose homology groups are the same as those of the 3-sphere, but which is not itself homeomorphic to the sphere. Henri Poincaré constructed it in 1904, in the fifth and final supplement to his Analysis Situs, and its discovery led him to formulate the famous Poincaré conjecture.

Construction

One construction begins with a solid dodecahedron. Opposite faces of the dodecahedron are glued together after a twist by one tenth of a full turn (36°), producing a closed 3-manifold. Equivalently, the space is the quotient of the 3-sphere by the binary icosahedral group, a perfect group of order 120 acting freely on the sphere. It can also be obtained by Dehn surgery on the trefoil knot, or as a Seifert fibre space over the sphere with three exceptional fibres.

Properties

Although the manifold has the same homology as S³, its fundamental group is the binary icosahedral group, of order 120 — nontrivial, and in fact perfect, equal to its own commutator subgroup. Poincaré originally asked whether a closed 3-manifold with the homology of the sphere must be the sphere; this example answered that question in the negative, and he refined his question to ask whether trivial fundamental group suffices — the statement that became the Poincaré conjecture, proved by Grigori Perelman in 2002–2003.

Cosmology

In 2003, analysis of the Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe data led Jean-Pierre Luminet and collaborators to propose that the universe might have the topology of the Poincaré dodecahedral space, which would explain the apparent suppression of large-angle fluctuations in the cosmic microwave background. Subsequent searches for the predicted pattern of matched circles in the sky have not confirmed the hypothesis.
