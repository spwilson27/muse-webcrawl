An independent record label (or indie label) is a record label that operates without the funding or distribution of the major multinational record companies. Independent labels range from hobbyist bedroom operations to substantial companies with international rosters, and they have historically been the incubators of new genres from rock and roll to punk, hip hop, and electronic music.

History

Small labels flourished in the post-war United States, where regional companies such as Sun Records in Memphis and Chess Records in Chicago recorded rhythm and blues and early rock and roll artists ignored by the majors. In the United Kingdom, labels including Island and Virgin began as independents in the 1960s–70s. The punk explosion of the late 1970s normalised self-releasing and small-label production, giving rise to the "indie" ethos and influential labels such as Rough Trade, Factory, and later Creation.

Business model

Independents typically offer smaller advances but greater artistic freedom, and often split net profits more evenly with artists than major-label royalty deals. Many sign distribution agreements with major-owned distributors while retaining ownership of their masters. The digital era lowered recording and distribution costs, allowing independents to retain a substantial share — roughly a third or more — of the global recorded-music market.

Indie as a genre label

Because so many guitar bands released music through independents in the 1980s, "indie" also became a genre descriptor (indie rock, indie pop), though many artists so described are signed to majors. Trade bodies such as AIM (UK) and A2IM (US) represent the sector.
