Dactylic hexameter (also known as heroic hexameter and the metre of epic) is a form of metre or rhythmic scheme used in classical Greek and Latin literature. It consists of lines made from six feet, where each of the first four feet may be either a dactyl (— ◡ ◡) or a spondee (— —), the fifth foot is normally a dactyl, and the sixth foot is a spondee or trochee (— — or — ◡). It was the standard metre of epic poetry in both languages — Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, Hesiod's works, Virgil's Aeneid, Ovid's Metamorphoses — and was also used for didactic, pastoral and satirical verse.

Structure

The scheme can be written:

— ⏑⏑ | — ⏑⏑ | — ⏑⏑ | — ⏑⏑ | — ⏑⏑ | — ×

with spondees able to replace dactyls in the first four feet. Because Greek and Latin metre is quantitative — based on syllable length rather than stress — a line may contain between twelve and seventeen syllables. The opening of the Iliad, mēnin aeide thea ("Sing, goddess, the anger…"), begins with a dactyl followed by spondees, giving a weighty start.

Caesura and rhythm

Lines are articulated by a caesura, a word-break within a foot, most often in the third foot (the "masculine" or penthemimeral caesura) or after the first short syllable of the third foot (the "feminine" or trochaic caesura). Virgil manipulates spondaic heaviness and the conflict between word accent and metrical ictus for expressive effect; Homer's verse, shaped by oral composition, is more uniformly dactylic and rapid. A pause at the end of the fourth foot, the bucolic diaeresis, is characteristic of pastoral poetry.

History

The hexameter's origins are debated; it may descend from an Indo-European or Aeolic lyric prototype expanded by doubling, and is already fully developed in the earliest surviving Greek poetry (8th century BC). Ennius introduced it into Latin in the 2nd century BC, displacing the native Saturnian metre, and Virgil perfected its Latin form. Apollonius of Rhodes used it for the Hellenistic epic Argonautica, and it remained the prestige metre of epic through late antiquity, including Christian epics.

In later literatures

Attempts to naturalise the quantitative hexameter in accentual languages have had mixed success. In German, Klopstock's Der Messias and Goethe's Hermann und Dorothea made it a serious form; in English, Longfellow's Evangeline (1847) is the best-known example, though critics often find English hexameters lumbering. Hexameter also entered Finnish and Hungarian literary practice, and survives in the stylised verse of classical pastiches and translations of Homer.
