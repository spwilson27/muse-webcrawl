YUV is a color model typically used as part of a color image pipeline. It encodes a color image or video taking human perception into account, allowing reduced bandwidth for chrominance components. The Y component represents brightness (luma), while the U and V components represent color (chrominance), specifically the blue-difference and red-difference signals. YUV was developed for the PAL analog television system and closely parallels NTSC's YIQ.

Rationale

Human vision has much finer spatial acuity for brightness than for color. YUV exploits this by devoting most of the signal to the Y channel and subsampling or compressing the chroma channels, as in chroma subsampling schemes such as 4:2:2 and 4:2:0. When color television was introduced, transmitting color as luma + chroma also kept the signal backward-compatible with black-and-white sets, which simply displayed Y.

Conversion from RGB

For standard-definition video (BT.601) the luma is a weighted sum of gamma-corrected RGB:

Y = 0.299R + 0.587G + 0.114B
U = 0.492(B − Y)
V = 0.877(R − Y)

The precise coefficients differ between standards; high-definition video (BT.709) uses weights favouring green somewhat differently.

YUV and YCbCr

YCbCr is the digital counterpart of YUV, with scaled and offset components suitable for digital representation (JPEG, MPEG, H.264). In informal video-engineering usage the two terms are often used interchangeably, though strictly YUV refers to the analog formulation.

Pixel formats

Common YUV pixel formats in software include YUY2 (packed 4:2:2), I420/YV12 (planar 4:2:0), and NV12 (semi-planar 4:2:0), widely used as the internal format of hardware video decoders and most digital video codecs.
