British cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A full English breakfast with eggs, bacon, beans and toast]

British cuisine is the set of cooking traditions associated with Britain and its constituent countries — England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland — and with the wider British diaspora. Built on quality meat, dairy, root vegetables, oats and seafood, it was long mocked abroad, but its staples — the full breakfast, fish and chips, the Sunday roast with Yorkshire pudding, pies, puddings such as sticky toffee and trifle, and afternoon tea — are enduring comforts.

Britain's imperial history transformed its table: tea from China and India, sugar from Caribbean plantations, and a national love of Curry that produced chicken tikka masala, while post-war immigration made the curry house and later every world cuisine part of British life.

Meals

The full English breakfast (eggs, bacon, sausage, beans, tomato, mushrooms, black pudding, toast) anchors the day; lunch is light; the evening meal centres on meat and two vegetables, a roast on Sunday. Afternoon tea — sandwiches, scones with clotted cream and jam, cakes — survives as a ritual treat.

Revival

Since the 1990s, chefs including Fergus Henderson (nose-to-tail eating), Heston Blumenthal and a generation of gastropub cooks have revived British ingredients and dishes, and farmers' markets and protected products (Melton Mowbray pork pies, Stilton cheese) have flourished.
