New Queer Cinema

New Queer Cinema is a term coined by film critic B. Ruby Rich in Sight & Sound magazine in 1992 to describe a movement in queer-themed independent filmmaking of the early 1990s. The films were typically directed by gay and lesbian filmmakers, were made outside the studio system, and presented homosexuality in defiant, formally innovative ways rather than seeking mainstream respectability.

Key works

Landmark New Queer Cinema films include Gregg Araki's The Living End (1992), Todd Haynes's Poison (1991), Gus Van Sant's My Own Private Idaho (1991), Jennie Livingston's documentary Paris Is Burning (1990), Derek Jarman's Edward II (1991), and Cheryl Dunye's The Watermelon Woman (1996). Many premiered at festivals such as Sundance and Toronto and benefited from new funding channels for independent film.

Context and legacy

The movement emerged in the shadow of the AIDS crisis and in reaction against both Hollywood stereotypes and the "positive images" approach of earlier gay cinema. Its irreverence, genre-mixing, and challenges to identity politics influenced subsequent queer media, even as some critics argued that its commercial successes were absorbed into the mainstream it had opposed.
