Guanche is an extinct language, or group of languages, formerly spoken by the Guanche people, the indigenous inhabitants of the Canary Islands. It died out by roughly the 18th century, displaced by Spanish after the Castilian conquest of the islands in the 15th century, though some words survive in the local Spanish dialect and in place names.

Attestation

Knowledge of Guanche comes from word lists collected by early European visitors and chroniclers, from the accounts of the conquest, from rock inscriptions in a Libyco-Berber-like script found across the archipelago, and from several hundred surviving toponyms. Each island appears to have had its own variety; "Guanche" strictly refers to Tenerife, but the term is commonly extended to the languages of all the islands. Recorded vocabulary includes numerals, terms for livestock and landscape features, and names of people and places, such as Tenerife, Tegueste, and Tacoronte.

Classification

The language is generally thought to be related to the Berber (Amazigh) languages of North Africa, consistent with genetic and archaeological evidence that the Guanches originated there. Comparisons of the numeral system and vocabulary support a Berber affiliation, but the surviving material is too fragmentary for a definitive classification, and Guanche is often treated as unclassified within or near Afro-Asiatic.

Legacy

Guanche words in Canarian Spanish include gofio (toasted grain flour), baifo (kid goat), and tenique (stone). Personal names of Guanche origin, such as Yeray, Idaira, and Ayoze, have become popular in the Canary Islands as a symbol of indigenous heritage.
