William Lyon Mackenzie King (December 17, 1874 – July 22, 1950) was a Canadian statesman and politician who served as the tenth prime minister of Canada for three non-consecutive terms from 1921 to 1926, 1926 to 1930, and 1935 to 1948. A Liberal, he was the dominant political figure in Canada from the 1920s through the 1940s, and Canada's longest-serving prime minister, with nearly 22 years in office.

King led Canada through the Great Depression and the Second World War, overseeing industrial expansion, the introduction of unemployment insurance, and the beginnings of the Canadian welfare state. He is often ranked among the greatest Canadian prime ministers in scholarly surveys.

Early life

King was born in Berlin, Ontario (now Kitchener), the grandson of William Lyon Mackenzie, leader of the Upper Canada Rebellion of 1837. He studied at the University of Toronto and Harvard University, earning a PhD in political economy. He entered public service as Canada's first deputy minister of labour and was elected to Parliament in 1908, serving as minister of labour under Wilfrid Laurier.

Prime Minister

King became leader of the Liberal Party in 1919 and prime minister in 1921. His cautious, consensus-building style — summed up in his famous formula "not necessarily conscription, but conscription if necessary" during the 1944 Conscription Crisis — kept his party united and Canada intact through the war. He built a close working relationship with U.S. President Franklin D. Roosevelt and British Prime Minister Winston Churchill.

Retirement and legacy

King retired in 1948 and was succeeded by Louis St. Laurent. His extensive personal diaries, published after his death, revealed a private interest in spiritualism that contrasted with his pragmatic public image. His estate at Kingsmere in Quebec's Gatineau Park is now a historic site, and his portrait appears on the Canadian fifty-dollar bill.
