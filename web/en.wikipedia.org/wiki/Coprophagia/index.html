Coprophagia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coprophagia (from Greek κόπρος kopros, "faeces", and φαγεῖν phagein, "to eat") is the consumption of faeces. The behaviour is normal and often essential in many animal species, but is uncommon and usually pathological in humans.

In animals

Many insects feed on dung: flies breed in it and dung beetles specialise in consuming and burying it, providing a major ecosystem service in nutrient recycling. Among mammals, coprophagy is widespread. Rabbits and many rodents practise cecotrophy, reingesting soft, nutrient-rich caecal pellets so that food passes through the gut twice, allowing absorption of microbially produced protein and B vitamins. Young elephants and hippos may eat the dung of adults to obtain gut bacteria needed for digestion, and dogs commonly eat faeces — their own or other animals' — which, while usually harmless to the dog, is a common owner complaint. Coprophagy in dogs can occasionally signal pancreatic insufficiency or other disease.

In humans

Human coprophagia is rare and is most often reported in association with severe psychiatric or neurocognitive conditions, including schizophrenia, dementia and developmental disorders, as well as in some cases of pica. It carries risks of infection with parasites and enteric pathogens such as hepatitis viruses, Clostridioides difficile and intestinal worms. Treatment addresses the underlying condition and may involve behavioural therapy and medication.

In culture

The practice has appeared as a transgressive motif in literature and film, and, treated comedically, in works such as the film Pink Flamingos. Historically, dung had medicinal uses in some folk traditions, a practice distinct from deliberate ingestion as a symptom.
