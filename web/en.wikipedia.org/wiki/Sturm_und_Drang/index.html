Sturm und Drang

Götz von Berlichingen (1773), Goethe's breakthrough Sturm und Drang play

Sturm und Drang (German for "storm and stress" or "storm and urge") was a proto-Romantic movement in German literature and music that flourished from the late 1760s to the early 1780s. Reacting against the rationalism of the Enlightenment and the rigid rules of French-inspired neoclassicism, its young writers exalted emotion, nature, individual genius and Shakespearean freedom of form.

Characteristics and works

The movement took its name from Friedrich Maximilian Klinger's play Sturm und Drang (1776). Its manifesto-like texts include Johann Gottfried Herder's essays on Shakespeare and folk poetry, which argued that literature should express the spirit of a people. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's play Götz von Berlichingen (1773) and his epistolary novel The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774) became the movement's defining successes, while Friedrich Schiller's first play, The Robbers (1781), carried its rebellious energy to a climax. Other figures included Jakob Michael Reinhold Lenz and Heinrich Leopold Wagner.

Legacy

In music, the term is applied to the turbulent minor-key works of Haydn around 1768–1772 and to dramatic passages in Mozart. Sturm und Drang quickly matured into Weimar Classicism, as Goethe and Schiller moderated its excesses, and it stands as the chief precursor of German Romanticism.
