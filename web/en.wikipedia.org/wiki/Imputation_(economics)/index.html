Imputation (economics)

The theory of imputation (German: Zurechnung) is a theory in economics developed by Carl Menger, founder of the Austrian School, and elaborated by his student Friedrich von Wieser, who coined the term. The theory addresses the question of how the value of factors of production — land, labour and capital goods — is determined.

The problem

Classical economists had tended to explain the value of consumer goods by the value of the inputs used to produce them, which created a problem of circularity and failed to explain cases such as a vintage wine whose value vastly exceeds the cost of its inputs. Menger inverted the reasoning: goods of the "first order" (consumer goods) have value because they satisfy human wants; goods of "higher order" (producer goods) derive — have imputed to them — their value from the expected value of the lower-order goods they help to produce. Value thus flows backwards from consumption to production, not forwards from costs to prices.

Marginal contribution

Wieser proposed that the value imputed to a factor is measured by its marginal contribution: the loss in the value of output that would result if one unit of the factor were withdrawn, the remaining factors being adjusted optimally. The distribution of the total product value among cooperating factors, so that the sum of the imputed values equals the value of the product, became known as the distribution problem; related solutions were developed in the marginal productivity theory of distribution associated with John Bates Clark and in the work of Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk and Léon Walras.

Significance

The imputation theory was a key component of the marginal revolution: it grounded the prices of inputs in consumer demand and subjective value rather than in embodied labour, undermining the classical labour theory of value. It remains the conceptual foundation for the modern treatment of factor pricing and the theory of distribution.
