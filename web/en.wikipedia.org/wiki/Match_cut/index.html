Match cut

Match cut
FieldFilm editing
Related techniquesMatch on action; graphic match; smash cut; jump cut
Famous examplesLawrence of Arabia (1962); 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968)

A match cut is a cut in film editing between two shots that are matched by the action or by compositional similarity — a graphic or spatial correspondence that creates a visual link across the edit, often to bridge a change in time, place, or subject. Where the matched element is the movement begun in one shot and completed in the next, the technique is called a match on action.

Famous examples

One of the most celebrated match cuts is in David Lean's Lawrence of Arabia (1962), where Lawrence blows out a match in close-up and the film cuts to the sun rising over the desert. In Stanley Kubrick's 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968), a bone thrown into the air by a prehistoric ape cuts to an orbiting spacecraft millions of years later, compressing the whole history of technology into a single edit. Alfred Hitchcock used a match between a scream and a train whistle in The 39 Steps (1935), an early example of a sound match.

Function

Match cuts can convey metaphor and thematic association, achieve seamless continuity, or deliberately create ironic contrast between the matched elements. The device is related to the graphic match of Soviet montage theory and is distinguished from the jump cut, which disrupts rather than smooths spatial and temporal continuity.
