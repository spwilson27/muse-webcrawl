Gauss's law

In physics and electromagnetism, Gauss's law, also known as Gauss's flux theorem, is one of Maxwell's equations. It states that the electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the total electric charge enclosed by that surface. The law was first formulated by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1773, followed by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1835, but was not published until 1867. It is equivalent to Coulomb's law for static charges and provides a powerful method for calculating electric fields in situations with symmetry.

Electric flux through a closed surface depends only on the enclosed charge.

Statement

In integral form, Gauss's law states:

∮ E · dA = Qenc / ε0

where E is the electric field, dA is a vector representing an infinitesimal area of the closed surface, Qenc is the total charge enclosed, and ε0 is the permittivity of free space. In differential form, the law becomes:

∇ · E = ρ / ε0

where ρ is the electric charge density. The two forms are equivalent by the divergence theorem.

Applications

When a charge distribution has spherical, cylindrical or planar symmetry, a suitably chosen "Gaussian surface" makes the flux integral trivial, yielding the electric field directly. Standard results include the field of a point charge (recovering Coulomb's law), the constant field inside a uniformly charged spherical shell being zero, the field of an infinite line of charge, and the field of an infinite charged plane, which is independent of distance from the plane.

Related laws

Analogous flux laws exist for magnetism (Gauss's law for magnetism, ∇ · B = 0, expressing the absence of magnetic monopoles) and for gravitation, where the flux of the gravitational field through a closed surface is proportional to the enclosed mass.
