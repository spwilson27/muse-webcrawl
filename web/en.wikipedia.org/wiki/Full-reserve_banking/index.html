Full-reserve banking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Full-reserve banking (also known as 100% reserve banking or narrow banking) is a proposed system of banking in which banks are required to keep the full amount of each depositor's funds in cash and cash equivalents ready for immediate withdrawal on demand, as reserves. Bank lending would then be funded not by demand deposits but by time deposits, investment funds or equity, so that the creation of money by private banks — a feature of fractional-reserve banking, the system used worldwide today — would end.

Under full-reserve banking, demand deposits would be backed one-for-one by safe reserves.

History of the idea

Proposals of this kind go back centuries, and were given their best-known form in the 1930s Chicago Plan, drawn up by University of Chicago economists including Henry Simons and promoted by Irving Fisher after the bank failures of the Great Depression. Variants were endorsed at times by Milton Friedman and James Tobin, and interest revived after the 2008 financial crisis: Iceland commissioned a report on monetary reform in 2015, the campaign group Positive Money advocated "sovereign money" in the United Kingdom, and Switzerland held a referendum on the Vollgeld initiative in 2018, which voters rejected.

Arguments

Supporters argue that separating the payment system from lending would make bank deposits completely safe, eliminate bank runs on transaction accounts, end the boom-and-bust credit cycles they blame on bank money creation, and return seigniorage on new money to the state. Critics respond that credit would become more expensive or migrate to unregulated "shadow banks", that the transition would be disruptive, and that maturity transformation by banks serves a useful economic function. Economists also debate whether full reserves would in practice end private money creation, since near-money substitutes tend to emerge.

Relation to other systems

Full-reserve banking differs from narrow banking proposals that would back deposits with short-term government securities, and from central bank digital currency proposals that would let the public hold central-bank money directly — though the ideas are often discussed together. No modern country operates a full-reserve system.
