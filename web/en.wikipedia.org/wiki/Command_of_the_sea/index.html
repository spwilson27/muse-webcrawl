Command of the sea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Command of the sea (also called sea control) is a naval concept meaning that a power's navy is so strong that its rivals cannot attack it directly, allowing that power to use the sea for its own military and commercial purposes while denying that use to an enemy. It is closely related to, but broader than, the modern doctrine of sea power articulated by Alfred Thayer Mahan.

Command of the sea underwrote Britain's maritime trade and empire in the 18th and 19th centuries.

Theory

Mahan argued in The Influence of Sea Power upon History (1890) that command of the sea, won by defeating the enemy's battle fleet, was the decisive factor in great-power conflict. Julian Corbett refined the idea: command is normally "in dispute", is relative to time and place, and its object is control of maritime communications rather than battle for its own sake. Theorists distinguish sea control — the ability to use a sea area for a defined period — from sea denial, the weaker strategy of preventing an enemy's use without using it oneself, classically pursued by submarines, mines, and commerce raiding (guerre de course).

History

The concept shaped the strategies of maritime empires from Athens and Rome to Spain, the Netherlands, and Britain, whose Royal Navy's command of the sea after Trafalgar (1805) underpinned the Pax Britannica. In the 20th century the submarine, the aircraft carrier, and land-based aviation complicated the picture; in the Cold War and after, the United States Navy exercised global sea control. Modern anti-access/area-denial (A2/AD) weapons — anti-ship ballistic and cruise missiles, quiet submarines, and smart mines — are designed precisely to contest command in littoral waters.
