Rewilding

Rewilding
FieldConservation biology, restoration ecology
Key conceptsKeystone species, trophic cascades, cores–corridors–carnivores, Pleistocene rewilding
Notable sitesYellowstone, Oostvaardersplassen, Knepp Estate, Iberá (Argentina)

Rewilding is a form of ecological restoration and conservation that aims to restore and protect natural processes and core wilderness areas, providing connectivity between such areas, and protecting or reintroducing apex predators and keystone species—ultimately allowing ecosystems to become self-sustaining with minimal human management. The term was developed in North American conservation circles in the 1990s, notably by Michael Soulé and Reed Noss of the Wildlands Project, around the "three Cs": cores, corridors and carnivores.

Approaches

Trophic rewilding

Trophic rewilding focuses on restoring top-down trophic interactions by reintroducing large animals. The canonical example is the 1995 reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park, which—via effects on elk behaviour and numbers—has been argued to trigger a trophic cascade benefiting willows, beavers and songbirds, though the strength and mechanisms of the cascade remain debated.

Pleistocene rewilding

A more radical variant proposes replacing extinct Pleistocene megafauna with ecological proxies—for example introducing elephants, lions or camels to parts of North America—to restore functions lost with the late-Quaternary extinctions. Russia's Pleistocene Park in Siberia experiments with herbivore-driven restoration of the mammoth steppe.

Passive rewilding

Where land is abandoned, natural succession may proceed without intervention. In Europe, rural depopulation has allowed spontaneous recovery of forests and large carnivores such as wolves, bears and lynx across large areas, a trend documented by the organisation Rewilding Europe.

Examples

  • Oostvaardersplassen (Netherlands) — a pioneering experiment in self-regulating ecosystems with introduced heck cattle, konik horses and red deer; controversial after mass starvation in severe winters prompted changes in management.
  • Knepp Estate (England) — a former farm converted from 2001 to free-roaming herbivore grazing, widely publicised by Isabella Tree's book Wilding (2018).
  • Iberá wetlands (Argentina) — reintroduction of jaguars, giant anteaters, giant otters and other extirpated species led by Fundación Rewilding Argentina.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns about animal welfare in hands-off reserves, disease and genetic issues in proxy species, impacts on farmers from large carnivores, and the absence of truly "natural" baselines in human-dominated landscapes. Supporters respond that rewilding delivers biodiversity, carbon storage, flood mitigation and ecotourism benefits.
