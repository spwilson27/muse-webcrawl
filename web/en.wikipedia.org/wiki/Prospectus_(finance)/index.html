Prospectus (finance)

A prospectus, in finance, is a disclosure document that describes a financial security for potential buyers. It commonly provides investors with material information about mutual funds, stocks, bonds, and other investments, including a description of the company's business, financial statements, biography of officers and directors, detailed information about their compensation, any litigation that is taking place, a list of material properties, and other material information.

Public offerings

In the United States, securities offered to the public must be registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). The offering document is divided into a preliminary prospectus (often called a "red herring" because of the red-ink disclaimer on its cover) used during the waiting period, and the final prospectus delivered after the registration statement becomes effective. Issuers are liable for material misstatements or omissions under the Securities Act of 1933.

In the European Union, the Prospectus Regulation requires an approved prospectus for public offers and admissions to trading on regulated markets, subject to exemptions for small offers and qualified investors. A "base prospectus" may be used for frequent issuers' programmes of debt securities.

Mutual funds

Mutual funds issue prospectuses describing investment objectives, strategies, risks, fees and expenses, and past performance. In the U.S., funds must also provide a shorter summary prospectus. Similar key investor information documents (KIID/KID) serve this purpose in Europe.
