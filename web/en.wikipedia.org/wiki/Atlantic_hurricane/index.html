Atlantic hurricane

An Atlantic hurricane is a type of tropical cyclone that forms in the Atlantic Ocean primarily between June and November. A hurricane differs from a cyclone or typhoon only on the basis of location. A hurricane is a storm that occurs in the Atlantic Ocean and northeastern Pacific Ocean, a typhoon occurs in the northwestern Pacific Ocean, and a cyclone occurs in the south Pacific or Indian Ocean.

Tropical cyclones can be categorized by the sustained wind speeds involved; in the Atlantic basin, those with maximum sustained winds of 74 mph (119 km/h) or greater are called hurricanes and are rated on the Saffir–Simpson scale from Category 1 to Category 5.

Formation

Most Atlantic tropical cyclones begin as tropical waves that move off the coast of West Africa and cross the tropical Atlantic. Warm sea surface temperatures (generally at least 26.5 °C), moist air, low vertical wind shear, and a sufficient Coriolis effect are required for development. The official Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 to November 30, with activity peaking around September 10.

Monitoring and forecasting

The National Hurricane Center (NHC) in Miami, Florida, is responsible for tracking and predicting tropical systems in the Atlantic basin. Reconnaissance aircraft, satellites, radar, and computer models are used to forecast track and intensity. Watches and warnings are issued for coastal areas threatened by landfall.

Impacts

Hurricanes can cause catastrophic damage through high winds, storm surge, inland flooding from heavy rainfall, and tornadoes. Storm surge—the abnormal rise of seawater pushed ashore by the storm—is historically the leading cause of hurricane-related deaths. Notable Atlantic hurricanes include the Galveston hurricane of 1900, Katrina (2005), Sandy (2012), Harvey (2017), and Ian (2022).

Climatology

On average, a season produces about 14 named storms, of which around 7 become hurricanes and 3 become major hurricanes (Category 3 or higher). Activity varies with multi-decadal cycles of Atlantic sea surface temperatures and with the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, which generally suppresses Atlantic activity in El Niño years.
