Change management

Change management (sometimes abbreviated as Cm) is a collective term for all approaches to prepare, support, and help individuals, teams, and organisations in making organisational change. It includes methods that redirect or redefine the use of resources, business processes, budget allocations, or other modes of operation that significantly change a company or organisation. Organisational change management considers both the processes and tools that managers use to make changes at an organisational level, and the human side of change — guiding employees through transitions so that the benefits of a change are realised and endure.

History

The foundations of the field are usually traced to Kurt Lewin, whose 1940s work on group dynamics produced the influential three-stage model of change: unfreeze the present state, move to the new state, and refreeze to stabilise it. Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations (1962) explained how innovations spread through populations along an adoption curve. From the 1980s onward, corporate restructuring and business process re-engineering made structured change programmes a standard part of management consulting, and practitioners formalised methodologies such as John Kotter's eight steps (1996) and the Prosci ADKAR model (Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement).

Approaches and models

Kotter's model prescribes creating a sense of urgency, building a guiding coalition, forming a strategic vision, enlisting volunteers, enabling action, generating short-term wins, sustaining acceleration, and instituting change. The McKinsey 7S framework and William Bridges' transition model (with its focus on endings, the neutral zone and new beginnings) are also widely used. Change management is commonly applied alongside project management: the project discipline delivers the technical change, while change management addresses adoption and usage — a distinction popularised by Prosci research claiming that projects with excellent change management are several times more likely to meet their objectives.

Criticism

Academic critics argue that much change-management literature is prescriptive, consultant-driven and weakly evidenced; that the oft-quoted claim that "70% of change programmes fail" lacks a solid empirical basis; and that models can understate conflict, power and emotion in organisations. Others note the tension between planned, top-down change and emergent change arising from everyday practice.

Other uses

In engineering and information technology, "change management" also denotes the controlled process for requesting, assessing, approving and implementing changes to systems or configurations — for example the change-enablement practice of ITIL — a usage distinct from, though related to, organisational change management.
