Patristics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Patristics or patrology is the study of the early Christian writers who are designated Church Fathers. The names derive from the combined forms of the Latin pater and the Greek patḗr, both meaning "father." The period of study is generally considered to run from the end of the New Testament era, around AD 100, until around AD 451 (the Council of Chalcedon) or as late as the eighth century, when the age of the Fathers is often held to close with John of Damascus in the East and Isidore of Seville or Bede in the West.

Scope

Patristics studies the lives, writings, doctrines and historical settings of the Fathers — figures such as Ignatius of Antioch, Irenaeus of Lyons, Tertullian, Origen, Athanasius, the Cappadocian Fathers (Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa and Gregory of Nazianzus), John Chrysostom, Jerome, Augustine of Hippo and Cyril of Alexandria. Their writings, mostly in Greek, Latin, Syriac and other languages of the ancient Near East, are the primary sources for the development of Christian doctrine, exegesis, liturgy and church order in the first centuries.

Periods

Apostolic Fathers

The earliest post-New Testament writers, including Clement of Rome, Ignatius of Antioch and Polycarp of Smyrna, are grouped as the Apostolic Fathers. Their letters and treatises, written in the late first and second centuries, show the church's teaching taking shape in the generation after the apostles.

Ante-Nicene Fathers

Writers before the First Council of Nicaea (325), including the Apologists (Justin Martyr, Athenagoras), Irenaeus, Tertullian, Cyprian and Origen, defended Christianity against pagan criticism and early heresies, and laid the foundations of systematic theology.

Nicene and post-Nicene Fathers

From the fourth century the great conciliar debates over the Trinity and the person of Christ produced the most influential patristic literature: Athanasius against Arianism, the Cappadocians on Trinitarian terminology, Augustine on grace, original sin and the church, and the Christological controversies leading to Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451).

Modern study

The discipline took scholarly form in the early modern period with critical editions such as Jacques Paul Migne's Patrologia Graeca and Patrologia Latina in the nineteenth century, and continues today with critical editions in series like Sources Chrétiennes and Corpus Christianorum. Important figures in the modern revival of patristic studies include Jean Daniélou and Henri de Lubac, associated with the nouvelle théologie and the ressourcement movement. Patristics is practised within theology, church history, classics and late antique studies, and its texts are studied for their theological, philosophical, rhetorical and social-historical content.
