Naval gun

A naval gun is a large-calibre artillery weapon mounted on a warship for use against other ships, aircraft, or shore targets. Naval guns evolved from muzzle-loading smoothbore cannon of the age of sail, through rifled breech-loaders of the ironclad era, to the enormous turret guns of dreadnought battleships, and finally to the rapid-fire automatic guns carried by modern warships.

History

Early naval cannon fired solid shot at close range. The 19th century introduced explosive shells, rifling, and breech loading, dramatically increasing range and power. The battleship era culminated in guns such as the British 15-inch and the Japanese 46 cm Type 94 — the largest ever mounted on a warship — aboard the Yamato class. After the Second World War, guided missiles displaced heavy guns as the primary ship-killing weapon.

Modern guns

Modern naval guns are typically 57 mm to 127 mm automatic weapons, such as the Italian OTO Melara 76 mm and the American 5-inch/54-calibre Mark 45, used for shore bombardment, anti-surface fire, and limited anti-air defence, with rates of fire of tens to over a hundred rounds per minute. Precision-guided extended-range shells and experimental railguns have been studied to restore gunfire support capabilities.

Terminology

Naval guns are described by bore diameter and barrel length in calibres (e.g. 5"/54), and historically by the weight of their shell (e.g. 12-pounder). Their mountings evolved from broadside batteries and barbettes to enclosed armoured turrets.
