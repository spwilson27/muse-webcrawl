Conn's syndrome, also called primary aldosteronism, is the overproduction of the hormone aldosterone by the adrenal glands, causing sodium and water retention and potassium loss. It was first described by American endocrinologist Jerome W. Conn in 1955. It is a common — and under-recognised — cause of secondary hypertension, estimated to account for up to 5–10 percent of all cases of high blood pressure.

Causes and symptoms

The two main causes are an aldosterone-producing adenoma (usually unilateral) and bilateral adrenal hyperplasia. Many patients have hypertension that is difficult to control; some have low potassium (hypokalaemia) with muscle cramps or weakness, but many are normokalaemic, which is one reason the condition is often missed. Diagnosis rests on an elevated aldosterone-to-renin ratio, confirmed by suppression testing and localised with adrenal venous sampling or imaging.

Treatment

A unilateral adenoma is usually cured by laparoscopic adrenalectomy. Bilateral disease is treated medically with mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists such as spironolactone or eplerenone, together with blood pressure control. Treated early, patients' cardiovascular risk falls substantially.
