Photolyase

Photolyases are DNA repair enzymes that reverse damage caused by ultraviolet light, a process called photoreactivation. They bind to DNA containing a cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer (or, for the related (6-4) photolyases, a pyrimidine (6-4) pyrimidone photoproduct) and, using energy absorbed from visible light, split the abnormal bonds and restore the original bases.

Mechanism

Photolyases contain a catalytic flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor in its reduced form, FADH−, and usually a second light-harvesting chromophore — either methenyltetrahydrofolate (MTHF) or 8-hydroxy-7,8-didemethyl-5-deazariboflavin (8-HDF). The antenna chromophore absorbs blue or near-UV light and transfers the energy to the flavin, which donates an electron to the pyrimidine dimer; the dimer splits, and the electron returns to the flavin. The enzyme flips the dimer out of the DNA helix into its active-site cavity to perform the repair.

Distribution

Photolyases are found in bacteria, archaea, fungi, plants, and many animals, but placental mammals — including humans — lack functional photolyases and rely instead on nucleotide excision repair to remove UV photoproducts. The related cryptochrome proteins, which evolved from photolyases, act as blue-light photoreceptors and as components of the circadian clock rather than as repair enzymes. Photolyases are used commercially, for example in some sunscreens and skin-care products intended to supplement DNA repair in human skin.
