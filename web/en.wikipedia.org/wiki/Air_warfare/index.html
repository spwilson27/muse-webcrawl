Air warfare

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Military aircraft in operation (image placeholder)

Air warfare is the use of military aircraft and other flying machines in warfare. It includes various types of operations: air-to-air combat between fighters, bombing of ground and sea targets, reconnaissance and surveillance, transport of troops and supplies, and the suppression of enemy air defences. Air warfare is conducted by air forces, naval aviation, and army aviation units.

History

Military aviation began with observation balloons in the eighteenth century and developed rapidly in the First World War, when aeroplanes progressed from reconnaissance to fighter combat, ground attack, and early strategic bombing by Zeppelins and heavy bombers. Theorists between the wars — Giulio Douhet, Hugh Trenchard, and Billy Mitchell — argued that air power could be independently decisive. In the Second World War air power became central: carrier battles decided the Pacific war, and massive strategic bombing campaigns struck Germany and Japan, culminating in the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Post-1945 developments

Jet aircraft, radar, guided missiles, and nuclear weapons transformed air warfare during the Cold War. The Korean and Vietnam wars showed both the reach and the political limits of bombing campaigns. From the 1980s, precision-guided munitions, stealth technology, and networked sensors reshaped practice, demonstrated in the 1991 Gulf War. In the twenty-first century, unmanned aerial vehicles (drones) — from high-altitude surveillance platforms to small loitering munitions — have become pervasive, notably in the Russo-Ukrainian War.

Types of air operations

  • Counter-air — gaining control of the air (see air supremacy) by destroying enemy aircraft and air defences.
  • Strike — strategic bombing and interdiction of infrastructure, industry, and supply lines.
  • Close air support — attacks in direct support of ground forces.
  • ISR — intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance by aircraft, satellites, and drones.
  • Airlift — transport of personnel, equipment, and humanitarian supplies, plus airborne assault.
  • Maritime air operations — anti-submarine and anti-ship warfare.

Law and ethics

Air warfare is regulated by the law of armed conflict, including the Hague Conventions and the Geneva Conventions' principles of distinction and proportionality; the bombing of civilians has been a recurring subject of ethical and legal controversy since the 1930s.
