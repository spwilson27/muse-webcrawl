Ager publicus

Ager publicus
Meaning: Public land of the Roman state
Central issue: Gracchan land reforms, 133 BC onward

The ager publicus (Latin: [ˈaɡer ˈpuːblikus]; "public land") is the Latin name for the state land of ancient Rome, acquired mainly by conquest from defeated Italian peoples. Portions were assigned to colonies, sold, or leased, but wealthy elites often occupied large tracts, displacing small farmers.

History

Occupation of public land by rich possessores, worked by slaves, undermined the smallholder citizen-soldier base. Attempts to enforce legal limits on holdings culminated in the reforms of the Gracchi brothers: Tiberius Gracchus's agrarian law of 133 BC redistributed ager publicus above 500 iugera per person, provoking violent political conflict.

Later developments

Subsequent agrarian laws under Gaius Gracchus, Saturninus, Julius Caesar, and others continued distribution, often to veterans. Under the Empire, remaining public land was largely absorbed into imperial estates or municipalities.
