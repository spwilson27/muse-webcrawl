Freewheel

In mechanical engineering, a freewheel or overrunning clutch is a device in a transmission that disengages the driveshaft from the driven shaft when the driven shaft rotates or can rotate faster than the driveshaft would permit. An overrunning clutch allows the driven shaft to turn freely of the transmission input, a condition known as freewheeling.

By far the most common use of a freewheel mechanism is on a bicycle, where it allows the rider to coast without the pedals turning. In older bicycle designs the freewheel was integrated into the rear sprocket assembly; in modern designs it may instead be built into the rear hub (a freehub), with the sprockets mounted as a removable cassette.

Mechanism

A typical bicycle freewheel contains spring-loaded pawls that engage a ratchet ring when the sprockets are driven forward, transmitting torque to the hub, and click over the ratchet teeth when the wheel turns faster than the sprockets. Other designs use rollers or sprags that wedge between races in one direction and roll freely in the other. Freewheels may be single-speed or carry a cluster of multiple sprockets for use with a derailleur.

Other uses

Freewheels are used in helicopter rotors, allowing the rotor to continue turning during autorotation after engine failure; in starter motors and some automatic transmissions; in agricultural machinery such as hay balers, where a heavy flywheel must overrun safely when power is cut; and historically in some automobiles, notably early Saab and Rover models, to reduce engine braking and fuel consumption with two-stroke or early four-stroke engines.
