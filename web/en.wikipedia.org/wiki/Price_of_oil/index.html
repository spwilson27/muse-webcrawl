Price of oil

Historical crude oil prices (nominal)

The price of oil, or more precisely the price of crude oil, is commonly quoted per barrel in United States dollars using benchmark grades such as Brent Crude (the international benchmark) and West Texas Intermediate (WTI, the North American benchmark). Because oil is a globally traded commodity central to transport, industry, and petrochemicals, its price is one of the most closely watched economic indicators.

Determinants

Oil prices are shaped by supply (including production decisions by OPEC and other major producers, shale output, and inventories), demand (driven by global economic growth, seasonality, and energy transitions), geopolitics (wars, sanctions, and embargoes), and financial markets, where futures contracts allow extensive speculative trading. Differentials between benchmarks reflect crude quality, location, and transport costs.

History

  • 1973–74 — The Arab oil embargo during the Yom Kippur War roughly quadrupled prices, causing the first oil shock.
  • 1979 — The Iranian Revolution and subsequent Iran–Iraq War drove a second major price spike.
  • 1986 — A price collapse followed Saudi Arabia's decision to increase production.
  • 1990 — Iraq's invasion of Kuwait caused a brief sharp spike before the Gulf War.
  • 2003–2008 — Surging demand, especially from China, and limited spare capacity pushed prices to a record nominal peak of about $147 per barrel in July 2008, before the global financial crisis caused a crash.
  • 2014–2016 — Rapid growth in US shale production and OPEC's decision not to cut output drove prices from over $100 to below $30.
  • 2020 — The COVID-19 pandemic collapsed demand; WTI futures briefly traded below zero (about −$37) in April 2020, an unprecedented event.
  • 2022 — Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent Brent above $120 per barrel before prices eased.

Effects

Sustained high oil prices tend to raise inflation, squeeze consumers, and benefit exporting countries, while low prices benefit importers but strain the budgets of producers and investment in new supply. Because energy costs feed into nearly every sector, oil price swings have repeatedly been associated with macroeconomic cycles, contributing to recessions in the 1970s and influencing monetary policy ever since.
