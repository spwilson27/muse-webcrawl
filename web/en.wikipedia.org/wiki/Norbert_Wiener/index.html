Norbert Wiener (November 26, 1894 – March 18, 1964) was an American computer scientist, mathematician and philosopher. He became a professor of mathematics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). A child prodigy, Wiener graduated from Tufts College at age 14 and received his PhD from Harvard at 18.

Wiener is considered the originator of cybernetics, the science of communication as it relates to living things and machines, with implications for engineering, systems control, computer science, biology, neuroscience, philosophy, and the organisation of society. His work laid the foundations for feedback control theory used in anti-aircraft fire-control systems during World War II, and for the Wiener filter in signal processing.

Biography

Wiener was born in Columbia, Missouri, the son of Leo Wiener, a professor of Slavic languages at Harvard. Educated largely at home, he entered Tufts College at age 11 and received his BA in mathematics in 1909 at 14. He earned his PhD at Harvard in 1913 with a dissertation on mathematical logic, studying under Karl Schmidt and Josiah Royce, and then studied in Cambridge with Bertrand Russell and G. H. Hardy and in Göttingen with David Hilbert and Edmund Landau. He joined MIT in 1919 and remained there for the rest of his career.

Work

Wiener made fundamental contributions to stochastic processes (the Wiener process, or Brownian motion), Fourier analysis on function spaces (the Paley–Wiener theorem), harmonic analysis, ergodic theory and prediction theory. In 1948 he published Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine, which became a bestseller and popularised feedback and information concepts across disciplines. His book The Human Use of Human Beings (1950) considered the social implications of automation.

Later life and legacy

Wiener received the National Medal of Science in 1963, presented by President Lyndon B. Johnson shortly before Wiener's death in Stockholm in March 1964. The Norbert Wiener Prize in Applied Mathematics and the Norbert Wiener Award for Social and Professional Responsibility commemorate his work. His ideas anticipated later developments in artificial intelligence, information theory and robotics.
