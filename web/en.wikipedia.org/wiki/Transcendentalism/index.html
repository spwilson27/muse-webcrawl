Transcendentalism is a philosophical, spiritual and literary movement that developed in the late 1820s and 1830s in the New England region of the United States. Rooted in English and German Romanticism, the biblical criticism of Herder and Schleiermacher, and the scepticism of Hume, the transcendentalists operated from a core belief that individuals are inherently good and that society and its institutions — particularly organised religion and political parties — corrupt the purity of the individual.

Beliefs

Transcendentalists believed that people are at their best when truly self-reliant and independent, and that divine experience is accessible directly through nature and intuition rather than through doctrine. Ralph Waldo Emerson's essay Nature (1836) became the movement's manifesto, and his "Divinity School Address" (1838) broke with Unitarian orthodoxy. The Transcendental Club met in Boston and Concord from 1836; its journal The Dial (1840–44), edited by Margaret Fuller and Emerson, published the movement's work.

Major figures and legacy

Henry David Thoreau's Walden (1854) and his essay "Civil Disobedience" became the movement's most enduring works, influencing Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr. Other figures included Bronson Alcott, whose utopian community Fruitlands (1843) briefly put transcendentalist social ideals into practice, and Fuller, whose Woman in the Nineteenth Century (1845) is a foundational feminist text. The movement shaped American literature, religious liberalism, and reform causes including abolition and women's rights.
