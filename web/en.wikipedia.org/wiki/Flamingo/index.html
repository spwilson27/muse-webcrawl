Flamingos are a type of wading bird in the family Phoenicopteridae, the only bird family in the order Phoenicopteriformes. There are six extant flamingo species, distributed across the Americas (including the Caribbean), Africa, southern Europe and parts of Asia.

Description

Flamingos are tall birds, typically 1.2 to 1.5 metres in height for the greater flamingo, with very long legs and necks and distinctive downward-bent bills adapted for filter feeding. Their famous pink or reddish plumage comes from carotenoid pigments in the algae and small crustaceans they eat; birds that do not obtain enough carotenoids fade to pale pink or white. In flight, the black flight feathers contrast vividly with the pink body.

Feeding

Flamingos feed with their heads upside down under water, using their bills to filter blue-green algae, diatoms and small invertebrates from the mud and water of shallow lagoons, salt pans and alkaline lakes. The tongue acts as a piston, pumping water through lamellae lining the bill.

Behaviour and breeding

Highly social birds, flamingos gather in flocks that can number in the thousands; the lesser flamingo gatherings on East African soda lakes are among the largest bird concentrations in the world. They perform synchronised group displays before breeding, build conical mud nests, and lay a single chalky-white egg, which both parents incubate. Chicks are fed a nutritious secretion called "crop milk".

Relationship with humans

Flamingos appear in the art and symbolism of many cultures, and plastic lawn flamingos became a famous piece of American garden kitsch. Several species are threatened by habitat loss and disturbance of their specialised wetland breeding sites.
