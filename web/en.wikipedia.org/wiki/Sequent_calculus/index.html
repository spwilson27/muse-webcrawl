Sequent calculus

A proof tree built from sequent rules.

Sequent calculus is a style of formal logical argumentation in which every inference line is a conditional tautology called a sequent. It was introduced by the German logician Gerhard Gentzen in the mid-1930s, in his landmark investigations into the foundations of logic, alongside his system of natural deduction. Sequent calculus is one of the principal frameworks of proof theory, valued because its symmetries make the structure of proofs amenable to mathematical analysis.

Sequents

A sequent has the form

Γ → Δ

where Γ (the antecedent) and Δ (the succedent) are finite sequences or multisets of formulas. The intended reading is that the conjunction of the formulas in Γ implies the disjunction of the formulas in Δ: "if everything on the left holds, then at least one thing on the right holds." A proof of a formula A is a derivation of the sequent with empty antecedent and A alone in the succedent.

The systems LK and LJ

Gentzen presented two systems: LK (Logistische Klassische Kalkül), for classical logic, in which succedents may contain several formulas, and LJ, for intuitionistic logic, obtained by restricting succedents to at most one formula. Rules divide into logical rules, which introduce a connective on the left or right of the arrow, and structural rules — weakening, contraction, and exchange — which manipulate the arrangement of formulas. The identity axiom and the cut rule, which combines a proof of a formula with a proof that uses it, complete the system.

Cut elimination

Gentzen's central result, the cut-elimination theorem (his Hauptsatz, "main theorem"), states that any derivation using the cut rule can be transformed into one that does not. Cut-free proofs have the subformula property: every formula appearing in the proof is a subformula of the conclusion. This yields proofs of consistency for the systems concerned — Gentzen applied the method to give a consistency proof for arithmetic — and underlies the use of sequent calculi in automated theorem proving, where proof search can proceed by working upward from the goal sequent without having to guess intermediate "cut" formulas.

Variants and influence

Sequent-style systems have been devised for modal, temporal, linear, relevance, and substructural logics, typically by adjusting the structural rules: linear logic, for instance, drops unrestricted weakening and contraction. Proof-theoretic semantics and the Curry–Howard correspondence connect sequent derivations with computation; the calculus also influenced the design of logic programming and proof assistants. Its left–right symmetry contrasts with Hilbert-style systems, which have many axioms and few rules, and with natural deduction, where the symmetry is less explicit.
