City upon a Hill

“A city upon a hill”

Source: John Winthrop, A Model of Christian Charity (1630)
Biblical origin: Matthew 5:14 (Sermon on the Mount)

The phrase “city upon a hill” (or “city on a hill”) comes from the Sermon on the Mount in the Gospel of Matthew: “You are the light of the world. A city set on a hill cannot be hidden.” In American history it is inseparably associated with the sermon A Model of Christian Charity, written in 1630 by the Puritan leader John Winthrop as the Massachusetts Bay colonists prepared to settle in New England: “For we must consider that we shall be as a city upon a hill. The eyes of all people are upon us.”

Winthrop's sermon

Winthrop's use of the image was a warning as much as a boast: if the colonists failed in their covenant with God, they would become a byword and a cautionary tale. The sermon laid out a vision of a closely knit, charitable community bound by mutual obligation, in which the rich and poor were knit together in brotherly affection.

Use in American politics

The phrase was revived in the 20th century as a shorthand for American exceptionalism. John F. Kennedy used it in a 1961 address to the Massachusetts legislature. Ronald Reagan made the “shining city upon a hill” a signature image of his presidency, notably in his 1989 farewell address, describing a city “teeming with people of all kinds living in harmony and peace”, with doors open to anyone with the will to come. Many later politicians of different parties have invoked the phrase, sometimes critically, as in Mario Cuomo's 1984 response contrasting the shining city with the poverty he saw in America.
