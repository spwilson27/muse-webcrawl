Dancing plague of 1518

The dancing plague of 1518, or dance epidemic of 1518, was a case of dancing mania that occurred in Strasbourg, Alsace (modern-day France, then part of the Holy Roman Empire), in July 1518. Somewhere between 50 and 400 people reportedly danced for weeks without rest, and some are said to have collapsed or died from exhaustion, strokes or heart attacks.

Events

The outbreak began in mid-July 1518 when a woman, known in later accounts as Frau Troffea, began to dance fervently in a Strasbourg street. Within a week dozens had joined her, and within a month the number of dancers had grown into the hundreds. Local authorities, believing the dancers would recover if they danced continuously, opened guildhalls, built a stage and hired musicians — a remedy that appeared only to worsen the epidemic. Authorities eventually resorted to removing the afflicted from the city, sending them to a mountain shrine of Saint Vitus to seek a cure.

Explanations

Contemporaries attributed the dancing to "hot blood" or the curse of Saint Vitus. Modern theories include mass psychogenic illness triggered by extreme stress and hardship, and ergot poisoning from fungi growing on damp rye — though ergot's symptoms make prolonged dancing unlikely. Similar dancing manias, sometimes called chorea or tarantism, were recorded in Europe between the 14th and 17th centuries.
