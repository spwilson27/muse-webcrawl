The United States courts of appeals are the intermediate appellate courts of the United States federal judiciary. There are thirteen: eleven numbered circuits covering geographic regions, the District of Columbia Circuit, and the Federal Circuit, which has nationwide jurisdiction over specialised subject matter such as patents and claims against the federal government.

Procedure

Appeals are ordinarily heard by panels of three judges drawn from the circuit's authorised judges, who are appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for life. The courts review the record of the district court for legal error; they do not hold trials or hear new evidence. A losing party may seek rehearing en banc before all active judges of the circuit, and thereafter petition the Supreme Court for a writ of certiorari, which is granted in only a small fraction of cases — making the courts of appeals the final word in most federal litigation. The Ninth Circuit, covering the western states, is by far the largest; the D.C. Circuit is disproportionately important for administrative law because many federal agencies are sued in Washington.

History

The modern system dates from the Judiciary Act of 1891, which created dedicated appellate courts to relieve the Supreme Court's backlog. The Tenth Circuit was split off in 1929 and the Eleventh in 1981; the Federal Circuit was created in 1982 by merging the Court of Claims and the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals.
