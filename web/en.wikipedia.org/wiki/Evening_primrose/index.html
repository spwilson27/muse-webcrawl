Evening primrose is a common name for plants in the genus Oenothera, a group of about 145 species of herbaceous flowering plants native to the Americas. The name most often refers to Oenothera biennis, the common evening primrose. The flowers of many species open in the evening, typically within a minute or two, and are pollinated by moths and bees active at dusk; the blooms are usually yellow, though some species have white, pink, or purple flowers.

Description

Species of Oenothera range from small alpine plants to tall biennials such as O. biennis, which forms a basal rosette in its first year and a flowering spike up to about 1.5 m (5 ft) tall in its second. The flowers have four petals, often notched, and a distinctive cross-shaped stigma. Many species are widely naturalised outside the Americas, including in Europe, where evening primroses became classic plants of railway banks and waste ground.

Genetics

Oenothera played an important historical role in genetics. Hugo de Vries based his mutation theory in part on variations observed in evening primroses around 1900. The genus is notable for permanent translocation heterozygosity, in which whole sets of chromosomes are inherited together, and for its plastid inheritance, much studied in the 20th century.

Uses

Evening primrose oil, extracted from the seeds of O. biennis, is rich in gamma-linolenic acid and is sold as a dietary supplement; scientific evidence for most of its claimed benefits is limited or inconclusive. The plant's roots and shoots were traditionally eaten, and several species are grown as ornamental garden plants.
