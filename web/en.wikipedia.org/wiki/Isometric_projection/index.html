Isometric projection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A cube in isometric projection: the three axes are foreshortened equally.

Isometric projection is a method for visually representing three-dimensional objects in two dimensions in technical and engineering drawings. It is an axonometric projection in which the three coordinate axes appear equally foreshortened and the angle between any two of them is 120 degrees. The term "isometric" comes from the Greek for "equal measure", reflecting that the scale along each axis of the projection is the same.

Characteristics

In isometric projection the viewing direction is chosen so that the three principal axes are symmetrically inclined to the projection plane (the direction makes equal angles, about 35.264°, with the axes). Lengths along all three axes are foreshortened by the same factor, √(2/3) ≈ 0.8165, so measurements can be taken directly along the axes from a drawing — unlike in perspective projection. In practice, engineering "isometric drawings" often ignore the foreshortening and use true lengths for convenience.

Rotation angles

The standard orientation is obtained by rotating the object 45° about the vertical axis and then tilting it by arcsin(tan 30°) ≈ 35.264° about the horizontal axis. Equivalent views are generated with the rotation matrices familiar from computer graphics; "isometric" cameras in games frequently approximate the true projection with a 2:1 pixel ratio dimetric view for clean raster art.

Applications

Isometric drawing has been used since the nineteenth century for engineering illustration, patent drawings, and assembly manuals, because it shows three faces of an object without distortion of scale along the axes. It is widespread in video games — classics such as Q*bert, SimCity 2000, and Diablo used isometric tiles to give a 3D impression cheaply — and in infographics, exploded-view diagrams, and architectural illustration. Optical illusions such as the Necker cube and impossible objects exploit the projection's depth ambiguity.

History

Axonometric forms appear in Chinese art and in military ("military projection") drawing long before formalisation. Professor William Farish of Cambridge published the first rules of isometric drawing in 1822; the method was standardised in engineering practice over the following decades.
