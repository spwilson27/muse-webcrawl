Drone (music)

In music, a drone is a harmonic or monophonic effect or accompaniment where a note or chord is continuously sounded throughout most or all of a piece. A drone may also be an instrument part — such as the drone pipes of a bagpipe — or a dedicated instrument, whose purpose is to produce such a sustained sound.

History and world traditions

Drones are among the oldest elements of music worldwide. Indian classical music is built over the continuous drone of the tanpura (and often a śruti box), against which the raga is performed. Byzantine chant uses the ison, a sustained bass note. Medieval European music used drones in instruments such as the hurdy-gurdy and various bagpipes, whose drone pipes sound constant notes while a chanter plays the melody. Drones also appear in Persian, Turkish, and various folk traditions, and in the didgeridoo-based music of Aboriginal Australians.

Structure and effect

A drone typically anchors the tonic (or tonic and dominant) of a piece, freeing melody and harmony from the need to restate the tonal centre. Because the drone never resolves or cadences, it can create a sense of timelessness or trance; in modal music it allows shifting melodic modes to be heard against a fixed reference pitch.

Modern and experimental use

In the 20th century, composers such as La Monte Young made drones the entire substance of works, exploring just intonation and slowly beating overtones; his collaborations with Marian Zazeela and the Theatre of Eternal Music are foundational to drone music as a genre. Drone music (or drone-based music) also spans ambient, drone metal (e.g., Earth, Sunn O)))), and electronic music, where long sustained tones are processed and layered. The drone element of Scottish pibroch and of Appalachian folk fiddling shows the technique's persistence in folk practice.

Instruments with drones

  • Bagpipes (many types)
  • Hurdy-gurdy
  • Tanpura and shruti box
  • Hardanger fiddle (sympathetic/under strings)
  • Didgeridoo
  • Jew's harp
