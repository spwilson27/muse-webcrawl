English modal verbs

The English modal verbs are a subset of the English auxiliary verbs used mostly to express modality — properties such as possibility, obligation, permission and prediction. The core modal verbs of English are can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should and must. A few other verbs, such as ought to, dare, need and used to, are sometimes classed as modals or "semi-modals".

Characteristics

The core modal verbs share a set of grammatical properties (sometimes called the NICE properties — negation, inversion, code and emphasis):

  • They have no third-person singular -s: she can, not *she cans.
  • They have no infinitive, gerund or participles: there is no *to must or *musting.
  • They form negatives directly with not: cannot, will not, without do-support.
  • They invert with the subject in questions: Can you swim?
  • They are followed by the bare infinitive of another verb: must go, not *must to go (except for ought to).
  • They occur only as the first verb in a verb phrase and (with a few exceptions) only one modal can appear in a given clause.

Historically, most of the modals are preterite-present verbs: their present-tense forms descend from Old English past-tense forms, which is why they lack the usual present-tense endings. Could is etymologically the past tense of can, and would of will, though in modern English these pairs function largely as independent items, with could and would marking tentativeness and conditionality as much as past time.

Meanings

Modals typically have two broad families of meaning. Epistemic meanings concern the speaker's judgement about the truth of a proposition (That must be true; she might be late). Deontic meanings concern obligation and permission (You must stop; you may leave), and dynamic meanings concern ability and volition (He can swim; I will help). The same verb often spans several of these: must marks inference (You must be tired) as well as obligation (You must rest).

Periphrastic alternatives

Because modals are defective — they lack infinitives and past-tense forms in some uses — English resorts to periphrastic expressions such as have to (for must), be able to (for can), be allowed to (for may) and be going to or be about to (for will) when a non-present or non-finite form is needed: I want to be able to swim, She had to leave early.
