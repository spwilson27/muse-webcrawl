Synaeresis

Synaeresis (also spelled syneresis) is a term used in several fields:

  • In phonetics and prosody, the contraction of two adjacent vowels (or a vowel sequence) within a word into a single syllable, producing a diphthong or long vowel where hiatus might otherwise occur. It is the opposite of diaeresis, in which such a sequence is pronounced in separate syllables. Synaeresis was a common metrical device in classical Greek and Latin verse and in early modern English poetry, where a word such as "heaven" could be scanned as one syllable.
  • In chemistry and materials science, syneresis is the extraction or expulsion of a liquid from a gel — for example, the whey that separates from curd in cheese-making, or the liquid that weeps from a gel as its network contracts on standing.

Phonetics

In linguistic usage, synaeresis merges a sequence of two vowels into one syllabic unit without an intervening consonant. In Ancient Greek, forms such as the contraction of ε + α sequences in verbs illustrate the process. Poets used synaeresis to fit words into metre; in English verse of the 16th and 17th centuries, words like "being" or "power" are often scanned as monosyllables. The phenomenon relates to hiatus resolution strategies across languages, alongside elision and glide formation.

Chemistry

In gel chemistry, syneresis occurs when a gel network continues to form cross-links after the initial gelation point, squeezing solvent out of the structure. It is observed in silica gels, in food gels such as yoghurt and custard, and is controlled in industry because excessive syneresis spoils texture and appearance.
