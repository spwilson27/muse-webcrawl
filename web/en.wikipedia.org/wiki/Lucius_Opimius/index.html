Lucius Opimius was a Roman politician of the late Republic, best known as the consul of 121 BC who suppressed the supporters of Gaius Gracchus under the first recorded use of the senatus consultum ultimum, the Senate's "final decree" calling on the magistrates to defend the Republic.

Career

Opimius first appears as praetor in 125 BC, when he was sent to reduce the town of Fregellae, which had revolted; the town was betrayed into his hands and destroyed. He stood unsuccessfully for the consulship of 123 BC, defeated in part through the influence of Gaius Gracchus, and was elected consul for 121 BC alongside Quintus Fabius Maximus Allobrogicus.

Suppression of Gaius Gracchus

During his consulship, the political conflict between the Senate and Gaius Gracchus came to a head over proposals to repeal Gracchan legislation. After a supporter of Opimius was killed by Gracchus's attendants, the Senate passed the decree later known as the senatus consultum ultimum, instructing Opimius to see that the Republic took no harm. Opimius armed senators and equestrians and occupied the Capitoline Hill. Gracchus and his supporters withdrew to the Aventine Hill; after negotiations failed, Opimius's forces attacked, and Gracchus died — by his own order, at the hand of his slave — while fleeing. Some three thousand of his supporters were afterwards arrested and strangled in prison on Opimius's authority, without trial.

Opimius commemorated the restoration of order by building (or restoring) a temple of Concord at the foot of the Capitol, alongside a basilica that bore his name, the Basilica Opimia. The temple was widely read by his enemies as a monument to discord rather than harmony.

Trial and exile

In 120 BC Opimius was prosecuted by the tribune Publius Decius for putting citizens to death without trial, but was defended by the consul Gaius Papirius Carbo and acquitted, a verdict that appeared to legitimise the final decree. In 116 BC he served on a commission sent to divide the kingdom of Numidia between Jugurtha and his brother Adherbal. The commissioners were widely believed to have accepted bribes from Jugurtha, and under the Mamilian commission of 109 BC Opimius was condemned and went into exile at Dyrrhachium, where he died in poverty and unpopularity.

Legacy

Opimius's name became proverbial in two ways: the "Opimian wine", the celebrated vintage of his consulship year (121 BC), long prized by Roman connoisseurs; and his precedent for the use of emergency senatorial authority against domestic opponents, invoked repeatedly in the following century, most famously against Catiline in 63 BC.
