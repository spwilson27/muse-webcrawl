Oily fish

ExamplesSalmon, trout, mackerel, herring, sardines, pilchards, anchovies, sprats, eel, tuna
Key nutrientsOmega-3 fatty acids (EPA, DHA), vitamin D, protein
Typical fat contentAbove ~5–10% in flesh (varies by season)

Oily fish are fish species that have oils distributed throughout their flesh and in the belly cavity, as opposed to white fish, which store oil mainly in the liver. Fillets of oily fish may contain up to 30% oil, although the figure varies both within and between species and across the year. Common oily fish include salmon, trout, mackerel, herring, sardines, pilchards, sprats, anchovies and eel.

Health benefits and risks

Oily fish are the principal dietary source of the long-chain omega-3 fatty acids eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), associated with cardiovascular health and normal brain development; they are also a major natural source of vitamin D. Health authorities in many countries recommend eating at least one portion of oily fish per week. The benefits are balanced against risks: larger predatory species (such as shark, swordfish and marlin) can accumulate mercury and other pollutants, so official advice limits portions for some species and gives special guidance for pregnant women and children.

Use

Oily fish spoil faster than white fish because of their fat content, and they are often preserved by smoking, curing, pickling or canning — as with smoked salmon, kippers, rollmops and canned sardines and tuna. Their rich flavour makes them staples of cuisines from Scandinavia and Japan to the Mediterranean.
