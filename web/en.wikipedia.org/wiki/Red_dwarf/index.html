A red dwarf is a small, cool, low-mass star on the main sequence, of spectral type late K or M. Red dwarfs are by far the most numerous type of star in the Milky Way — roughly 70 percent of all stars — yet each is so faint that none is visible to the naked eye from Earth; even the nearest star to the Sun, Proxima Centauri, requires a telescope. They fuse hydrogen so slowly that their main-sequence lifetimes run to hundreds of billions or even trillions of years, far longer than the current age of the universe, so no red dwarf has yet evolved off the main sequence.

Characteristics

Red dwarfs range from about 0.6 solar masses down to the hydrogen-burning limit near 0.075–0.08 solar masses, below which an object is a brown dwarf. The least massive are fully convective, mixing their interiors so thoroughly that helium ash does not accumulate in the core — a key reason for their extreme longevity. Surface temperatures of roughly 2,300–3,800 K give them their red colour. Many, especially young ones, are flare stars, producing sudden outbursts and starspots that can affect the habitability of orbiting planets.

Planets and habitability

Exoplanet surveys show that red dwarfs commonly host rocky planets: examples include Proxima Centauri b and the seven-planet system of TRAPPIST-1, several of whose planets lie in the habitable zone. Because a red dwarf is dim, its habitable zone lies close in, where planets are likely tidally locked and exposed to flares and high early X-ray and ultraviolet fluxes — factors debated in assessing whether such worlds can retain atmospheres and liquid water.

End state

Models predict that red dwarfs will slowly brighten and heat as hydrogen is consumed, eventually becoming "blue dwarfs" before finally cooling as white dwarfs — a fate no red dwarf has yet reached, since the universe is not old enough.
