Fractional ownership

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fractional ownership is a method in which several unrelated parties can share in, and mitigate the risk of, ownership of a high-value tangible asset, such as a jet aircraft, yacht, or piece of resort real estate. Each owner holds a percentage share of the asset and a corresponding right to use it for a proportion of the time, while sharing the costs of purchase, maintenance, insurance, and management. The arrangement is typically administered by a management company that handles scheduling, upkeep, and the rotation of use among owners.

Aircraft

Fractional ownership is best known from business aviation. The modern model was launched in 1986 by NetJets (then Executive Jet Aviation), whose founder Richard Santulli applied the timeshare concept to corporate jets. Customers buy a share — commonly 1/16 (equivalent to about 50 flight hours per year) up to 1/2 — of a specific aircraft type, and are guaranteed access to that type, or an equivalent, with as little as a few hours' notice. Competitors include Flexjet and various regional operators. The model sits between full ownership and charter, and differs from both in that owners hold equity in an actual airframe.

Real estate

In real estate, fractional ownership gives each co-owner a deeded share of a property — usually a luxury vacation home — and a scheduled right of occupancy, often four to six weeks per year under a rotating calendar. It is distinct from a timeshare: fractional owners own an interest in the real property itself (which can appreciate or be resold), whereas timeshare purchasers typically buy only a right to use accommodation owned by a developer or association. Fractional shares are commonly sold in fractions from 1/4 down to 1/13.

Other assets and developments

The model has been applied to yachts, racehorses, art, and classic cars. Since the 2010s, digital platforms have extended the term to tokenised shares in collectibles and securities, though such products are regulated as investments rather than as use-sharing arrangements.
