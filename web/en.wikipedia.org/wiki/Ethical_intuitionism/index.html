Ethical intuitionism

Ethical intuitionism (also called moral intuitionism) is a view in meta-ethics that certain moral facts are known by intuition — that is, by direct, non-inferential awareness — and that moral truths are self-evident to a sufficiently mature rational mind. It is a form of moral realism and foundationalism about moral knowledge.

Doctrine

Intuitionists hold that moral judgements such as "cruelty is wrong" are not derived from more basic premises but are apprehended directly, somewhat as mathematical truths are apprehended. Classical intuitionists typically endorse objective moral values that are irreducible to natural properties. G. E. Moore argued in Principia Ethica (1903) that "good" is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property, and that any attempt to define it in natural terms commits the naturalistic fallacy.

History

Ideas recognisable as intuitionism appear in the moral sense theories of Shaftesbury and Francis Hutcheson and in the common-sense philosophy of Thomas Reid. In the early 20th century the view was developed by Moore, H. A. Prichard, W. D. Ross and C. D. Broad. Ross's version, in The Right and the Good (1930), posited a plurality of self-evident prima facie duties — fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement and non-maleficence — which must be weighed against each other in particular cases. Intuitionism declined under criticism from emotivists and error theorists, but was revived in late 20th- and early 21st-century moral philosophy by writers such as Robert Audi and Michael Huemer.

Criticism

Opponents object that people and cultures "intuit" contradictory moral claims, and that intuitionism offers no method to resolve such disagreement. J. L. Mackie argued that objectively prescriptive moral values would be metaphysically "queer" and that their supposed perception requires an equally queer faculty.
