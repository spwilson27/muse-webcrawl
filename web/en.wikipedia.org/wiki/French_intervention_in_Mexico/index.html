The French intervention in Mexico (1861–1867), also known as the Franco-Mexican War, was an invasion of Mexico by the Second French Empire of Napoleon III, acting with Mexican conservatives after Mexico suspended foreign debt payments. It installed the Austrian Archduke Maximilian as Emperor of Mexico, but guerrilla resistance under President Benito Juárez, United States pressure after the Civil War, and France's deteriorating position in Europe forced a French withdrawal; Maximilian was captured and executed in 1867.

Background

Bankrupt after the Reform War, Juárez's government suspended payments on foreign debts in 1861. Britain, Spain and France agreed the Convention of London to intervene; when the others withdrew on seeing France's ambitions, France landed an expedition alone. Its first advance on Puebla was defeated on 5 May 1862 — the origin of the Cinco de Mayo celebration — but reinforcements under General Forey took Puebla and Mexico City in 1863.

The Second Mexican Empire

A conservative assembly offered the crown to Ferdinand Maximilian of Austria, who arrived in 1864 with his wife Carlota. Maximilian, unexpectedly liberal, retained many reform laws and alienated his conservative backers, while never defeating the republican forces, which controlled much of the countryside with commanders such as Porfirio Díaz and Mariano Escobedo. The United States, after 1865, massed troops on the Rio Grande and supplied the republicans, invoking the Monroe Doctrine.

Collapse

Pressed by the growing Prussian threat, Napoleon III announced a phased withdrawal in 1866. Maximilian refused to abdicate and leave with the French; the last French troops sailed in March 1867. Besieged at Querétaro, Maximilian surrendered in May and, rejecting pleas for clemency, was executed by firing squad on 19 June 1867 alongside generals Miramón and Mejía. Juárez re-entered Mexico City in July, restoring the Republic.
