Utopia (Latin: Libellus vere aureus, nec minus salutaris quam festivus, de optimo rei publicae statu deque nova insula Utopia) is a work of fiction and socio-political satire by Thomas More, published in Leuven in 1516. The book gave the English language the word "utopia", a pun on the Greek ou-topos ("no place") and eu-topos ("good place").

Content

Written in the form of a dialogue, the work recounts the traveller Raphael Hythlodaeus's description of the island nation of Utopia: a communal society without private property, where goods are stored in warehouses and distributed on request, gold is deliberately despised, workdays are six hours, and religious toleration is practised. Book One frames this account with a critique of contemporary European society — enclosures, harsh punishments for theft, and mercenary warfare.

Interpretation and influence

Scholars have long debated whether More endorsed the arrangements he described or intended them ironically. Whatever its intent, Utopia founded the literary tradition of utopian and, by reaction, dystopian fiction.
