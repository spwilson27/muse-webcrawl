Primary health care

Primary health care (PHC) is a whole-of-society approach to health that aims to ensure the highest possible level of health and well-being, with services centred on people's needs and delivered as close as possible to their everyday environment. It is the first level of contact between individuals, families, and communities and the health system.

Definition and principles

The modern concept was defined by the Declaration of Alma-Ata (1978), adopted at the International Conference on Primary Health Care convened by the WHO and UNICEF in the Soviet Union. The declaration called for "Health for All" and identified essential elements: health education, promotion of food supply and nutrition, safe water and sanitation, maternal and child health care including family planning, immunization, prevention and control of endemic diseases, treatment of common diseases and injuries, and provision of essential drugs — delivered with community participation, equity, and appropriate technology. The 2018 Declaration of Astana reaffirmed PHC as the foundation for universal health coverage.

Components and delivery

The WHO now frames PHC around three components: integrated health services with an emphasis on primary care and public health; multisectoral policy and action addressing the broader determinants of health; and empowered people and communities. Delivery typically relies on general practitioners, nurses, midwives, and community health workers, as in Brazil's Family Health Strategy and Ethiopia's Health Extension Programme.

Evidence

Research associated with Barbara Starfield and others shows that health systems oriented toward strong primary care tend to achieve better population health outcomes, lower costs, and reduced inequalities than systems centred on specialist and hospital care.
