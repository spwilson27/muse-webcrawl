The Sedlec Ossuary (Czech: Kostnice v Sedlci), also known as the Bone Church, is a small Roman Catholic chapel located beneath the Cemetery Church of All Saints in Sedlec, a suburb of Kutná Hora in the Czech Republic. The ossuary is famous for containing the skeletons of an estimated 40,000 to 70,000 people, whose bones have been artistically arranged to form decorations and furnishings for the chapel.

History

According to tradition, in 1278 the abbot of the Cistercian monastery at Sedlec brought back a handful of earth from the Holy Land and sprinkled it over the abbey cemetery, making it a highly desirable burial place across Central Europe. Burials from the Black Death in the 14th century and the Hussite Wars in the 15th century filled and expanded the cemetery; around 1400 a Gothic church was built among the graves, with its lower level used as an ossuary for bones exhumed to make room for new burials.

The bone decorations

In 1870 the Schwarzenberg family, owners of the chapel, employed woodcarver František Rint to put the accumulated bones into order. Rint created the chapel's striking arrangements, including a chandelier said to contain at least one of every bone in the human body, garlands of skulls, pyramids of bones, and a large Schwarzenberg coat of arms rendered in bone. Rint signed his work, also in bone, on a wall near the entrance.

Tourism

The Sedlec Ossuary is one of the most visited tourist attractions in the Czech Republic, drawing hundreds of thousands of visitors a year. It has featured widely in popular culture and is often compared with other European ossuaries such as the Paris Catacombs and the Capuchin Crypt in Rome.
