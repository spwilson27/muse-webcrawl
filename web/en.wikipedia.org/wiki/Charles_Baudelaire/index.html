Charles Pierre Baudelaire (9 April 1821 – 31 August 1867) was a French poet, essayist, art critic, and pioneering translator of Edgar Allan Poe. His most famous work, the poetry collection Les Fleurs du mal (The Flowers of Evil, 1857), expresses the changing nature of beauty in rapidly industrialising Paris during the mid-19th century.

Life

Born in Paris, Baudelaire lost his father at age six and had a difficult relationship with his stepfather, General Jacques Aupick. After a voyage to India arranged to distract him from a dissolute life in Paris, he inherited money at 21 and spent much of it, incurring debts that left his finances under court control. His love affairs, notably with Jeanne Duval, inspired many poems.

Work and influence

On publication in 1857, Les Fleurs du mal was prosecuted for offences against public morals; six poems were banned, a ban lifted only in 1949. Baudelaire's prose poems, published posthumously as Le Spleen de Paris (1869), helped found the prose poem genre. His aesthetic of "correspondences" and modernity influenced the Symbolists, including Verlaine and Rimbaud, and modernist poets such as T. S. Eliot. He died in Paris in 1867 after suffering aphasia, likely from syphilis.
