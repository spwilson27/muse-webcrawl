Superfund

Superfund is the informal name for the United States federal government's program to clean up uncontrolled or abandoned hazardous waste sites, as well as accidents, spills, and other emergency releases of pollutants into the environment. It was established by the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of 1980 (CERCLA) and is administered by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA).

Background

Public alarm over toxic waste sites such as Love Canal in Niagara Falls, New York — where a neighbourhood had been built over a buried chemical waste dump — drove Congress to enact CERCLA in December 1980. The law created a trust fund, financed initially by taxes on the chemical and petroleum industries, to pay for cleanups where responsible parties could not be found or could not pay.

How the program works

The EPA evaluates candidate sites using the Hazard Ranking System and places the most serious on the National Priorities List (NPL), which makes them eligible for long-term, fund-financed remedial action. Remedial investigations and feasibility studies determine the cleanup remedy, which may involve removing contaminated soil, treating groundwater, or containing waste in place. The EPA can also undertake short-term "removal actions" to address immediate threats.

Liability

CERCLA imposes strict, and generally joint and several, liability on potentially responsible parties — including current and past owners and operators, generators, and transporters of hazardous waste — regardless of fault. This "polluter pays" framework allows the government to compel private parties to perform or fund cleanups, and it profoundly influenced American environmental law and real-estate practice.

History and funding

The Superfund Amendments and Reauthorization Act of 1986 (SARA) strengthened the program and increased the fund. The dedicated industry taxes expired in 1995, after which cleanups were funded mainly through general appropriations, slowing the pace of work. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021 reinstated an excise tax on chemical manufacturers to replenish the fund.

Criticism

The program has been criticised for slow cleanups, high legal and transaction costs, and disputes over liability, but it has also overseen the remediation of well over a thousand sites and remains the principal federal mechanism for addressing legacy hazardous waste contamination.
