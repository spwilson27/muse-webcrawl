Load line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Load line marks on a hull

The load line, also called the Plimsoll line, is a set of marks painted on the hull of a merchant ship indicating the maximum depth to which the vessel may be legally loaded in specified conditions of water density and temperature. The best-known element is the Plimsoll mark — a circle bisected by a horizontal line — alongside a "grid" of lines for tropical fresh (TF), fresh (F), tropical (T), summer (S), winter (W) and winter North Atlantic (WNA) conditions.

History and regulation

Load lines were campaigned for by the British MP Samuel Plimsoll, whose 1873 book Our Seamen exposed the scandal of overloaded, unseaworthy "coffin ships" sent to sea for their insurance value. The Merchant Shipping Act 1876 made a load line compulsory in Britain, though shipowners were at first left to place the mark themselves; authority over positioning passed to the Board of Trade in 1890. The rules were internationalised by the International Convention on Load Lines of 1930 and its successor, the 1966 Convention administered by the International Maritime Organization. Marks are assigned by classification societies after calculation of the ship's freeboard — the distance from the waterline to the main deck — taking account of hull form, sheer, superstructures and seasonal zones shown on an official chart. A ship found floating deeper than the applicable mark is overloaded and may be detained in port. In electronics and engineering, "load line" has a separate sense: a straight line drawn on a device's characteristic curves to represent the constraint imposed by the external circuit, used to find an amplifier's operating (Q) point.
