Repurchase agreement

A repurchase agreement, commonly called a repo, is a form of short-term borrowing, mainly in government securities, in which one party sells securities to another and agrees to repurchase them at a slightly higher price on a specified future date, often the next day. The difference between the sale and repurchase price is effectively the interest on a collateralised loan. Viewed from the counterparty's side, the same transaction is a reverse repurchase agreement (reverse repo).

Mechanics

In a repo, a dealer needing cash sells Treasury or agency securities to an investor — typically a money market fund or another bank — and buys them back the following day at a price implying the repo rate. The collateral is usually worth more than the cash lent; the excess is the haircut, protecting the lender against default and price movements. Tri-party repo, in which a clearing bank handles settlement and collateral management, accounts for much of the market.

Role in the financial system

The repo market is a core part of the money market, funding dealer inventories of bonds and transmitting monetary policy: the Federal Reserve conducts open market operations through repo and reverse repo, including its overnight reverse repurchase agreement (ON RRP) facility, to control short-term interest rates. During the 2007–2008 financial crisis, a run on repo funding — as lenders raised haircuts on mortgage-related collateral — played a central role in the collapse of institutions such as Lehman Brothers.
