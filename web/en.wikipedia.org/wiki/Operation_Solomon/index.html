Operation Solomon (Hebrew: מבצע שלמה, Mivtza Shlomo) was a covert Israeli military operation in 1991 to airlift Ethiopian Jews (the Beta Israel) to Israel. In a span of 36 hours, non-stop flights of 34 aircraft transported 14,325 Ethiopian Jews to Israel, as the regime of Ethiopian dictator Mengistu Haile Mariam collapsed and rebel forces closed on Addis Ababa.

Background

Following Operation Moses (1984–85) and Operation Joshua (1985), most of the Beta Israel community remained in Ethiopia. Under pressure from the United States and fearing for the Jews' safety as the Ethiopian Civil War reached its climax, Israel negotiated a deal with the Mengistu government — reportedly including a payment of about $35 million — allowing the community to leave. When rebel forces advanced on the capital in May 1991, the evacuation was launched in haste under the code name of King Solomon, famed in Ethiopian tradition through the Queen of Sheba legend.

The airlift

Beginning on Friday 24 May, El Al and Israeli Air Force aircraft flew continuously between Tel Aviv and Addis Ababa. To maximise capacity, seats were removed from the passenger jets; one El Al Boeing 747 carried 1,088 passengers (1,090 after two babies were born in flight), setting a world record for the most passengers on a single aircraft. In total, 34 flights carried 14,325 people, among them many children and elderly carried aboard on stretchers.

Aftermath

Operation Solomon brought across the great majority of the Beta Israel community then seeking to leave. The newcomers were housed initially in caravan sites and absorption centres; their integration into Israeli society proved a long and often difficult process. Later, smaller operations airlifted members of the Falash Mura — descendants of Jews who had converted to Christianity — whose status under the Law of Return remained controversial.
