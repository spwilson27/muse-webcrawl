Metric space

An illustration of distances between points

In mathematics, a metric space is a set together with a notion of distance between its elements, usually called points. The distance is measured by a function called a metric or distance function. Metric spaces are the most general setting in which many of the basic concepts of analysis—limits, continuity, convergence—can be developed, and they underlie the more general notion of a topological space. The concept was introduced by Maurice Fréchet in his 1906 doctoral work on functional analysis.

Definition

A metric space is an ordered pair (M, d) where M is a set and d : M × M → ℝ is a function satisfying, for all x, y, z in M: (1) d(x, y) ≥ 0 (non-negativity); (2) d(x, y) = 0 if and only if x = y (identity of indiscernibles); (3) d(x, y) = d(y, x) (symmetry); and (4) d(x, z) ≤ d(x, y) + d(y, z) (the triangle inequality).

Examples

The real numbers with d(x, y) = |x − y| form a metric space, as do Euclidean spaces with the usual Euclidean distance. Other examples include the Manhattan (taxicab) metric, the discrete metric, the space of continuous functions with the supremum metric, and the Hamming distance on strings. The distance between two vertices in a graph, and the Hausdorff distance between sets, also define metrics in suitable settings.

Basic notions

Metric spaces come equipped with open balls, open and closed sets, convergent sequences, Cauchy sequences, and continuity. A metric space in which every Cauchy sequence converges is called complete; the real numbers are complete, while the rationals are not. Other important properties include compactness, connectedness, and separability. Every metric space is first-countable and Hausdorff as a topological space.
