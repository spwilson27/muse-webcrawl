The petroleum fly, Helaeomyia petrolei (formerly Psilopa petrolei), is a species of shore fly (family Ephydridae) found in California. It is famous because its larvae live in pools of crude petroleum — the only known insect, and one of very few animals, to develop in a substance so toxic to most life.

Biology

The larvae inhabit naturally occurring asphalt and oil seeps, such as the La Brea Tar Pits near Los Angeles. They swim through the oil, feeding on insects and other arthropods that become trapped in the petroleum, and breathe through spiracles held at the surface film. The larvae ingest large quantities of oil and asphalt without harm; their gut is filled with petroleum, yet they develop normally — studies show the oil itself has no toxic effect on them, though they must still obtain nutrition from their prey rather than the oil. Their gut also hosts specialised bacteria.

Adult flies can walk on the surface of the oil but avoid submerging, and mate and lay eggs at the pool margins; the wings and bodies of adults are not specially protected if fully immersed. Pupation occurs on grass stems near the pool's edge. The species exemplifies extreme adaptation to an environment that is lethal to nearly all other insects, and it has no close relatives sharing its petroleum-based habitat.
