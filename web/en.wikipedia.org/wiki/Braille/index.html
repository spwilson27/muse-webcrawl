Braille

Braille
Type: Tactile alphabet (raised dots)
Created: Louis Braille, 1824 (published 1829; revised 1837)
Cell: 6 dots in a 3×2 grid — 64 possible combinations
Used for: writing for people who are blind or visually impaired, in most world languages
[Image: Fingertips resting on a page of embossed braille dots, with the six-dot cell pattern shown enlarged beside them.]

Braille is a tactile writing system used by people who are blind or visually impaired. It represents characters with patterns of raised dots in cells of six positions, read by touch. With 64 possible combinations it covers letters, digits, punctuation and, in contracted (Grade 2) braille, common words and letter groups, as well as notation for mathematics and music.

Invention

Louis Braille (1809–1852), blinded in childhood by an accident in his father's leather workshop and sent to the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, developed the system as a teenager, adapting Charles Barbier's twelve-dot "night writing" — a military code for silent communication — into the compact six-dot cell. He published it in 1829 and refined it in 1837. The system spread slowly against institutional resistance (Braille himself was buried with honours only later; his remains were moved to the Panthéon in 1952), becoming the worldwide standard by the early 20th century.

Modern use

Braille is adapted to many languages, including non-alphabetic uses for Chinese and Japanese kana. Books are embossed or printed on refreshable braille displays; signage, elevator buttons, banknotes and medicine packaging widely carry braille. Literacy advocates stress that, despite audio technology, braille remains essential for spelling, grammar and mathematics among blind readers. See also Sign language.
