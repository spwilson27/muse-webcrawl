The Boeing B-17 Flying Fortress is an American four-engined heavy bomber aircraft developed in the 1930s for the United States Army Air Corps (USAAC). A fast and high-flying bomber, the B-17 was used primarily in the European theater of World War II, dropping more bombs than any other aircraft during the war. It is the third-most produced bomber of all time, behind the American four-engined Consolidated B-24 Liberator and the German multirole, twin-engined Junkers Ju 88.

The B-17 was designed by the Boeing Aircraft Company in response to a May 1934 Army Air Corps specification for a multi-engined bomber to replace the Martin B-10. The prototype, the Model 299, first flew on 28 July 1935; a newspaper reporter dubbed it the "Flying Fortress" because of its many machine guns, a name Boeing adopted and trademarked.

Operational history

The B-17 was primarily employed by the USAAF in the daylight strategic bombing campaign against German industrial and military targets. The United States Eighth Air Force, based at airfields in central, southern and eastern England, and the Fifteenth Air Force, based in Italy, conducted the Combined Bomber Offensive. Early unescorted raids in 1943, such as the Schweinfurt–Regensburg mission, suffered heavy losses, but the arrival of long-range escort fighters such as the P-51 Mustang transformed the campaign in 1944.

The definitive variant, the B-17G, carried thirteen .50 calibre (12.7 mm) machine guns and a crew of ten: pilot, co-pilot, navigator, bombardier/nose gunner, flight engineer/top turret gunner, radio operator, two waist gunners, ball turret gunner and tail gunner.

Legacy

Of the 12,731 B-17s built, several dozen survive, and a small number remain airworthy. Famous individual aircraft include the Memphis Belle, one of the first to complete 25 missions, and Yankee Lady. The B-17 became a symbol of American air power and of the strategic bombing campaign in Europe.
