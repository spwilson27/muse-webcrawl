The lunar maria (singular mare) are large, dark, basaltic plains on Earth's Moon, formed by lava flowing into ancient impact basins. They are less reflective than the lunar highlands because of their iron-rich composition, and so appear as the dark features of the "Man in the Moon" seen from Earth. Early astronomers, who thought they might be actual seas, called them maria (Latin for "seas"); the names—such as Mare Tranquillitatis (Sea of Tranquillity) and Oceanus Procellarum (Ocean of Storms)—were largely given by Giovanni Battista Riccioli in 1651 and remain in use.

Formation

Most maria occupy giant impact basins excavated during the Late Heavy Bombardment, about 3.9 billion years ago. Hundreds of millions of years later, basaltic lava from the lunar interior, generated by partial melting in the mantle, flooded the low-lying basins in successive flows, in places accumulating several kilometres thick. Samples returned by the Apollo and Luna missions show mare basalts are typically 3.0 to 3.5 billion years old, though some far-side and Oceanus Procellarum flows sampled by Chang'e 5 date to about 2 billion years. The maria are concentrated on the near side because the crust there is thinner, making eruption easier.

Features

Mare surfaces show relatively few craters compared with the highlands, wrinkle ridges formed as the lava cooled and subsided, sinuous rilles cut by lava channels, and domes thought to be shield volcanoes. Dark halo craters and the rays of young craters such as Tycho cut across many maria. The Apollo 11 landing in 1969 took place in Mare Tranquillitatis.
