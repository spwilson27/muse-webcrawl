Leopold Kronecker (7 December 1823 – 29 December 1891) was a German mathematician who worked on number theory, algebra, and logic. A powerful and combative figure at the University of Berlin, he is remembered equally for his theorems — in algebraic number theory, elliptic functions, and the theory of equations — and for his philosophy of mathematics, which insisted that all of mathematics be founded on the natural numbers and finite, constructible operations.

Life

Kronecker was born in Liegnitz into a prosperous Jewish family (he converted to Christianity late in life). At the University of Berlin he studied under Dirichlet and was befriended by his former schoolteacher Ernst Kummer. He took his doctorate in 1845 with a dissertation on complex units in algebraic number fields. Financially independent, he managed the family business for a decade while pursuing mathematics privately, then returned to Berlin in 1855 and, as a member of the Prussian Academy of Sciences, lectured at the university for the rest of his life, becoming a full professor in 1883.

Work

Kronecker's research united algebra and number theory. The Kronecker–Weber theorem states that every finite abelian extension of the rational numbers is contained in a cyclotomic field — a result he announced and that was completed by Weber and Hilbert. His Jugendtraum ("dream of youth") proposed an analogous explicit generation of abelian extensions of imaginary quadratic fields by elliptic functions, a programme that fed into class field theory. He developed the theory of divisors in algebraic number fields in parallel with Dedekind's ideals, worked on elliptic functions (the Kronecker limit formula; Kronecker's theorem in Diophantine approximation), and his name attaches to the Kronecker delta, the Kronecker product, and the Kronecker symbol.

Philosophy and controversy

Kronecker held that "God made the integers; all else is the work of man" — a remark reported by Heinrich Weber. He rejected completed infinities, set-theoretic constructions, and irrational numbers defined non-constructively, and he opposed the work of his Berlin colleagues Weierstrass and, most bitterly, Georg Cantor, whose set theory he attacked and whose career he is accused of obstructing. His insistence that mathematical objects must be finitely constructible makes him a forerunner of finitism and intuitionism, and although mainstream mathematics took the Cantorian path, his algorithmic outlook in algebra has been vindicated in the era of computer algebra.
