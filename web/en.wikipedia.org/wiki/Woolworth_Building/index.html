The Woolworth Building is an early American skyscraper in the Tribeca neighbourhood of Manhattan, New York City, at 233 Broadway. Designed by architect Cass Gilbert and financed in cash by retail magnate Frank Winfield Woolworth, it opened on April 24, 1913, and at 792 feet (241 m) was the tallest building in the world until 1930. Its ornate neo-Gothic terra-cotta facade earned it the nickname the "Cathedral of Commerce," and it remains one of the most celebrated skyscrapers in the United States.

History

Woolworth, founder of the F. W. Woolworth Company five-and-dime empire, commissioned the building as his company's headquarters and paid the roughly $13.5 million cost in cash. President Woodrow Wilson ceremonially turned on its lights from the White House at the opening. The Woolworth Company owned the building for 85 years, selling it in 1998. A National Historic Landmark since 1966, its lobby — a vaulted, mosaic-decorated "cathedral" with gargoyles caricaturing Woolworth and Gilbert — is a designated New York City interior landmark.

Architecture

The steel-framed tower rises in a U-shaped base to a pyramidal copper-clad crown, originally bright green-gold and now weathered green. Gilbert adapted Gothic ornament — flying buttresses, gargoyles, pinnacles — to a modern office building, cladding it in cream-coloured architectural terra cotta. The building pioneered amenities including high-speed elevators and its own power plant.

Modern use

The lower floors remain offices, while the upper portion, marketed as the Woolworth Tower Residences, was converted in the 2010s–2020s into luxury condominiums, topped by a five-level penthouse in the crown known as the Pinnacle. The building has featured in numerous films, including Fantastic Beasts and Where to Find Them (2016), as the headquarters of the Magical Congress of the United States.
