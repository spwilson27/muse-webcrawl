Mastering (audio)

A mastering studio; accurate monitoring is central to the craft.

Mastering, a form of audio post production, is the process of preparing and transferring recorded audio from a source containing the final mix to a data storage device (the master), the source from which all copies will be produced (via methods such as pressing, duplication or replication). In recent years digital masters have become the usual form, although analogue masters — such as audio tapes — are still used by the manufacturing industry, particularly by a few engineers who specialize in analogue mastering.

Process

Mastering requires critically accurate listening environments and equipment. The mastering engineer applies corrective and creative processing — typically equalization, compression, limiting, stereo enhancement, and noise reduction — balances the levels and fades between tracks, and prepares the final master to meet the technical specifications of the release format (CD, vinyl, streaming). For vinyl, additional constraints on bass content and level must be observed because of the physics of the cutting process.

History

The craft emerged with the advent of magnetic tape recording after the Second World War, when the transfer from tape to disc became a distinct, skilled operation. The introduction of the compact disc in 1982 made mastering a separate digital discipline. The "loudness war" of the 1990s and 2000s — in which masters were pushed to ever higher average levels at the cost of dynamic range — generated widespread criticism; the adoption of loudness normalization by streaming services has since reduced the incentive for extreme limiting.

Remastering is the process of making a new master for an album or song from existing recordings, usually to improve audio quality for reissue.

Notable engineers

Well-known mastering engineers include Bob Ludwig, Bernie Grundman, George "Porky" Peckham (famous for messages inscribed in vinyl run-out grooves), and Emily Lazar, the first female mastering engineer to win a Grammy Award for Best Engineered Album.
