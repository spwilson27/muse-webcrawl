Pacific Scandal

The Pacific Scandal was a political scandal in Canada in 1873 involving bribes paid by businessman Sir Hugh Allan and associates to members of the Conservative government of Prime Minister Sir John A. Macdonald in exchange for the charter to build the transcontinental Canadian Pacific Railway. The scandal forced Macdonald's resignation in November 1873 and remains one of the most famous political scandals in Canadian history.

Background

When British Columbia joined Confederation in 1871, one of the terms was the construction of a railway linking it to eastern Canada within ten years. Two rival syndicates competed for the charter: Allan's Canada Pacific Railway Company, backed in part by American capital, and the Interoceanic Railway Company associated with Toronto interests. During the 1872 election campaign, Allan and his associates contributed very large sums — around $350,000 — to the Conservative Party's campaign funds.

The scandal breaks

In April 1873, Liberal member of Parliament Lucius Seth Huntington rose in the House of Commons and charged that the charter had been sold to Allan's group in return for the campaign contributions. Telegrams between Allan, his lawyer, and government ministers — including one from Macdonald reading "I must have another ten thousand; will be the last time of calling; do not fail me" — were published and appeared to confirm a corrupt bargain.

Consequences

A royal commission heard evidence through the summer of 1873. Macdonald defended himself in a marathon speech in the Commons in November, but with his majority crumbling he resigned on 5 November 1873. Liberal leader Alexander Mackenzie formed a government and won the ensuing 1874 election. The railway project was delayed for years before Macdonald, returned to power in 1878, completed it under a new syndicate in 1885.
