Treponema pallidum is a slender, spiral-shaped (spirochaete) bacterium, 6–20 μm long and only about 0.15 μm thick — too thin to be seen by ordinary light microscopy, hence the name pallidum, "pale". Its subspecies cause the human treponemal diseases: subsp. pallidum causes syphilis, a sexually transmitted infection; subsp. pertenue causes yaws; subsp. endemicum causes bejel (endemic syphilis); and subsp. carateum causes pinta.

Biology

The organism has a characteristically small genome (about 1.14 million base pairs) and lacks many metabolic pathways, making it an obligate human parasite dependent on its host. It moves by rotating its corkscrew body, driven by endoflagella in the periplasmic space, which lets it burrow through viscous tissue and between cells. Its fragile outer membrane contains few exposed proteins — a "stealth" strategy that complicates vaccine development — and it cannot synthesise its own fatty acids. For most of its history it could not be cultured continuously in vitro; a long-term culture system in co-culture with epithelial cells was reported only in 2018.

History

The bacterium was first identified in syphilitic lesions in 1905 by Fritz Schaudinn and Erich Hoffmann (as Spirochaeta pallida). Syphilis caused by subsp. pallidum remains a major global infection, and it is highly sensitive to penicillin, which has been the treatment of choice since the 1940s and to which no resistance has emerged.
