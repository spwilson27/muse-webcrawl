Second Battle of Champagne

Date25 September – 6 November 1915
LocationChampagne, France
BelligerentsFrance vs. the German Empire
ResultGerman defensive success

The Second Battle of Champagne (French: Deuxième bataille de Champagne) was a French offensive on the Western Front of the First World War, fought from 25 September to 6 November 1915. It formed the main French effort of the autumn 1915 Allied offensive, coordinated with the Third Battle of Artois and the British attack at Loos. General Joffre hoped that simultaneous blows in Champagne and Artois would rupture the German front and force a war of movement before winter.

The offensive

The attack in Champagne was made by the French Fourth Army (General de Langle de Cary) and the newly formed Second Army (General Pétain), with a total of about 35 divisions, following the longest and heaviest artillery preparation the French had yet mounted — some three days of bombardment by more than 2,500 guns. On 25 September, advancing in heavy rain over the open chalk ground east of Reims, French troops overran the German first position along a 24-kilometre front and took thousands of prisoners.

The advance then stalled before the German second position, sited on reverse slopes beyond the effective observation of French artillery, which the bombardment had not destroyed. Repeated attacks in late September and October, including the costly fighting around the Tahure salient and the Butte de Tahure, gained only fragments of ground. Joffre suspended the offensive in early November.

Outcome

The French gained a belt of territory up to about 3–4 kilometres deep but failed to break through. French casualties were roughly 145,000 killed, wounded, and missing; German losses were about 85,000, plus some 25,000 prisoners. The battle confirmed the defensive lessons of 1915: a well-prepared second position, protected from observed artillery fire, could absorb the heaviest bombardment then possible. Among the participants was the young German officer Erwin Rommel, and among the dead the French writer Alain-Fournier had already fallen in the region in 1914; the Champagne battles of 1915 left a devastated, cratered landscape parts of which remain military land today.
