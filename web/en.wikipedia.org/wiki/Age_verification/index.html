Age verification

Age verification is the process of confirming that an individual meets a minimum age requirement to access a product, service, or content. It is used for age-restricted goods such as alcohol, tobacco, and gambling, and increasingly for online services, social media, and adult content. Methods range from visual inspection of identity documents to digital systems using credit cards, mobile operator data, facial age estimation, and government-issued digital identity.

Offline verification

Sellers of alcohol and tobacco in many jurisdictions are legally required to check photographic identification—such as a driver's licence or passport—when a customer appears under a threshold age, under schemes such as "Challenge 25" in the United Kingdom. Fake IDs and inconsistent enforcement remain persistent challenges.

Online verification

Online, age checks historically relied on self-declaration (entering a birthdate or ticking a box), criticised as ineffective. Stronger approaches include document scanning with liveness checks, credit-card verification, third-party age-verification providers, and AI-based facial age estimation. The U.S. Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA, 1998) requires verifiable parental consent for collecting data from children under 13, shaping platform age gates.

From the 2020s, several jurisdictions mandated stricter online age verification. The United Kingdom's Online Safety Act 2023 requires services hosting pornographic or other harmful content to use "highly effective" age assurance, enforced by Ofcom from 2025. Australia legislated a minimum age of 16 for social media accounts in 2024. Numerous U.S. states enacted age-verification laws for adult websites, upheld by the Supreme Court in Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton (2025). The European Union has promoted age verification through digital identity wallets under eIDAS 2.0.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that mandatory verification threatens privacy and anonymity, creates honeypots of sensitive identity data, risks excluding adults without documents, and can be circumvented with VPNs. Supporters contend it is necessary to protect children from harmful content. Privacy-preserving approaches such as zero-knowledge proofs and double-blind token systems have been proposed to reconcile the goals.
