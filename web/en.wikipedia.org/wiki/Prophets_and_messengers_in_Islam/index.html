Prophets and messengers in Islam

Prophets and messengers in Islam (Arabic: ٱلْأَنْبِيَاء‎, al-anbiyāʾ) are individuals chosen by God (Allah) to convey His revelation to humankind. Belief in the prophets is one of the six articles of faith in Islam. Muslims believe that God sent messengers to every nation; the Qur'an states that many prophets are not named in it, but twenty-five are mentioned by name. Muhammad is held to be the last of them, the "Seal of the Prophets," after whom no further prophet will come.

Prophets and messengers

Islamic tradition distinguishes between a prophet (nabī), who receives revelation and preaches the law of a previous messenger, and a messenger (rasūl), who is commissioned with a new scripture or law — though scholars differ on the precise distinction. Five prophets are given special rank as ulū al-ʿazm, "those of determination": Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad.

Named prophets

The twenty-five prophets named in the Qur'an include Adam, Enoch (Idris), Noah (Nuh), Hud, Saleh, Abraham (Ibrahim), Lot (Lut), Ishmael (Isma'il), Isaac (Ishaq), Jacob (Ya'qub), Joseph (Yusuf), Job (Ayyub), Shu'ayb, Moses (Musa), Aaron (Harun), David (Dawud), Solomon (Sulayman), Elijah (Ilyas), Elisha (Al-Yasa), Jonah (Yunus), Ezekiel (Dhu al-Kifl), Zechariah (Zakariya), John the Baptist (Yahya), Jesus (Isa), and Muhammad.

Characteristics

Prophets are regarded as the best of humanity: models of conduct, protected (in mainstream doctrine) from major sin, and in particular from any error in conveying the revelation. Stories of the prophets (qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ) form a major genre of Islamic literature, expanding Qur'anic accounts with material from hadith and earlier traditions. Because the prophets are revered, visual depiction of them — above all of Muhammad — is avoided in most Islamic practice.
