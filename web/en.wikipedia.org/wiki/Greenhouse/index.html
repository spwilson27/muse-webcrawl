Greenhouse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A greenhouse is a structure with walls and a roof made chiefly of transparent material, such as glass, in which plants requiring regulated climatic conditions are grown. These structures come in many sizes, from small sheds to industrial complexes such as the Dutch glasshouse districts and Almería's "sea of plastic" in Spain.

Sunlight enters the glazing and warms the interior; the glass impedes the escape of warmed air and (to a lesser extent) re-radiates heat, keeping the inside warmer than the outside environment. This allows cultivation of tender or out-of-season plants and extends growing seasons in cold climates.

History

The Romans grew cucumbers for the Emperor Tiberius in wheeled carts covered with translucent stone or mica. Large glasshouses flourished in the 17th–19th centuries for botanical collections and citrus "orangeries"; Victorian structures such as Kew Gardens' Palm House and Temperate House became landmarks. Joseph Paxton's Crystal Palace (1851) demonstrated greenhouse construction at monumental scale.

Uses

Commercial greenhouses produce vegetables (tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers), flowers, and bedding plants year-round, increasingly with hydroponics, supplemental lighting, and climate computers. Botanical gardens use them to maintain tropical collections, and scientists use phytotrons and growth chambers for experiments.
