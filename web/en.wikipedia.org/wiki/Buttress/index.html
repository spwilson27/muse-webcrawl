Buttress

A buttress is an architectural structure built against or projecting from a wall to support or reinforce it, resisting the lateral forces that push a wall outwards. Buttresses are common in the masonry of churches, cathedrals, castles, and retaining walls. By transferring thrust from the wall to the ground away from the structure, they allowed medieval builders to raise taller walls pierced by larger windows.

Types

  • Clasping (or clenched) buttress — wraps around a corner, buttressing two meeting walls.
  • Setback buttress — set back from the corner, attached to each wall separately.
  • Angle buttress — two buttresses meeting at right angles at a corner.
  • Diagonal buttress — set at the corner of two walls, at 45 degrees.
  • Flying buttress — a half-arch ("flyer") of masonry that leaps from a detached pier to the wall, carrying the thrust of a high vault over an aisle roof; the signature structural device of Gothic architecture, often crowned with a decorative pinnacle whose weight helps stabilise the pier.

History

Buttresses appear in ancient Mesopotamian and Egyptian construction and were used in Romanesque churches as plain, wall-hugging masses of masonry. In Gothic architecture from the 12th century onward — notably at the nave of Notre-Dame de Paris and the great cathedrals of Chartres, Amiens, and Cologne — the flying buttress became both a structural necessity and an expressive design element, enabling the skeletal stone framework and vast stained-glass windows of the style. Buttresses are also used in secular engineering, for example on dams and retaining walls, where they resist the lateral pressure of water and earth.

Figurative use

In figurative language, to "buttress" an argument or institution is to support or reinforce it, and a "buttress" may denote a person or thing that provides support.
