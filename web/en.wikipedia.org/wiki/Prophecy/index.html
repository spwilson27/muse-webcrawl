Prophecy

Prophecy is a message that is claimed by a prophet to have been communicated to them by a deity or supernatural being. Such messages typically involve inspiration, interpretation, or revelation of divine will concerning the prophet's social world and events to come. Prophecy is a feature of many religions and cultures throughout history, from the oracles of ancient Greece and the prophets of ancient Israel to medieval visionaries and modern charismatic movements.

Definitions and forms

Prophecy is not always concerned with predicting the future; in the Hebrew Bible the prophet (navi) is primarily a spokesperson for God, delivering messages that may denounce injustice, call for repentance, interpret current events, or promise future deliverance. Predictive prophecy — of the coming of a messiah, the end of the age, or specific future events — is prominent in Jewish, Christian and Islamic eschatology. Related practices include divination (seeking hidden knowledge through ritual means, lots, entrails or astrology) and oracles, such as the Pythia at Delphi, through whom the god Apollo was believed to speak.

Prophecy in the Abrahamic religions

In Judaism, the books of the Nevi'im preserve the oracles of prophets such as Isaiah, Jeremiah and Ezekiel. In Christianity, prophecy is listed among the gifts of the Holy Spirit, and the Book of Revelation is presented as a prophecy of the last things. Islam regards Muhammad as the final prophet ("Seal of the Prophets"), preceded by a line including Abraham, Moses and Jesus. New religious movements — including the Millerites and their successors, and the Latter Day Saint movement founded on Joseph Smith's revelations — have repeatedly centred on prophetic claims, and failed predictions (such as the "Great Disappointment" of 1844) are studied as classic cases of cognitive dissonance.

Famous prophecies and prophets

  • The Delphic oracle's pronouncements in Greek tradition
  • The prophecies of Nostradamus (Michel de Nostredame, 1555)
  • Messianic prophecies in the Hebrew Bible
  • Eschatological texts such as the Book of Daniel and Revelation
  • Modern claims, from Fatima to contemporary Pentecostal prophecy

Sceptical analysis

Sceptics note that prophecies are typically vague, symbolic and open to retrospective fitting ("postdiction"); that hits are remembered and misses forgotten (selection bias); and that some "fulfilled" prophecies were written after the events they describe (vaticinium ex eventu). Self-fulfilling prophecies can also bring about the outcomes they predict through ordinary social mechanisms. Psychologists and historians therefore usually explain prophecy in naturalistic terms, while adherents regard it as evidence of divine communication.
