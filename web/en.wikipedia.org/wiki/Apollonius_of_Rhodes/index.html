Apollonius of Rhodes (Ancient Greek: Ἀπολλώνιος Ῥόδιος; Latin: Apollonius Rhodius) was an ancient Greek author, best known for the Argonautica, an epic poem about Jason and the Argonauts' quest for the Golden Fleece. He was a scholar of the Library of Alexandria and succeeded Zenodotus as its head, serving in the mid-3rd century BC.

Life

Ancient biographies of Apollonius are contradictory. He was born in Alexandria (some sources say Naucratis) and studied under the poet Callimachus. A famous anecdote recounts that an early recitation of the Argonautica in Alexandria was poorly received, prompting Apollonius to withdraw to Rhodes — hence his epithet "the Rhodian" — where he revised the poem to acclaim. He later returned to Alexandria and became chief librarian, a predecessor or successor in that role to Eratosthenes depending on the source. A literary quarrel with Callimachus, champion of short, polished poems over long epics, is suggested by Callimachus's poem Ibis and other allusions, though its details are debated.

The Argonautica

The Argonautica, in four books of Homeric hexameters, retells the voyage of the ship Argo to Colchis and back. Book 3, centred on Medea's falling in love with Jason and aiding his trials through magic, is celebrated for its psychological portrait of love — a striking departure from Homer — and profoundly influenced Virgil's depiction of Dido in the Aeneid. Apollonius combined heroic narrative with the geography, aetiology, and erudition characteristic of Hellenistic poetry.

Other works

Apollonius also wrote foundation poems (ktiseis) for cities such as Alexandria, Rhodes, and Cyrene, epigrams, and a scholarly work against Zenodotus's edition of Homer; these survive only in fragments.
