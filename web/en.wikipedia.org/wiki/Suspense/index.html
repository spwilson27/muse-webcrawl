Suspense

Suspense is a state of anxiety or excitement caused by mysteriousness, uncertainty, doubt, or undecidedness. In literature, film, and drama, suspense is the pleasurable feeling of anticipation and anxious uncertainty about what will happen next, produced when the audience knows more — or at crucial moments less — than the characters. Alfred Hitchcock, the "Master of Suspense," famously distinguished suspense from surprise: a bomb suddenly exploding gives fifteen seconds of surprise, while showing the audience the bomb ticking under the table gives fifteen minutes of suspense.

Suspense arises from anticipation and uncertainty about a threatened outcome.

Mechanics

Suspense is typically built from a sympathetic character facing a threat, a deadline or ticking clock, withheld information, and delays that postpone the resolution. Dramatic irony — the audience's awareness of danger unknown to the characters — intensifies the effect. Aristotle's Poetics already identified fear and pity for a character as engines of tragic tension. Modern theories, such as Noël Carroll's, analyze suspense as arising from questions with morally weighted outcomes that seem roughly balanced between hope and dread.

In film and literature

Hitchcock's films — Psycho, Rear Window, Vertigo — codified cinematic suspense techniques: point-of-view editing, the MacGuffin, and sequences that stretch time. In literature, suspense drives the thriller and mystery genres, from Wilkie Collins's The Woman in White to the novels of Patricia Highsmith and Gillian Flynn. Serialized fiction and television exploit suspense through cliffhangers, deferring resolution between installments.
