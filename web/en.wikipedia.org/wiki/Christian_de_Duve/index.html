Christian de Duve


Placeholder image: Christian de Duve

Christian René Marie Joseph, Viscount de Duve (2 October 1917 – 4 May 2013) was a Belgian cytologist and biochemist who shared the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Albert Claude and George E. Palade "for their discoveries concerning the structural and functional organization of the cell". He discovered two cell organelles, the lysosome and the peroxisome, and coined both names.

Life and career

De Duve was born in Thames Ditton, England, to Belgian refugee parents, and educated at the Catholic University of Leuven, where he took his medical degree in 1941 and a doctorate in 1945. He worked on the mechanism of insulin action before turning to cell fractionation — the technique, pioneered by Albert Claude, of separating cell components by differential centrifugation and characterising them biochemically.

In the early 1950s his group noticed that acid phosphatase activity in rat liver fractions increased after storage or membrane damage, leading them to postulate a new organelle bounded by a membrane and filled with digestive enzymes, which de Duve named the lysosome (1955), the cell's "suicide bag" and digestive system. A decade later his laboratory characterised a second single-membrane organelle rich in oxidases, the peroxisome. He held chairs at Leuven and at Rockefeller University in New York, where he worked from 1962, and in 1974 founded the International Institute of Cellular and Molecular Pathology (now the de Duve Institute) in Brussels.

Later work

In retirement de Duve wrote widely on the origin and evolution of life, including A Guided Tour of the Living Cell (1984), Blueprint for a Cell (1991) and Vital Dust (1995), proposing that peroxisome-like and thioester chemistry played a role in early evolution. He died in 2013 at Grez-Doiceau, Belgium, aged 95, by euthanasia, which was legal in Belgium and which he had publicly supported.
