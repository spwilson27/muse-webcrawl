Plantations of Ireland

The plantations of Ireland were a series of colonisation schemes in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries in which land confiscated from rebellious Irish landowners was granted to settlers ("planters") from England and Scotland. Organised by the English Crown to extend royal control, reward loyal subjects, and spread Protestantism, the plantations reshaped Irish landownership, demography, and politics, and their legacy remains central to the history of Ireland and to the origins of the Northern Ireland conflict.

Early plantations

The first schemes under Mary I settled King's County and Queen's County (modern Offaly and Laois) in the 1550s, followed under Elizabeth I by the Plantation of Munster after the Desmond Rebellions of the 1580s, which granted some 300,000 acres to English undertakers. Munster's plantation was devastated in the Nine Years' War, but the defeat of the Ulster earls and their departure in the Flight of the Earls (1607) opened the way to the largest scheme of all.

Plantation of Ulster

From 1609, six counties of Ulster — Armagh, Cavan, Coleraine (Londonderry), Donegal, Fermanagh, and Tyrone — were systematically settled with tens of thousands of English and especially Scottish Presbyterian tenants, alongside walled towns such as Derry and Enniskillen. Native Irish were to be removed to designated areas, though many remained as labourers. Further settlements followed in the seventeenth century, and confiscations after the Irish Confederate Wars and the Cromwellian conquest transferred the great majority of Irish land to Protestant hands by 1700.

Legacy

The plantations created a durable divide between a largely Protestant, unionist settler-descended population concentrated in Ulster and the Catholic Irish majority, whose dispossession fuelled grievances expressed in rebellions in 1641, 1798, and beyond. The Ulster plantation in particular underlies the demographic and political geography that led to the partition of Ireland in 1921.
