Trinucleotide repeat disorder

Key featureAnticipation — earlier onset in successive generations

Trinucleotide repeat disorders, also known as triplet repeat expansion diseases, are a group of inherited genetic disorders caused by abnormal expansion of sequences in which a set of three nucleotides (for example CAG, CGG, or GAA) is repeated many times in tandem within or near a gene. When the repeat number exceeds a threshold, the gene malfunctions or its product becomes toxic, producing disease. Because the repeat tract is unstable, it tends to grow longer when passed from parent to child, causing the clinical phenomenon of anticipation: symptoms appear earlier and often more severely in successive generations.

Mechanism

Expansion is thought to occur chiefly through slipped-strand mispairing during DNA replication and through errors in DNA repair: the repetitive strand can fold into hairpin structures that the replication machinery miscounts, adding extra repeat units. Disorders fall into broad mechanistic classes. In the polyglutamine (polyQ) diseases, an expanded CAG repeat within a coding region produces a protein with an abnormally long glutamine tract that misfolds and aggregates — examples include Huntington's disease and several spinocerebellar ataxias. In other disorders the repeat lies outside the coding region: the CGG expansion in the 5′ untranslated region of FMR1 in fragile X syndrome silences the gene by methylation; the CTG expansion in myotonic dystrophy type 1 produces toxic RNA; and the intronic GAA expansion in Friedreich's ataxia suppresses frataxin expression.

Examples

  • Huntington's disease — CAG repeat in HTT; full disease typically above ~39 repeats; autosomal dominant.
  • Fragile X syndrome — CGG repeat in FMR1; full mutation above ~200 repeats; the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability.
  • Myotonic dystrophy — CTG (type 1) expansion, sometimes to thousands of repeats, with marked anticipation.
  • Friedreich's ataxia — GAA repeat in FXN; autosomal recessive.
  • Spinocerebellar ataxias — several types caused by CAG expansions in different genes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by genetic testing that sizes the repeat tract (PCR-based assays, with Southern blotting or long-read sequencing for very large expansions). Premutation carriers — people with repeat lengths below the disease threshold — may themselves have milder conditions (such as fragile X–associated tremor/ataxia syndrome) and a risk of expansion in their children. No cure exists for most of these disorders; management is symptomatic and supportive, though gene-silencing approaches (antisense oligonucleotides, RNA interference) targeting the mutant transcript are an active area of clinical research.
