Charles Franklin Kettering (August 29, 1876 – November 25, 1958), sometimes known as Charles "Boss" Kettering, was an American inventor, engineer, businessman, and holder of 186 patents. He was a founder of Delco, introduced the first successful electric self-starter for automobiles, and from 1920 to 1947 headed research at General Motors, where his laboratory developed leaded petrol, the refrigerant Freon, improved diesel engines and many other innovations.

Charles F. Kettering (1876–1958)

Early life

Kettering was born in Loudonville, Ohio, the fourth of five children in a farming family. Poor eyesight hampered his schooling, but he eventually graduated from Ohio State University in 1904 with a degree in electrical engineering. He first worked for the National Cash Register Company (NCR) in Dayton, where he invented an electric cash register and an accounting machine.

Delco and the self-starter

In 1909 Kettering and Edward A. Deeds founded the Dayton Engineering Laboratories Company (Delco). There Kettering's team developed the electric self-starter, first fitted to the 1912 Cadillac, which eliminated the dangerous hand crank and greatly widened the appeal of the motor car. Delco also produced complete ignition, lighting and starting systems. In 1916 Delco was sold to General Motors.

General Motors research

From 1920 until his retirement in 1947, Kettering directed GM's research laboratories. His work there included tetraethyllead as an anti-knock additive (marketed as Ethyl petrol), the refrigerant Freon with Thomas Midgley Jr., high-compression engines, fast-drying automotive lacquers and the lightweight two-stroke diesel engines that powered locomotives and buses. Some of these products, notably leaded petrol and Freon, were later recognised as environmentally harmful and phased out.

Later life and legacy

Kettering was a co-founder of the Sloan-Kettering Institute for cancer research and of what became Kettering University. He received the Franklin Medal and numerous honorary degrees, and appeared on the cover of Time magazine. He died in Dayton in 1958. His home, Ridgeleigh Terrace, was among the first houses in the United States to have electric air conditioning.
