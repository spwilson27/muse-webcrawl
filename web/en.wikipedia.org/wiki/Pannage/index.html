Pannage

Pannage
DefinitionRight or practice of grazing pigs in woodland
SeasonAutumn (about 60 days, from late September)
Surviving practiceNew Forest, England

Pannage is the practice of releasing livestock — chiefly domestic pigs — into a woodland so that they can feed on fallen mast: acorns, beech mast, chestnuts, and other tree nuts. Historically it was a valuable common right in medieval England, recorded in Domesday Book, for which commoners paid a fee per pig and from which the lord of the manor profited by the fattening of the pigs before slaughter at Martinmas.

History

Pannage rights appear in charters from Anglo-Saxon times and were carefully regulated by forest law under the Normans. The practice declined with the enclosure of woodlands and the fall in oak mast grazing, although acorn-fed pig husbandry survives famously in Spain's dehesa system producing jamón ibérico de bellota.

New Forest pannage

In the New Forest of Hampshire, pannage remains a living common right: each autumn, for a season of at least 60 days set by the Forestry Commission and the Verderers, several hundred pigs are turned out to eat green acorns, which are poisonous to the New Forest ponies and cattle that graze there year-round. The pigs wear nose rings in some cases to limit rooting damage.
