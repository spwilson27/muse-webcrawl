The Battle of the Mediterranean was the naval campaign fought in the Mediterranean Sea during the Second World War, from Italy's declaration of war on 10 June 1940 until the end of the war in Europe. The principal combatants were the Italian Regia Marina and the British Royal Navy, later joined by German air and naval forces and, from late 1942, by the United States Navy. Control of the sea was essential to the land campaigns in North Africa, since both sides' armies in Egypt, Libya and Tunisia depended on seaborne supply.

Course of the campaign

Britain held the two ends of the sea — Gibraltar and the Suez Canal — and the island fortress of Malta in the middle. Italy sought to sever this route and supply its army in Libya. Early British successes included the carrier strike on the Italian fleet at Taranto (November 1940) and the night action at Cape Matapan (March 1941). German intervention from 1941 — U-boats and the Luftwaffe — sharply raised British losses, sinking the carrier Ark Royal and the battleship Barham, while Italian frogmen disabled two battleships at Alexandria. Malta was besieged and repeatedly bombed, and its supply became the object of desperate convoy operations, notably Operation Pedestal in August 1942.

Turning point

The Allied landings in French North Africa (Operation Torch, November 1942), the victory at El Alamein and the eventual surrender of Axis forces in Tunisia in May 1943 gave the Allies command of the sea. The Mediterranean route to Suez reopened, shortening the voyage to India and the Far East. The campaign continued with the invasions of Sicily and Italy in 1943, the Italian armistice of September 1943, and operations in the Aegean and Adriatic until 1945.

Significance

The campaign demonstrated the vulnerability of surface fleets to land-based air power and the decisive importance of logistics and signals intelligence (Ultra) in a maritime theatre fought around a handful of chokepoints and island bases.
