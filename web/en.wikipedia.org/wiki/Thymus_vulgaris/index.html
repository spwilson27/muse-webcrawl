Thymus vulgaris

Thymus vulgaris
Common name: Common thyme, garden thyme
Family: Lamiaceae
Genus: Thymus
Native range: Southern Europe, Mediterranean to Iran
Binomial name: Thymus vulgaris L.

Thymus vulgaris, commonly called common thyme or garden thyme, is a species of flowering plant in the mint family Lamiaceae, native to southern Europe from the western Mediterranean eastwards to southern Italy and widely naturalised elsewhere. It is the thyme most commonly grown for culinary use. A bushy, woody-based evergreen subshrub, it grows 15–30 cm tall with small, highly aromatic, grey-green leaves and dense clusters of tiny pink-purple or white flowers in summer.

Cultivation

Thyme thrives in well-drained, even poor and rocky soils and full sun, and is drought-tolerant once established. It is propagated from seed, cuttings, or division, and is hardy in temperate gardens (roughly USDA zones 5–9). Leaves are harvested as needed; flavour is strongest just before flowering. Numerous cultivars exist, including lemon-scented and variegated forms.

Uses

Thyme is a fundamental culinary herb across Mediterranean and European cuisines: it is a component of the French bouquet garni and herbes de Provence, of the Middle Eastern spice blend za'atar (in part), and of the Levantine and Egyptian blend dukkah. It is used fresh or dried in soups, stews, roasts, and sauces. Steam distillation of the flowering tops yields thyme oil, rich in thymol, used as an antiseptic, in mouthwashes, and as a flavouring. The plant is also an important nectar source for bees and is a larval food plant for some Lepidoptera.

History

Thyme was used by the ancient Egyptians in embalming, by the Greeks — who associated it with courage and burned it as incense — and by the Romans, who spread its cultivation through Europe and used it to flavour cheese and liqueurs. In the Middle Ages it was placed beneath pillows to aid sleep and ward off nightmares, and sprigs of thyme were traditionally included in funeral bouquets.
