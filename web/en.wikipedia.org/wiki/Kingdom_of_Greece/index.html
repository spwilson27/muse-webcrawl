The Kingdom of Greece (Greek: Βασίλειον τῆς Ἑλλάδος) was the Greek state established in 1832 by the great powers after the Greek War of Independence, governed as a monarchy under dynasties of Bavarian and then Danish (Glücksburg) origin. It lasted, with interruptions — the Second Hellenic Republic (1924–1935) and the military junta (1967–1974) — until the monarchy was abolished in 1973 and the decision confirmed by referendum in 1974, inaugurating the Third Hellenic Republic.

History

Otto and the Bavarian monarchy

The London Protocol of 1830 recognised Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire; the Convention of Constantinople (1832) established Greece as a kingdom under the Bavarian prince Otto, who arrived in 1833 as king with a Bavarian regency and army. Athens became the capital in 1834. Bavarian authoritarianism and taxation provoked the Revolution of 3 September 1843, which forced Otto to grant a constitution. Continued misgovernment and diplomatic isolation (notably after the Don Pacifico affair and the Crimean War occupation of Piraeus) led to Otto's deposition and expulsion in 1862.

The Glücksburg dynasty and expansion

In 1863 the powers installed the Danish prince George I, whose long reign (1863–1913) saw the cession of the Ionian Islands by Britain (1864) and the annexation of Thessaly (1881), a new constitution (1864) making Greece a "crowned democracy", and growing nationalist ambition — the Megali Idea of uniting all Greek lands. Defeat in the Greco-Turkish War of 1897 imposed international financial control. Under Prime Minister Eleftherios Venizelos after 1910, Greece modernised and, in the Balkan Wars (1912–13), nearly doubled its territory, gaining Macedonia, Epirus, Crete and the Aegean islands; George I was assassinated in Thessaloniki in 1913.

National Schism, catastrophe and republic

The First World War split Greece between Venizelos and King Constantine I (the National Schism). The subsequent Greek occupation of Smyrna ended in the Asia Minor Catastrophe of 1922, the burning of Smyrna and the compulsory Greco-Turkish population exchange of 1923, which brought some 1.2 million refugees into Greece. The monarchy was abolished in 1924 and a republic proclaimed, but instability, the Metaxas dictatorship (1936), and the restoration of George II by a contested plebiscite (1935) preceded the Second World War.

War, civil war and the end of the monarchy

Greece repelled Mussolini's invasion in 1940–41 before German occupation, famine and resistance; liberation in 1944 was followed by civil war (1946–1949) between the government of Paul I and communist insurgents. In the postwar decades the crown remained politically divisive: Constantine II's clash with Prime Minister Georgios Papandreou in 1965 (the Apostasia) destabilised politics, and after the colonels' coup of 1967 the king's failed counter-coup forced him into exile. The junta abolished the monarchy in 1973; after democracy was restored, the referendum of 8 December 1974 voted by roughly two to one to confirm the republic, and Constantine accepted the result.
