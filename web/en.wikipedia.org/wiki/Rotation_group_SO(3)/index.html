Rotation group SO(3)

In mechanics and geometry, the rotation group SO(3), also known as the special orthogonal group in three dimensions, is the group of all rotations about the origin of three-dimensional Euclidean space R3 under the operation of composition. By definition, a rotation about the origin is a transformation that preserves the origin, all distances (an isometry), and orientation (as opposed to a reflection).

Every rotation can be specified by an axis through the origin and an angle — the content of Euler's rotation theorem. The set of such rotations forms a non-abelian (non-commutative) group: performing two rotations in different orders generally gives different results. As matrices, the elements of SO(3) are exactly the 3 × 3 orthogonal matrices with determinant +1; the group is a compact, connected Lie group of dimension 3.

Topology

SO(3) is diffeomorphic to the real projective space RP3: a rotation may be identified with a point inside a ball of radius π whose antipodal boundary points are identified, the point's direction giving the axis and its length the angle. Consequently SO(3) is not simply connected — its fundamental group is the two-element group Z/2. Intuitively, a 360° rotation cannot be continuously shrunk to the identity while a 720° rotation can, a fact demonstrated by the Dirac belt trick (or plate trick).

Lie algebra and covering group

The Lie algebra of SO(3), called so(3), consists of the 3 × 3 skew-symmetric matrices; equivalently it can be identified with R3 equipped with the cross product, the generators corresponding to infinitesimal rotations about the three axes. The exponential map from so(3) to SO(3) is given by Rodrigues' rotation formula.

SO(3) has a simply connected double cover, the group SU(2), which can be realised as the unit quaternions. This is why unit quaternions — with each rotation represented twice, by q and −q — are used so widely to parametrise rotations in computer graphics, robotics, and quantum mechanics, where the rotation group governs the behaviour of spin.

Parametrisations

Common coordinate systems on SO(3) include the three Euler angles, the axis-angle representation, rotation matrices, and unit quaternions. Each has trade-offs: Euler angles are intuitive but suffer from gimbal lock, while quaternions are compact, numerically stable, and interpolate smoothly.
