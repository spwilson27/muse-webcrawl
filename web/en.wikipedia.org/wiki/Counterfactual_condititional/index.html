Counterfactual conditionals

[Image placeholder: counterfactual conditional structure]

Counterfactual conditionals (also called subjunctive conditionals or counterfactuals) are conditional statements whose antecedent is supposed — usually known or assumed — to be false, as in "If Bizet and Verdi had been compatriots, Bizet would have been Italian." Unlike indicative conditionals ("If Bizet and Verdi were compatriots, Bizet was Italian"), counterfactuals ask what would follow in the nearest possible circumstances in which the antecedent holds.

Counterfactuals resist analysis as material implication, since a conditional with a false antecedent is trivially true in classical logic regardless of its consequent. Nelson Goodman posed the "counterfactual problem": specifying which generalisations and background facts may legitimately be carried into the hypothetical situation. David Lewis and Robert Stalnaker answered with similarity-based semantics over possible worlds, while other theorists defend epistemic, probabilistic or inferential accounts.

In English, counterfactuals are typically marked by past-tense morphology used non-temporally ("fake past") and by modal auxiliaries — "If I had known, I would have come" — and many languages have dedicated subjunctive or conditional moods for the same purpose. Experimental work in linguistics and cognitive science shows that counterfactual reasoning develops in childhood and is intertwined with reasoning about causes and regret.
