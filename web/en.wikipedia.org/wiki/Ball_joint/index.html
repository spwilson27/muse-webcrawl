Ball joint

A ball joint is a spherical bearing that connects two components while allowing pivoting movement in multiple directions. In vehicles it forms the pivot between the wheels and the suspension, connecting steering knuckles to control arms; the same principle appears in machinery, robotics, and prosthetics.

Construction

A typical automotive ball joint consists of a steel ball stud whose spherical end sits in a socket (housing) lined with a polymer or sintered bearing, retained by a preload spring or Belleville washer and sealed by a rubber dust boot filled with grease. Older designs were serviceable with grease fittings; most modern units are sealed "lubed for life". The stud's tapered shaft bolts to the steering knuckle.

Function

Ball joints carry load and allow the suspension to move vertically while the wheels steer. In double-wishbone suspension, upper and lower joints define the steering axis; in MacPherson strut systems, a single lower joint does so. Depending on geometry, a joint may be load-carrying (supporting the vehicle's spring force) or a follower joint that merely locates the wheel.

Wear and failure

Wear produces looseness, clunking over bumps, uneven tyre wear, and vague steering. A joint that separates can cause the wheel to collapse outward and loss of control, so worn joints are a safety-critical inspection item. Replacement typically requires pressing the old unit out of the control arm or replacing the arm assembly, followed by wheel alignment.
