I²C

I²C (Inter-Integrated Circuit), pronounced "eye-squared-see", is a synchronous, multi-controller/multi-target, single-ended serial communication bus invented in 1982 by Philips Semiconductors (now NXP Semiconductors). It is widely used for attaching lower-speed peripheral integrated circuits to processors and microcontrollers over short distances.

Design

I²C uses only two bidirectional open-collector lines, Serial Data Line (SDA) and Serial Clock Line (SCL), pulled up to a supply voltage with resistors. Each device on the bus has a 7-bit (or, in an extension, 10-bit) address. Standard-mode transfers run at up to 100 kbit/s, Fast-mode at 400 kbit/s, and later revisions reach 1, 3.4, and 5 Mbit/s.

Applications

The protocol is found in EEPROMs, temperature sensors, real-time clocks, display drivers, and power-management chips. Related buses derived from it include SMBus and the PMBus. Its simplicity and two-wire economy have made it a fixture of embedded systems design for over four decades.
