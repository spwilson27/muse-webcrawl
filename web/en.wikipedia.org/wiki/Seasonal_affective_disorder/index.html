Seasonal affective disorder

Seasonal affective disorder
SpecialtyPsychiatry
SymptomsLow mood, hypersomnia, overeating, low energy, social withdrawal

Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a mood disorder in which people who have normal mental health throughout most of the year experience depressive symptoms at the same time each year, most commonly in winter. In the DSM-5 it is not a separate diagnosis but a specifier — major depressive disorder "with seasonal pattern" — applied to recurrent depression that regularly begins and remits with the seasons. A much rarer summer-pattern form also exists.

Signs and symptoms

Winter-pattern SAD is frequently associated with atypical features of depression: oversleeping (hypersomnia), overeating with a craving for carbohydrates and weight gain, low energy, irritability and a heavy feeling in the limbs, alongside low mood and loss of interest. Symptoms typically begin in autumn as daylight shortens and remit in spring. Prevalence increases with latitude, and rates are higher in women and in young adults.

Causes

The disorder is linked to the effect of reduced sunlight on the body's circadian rhythm and on the neurotransmitter serotonin and the hormone melatonin, which regulates sleep. A leading hypothesis, the phase-shift hypothesis, holds that in vulnerable individuals the circadian clock becomes misaligned with the sleep–wake cycle in winter. Genetic factors may contribute: variations affecting melanopsin, a photopigment in the eye involved in circadian responses to light, have been associated with the condition.

Treatment

First-line treatments include bright light therapy — sitting before a light box emitting around 10,000 lux for roughly 30 minutes in the morning — and antidepressant medication, particularly SSRIs; bupropion is approved in the United States for prevention of seasonal depressive episodes. Cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for SAD (CBT-SAD) has shown durable benefits, and dawn simulation and regular outdoor exercise are also used.

History

The condition was formally described and named in 1984 by Norman Rosenthal and colleagues at the National Institute of Mental Health, following Rosenthal's observation of his own winter depression after moving from sunny South Africa to New York. It entered the DSM-III-R in 1987 as a pattern specifier.
