Wheatstone bridge

A Wheatstone bridge is an electrical circuit used to measure an unknown electrical resistance by balancing two legs of a bridge circuit, one leg of which includes the unknown component. The circuit was invented by Samuel Hunter Christie in 1833 and improved and popularised by Sir Charles Wheatstone in 1843.

Operation

The bridge consists of four resistors arranged in a diamond. A voltage is applied across one pair of opposite corners and a galvanometer (or other null detector) connected across the other pair. Two of the resistors are known fixed values, one is a calibrated variable resistor, and the fourth is the unknown. The variable resistor is adjusted until the galvanometer reads zero, at which point the bridge is "balanced" and the unknown resistance can be calculated from the known values: Rx = (R2/R1) × R3.

Applications and variants

Because the measurement is a null comparison, the Wheatstone bridge can be extremely accurate and was the standard laboratory method for resistance measurement for over a century. Variants include the Kelvin bridge for very low resistances and AC bridges for inductance and capacitance. The principle survives today in strain-gauge and sensor circuits, where small resistance changes in one arm unbalance the bridge by a measurable voltage.
