John Robert Lewis (21 February 1940 – 17 July 2020) was an American politician and civil rights activist who served in the United States House of Representatives for Georgia's 5th congressional district from 1987 until his death in 2020. He was the chairman of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC) from 1963 to 1966 and one of the "Big Six" leaders of the civil rights movement. Lewis endured more than forty arrests and repeated beatings in the struggle against racial segregation, most famously leading the first Selma march across the Edmund Pettus Bridge on "Bloody Sunday" in 1965. In Congress he was widely revered — often called the "conscience of the Congress" — and he received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2011.

Early life

Lewis was born near Troy, Alabama, the third of ten children of sharecroppers Eddie and Willie Mae Lewis. Inspired by the Montgomery bus boycott and the sermons of Martin Luther King Jr. on the radio, he resolved as a teenager to fight segregation. He studied at American Baptist College in Nashville, where workshops in nonviolence led by James Lawson trained him for the sit-in movement, and earned a degree from Fisk University in 1967.

Civil rights movement

Lewis helped desegregate Nashville lunch counters in 1960 and in 1961 was one of the original thirteen Freedom Riders, beaten by mobs in South Carolina and Alabama and imprisoned in Mississippi's Parchman Penitentiary. As SNCC chairman he was the youngest speaker at the March on Washington in August 1963, delivering a speech toned down under pressure from elders for its sharp criticism of the Kennedy administration. He directed SNCC's Mississippi voter registration work during Freedom Summer 1964.

On 7 March 1965 — Bloody Sunday — Lewis and Hosea Williams led some 600 marchers toward Montgomery in support of voting rights. Alabama state troopers attacked them on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma; Lewis's skull was fractured. Televised nationwide, the brutality helped galvanise support for the Voting Rights Act of 1965, signed five months later as marchers completed the route to Montgomery under federal protection.

Later activism and Congress

After leaving SNCC, Lewis worked for the Southern Regional Council's Voter Education Project, registering millions of Black voters in the South. Elected to the Atlanta City Council in 1981, he won election to Congress in 1986 and was re-elected sixteen times. A liberal Democrat and senior member of the Ways and Means Committee, he championed health care, voting rights and gun control — leading a sit-in on the House floor in 2016 — and opposed the Iraq War. His memoir Walking with the Wind (1998) won acclaim, and his graphic-novel trilogy March (2013–2016), co-written with Andrew Aydin and illustrated by Nate Powell, won the National Book Award.

Death and legacy

Lewis announced in December 2019 that he had stage IV pancreatic cancer. He died in Atlanta on 17 July 2020. His body lay in state in the United States Capitol Rotunda — the first Black lawmaker so honoured — and at the Alabama and Georgia state capitols. Former President Barack Obama eulogised him at Ebenezer Baptist Church. Lewis's philosophy of getting into "good trouble, necessary trouble" in defence of justice remains a watchword of American civic life.
