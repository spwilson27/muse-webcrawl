Acrolein

Acrolein (systematic name propenal) is the simplest unsaturated aldehyde, with the formula CH2=CHCHO. It is a colourless to yellow liquid with a pungent, acrid odour — the smell of burnt fat — and is a powerful lachrymator. Its name derives from the Latin acer (sharp) and oleum (oil).

Production

Acrolein is produced industrially by the catalytic oxidation of propylene over bismuth molybdate catalysts. It also forms when fats and cooking oils are overheated, when glycerol is heated above about 280 °C, and in cigarette smoke and exhaust fumes. Small amounts arise naturally during fermentation.

Uses

Most acrolein is converted to acrylic acid and its esters, or used as an intermediate in the synthesis of the amino acid methionine. It is an effective broad-spectrum biocide and is applied as an aquatic herbicide and molluscicide in irrigation systems, and as a slimicide in paper manufacture. During the First World War it was used by France as a chemical warfare agent under the name "Papite".

Health effects

Acrolein is severely irritating to the eyes, skin and respiratory tract and is toxic at low concentrations. It is generated endogenously during lipid peroxidation and has been implicated in oxidative-stress-related disease processes. Because of its reactivity it readily forms adducts with proteins and DNA.
