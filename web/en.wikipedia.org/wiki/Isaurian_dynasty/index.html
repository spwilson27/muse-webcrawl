The Isaurian dynasty was a Byzantine imperial dynasty that ruled the Byzantine Empire from 717 to 802, founded by Leo III, a general from the rough hill country of Isauria in Asia Minor (or, according to some sources, Syria). The dynasty's reign was dominated by the defence of the empire against the Umayyad Caliphate, by the great religious controversy of Iconoclasm, and by the renewal of hostility with the Papacy and the West.

Leo III and the defence of Constantinople

Leo III seized power in 717 amid the chaos of the "Twenty Years' Anarchy", and immediately faced the great Arab siege of Constantinople of 717–718. His defence of the capital — using Greek fire, diplomacy with the Bulgars, and the city's Theodosian Walls — inflicted a decisive defeat on the Umayyad forces and is considered a turning point in the Arab–Byzantine wars. Leo reorganised the army, issued the law code known as the Ecloga, and in 726/730 launched the policy of Iconoclasm, the prohibition of religious images, which would dominate Byzantine politics for over a century.

Constantine V

Leo's son, Constantine V (r. 741–775), weathered the revolt of his brother-in-law Artabasdos, campaigned successfully against both Arabs and Bulgars, and pressed Iconoclasm with ideological zeal, formalising it at the Council of Hieria (754). Iconophile sources gave him the scatological nickname "Kopronymos" ("dung-named"), but modern historians regard him as an able soldier-emperor who consolidated his father's gains.

Later Isaurians and Irene

Leo IV (r. 775–780) moderated the persecution of iconophiles. After his death his widow Irene of Athens ruled as regent for their son Constantine VI and, in 797, had him blinded and deposed, ruling alone as basileus until 802. Irene restored the veneration of icons at the Second Council of Nicaea (787), but her reign saw the loss of Byzantine influence in Italy: in 800 Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne as emperor in Rome, challenging Constantinople's claim to be the sole Roman Empire. Irene was deposed by her finance minister Nikephoros I in 802, ending the dynasty.

Legacy

The Isaurian emperors are credited with saving the Byzantine state from Arab conquest and restoring its military and fiscal strength, but their Iconoclasm split the empire religiously and contributed to the lasting estrangement between Eastern and Western Christianity.
