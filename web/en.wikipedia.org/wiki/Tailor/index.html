Tailor

A tailor is a person who makes, repairs, or alters clothing professionally, especially suits and men's clothing. Although the term dates to the thirteenth century, it took on its modern sense in the late 18th century and now refers to makers of men's and women's suits, coats, and trousers usually made from wool, linen, or silk.

The tailor fits the garment to the individual customer, in contrast to ready-to-wear clothing made to standardized sizes. Bespoke tailoring, epitomized by the workshops of Savile Row in London, involves the creation of a unique pattern for each client and multiple fittings; made-to-measure work adjusts an existing pattern, while alterations tailors adjust garments already made.

History

The trade developed from medieval makers of doublets and hose. In London, the Worshipful Company of Merchant Taylors was established by royal charter in 1503, and Savile Row became the heart of the trade in the early 19th century, dressing statesmen, officers, and royalty. Historically the trade was dominated by men, while women working with garments were more often dressmakers or seamstresses.

Techniques

Traditional tailoring involves taking detailed measurements, drafting a pattern, cutting the cloth, basting pieces together for a first fitting, and finishing with hand-sewn elements such as pad-stitched lapels and buttonholes. Tools of the trade include shears, needles, thimbles, tailor's chalk for marking, measuring tapes, and steam irons for shaping ("pressing") the fabric.

Regional traditions

Regional tailoring traditions maintain distinctive cuts and garments, from the Neapolitan soft shoulder in Italy to formal military tailoring and made-to-order workshops throughout South and Southeast Asia, where tailoring remains a common way of obtaining clothing.
