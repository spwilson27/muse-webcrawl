Britannia was the Roman province covering much of the island of Great Britain, from the Roman invasion of 43 AD until the end of Roman rule in Britain around 410 AD. The Latin name Britannia had earlier been used for the island as a whole, and personified "Britannia" later became a national symbol of Britain.

Conquest

Although Julius Caesar had raided Britain in 55 and 54 BC, the systematic conquest began under the emperor Claudius in 43 AD, when an invasion force under Aulus Plautius landed in Kent and defeated the forces of the Catuvellauni. Over the following decades Roman armies subdued the lowland tribes; resistance included the revolt of Boudica in 60/61 AD, which destroyed Camulodunum, Verulamium and Londinium before being crushed. Under Agricola (governor 77–85 AD) Roman forces advanced into Scotland and won the battle of Mons Graupius, but the far north was never permanently held.

Frontier and administration

The northern frontier was marked first by Hadrian's Wall (begun 122 AD) between the Tyne and the Solway, and briefly by the Antonine Wall further north. The province was divided around the end of the 2nd century into Britannia Superior (in the south, governed from London) and Britannia Inferior (in the north, governed from York), and later reorganised by Diocletian into four provinces within the Diocese of the Britains.

Roman rule brought towns with forums, baths and theatres (including Londinium, Aquae Sulis (Bath), Eboracum (York) and Deva (Chester)), a network of straight roads, villas in the countryside, and industries including pottery, mining and agriculture. Latin became the language of administration, and Christianity spread in the later Roman period.

End of Roman Britain

In the late 4th and early 5th centuries, troops were repeatedly withdrawn to fight on the continent, and in 410 the emperor Honorius is traditionally said to have told the Britons to look to their own defence. Roman administration ended, and the island fragmented into the kingdoms of the early medieval period.
