Berlin Philharmonic

The orchestra in its home, the Philharmonie, opened in 1963.

The Berlin Philharmonic (German: Berliner Philharmoniker) is a German orchestra based in Berlin, widely regarded as one of the finest in the world. Unusually among major orchestras, it is self-governing: its members elect their chief conductor and participate in the orchestra's decisions through its foundation structure.

History

The orchestra was founded in 1882 by 54 musicians who had broken away from Benjamin Bilse's ensemble over pay and conditions. Early conductors included Hans von Bülow, who forged its discipline, and Arthur Nikisch (1895–1922), who established its international fame. Wilhelm Furtwängler (1922–1945, and again after the war) deepened its association with the German Romantic repertoire. The orchestra's original hall was destroyed in 1944; Furtwängler's relationship with the Nazi regime left a contested legacy.

Herbert von Karajan, appointed in 1955 and chief conductor until shortly before his death in 1989, made the orchestra a global recording institution and presided over the opening of the Philharmonie in 1963 — Hans Scharoun's tent-like hall with "vineyard" terracing that revolutionised concert-hall design. Claudio Abbado (1989–2002) broadened the repertoire, Simon Rattle (2002–2018) expanded education work and contemporary programming, and Kirill Petrenko became chief conductor in 2019. The orchestra pioneered streaming with its Digital Concert Hall, launched in 2008.

Chief conductors

  • Ludwig von Brenner (1882–1887); Hans von Bülow (1887–1893)
  • Arthur Nikisch (1895–1922)
  • Wilhelm Furtwängler (1922–1945; 1952–1954)
  • Herbert von Karajan (1955–1989)
  • Claudio Abbado (1989–2002)
  • Simon Rattle (2002–2018)
  • Kirill Petrenko (2019– )
