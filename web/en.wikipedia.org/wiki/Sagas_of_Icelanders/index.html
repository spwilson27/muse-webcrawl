Sagas of Icelanders

The Sagas of Icelanders (Icelandic: Íslendingasögur), also known as family sagas, are a group of prose narratives written in Iceland in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, mostly in Old Icelandic. They describe events that supposedly took place between the settlement of Iceland in the late ninth century and the early eleventh century, the so-called Saga Age. They are widely regarded as the finest achievement of medieval Icelandic literature and among the great prose works of the European Middle Ages.

A page of the Möðruvallabók manuscript, which preserves several family sagas.

Characteristics

The sagas are written in a spare, laconic prose style that reports dialogue and action with little overt authorial comment. They follow the fortunes of Icelandic families over several generations, interweaving genealogy, legal disputes, blood feuds, voyages abroad and love affairs. Although composed two or more centuries after the events they describe, they blend oral tradition with the authors' own literary artistry, and their historical accuracy remains a subject of scholarly debate.

Principal sagas

  • Njáls saga — the longest and most celebrated, centred on the wise lawyer Njáll Þorgeirsson and the warrior Gunnar Hámundarson.
  • Egils saga — the life of the Viking warrior-poet Egill Skallagrímsson, sometimes attributed to Snorri Sturluson.
  • Laxdæla saga — a tale of the people of the Laxárdalr, featuring the tragic heroine Guðrún Ósvífrsdóttir.
  • Eyrbyggja saga — events around Snorri goði and the settlement of the Snæfellsnes peninsula.
  • Gísla saga — the outlawry of Gísli Súrsson.
  • Grettis saga — the life of the doomed strongman Grettir Ásmundarson.
  • Eiríks saga rauða and Grœnlendinga saga — the Vinland sagas, describing Norse voyages to North America.

Preservation and influence

The sagas survive in vellum manuscripts such as Möðruvallabók (c. 1350) and in later paper copies. They have profoundly shaped Icelandic national identity and have influenced writers from William Morris to Jorge Luis Borges. Modern editions and translations have made them a staple of world literature courses.
