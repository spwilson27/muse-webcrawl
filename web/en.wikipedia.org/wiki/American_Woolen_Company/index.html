The American Woolen Company was a textile manufacturer incorporated in 1899 under the leadership of William Madison Wood. Formed by consolidating numerous New England woolen mills, it became the largest woolen manufacturer in the United States — at times described as the largest in the world — producing cloth for men's and women's clothing, including uniforms in both world wars.

History

Wood, a Portuguese immigrant's son who rose through the textile business, merged eight failing Massachusetts mills into the new trust in 1899 and expanded aggressively, eventually controlling dozens of mills in Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, Vermont, New Hampshire, Maine, and New York. Its great Wood Worsted Mill in Lawrence, Massachusetts, opened in 1906, was among the largest worsted mills in the world.

The company's Lawrence mills were the scene of the famous 1912 "Bread and Roses" strike, when some 20,000 workers — mostly immigrant women — struck after a cut in hours led to reduced pay; American Woolen was the largest employer involved. The strike's success brought national attention to industrial labour conditions.

After Wood's death in 1926 the company declined amid competition from synthetics, southern mills, and imports. It merged with Bachmann Uxbridge Worsted in 1954 and then into the conglomerate Textron in 1955, ending its independent existence, though many of its monumental mill buildings survive, repurposed, across New England.
