Black shale is a dark-coloured, fine-grained sedimentary rock formed from organic-rich mud deposited in oxygen-poor (anoxic) water. Its dark colour comes from finely disseminated carbonaceous matter and iron sulfides such as pyrite. Black shales are the principal source rocks for most of the world's conventional oil and natural gas, and in the twenty-first century have themselves become major unconventional reservoirs produced by hydraulic fracturing.

Formation

Black shales accumulate where abundant organic matter rains into still, stratified water whose bottom layer lacks oxygen, so the organic material escapes decay and is buried with fine silt and clay. Total organic carbon contents commonly range from 1 to more than 10 percent. Famous units include the Cambrian Alum Shale of Scandinavia, the Devonian Marcellus Shale of the Appalachian Basin, the Jurassic Kimmeridge Clay of the North Sea, and the Cretaceous Mancos Shale of the western United States.

Economic significance

On burial and heating, the organic matter (kerogen) in black shale generates petroleum; some shales, called oil shales, are mined or retorted directly. Black shales also concentrate metals including uranium, vanadium, molybdenum, nickel, and zinc, and some are mined for these elements. As hydrocarbon reservoirs, gas shales such as the Barnett and Marcellus transformed United States energy production after 2000 through horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing.

Environmental considerations

Weathering of black shale can generate acid drainage and release toxic metals and naturally occurring radioactivity, and black shale outcrops are associated with elevated radon in overlying soils.
