Magnetohydrodynamic generator

TypeElectricity generator (direct conversion)
PrincipleLorentz force on a conductive fluid crossing a magnetic field
Working fluidIonised gas (plasma), liquid metal, or electrolyte
Moving partsNone in the generator itself

A magnetohydrodynamic generator (MHD generator) converts the thermal and kinetic energy of an electrically conducting fluid directly into electricity, without the rotating machinery of a conventional generator. Hot, ionised gas (or a liquid metal) is driven through a channel across a strong magnetic field; the Lorentz force deflects positive and negative charge carriers toward opposite electrodes, producing direct current that can be drawn off as power. The concept rests on Faraday's principles, applied to fluids rather than solid conductors, and was first investigated practically by Bela Karlovitz in the 1930s–40s.

Operation

Because gases are poor conductors at ordinary temperatures, combustion gases must be heated to around 2,000–3,000 K and "seeded" with an easily ionised alkali such as potassium or caesium compounds. Electrode configurations include the Faraday generator, with segmented electrode pairs, and the Hall generator, which exploits the Hall effect to take current axially. MHD generators were envisioned chiefly as "topping cycles": the still-hot exhaust gas would drive a conventional steam plant, raising total efficiency potentially above 50–60%.

History and status

Large research programmes ran in the United States, the Soviet Union and Japan from the 1960s to the 1980s. The Soviet U-25 facility near Moscow, fuelled by natural gas, fed power into the Moscow grid experimentally, and the US Department of Energy's proof-of-concept programme operated at Butte, Montana, and the CDIF in the 1980s–90s. Despite promising demonstrations, high-temperature materials problems, electrode erosion, seed recovery costs and the falling price of gas-turbine combined cycles prevented commercial deployment, and most programmes were wound down in the 1990s. MHD principles remain in use in some plasma and space-propulsion contexts, and the generator's mirror image — applying a field to accelerate a fluid — appears in proposed MHD ship drives.
