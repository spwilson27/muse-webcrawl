Urticaria


Urticarial wheals on the arm

Urticaria, commonly known as hives, is a skin condition characterised by the sudden appearance of itchy, raised, red or skin-coloured welts (wheals) that blanch with pressure and typically resolve within 24 hours without trace. The name derives from Urtica, the nettle genus, because the lesions resemble a nettle sting. Roughly one in five people experience urticaria at some point in life.

Causes and mechanism

Wheals result from histamine and other mediators released by mast cells in the skin, causing local vasodilation and fluid leakage. Acute urticaria (under six weeks) is often triggered by foods, medicines (notably NSAIDs and antibiotics), infections or insect stings. Chronic spontaneous urticaria (over six weeks) is frequently autoimmune, with no external trigger found. Physical urticarias are provoked by cold, pressure, heat, sunlight, vibration or water. When swelling involves deeper tissues of the lips, tongue or airway it is called angioedema, which can be dangerous.

Treatment

Second-generation H1 antihistamines (such as cetirizine or loratadine) are first-line; doses may be increased in chronic cases. For antihistamine-resistant chronic urticaria, the monoclonal antibody omalizumab is effective, and ciclosporin is used in severe disease. Severe episodes with airway involvement are treated as anaphylaxis with adrenaline.
