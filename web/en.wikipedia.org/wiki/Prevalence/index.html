Prevalence

In epidemiology, prevalence is the proportion of a defined population that has a particular disease or characteristic at a specified point in time, or during a specified period. It is a measure of disease burden: unlike incidence, which counts only new cases, prevalence counts all existing cases, old and new.

Types

Point prevalence is the proportion of a population with the condition at a single point in time — for example, the share of adults with diagnosed diabetes on 1 January of a given year. Period prevalence counts everyone who had the condition at any time during an interval, combining those who already had it at the start with those who developed it during the period. Lifetime prevalence, used especially in psychiatric epidemiology, is the proportion of people who have ever experienced the condition up to the time of assessment.

Calculation

Prevalence is expressed as a proportion or percentage: the number of existing cases divided by the total population at risk. For example, if a town of 10,000 people has 400 residents living with asthma, the point prevalence is 400/10,000 = 4 percent. Under steady-state conditions, prevalence is approximately the product of incidence and average disease duration (P ≈ I × D), which is why chronic diseases with low incidence can still have high prevalence, and why improvements in survival raise prevalence even when incidence falls.

Uses and limitations

Prevalence data guide the planning of health services, since they estimate how many people need care at any moment. Cross-sectional surveys and disease registries are the usual sources. Prevalence is less useful for studying causes, because it mixes factors that produce disease with factors that prolong it (survival bias); a factor associated with higher prevalence may act by extending survival rather than by causing onset. Prevalence can also be distorted by migration of cases into or out of the study population and by how completely cases are ascertained.

Related concepts

Prevalence contrasts with incidence rate (new cases per person-time) and cumulative incidence (the proportion developing disease over a period). In diagnostic testing, the prevalence of disease in the tested population strongly affects the positive predictive value of a test: the same test yields far more false positives relative to true positives when the condition is rare.
