Anton Webern (3 December 1883 – 15 September 1945) was an Austrian composer and conductor. Together with his teacher Arnold Schoenberg and fellow student Alban Berg, he formed the Second Viennese School. His music became progressively more concise and structurally concentrated, culminating in twelve-tone works of extreme brevity and clarity that profoundly influenced the post-war avant-garde.

Life

Born Anton von Webern (he dropped the nobiliary particle in 1918), he studied musicology at the University of Vienna and composition privately with Schoenberg from 1904. He worked as a conductor in German and Austrian theatres and in Prague, and after the First World War in Vienna he conducted workers' choirs and concerts of the Society for Private Musical Performances. Following the Nazi annexation of Austria his music was condemned as "degenerate" and performances dried up. In 1945, shortly after the war's end, he was accidentally shot and killed by an American soldier during a raid on his son-in-law's house in Mittersill.

Music

Webern's complete published output runs to only 31 opus numbers and about six hours of music. Early works such as the Passacaglia, Op. 1 are late-Romantic; his atonal pieces of 1909–1914 (Opp. 3–16) are aphoristic, some lasting under a minute, with sparse textures and carefully chosen timbres (Klangfarbenmelodie). His later twelve-tone works — including the Symphony, Op. 21, the Concerto for Nine Instruments, Op. 24, and the piano Variations, Op. 27 — are built from highly symmetrical tone rows. After 1945, composers such as Boulez and Stockhausen took Webern's serial rigour and pointillistic textures as the starting point for total serialism, making him arguably the most influential of the Second Viennese School on post-war music.
