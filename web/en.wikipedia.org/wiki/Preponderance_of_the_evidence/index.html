Preponderance of the evidence

Preponderance of the evidence is one of the standards of proof used in legal proceedings, and is the standard that applies in most civil litigation in common law jurisdictions. Under this standard, the party bearing the burden of proof must persuade the fact-finder that a claim is more likely than not to be true — often described as a probability greater than fifty per cent. If the evidence is exactly in balance, the party with the burden loses.

Comparison with other standards

United States law recognises three principal standards of proof, in ascending order of stringency:

  • Preponderance of the evidence — more likely than not; the ordinary civil standard.
  • Clear and convincing evidence — an intermediate standard, substantially more likely than not, applied in contexts such as civil fraud claims, termination of parental rights (Santosky v. Kramer), and civil commitment (Addington v. Texas).
  • Beyond a reasonable doubt — the standard required for criminal convictions, reflecting the gravity of criminal punishment and the presumption of innocence.

Application

In civil cases the plaintiff ordinarily bears the burden of proving each element of the claim by a preponderance, while defendants bear the burden on affirmative defences. The standard also governs many administrative proceedings and factual determinations in criminal cases that do not constitute elements of the offence. Some statutory schemes prescribe a different standard; certain civil claims involving allegations such as fraud or reformation of a deed traditionally require clear and convincing evidence in many jurisdictions.

The term derives from the Latin praeponderare, "to outweigh", and the metaphor most commonly used to explain it to juries is that of a pair of scales tipping, however slightly, in one party's favour.
