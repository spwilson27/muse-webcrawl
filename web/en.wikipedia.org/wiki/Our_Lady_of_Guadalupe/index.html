Our Lady of Guadalupe (Spanish: Nuestra Señora de Guadalupe) is a Catholic title of Mary associated with a series of apparitions in December 1531 and with the image of the Virgin on the cloak (tilma) of Saint Juan Diego. The image is venerated in the Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe in Mexico City, the world's most visited Catholic shrine, and Guadalupe is a powerful religious and national symbol of Mexico.

The apparitions

According to tradition, preserved in the Nahuatl account Nican Mopohua, the Virgin appeared four times to the indigenous convert Juan Diego on Tepeyac Hill in December 1531, asking that a church be built there. As a sign for the sceptical Bishop Juan de Zumárraga, Juan Diego gathered Castilian roses in his tilma on the hilltop and, opening it before the bishop, revealed the image of the Virgin imprinted on the cloth. The bishop ordered the shrine built.

Significance

The dark-skinned Virgin of Guadalupe, appearing so soon after the Spanish conquest and speaking to an indigenous visionary in his own culture's terms, became a focus of Mexican identity—carried on the banner of Miguel Hidalgo's independence army in 1810 and later by Emiliano Zapata's forces. She was proclaimed Patroness of Latin America and, in 1999, of the Americas. Juan Diego was canonised in 2002; the feast of Our Lady of Guadalupe is celebrated on 12 December, drawing millions of pilgrims to the basilica each year.
