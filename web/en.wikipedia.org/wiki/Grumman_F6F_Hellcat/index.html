The Grumman F6F Hellcat is an American carrier-based fighter aircraft of World War II. Designed to replace the earlier F4F Wildcat and to counter the Japanese Mitsubishi A6M Zero, the Hellcat was the United States Navy's dominant carrier fighter in the second half of the Pacific War, alongside the faster Vought F4U Corsair. In total, 12,275 Hellcats were built in just over two years, and the type is credited with destroying 5,223 enemy aircraft — more than any other Allied naval aircraft — while Hellcat pilots achieved an overall kill-to-loss ratio of just over 19:1.

Design and development

Grumman had begun work on a successor to the Wildcat even before the United States entered the war, and lessons from combat over Europe and the Pacific — including examinations of a captured Zero — informed the design. The XF6F-1 first flew on 26 June 1942 powered by a Wright radial engine, but the design was quickly revised around the more powerful Pratt & Whitney R-2800 Double Wasp, yielding the XF6F-3. The Hellcat was a rugged, generously proportioned monoplane with a large wing, armour protection, self-sealing fuel tanks, and six .50-inch (12.7 mm) Browning machine guns. Production aircraft entered service in early 1943, first with squadron VF-9 aboard USS Essex.

Operational history

The Hellcat made its combat debut on 1 September 1943 against a Japanese flying boat near Marcus Island. Hellcats formed the backbone of the fast carrier task forces that swept across the Pacific in 1944–1945, taking part in the Philippine Sea, Leyte Gulf, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa operations. In the Battle of the Philippine Sea (19–20 June 1944) — the "Great Marianas Turkey Shoot" — Hellcat pilots claimed over 300 Japanese aircraft in a single day. Night-fighter and photo-reconnaissance variants (F6F-3N/5N and F6F-5P) were also produced. The Royal Navy's Fleet Air Arm operated 1,263 Hellcats (as the Gannet, then Hellcat) under Lend-Lease.

Fifty-two Hellcat pilots became aces; the top-scoring was Captain David McCampbell, the U.S. Navy's ace of aces with 34 victories, who famously shot down nine Japanese aircraft in a single mission on 24 October 1944.

Variants

  • F6F-3 — the first major production version, with the R-2800-10 engine.
  • F6F-5 — improved version with a redesigned cowling, stronger airframe, and provision for rockets and bombs; some carried two 20 mm cannon.
  • F6F-3N / F6F-5N — night fighters fitted with radar in a wing pod.

Specifications (F6F-5)

Crew: 1. Length: 10.24 m (33 ft 7 in). Wingspan: 13.06 m (42 ft 10 in). Maximum speed: 629 km/h (391 mph) at altitude. Range: 1,520 km (945 mi). Service ceiling: 11,370 m (37,300 ft). Armament: six 0.50 in (12.7 mm) M2 Browning machine guns, or two 20 mm cannon and four machine guns; up to 1,800 kg (4,000 lb) of bombs or rockets.
