A quaestor (Latin: quaestor, "investigator", from quaerere, "to seek") was a public official in ancient Rome. Quaestors were initially appointed to assist in murder investigations (quaestores parricidii) under the kings, but the office soon developed into a financial magistracy. Elected under the Republic as part of the cursus honorum, quaestors administered the state treasury (aerarium), paid the army, and supervised public accounts in Rome and the provinces.

History

Tradition held that the first quaestors were created by Romulus or Numa Pompilius. By the mid-5th century BC, two quaestors were elected annually; the number rose to four in 421 BC, when the office was opened to plebeians. The number continued to increase with Rome's expansion: eight under Sulla, twenty under Julius Caesar, who also made election to the quaestorship an automatic qualification for membership of the Senate. Augustus set the number at twenty and fixed the minimum age at 25.

Duties

In Rome, urban quaestors guarded the treasury in the Temple of Saturn, received revenues and authorized payments, and kept the state archives. Provincial quaestors accompanied governors, handled finances, and sometimes acted as deputies or military paymasters. After their term, quaestors typically entered the Senate.

Later history

The office survived into the Empire in reduced form and in the Byzantine period: the quaestor sacri palatii was the emperor's chief legal officer, while in Constantinople the quaesitor supervised the city's population and law courts.
