Independence of irrelevant alternatives

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) is an axiom of decision theory and social choice theory stating that the relative ranking or choice between two options should not change merely because a third, "irrelevant" option is added to or removed from the set of alternatives.

In social choice

In voting theory, IIA requires that if candidate A is preferred to candidate B by the electorate, introducing or withdrawing a third candidate C should not reverse that result. Many familiar electoral systems violate IIA, which underlies the phenomenon of the spoiler effect: a minor candidate can draw support from a similar major candidate and change the winner. Arrow's impossibility theorem shows that no ranked voting system satisfying a short list of fairness conditions, including IIA, can exist for elections with three or more candidates. A famous quip by the economist Sidney Morgenbesser illustrates the axiom's failures in everyday choice: offered apple or blueberry pie, he chose apple; told that cherry pie was also available, he replied, "Then I'll have blueberry."

In economics

In discrete-choice models such as the multinomial logit, IIA means the odds of choosing one option over another are independent of what other options are present. This produces the notorious "red bus/blue bus problem": adding a blue bus to a choice between a car and a red bus should not draw riders equally from both, yet the model predicts it will.
