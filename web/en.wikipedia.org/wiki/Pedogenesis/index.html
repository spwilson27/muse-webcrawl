Pedogenesis

Pedogenesis (from Greek pedon, "soil", and genesis, "origin") is the process of soil formation — the natural body synthesis of soils from parent rock material under the influence of climate, organisms, topography and time. It is also used in biology for a form of reproduction in juvenile stages (see below).

Soil formation

The modern study of pedogenesis began with Vasily Dokuchaev in the 1880s, who recognised soil as an independent natural body rather than merely weathered rock. Hans Jenny formalised the idea in 1941 with the "state factor" equation, in which soil (S) is a function of climate (cl), organisms (o), relief (r), parent material (p) and time (t): S = f(cl, o, r, p, t). Processes of pedogenesis include additions (such as leaf litter), losses (leaching), translocations (movement of clay, iron or organic matter within the profile — for example in podzolisation and laterisation) and transformations (weathering and humification). Over time these processes differentiate the soil into horizons.

Timescales

Soil formation is slow: a centimetre of topsoil may take hundreds to thousands of years to form, depending on climate and parent material. Young soils (such as Entisols) show little horizon development, while old, strongly weathered soils (such as Oxisols in the tropics) reflect millions of years of pedogenesis.

In biology

In zoology, pedogenesis is reproduction by an organism in a larval or juvenile stage, a phenomenon found in some insects — for example gall midges, whose larvae reproduce without maturing — and it is closely related to neoteny and paedogenesis terminology in the literature on heterochrony.
