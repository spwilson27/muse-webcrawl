Probable prime

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A probable prime is an integer that satisfies a specific condition — typically a form of Fermat's little theorem or a related congruence — that is also satisfied by all prime numbers, but which may occasionally be satisfied by a composite number (a pseudoprime). A probable prime that turns out to be composite is called a pseudoprime with respect to the test used; one that is genuinely prime is a true prime, but the term "probable" reflects that, at the moment of testing, primality has not been proved by a deterministic method.

Primality tests

The most common tests are the Fermat test, based on an−1 ≡ 1 (mod n), and the stronger Miller–Rabin test, based on writing n − 1 = 2sd and checking a sequence of congruences. Euler probable primes and strong probable primes use progressively more stringent conditions; the Baillie–PSW test combines a strong Miller–Rabin test with a Lucas probable prime test, and no counterexample is known.

Applications

Probable primes are central to practical cryptography. Systems such as RSA require primes of hundreds of digits; trial division and deterministic proofs would be far too slow for routine key generation, so implementations test random candidates with several rounds of Miller–Rabin. With enough rounds, the probability that a composite passes is below 4−k for k rounds — smaller than the probability of a hardware error corrupting the computation.

Industry-grade primes

In cryptographic engineering, an "industry-grade prime" is informally a number that has passed enough probabilistic testing to be treated as prime for all practical purposes. Deterministic proofs (such as elliptic-curve primality certificates) are reserved for applications that demand certainty.
