SWAT (Special Weapons And Tactics) is a generic term, originating in the United States, for law-enforcement units that use specialised or military equipment and tactics to handle high-risk situations beyond the capability of regular patrol officers — hostage rescues, barricaded gunmen, sniper incidents, and the service of high-risk arrest and search warrants.

History

The first SWAT unit is generally credited to the Los Angeles Police Department, where Inspector Daryl Gates championed the concept after the Watts riots of 1965 and a series of sniper attacks; the name was settled in 1967 (Gates later recalled that "Special Weapons Attack Team" was rejected by a deputy chief, giving the now-famous backronym). LAPD SWAT gained national attention in a 1969 shootout with Black Panthers and especially in the 1974 Symbionese Liberation Army shootout, broadcast live on television. The model spread rapidly through American policing in the 1970s and 1980s, accelerated by the War on Drugs; today most medium and large U.S. departments maintain a SWAT or emergency-services unit, and federal teams include the FBI's Hostage Rescue Team and regional FBI SWAT teams.

Equipment and tactics

SWAT officers typically carry carbines or submachine guns, wear ballistic helmets and heavy body armour, and use equipment such as flashbang grenades, ballistic shields, breaching tools, night-vision devices, police robots and armoured vehicles (including surplus military vehicles obtained under the U.S. Department of Defense's 1033 programme). Operations emphasise dynamic entry, containment, negotiation and coordinated team movement, usually integrated with crisis negotiators and tactical medics.

Criticism

Civil-liberties groups and scholars criticise the increasing use of SWAT teams for routine warrant service — the ACLU reported that the large majority of deployments in its 2014 study of militarisation were for search warrants, mostly in drug investigations — and high-profile botched or fatal raids have fuelled debates about "no-knock" warrants, police militarisation and accountability.
