LGBT social movements

Lesbian, gay, bisexual and transgender (LGBT) social movements are social movements that advocate for the equal treatment and acceptance of LGBT people in society. Their goals have included decriminalization of homosexuality, anti-discrimination protections, recognition of same-sex relationships and marriage, adoption rights, protection of transgender people, and an end to violence against LGBT people. The movements share much history with, and often overlap, feminist and civil-rights movements.

Early organizing

The first sustained movement arose in Germany: Magnus Hirschfeld founded the Scientific-Humanitarian Committee in 1897, petitioning against Paragraph 175, which criminalized male homosexuality, and his Institute for Sexual Science (1919) pioneered research and advocacy until the Nazis destroyed it in 1933. In the United States, the Mattachine Society (1950) and the Daughters of Bilitis (1955) led the cautious "homophile" movement, picketing government buildings in the 1960s for an end to employment bans.

Liberation era

The Stonewall uprising of June 1969 in New York — resistance to a police raid on a gay bar — catalysed a more radical gay liberation movement: the Gay Liberation Front, and later ACT UP during the AIDS crisis of the 1980s, which transformed drug-approval politics and public awareness. Pride marches commemorating Stonewall became global annual events.

Modern era and debates

From the 1990s the movement in many Western countries centred on marriage equality, achieved first in the Netherlands (2001) and later across much of Europe, the Americas and elsewhere, alongside anti-discrimination law and military service. Transgender rights — legal gender recognition, healthcare access and protection from violence — became a central and sometimes fiercely contested front in the 2010s and 2020s. Globally, the picture diverges sharply: while some countries liberalize, others have enacted harsher penalties, and dozens of states still criminalize same-sex conduct. Debates within the movements concern assimilation versus liberation, corporate sponsorship of Pride, intersectionality, and the relationship between LGB and transgender advocacy.
