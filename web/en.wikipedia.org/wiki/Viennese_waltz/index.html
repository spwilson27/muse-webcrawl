Viennese waltz

The Viennese waltz (German: Wiener Walzer) is a genre of dance music and a ballroom dance. It is the oldest of the current ballroom dances, having emerged in the second half of the 18th century from the German dance and the Ländler, and gained enormous popularity throughout Europe in the 19th century. The Viennese waltz is danced at about 29–30 measures per minute (about 180 beats per minute for its 3/4 time), considerably faster than the slower "English" or American slow waltz.

Music

Viennese waltz music is written in 3/4 time, six steps to a bar, with a strongly accented first beat. The genre is dominated by the Strauss family — Johann Strauss I, his son Johann Strauss II (the "Waltz King"), and Josef and Eduard Strauss — whose orchestral waltzes such as The Blue Danube (1866) became world famous. Other notable composers include Joseph Lanner, Franz Lehár, and Emmerich Kálmán.

Dance

In its modern competitive form, the Viennese waltz has a very restricted vocabulary of figures: natural and reverse turns, changes, and the fleckerl. Partners remain in a closed hold and travel rapidly around the floor with continuous rotation. The dance is the first of the five standard ballroom dances in international competition.

Cultural role

The waltz is central to Viennese social life, most famously at the Vienna Opera Ball, where debutantes open the evening dancing to Strauss. Austria has inscribed its ball culture and the Viennese waltz as elements of national heritage.
