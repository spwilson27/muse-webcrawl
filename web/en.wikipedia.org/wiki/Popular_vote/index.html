Popular vote

[Image: Voters queuing at a polling station on election day]

The popular vote is the total number of votes cast for each candidate or option by individual voters in an election, as distinct from the outcome produced by an electoral system that aggregates those votes indirectly — such as an electoral college, or a parliamentary seat allocation. In most democracies the popular vote determines the result directly; in some systems the two can diverge, a situation often described as the winner "losing the popular vote".

United States

The term is most prominent in United States presidential elections, where the presidency is decided by the Electoral College rather than by the national popular vote. In five elections (1824, 1876, 1888, 2000 and 2016) the candidate who won the presidency received fewer popular votes nationally than an opponent; in 2000 the margin was about half a million votes, and in 2016 about 2.9 million. These outcomes drive recurrent reform debates, including proposals to abolish the College and the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact. There is no official national tally; figures are compiled from state returns, and turnout and eligibility rules vary by state (see Voter registration).

Other systems

Divergences also occur in parliamentary systems using plurality voting: in the United Kingdom, for example, parties have won a majority of seats with well under half the vote, and in 1951 the party winning the most votes (Labour) lost the election to the Conservatives in seats. Under proportional representation the popular vote maps closely onto seats, though thresholds can still exclude small parties' votes. In referendums, the popular vote is normally decisive by definition.
