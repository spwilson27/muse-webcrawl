Tone (linguistics)

In linguistics, tone is the use of pitch in language to distinguish lexical or grammatical meaning — that is, to distinguish or to inflect words. All oral languages use pitch to express emotional and other paralinguistic information and to convey emphasis, contrast and other such features in what is called intonation, but not all languages use tones to distinguish words or inflectional categories. Languages that do have this feature are called tonal languages; the distinctive patterns of such a language are sometimes called tonemes, by analogy with phonemes.


The four tones of Mandarin Chinese

Types of tone systems

Tonal languages fall into two broad types. Register tone systems distinguish words by level pitch heights (high, mid, low), as in many Niger-Congo languages such as Yoruba and Igbo. Contour tone systems distinguish tones by pitch shape (rising, falling, level, dipping), as in Sino-Tibetan languages: Mandarin Chinese has four main tones plus a neutral tone, while Cantonese has six or more. Some languages, such as many Bantu languages, also use tone grammatically, to mark tense or case.

Distribution

Tone is common in languages of East and Southeast Asia (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Burmese), in sub-Saharan Africa, and in many indigenous languages of the Americas and Papua New Guinea. Estimates suggest that between 60 and 70 per cent of the world's languages are tonal. Tone is comparatively rare in the languages of Europe.

Tonogenesis

Tones often arise historically from the loss of consonantal distinctions: a process called tonogenesis. In Vietnamese and Chinese, for example, tones developed from the loss of final consonants and the voicing contrast of initial consonants, a pattern first explained by André-Georges Haudricourt in 1954.
