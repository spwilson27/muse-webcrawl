Neutrality (international relations)

European states with traditions of neutrality.

Neutrality, in international relations and international law, is the legal status of a state that abstains from all participation in a war between other states and maintains an attitude of impartiality toward the belligerents. The rights and duties of neutral states were codified in the Hague Conventions of 1907 (Conventions V and XIII). Belligerents may not move troops or war material across neutral territory, while neutrals must intern belligerent forces that enter their jurisdiction.

Types of neutrality

Permanent neutrality binds a state to neutrality in all future wars; Switzerland is the classic case, its neutrality recognized by the great powers at the Congress of Vienna in 1815. Austria adopted permanent neutrality by constitutional law in 1955 as the condition of its State Treaty and the withdrawal of occupation forces. Other states, such as Ireland, Sweden historically, Finland during the Cold War, and Turkmenistan (recognized by the UN in 1995), have pursued policies of neutrality or military non-alignment without identical legal forms.

History

The concept developed from early-modern practices of non-belligerence; armed neutrality leagues were formed in 1780 and 1800 by northern European states to protect neutral shipping. The United States maintained neutrality in the early years of both World Wars, notably under the Neutrality Acts of the 1930s. In the era of the UN Charter, collective-security obligations (such as sanctions under Chapter VII) sit in tension with strict neutrality, and many states prefer the softer policy of non-alignment associated with the Non-Aligned Movement founded in 1961.

Debates

After the Cold War, several historically neutral European states joined the European Union, accepting its common foreign and security policy; Sweden and Finland abandoned military non-alignment entirely by joining NATO in 2023–2024 following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Switzerland continues to maintain armed neutrality while adopting most international sanctions.
