The Spirit of Law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Spirit of Law (French: De l'esprit des lois, originally De l'esprit des loix) is a treatise on political theory and comparative law published in 1748 by Charles de Secondat, Baron de Montesquieu. Initially published anonymously and banned by the Catholic Church (placed on the Index in 1751), it became one of the most influential books of the Enlightenment and of constitutional thought worldwide.

Contents

Montesquieu classifies governments (republics, monarchies, despotisms) by their principles (virtue, honour, fear), examines the relations of laws to climate, terrain, commerce, religion and manners — an early sociology of law — and, in the famous Book XI, analyses the English constitution as distributing legislative, executive and judicial power among separate bodies, the celebrated (if partly idealised) source of the modern doctrine of the separation of powers. His typology of laws "relative to the nature of government" and his praise of federal republics influenced the American framers deeply; the Federalist Papers cite him as an "oracle". The work also criticises slavery and defends commerce's civilising ("sweet") effects, positions argued within its deliberately indirect style.
