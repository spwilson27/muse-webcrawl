The Italian Wars were a series of conflicts fought between 1494 and 1559, primarily in Italy, over control of the Italian states. Beginning with Charles VIII of France's invasion to press his claim to the Kingdom of Naples, the wars drew in Spain, the Holy Roman Empire, England, Scotland, the Swiss and the Ottoman Empire, and became the central arena of the Valois–Habsburg rivalry. They ended the independence of most Italian states and established Spanish hegemony over the peninsula for a century and a half.

Course

French invasions, 1494–1516

Charles VIII's march to Naples (1494–95) demonstrated the vulnerability of the Italian state system — and the shock helped provoke Machiavelli's political writings. Louis XII took Milan (1499) and, with Spain, partitioned Naples, but the partners quarrelled; Spanish general Gonzalo de Córdoba expelled the French from the south. The War of the League of Cambrai (1508–16) saw shifting coalitions against Venice, then against France; Francis I's victory at Marignano (1515) restored French Milan.

Habsburg–Valois struggle

With Charles V ruling Spain and the Empire, the contest intensified. At Pavia (1525) the French king himself was captured. The League of Cognac against Charles provoked the unpaid Imperial army's Sack of Rome (1527), a trauma that symbolically ended the High Renaissance. France's ally Suleiman the Magnificent added an Ottoman front. Fighting resumed under Henry II of France and Philip II of Spain until exhaustion and bankruptcy on both sides produced the settlement of 1559.

Consequences

The Peace of Cateau-Cambrésis (1559) confirmed Spanish control of Milan, Naples, Sicily and Sardinia; Italy, the richest region of Renaissance Europe, became a strategic dependency. Militarily the wars are noted for the rise of combined-arms warfare — Spanish tercios, field artillery, trace italienne fortifications (an Italian invention exported across Europe) — and for the obsolescence of heavy cavalry and Swiss pike supremacy after Pavia.
