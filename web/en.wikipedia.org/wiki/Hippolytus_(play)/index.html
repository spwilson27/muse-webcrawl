Hippolytus (play)

Hippolytus (Ancient Greek: Ἱππόλυτος, Hippolytos) is an Ancient Greek tragedy by Euripides, first produced in 428 BC, when it won first prize at the City Dionysia. It dramatises the myth of Hippolytus, son of Theseus, who rejects Aphrodite for Artemis and is destroyed when his stepmother Phaedra falls in love with him.

Hippolytus
Author: Euripides
First performed: 428 BC, Athens
Genre: Tragedy
Characters: Aphrodite, Hippolytus, Phaedra, Nurse, Theseus, Artemis

Background

Euripides had earlier written a lost Hippolytus Veiled in which Phaedra was shameless; this surviving revision, Hippolytus Garland-bearer, made Phaedra struggle against her passion, answering criticism of the first version.

Plot

Aphrodite, in the prologue, vows revenge on Hippolytus for scorning her. She makes Phaedra fall in love with him. Phaedra, wasting away, confesses to her Nurse, who secretly reveals it to Hippolytus; his furious misogynistic outburst drives Phaedra to hang herself, leaving a letter falsely accusing Hippolytus of rape. Theseus curses his son using a wish granted by Poseidon; a monstrous bull from the sea panics Hippolytus's horses and he is mortally dragged. Artemis reveals the truth; father and son reconcile before Hippolytus dies, forgiving Theseus.

Themes and reception

The play explores sophrosyne (self-control), the conflict of Aphrodite and Artemis, speech and silence, and the danger of absolute virtue. It influenced Seneca's Phaedra, Racine's Phèdre (1677), and many operatic and modern adaptations.
