René Spitz

René Árpád Spitz (29 January 1887 – 11 September 1974) was an Austrian-American psychoanalyst, known for his studies of the effects of maternal deprivation and institutional care on infant development. His observational work with orphanages and foundling homes in the 1940s provided influential evidence that infants need sustained emotional contact with a caregiver, and helped to transform child-care practices in hospitals and institutions.

Life

Spitz was born in Vienna and studied medicine, joining the circle around Sigmund Freud and the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society. He taught and practised in Vienna, Berlin, Paris and Budapest before emigrating to the United States in 1939, where he worked at the New York Psychoanalytic Institute, the City College of New York and later the University of Colorado, where he became professor of psychology. He died in Denver in 1974.

Research

In studies comparing a foundling home with a prison nursery where mothers cared for their babies, Spitz documented developmental decline in institutionalised infants despite adequate nutrition and hygiene. He coined or popularised concepts including hospitalism and anaclitic depression (1945), describing the progressive deterioration of infants separated from their mothers for prolonged periods. His films, such as Grief: A Peril in Infancy (1947), were widely shown. Spitz also described developmental "organizers" of the psyche, such as the social smile (around three months), stranger anxiety (around eight months) and the semantic "no" gesture.

Legacy

Spitz's work anticipated attachment theory (John Bowlby, Mary Ainsworth) and supported reforms promoting rooming-in, foster care over institutional care, and reduced separation of young children from caregivers. His major book is The First Year of Life (1965).
