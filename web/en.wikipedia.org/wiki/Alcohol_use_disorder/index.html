Alcohol use disorder

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A row of empty bottles on a bar counter in dim light]
A row of empty bottles on a bar counter in dim light

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a medical condition characterised by an impaired ability to stop or control alcohol use despite adverse social, occupational or health consequences. It encompasses the conditions previously separated as alcohol abuse and alcohol dependence ('alcoholism') in the DSM-5, which grades severity by symptom count. AUD is among the most prevalent substance disorders worldwide and a leading cause of preventable death.

Diagnosis and course

DSM-5 criteria include craving, loss of control, tolerance, withdrawal (Delirium tremens in severe cases), continued use despite harm and failure to fulfil obligations; two to three criteria indicate mild disorder, six or more severe. Risk rises with early drinking, family history (genetics account for roughly half of vulnerability), trauma and comorbid mental illness. Untreated, AUD progresses to liver disease, cardiovascular damage, cancers, cognitive impairment (Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome) and social collapse.

Treatment

Effective treatments exist but reach a minority: behavioural therapies (motivational interviewing, CBT), mutual-help groups (Alcoholics Anonymous), and medications — Naltrexone, Acamprosate and Disulfiram — with medically managed withdrawal (benzodiazepines) where dependence is severe. Harm-reduction approaches (managed reduction, nalmefene in Europe) complement abstinence goals.

Public health

Alcohol causes about 2.6 million deaths per year globally (WHO estimates), including roughly 1 in 5 deaths among US adults aged 20–49 by CDC analysis. Policy levers with strong evidence include taxation and minimum pricing, availability limits and marketing restrictions — measures perennially contested by industry lobbying.
