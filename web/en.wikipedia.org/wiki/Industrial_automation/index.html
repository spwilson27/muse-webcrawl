Industrial automation

Industrial automation is the use of control systems, such as computers or robots, and information technologies for handling different processes and machinery in an industry to replace a human being. It is the second step beyond mechanization in the scope of industrialization.

History

Early automation relied on mechanical devices such as the centrifugal governor used by James Watt to regulate steam engine speed. The twentieth century brought relay logic, and later programmable logic controllers (PLCs), first developed in the late 1960s for the automotive industry. The introduction of computer numerical control (CNC) machines, industrial robots, and distributed control systems transformed manufacturing through the late twentieth century.

Types

  • Fixed automation – a production sequence fixed by the configuration of the equipment, used for high-volume production.
  • Programmable automation – equipment designed with the capability to change the sequence of operations, used in batch production.
  • Flexible automation – systems able to produce a variety of products with virtually no time lost for changeovers.

Components

Typical systems combine sensors and actuators, PLCs and human–machine interfaces (HMIs), supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems, and networks compliant with standards such as OPC UA. Modern plants increasingly integrate industrial Internet of things (IIoT) devices and machine learning for predictive maintenance.

Advantages and disadvantages

Automation increases productivity, consistency, and safety, and reduces labour costs and human error. Disadvantages include high initial investment, displacement of workers, and vulnerability to system failures and cyberattacks.
