Lobbying

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Lobbying, in politics, is the act of lawfully attempting to influence the actions, policies or decisions of government officials, most often legislators or members of regulatory agencies. Lobbying is performed by individuals and by organised interest groups — businesses, trade associations, unions, charities and NGOs — directly or through professional lobbyists and law and public-affairs firms. The word is traditionally connected with the lobbies of legislatures (and, in a famous Washington story, the Willard Hotel lobby frequented by President Grant).

Regulation

Many jurisdictions regulate lobbying through registration and disclosure: the US Lobbying Disclosure Act (1995), the EU Transparency Register, and registers in Canada, Germany and elsewhere. Concerns centre on unequal access (well-resourced interests out-organising diffuse publics), the "revolving door" between government and lobbying firms, and foreign-influence lobbying (regulated in the US under FARA, 1938). Defenders note that lobbying supplies information and representation of affected interests and is protected as petitioning in many constitutional traditions (the US First Amendment expressly protects the right "to petition the Government").

Practice

Modern practice blends direct advocacy with grassroots mobilisation, coalition-building, litigation, research funding and media campaigns ("outside lobbying"), and is a major industry in Washington, Brussels and other capitals — a standing case study in how democracies balance voice and money.
