Modulation (music)

In music, modulation is the change from one tonality (tonic, or tonal centre) to another. This may or may not be accompanied by a change in key signature. Modulations articulate or create the structure or form of a musical work, often associated with formal units such as phrases, periods or larger sections. A piece may begin in one key, modulate to a related key — in sonata form, classically the dominant, or the relative major in minor-key movements — and return to the tonic for recapitulation.

Types

Common-chord (pivot) modulation

A chord diatonic to both the old and new keys is reinterpreted in the new key, allowing a smooth, often barely noticeable transition.

Chromatic and enharmonic modulation

Chromatic modulation introduces a chromatically altered chord — such as a secondary dominant or a Neapolitan chord — to pivot abruptly. Enharmonic modulation exploits the double identity of a chord spelled two ways, most famously the diminished seventh chord and the German augmented sixth chord reinterpreted as a dominant seventh, enabling moves to distant keys; it was a favourite device of Schubert and other Romantics.

Direct (phrase) modulation

The music simply begins a new phrase in a new key without any pivot. In popular music, the abrupt shift up a semitone or whole tone near the end of a song — the so-called "truck driver's gear change" — is a clichéd form used to inject final-chorus energy.

Closely related keys

Modulations most commonly target closely related keys: the dominant, subdominant, relative major/minor, and the parallel major or minor. Nineteenth-century composers increasingly modulated by thirds and other remote relationships, and in atonal and jazz contexts "modulation" may describe shifts of tonal centre achieved by sequence (real sequence being a routine form of temporary modulation within a phrase).
