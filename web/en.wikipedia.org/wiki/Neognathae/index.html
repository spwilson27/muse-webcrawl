Neognathae

Neognathae is an infraclass of birds within the class Aves, comprising the great majority of living birds — nearly 10,000 species. Together with its sister group, the Palaeognathae (ratites and tinamous), it forms the subclass Neornithes, the modern birds. Neognaths are distinguished by the structure of their palate, in which the bones of the upper jaw are more mobile than in palaeognaths, along with other skeletal features.

Classification

Neognathae is divided into two superorders. Galloanserae includes the gamebirds (Galliformes) and waterfowl (Anseriformes), among the earliest-diverging lineages. All remaining neognaths belong to Neoaves, a huge radiation containing most bird orders: passerines (perching birds, more than half of all bird species), raptors, owls, parrots, penguins, shorebirds, seabirds, and many others. The relationships among Neoaves lineages were long obscure and have been substantially clarified by whole-genome phylogenetic studies, though some nodes remain debated.

Evolution

Molecular clocks suggest neognaths originated in the Late Cretaceous, and the fossil Vegavis iaai from Antarctica shows that modern-type ducks existed before the end-Cretaceous mass extinction. The group then diversified explosively in the early Cenozoic, filling niches vacated by extinct dinosaurs and pterosaurs; some studies instead place most of the Neoaves radiation after the extinction event.

Characteristics

Beyond the mobile palate, neognaths generally have fused metacarpal bones, an elongated fourth toe arrangement in most groups, and a reduced or absent vomer. Most are capable of powered flight, though flightlessness has evolved repeatedly — in penguins, many rails, the kakapo, and others.
