Venetian glass

Venetian glass (Italian: vetro veneziano) is glassware made in Venice, typically on the island of Murano near the city, which has been a glassmaking centre since the 13th century. Prized for its brilliant colours, lightness and technical virtuosity, Venetian glass dominated European luxury glassmaking from the Renaissance into the 17th century, and Murano workshops still produce it today.

History

Venice inherited glassmaking skills from Byzantium and the Islamic world. In 1291, fearing fire, the Venetian Republic ordered all glass furnaces moved to Murano, concentrating the industry; guild regulations strictly protected trade secrets and forbade glassmakers to emigrate. Around 1450, Angelo Barovier developed cristallo, a glass of exceptional clarity made with purified ashes and manganese, rivalling rock crystal and revolutionising tableware. Venetian craftsmen perfected enamelling and gilding on glass, latticino (embedded threads of white or coloured glass), millefiori ("thousand flowers") caneworking, and imitation gemstones.

Decline and revival

As French and Bohemian glass — and later English lead crystal — competed, and as Venetian secrecy leaked through emigrating craftsmen, Murano's supremacy faded in the 18th century. The industry revived in the mid-19th century with firms such as Salviati, and again in the 20th century with designers and studios (Venini, Barovier & Toso, Seguso) producing art glass prized by collectors. Tourism sustains many workshops today, though authentic Murano glass competes with cheap imitations, prompting consortium trademarks guaranteeing origin.

Techniques

Venetian glass is a soda-lime "long" glass, workable for extended periods, favouring elaborate manipulation: blown vessels with applied prunts and pincered decoration, filigrana and reticello cane patterns, avventurina (glass flecked with copper crystals), and chalcedony glass imitating agate. Mirror plates and glass beads (conterie) were also major Venetian exports.
