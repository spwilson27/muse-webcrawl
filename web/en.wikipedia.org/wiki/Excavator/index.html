Excavator

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Excavators are heavy construction equipment primarily consisting of a boom, dipper (or stick), bucket and cab on a rotating platform known as the "house". The house sits atop an undercarriage with tracks or wheels. All movement and functions of a hydraulic excavator are accomplished through the use of hydraulic fluid, with hydraulic cylinders and hydraulic motors; mechanical cables, traditional for cable-operated excavators, have become obsolete.

History

The first excavators were steam-powered shovels developed in the 19th century; William S. Otis received a patent for the single-bucket steam shovel in 1839, and rail-mounted steam shovels built canals, railways and mines throughout the industrial era. In the early 20th century, manufacturers such as Bucyrus and Marion dominated the market. The decisive modern step came after the Second World War, when hydraulics replaced cables: the French Yumbo and the firms that became Poclain pioneered fully hydraulic machines in the 1950s–60s, and Japanese makers including Komatsu and Hitachi, plus Caterpillar, industrialised the design worldwide.

Types and uses

Excavators range from mini or compact excavators of under a tonne, used in landscaping and utilities work, through standard crawler and wheeled machines on construction sites, to giant mining excavators weighing hundreds of tonnes, such as the Caterpillar 6090 and the rope shovels descended from steam designs. Variants include dragline excavators, long-reach demolition machines, suction excavators, amphibious excavators and walking ("spider") excavators for steep slopes.

Beyond digging trenches, foundations and mines, excavators with interchangeable attachments — hydraulic breakers, grapples, augers, compactors and shears — handle demolition, forestry, material handling and dredging. The bucket can be reversed to form a backhoe configuration, and smaller tractor-mounted backhoe loaders are among the world's most common construction machines.
