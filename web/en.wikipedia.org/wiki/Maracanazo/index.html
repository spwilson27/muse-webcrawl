The Maracanazo (Portuguese: Maracanaço) is the name given to Uruguay's unexpected 2–1 victory over Brazil in the deciding match of the 1950 FIFA World Cup, played on 16 July 1950 at the newly built Maracanã Stadium in Rio de Janeiro. It is considered one of the greatest upsets in football history and a national trauma in Brazil, sometimes compared in its psychological impact to a military defeat.

Background

The 1950 World Cup had no single final; the title was decided by a four-team final round. Brazil had swept aside Sweden (7–1) and Spain (6–1), while Uruguay had drawn with Spain and beaten Sweden narrowly. Brazil needed only a draw in the last match to become champions. A crowd officially recorded at 173,850 — still the largest ever for a football match — filled the Maracanã, and Rio's mayor reportedly addressed the Brazilian players before the game as already-conquered world champions; gold medals with the players' names had reportedly been prepared.

The match

Uruguay's captain, Obdulio Varela, famously bought stacks of newspapers predicting Brazil's triumph and urged his teammates to ignore the crowd ("Los de afuera son de palo" — "the outsiders are made of wood"). Friaça put Brazil ahead two minutes into the second half, but Uruguay equalised through Juan Alberto Schiaffino in the 66th minute. In the 79th minute, Alcides Ghiggia dribbled past defender Bigode and shot inside the near post, beating goalkeeper Barbosa. The stadium fell into a silence Ghiggia later described by saying that only three people had silenced the Maracanã: Frank Sinatra, Pope John Paul II, and himself.

Aftermath

FIFA president Jules Rimet, who had prepared a speech in Portuguese congratulating Brazil, had to improvise as he handed the trophy to Varela amid near-silence. In Brazil, the defeat provoked a national inquiry into the team's character; goalkeeper Moacir Barbosa was scapegoated for the rest of his life, and the white kit worn by the team was abandoned as "cursed," replaced by the now-famous yellow shirt with green trim adopted in 1953. In Uruguay, the victory — the country's second World Cup, after 1930 — is celebrated as the pinnacle of its football history. The term Maracanazo has since been applied to other famous upsets, and Brazil's 7–1 semifinal defeat by Germany at the 2014 World Cup, in Belo Horizonte, was dubbed the Mineirazo in conscious echo of it.
