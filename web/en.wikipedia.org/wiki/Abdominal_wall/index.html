Abdominal wall

Part ofAbdomen
SystemMusculoskeletal
LayersSkin, superficial fascia, muscles, transversalis fascia, peritoneum

In human anatomy, the abdominal wall represents the boundaries of the abdominal cavity. It encloses the cavity and protects the internal organs, assists in breathing and posture, and can increase intra-abdominal pressure for coughing, defecation and childbirth.

Structure

The abdominal wall is commonly divided into the anterolateral wall and the posterior wall. From superficial to deep, its layers are the skin, superficial fascia (Camper's and Scarpa's fasciae), the external oblique, internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles with their aponeuroses, the transversalis fascia, extraperitoneal fat, and the parietal peritoneum.

In the midline the aponeuroses of the flat muscles interlace to form the linea alba and enclose the rectus abdominis within the rectus sheath. The posterior wall is formed by the lumbar vertebrae, the psoas major, quadratus lumborum and iliacus muscles, and the diaphragm.

Function

The abdominal wall protects the viscera, stabilises the trunk, flexes and rotates the vertebral column, and works with the diaphragm and pelvic floor to regulate intra-abdominal pressure.

Clinical significance

Weaknesses in the wall predispose to hernias, including inguinal, umbilical, incisional and Spigelian hernias. Surgical access to the abdominal cavity is gained through incisions through the wall, and defects may be repaired with sutures or prosthetic mesh.
