House of Valois

The House of Valois (French: Maison de Valois) was a cadet branch of the Capetian dynasty that succeeded the direct Capetians as kings of France in 1328. Valois kings ruled France until 1589, a span of 261 years, and cadet branches — Valois-Anjou, Valois-Burgundy and Valois-Alençon among them — held lands across France, the Low Countries, Italy and Naples.

Accession

Charles, Count of Valois, brother of Philip IV, founded the line. When the last direct Capetian, Charles IV, died in 1328 without a son, the French magnates chose Charles of Valois's son Philip VI as king, invoking the principle later called Salic law to exclude Edward III of England, whose claim came through his mother. The dispute ignited the Hundred Years' War (1337–1453).

The Hundred Years' War and recovery

The early Valois suffered catastrophic defeats at Crécy (1346) and Poitiers (1356), where John II was captured, and under Charles VI's madness France descended into civil war and the disaster of Agincourt (1415). Recovery came under Charles VII, aided by Joan of Arc, and France expelled the English from all but Calais by 1453. Louis XI then curbed the great feudatories, notably defeating Charles the Bold of the Valois-Burgundy branch in 1477.

Renaissance and religious wars

Francis I (1515–1547) made France a Renaissance power and a patron of Leonardo da Vinci, though his Italian ambitions ended in defeat at Pavia. The last Valois kings — Henry II and his sons Francis II, Charles IX and Henry III — presided over the French Wars of Religion between Catholics and Huguenots, including the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572. Henry III's assassination in 1589 ended the dynasty, and the crown passed to Henry IV of the House of Bourbon.
