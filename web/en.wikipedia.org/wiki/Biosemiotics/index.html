Biosemiotics

Biosemiotics studies sign processes (semiosis) in living organisms.
FieldSemiotics, biology, philosophy of biology
Key figuresJakob von Uexküll, Thomas Sebeok, Jesper Hoffmeyer, Kalevi Kull

Biosemiotics (from the Greek bios meaning "life" and semeion meaning "sign") is a field of semiotics and biology that studies the prelinguistic meaning-making, biological interpretation processes, production of signs and codes and communication processes in the biological realm.

Biosemiotics integrates findings from biology and semiotics and proposes a paradigmatic shift in the scientific view of life, demonstrating that semiosis (sign process, including meaning and interpretation) is one of its key features. The term "biosemiotic" was first used by Friedrich S. Rothschild in 1962, but Thomas Sebeok and Thure von Uexküll implemented the term and the field in the 1960s–1970s.

History

The roots of biosemiotics lie in the work of the Baltic German biologist Jakob von Uexküll (1864–1944), whose concept of the Umwelt — the perceptual world unique to each organism — anticipated the idea that organisms interpret their surroundings through species-specific signs. His Funktionskreis (functional circle) model described how an organism's perceptual signs (Merkzeichen) and effector signs (Wirkzeichen) form a meaningful whole.

In the second half of the 20th century, Thomas Sebeok developed zoosemiotics, the study of animal communication, and later promoted "global semiotics" in which semiosis is coextensive with life itself. The Copenhagen–Tartu school, including Jesper Hoffmeyer and Kalevi Kull, developed biosemiotics into an organised research field from the 1990s onward, with the journal Sign Systems Studies and later Biosemiotics (founded 2008) as central publication venues.

Main ideas

Biosemioticians hold that the cell is the minimal semiotic unit: DNA transcription, protein synthesis and cellular signalling all involve the interpretation of signs according to codes. Unlike the purely mechanistic metaphors of molecular biology (such as "genetic code" used only figuratively), biosemioticians argue that such codes are literal examples of sign processes. Related concepts include semiotic scaffolding, the code-duality of digital and analogue codes, and endosemiotics — sign processes within the body, such as the immune system's discrimination of self and non-self.

Branches

The field is commonly divided into several branches, including zoosemiotics (animal signs), phytosemiotics (sign processes in plants), endosemiotics, and eco-semiotics, as well as the study of sign processes in bacteria and fungi (microbial semiosis). Biosemiotics is closely related to, but distinct from, zoosemiotics and is associated with the Copenhagen–Tartu school of biosemioticians.
