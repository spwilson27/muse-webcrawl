Labour Day

Labour Day is an annual holiday celebrating the achievements of workers. In most countries it is observed on 1 May as International Workers' Day, arising from the international labour movement's campaign for the eight-hour day. In the United States and Canada, Labor Day falls on the first Monday of September. More than 80 countries celebrate an official Labour Day holiday on 1 May, and many others mark the occasion on different dates.

Observed1 May (most countries); first Monday of September (US, Canada)
SignificanceHonours workers and the labour movement
OriginHaymarket affair (1886), eight-hour day movement

Origins

In 1886, American labour unions called a general strike for 1 May demanding the eight-hour working day. In Chicago, the campaign culminated in the Haymarket affair, where a bomb thrown at police during a rally led to deaths and the controversial trial and execution of anarchist labour leaders. In 1889, the founding congress of the Second International in Paris declared 1 May a day of international demonstrations in their memory.

Observance

May Day is a public holiday across much of Europe, Asia, Latin America, and Africa, typically marked by union parades, speeches, and demonstrations for workers' rights. In the United Kingdom, Labour Day is observed as the May Day bank holiday on the first Monday of May. Australia celebrates Labour Day on varying dates by state, and New Zealand on the fourth Monday of October.

United States and Canada

To distance the observance from the Haymarket events, the US and Canada celebrate in September — the date of New York's first Labor Day parade in 1882. President Grover Cleveland signed it into federal law in 1894, days after the Pullman Strike.
