Aortic valve replacement

Aortic valve replacement is a cardiac surgical and interventional procedure in which a patient's diseased aortic valve is replaced with a prosthetic valve. It is performed most commonly for aortic stenosis — narrowing of the valve, usually from calcific degeneration or a congenital bicuspid valve — and for aortic regurgitation, in which the valve leaks. Without treatment, severe symptomatic aortic stenosis carries a poor prognosis; replacement restores valve function and survival.

SpecialtyCardiothoracic surgery, interventional cardiology
IndicationsSevere aortic stenosis or regurgitation
ApproachesOpen surgery (SAVR); transcatheter (TAVR/TAVI)
Prosthesis typesMechanical; bioprosthetic (porcine, bovine pericardial); homograft

Surgical replacement (SAVR)

In conventional surgical aortic valve replacement, the chest is opened (usually by median sternotomy, though minimally invasive approaches exist), the patient is placed on cardiopulmonary bypass, the heart is stopped, and the diseased valve is excised and replaced with a sewn-in prosthesis. First performed successfully in 1960 using the Starr–Edwards ball-and-cage valve, the operation became one of the most common major cardiac procedures.

Transcatheter replacement (TAVR)

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR, or TAVI), first performed in a human by Alain Cribier in 2002, delivers a collapsible bioprosthetic valve on a catheter, usually through the femoral artery, and expands it inside the native valve without removing it. Initially reserved for patients at prohibitive or high surgical risk, trials such as the PARTNER and CoreValve/Evolut series extended its use to intermediate- and low-risk patients, and it is now the most common method of aortic valve replacement in many countries.

Choice of prosthesis

Mechanical valves are very durable but require lifelong anticoagulation with warfarin because of thrombosis risk. Bioprosthetic (tissue) valves avoid long-term anticoagulation in most patients but degenerate over roughly 10–20 years, a trade-off weighed by age and patient preference: guidelines generally favour mechanical valves in younger patients and tissue valves in older patients. The Ross procedure, using the patient's own pulmonary valve in the aortic position, is an option mainly in children and young adults.

Complications

Risks include operative mortality (around 1–3% for isolated surgical replacement in contemporary series), stroke, bleeding, infection of the prosthesis (endocarditis), paravalvular leak, conduction disturbances requiring a pacemaker (more common after TAVR), and structural valve deterioration. Long-term follow-up with echocardiography is standard.
