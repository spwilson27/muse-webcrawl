Swing music is a style of jazz that developed in the United States during the late 1920s and early 1930s and became the dominant form of American popular music from 1935 until 1946 — the period known as the Swing Era. Swing uses a strong rhythmic "groove" in 4/4 time, in which the beat is swung (long–short subdivision of the beat), and is typically performed by big bands of 12–25 musicians arranged in saxophone, trumpet, trombone and rhythm sections, playing arrangements built around riffs and brief improvised solos.

History

Swing grew out of the dance-band tradition and the innovations of Fletcher Henderson, Duke Ellington, and the Kansas City bands of Bennie Moten and Count Basie, whose loose, riff-based style contrasted with the written arrangements of the East Coast. Benny Goodman's 1935 engagement at Los Angeles's Palomar Ballroom — broadcast nationwide — is conventionally taken as the start of the Swing Era, and his 1938 Carnegie Hall concert, with guests from the Basie and Ellington bands, legitimised jazz as concert music. Wartime ballrooms thrived on bands led by Glenn Miller, Tommy Dorsey, Artie Shaw and Harry James, while Black bands — Ellington, Basie, Chick Webb (with Ella Fitzgerald), Jimmie Lunceford, Lionel Hampton — supplied much of the music's creative core within a segregated industry.

Recording bans, wartime taxes on dancing, changing tastes and the rise of singers and small-group bebop ended the big bands' dominance after 1946. Swing persisted in the work of Basie and Ellington, fed into rhythm and blues and rock and roll, and enjoyed a popular "swing revival" in the 1990s alongside swing dancing (Lindy Hop, jitterbug). Swing's emphasis on a propulsive rhythm section — exemplified by Basie's team of Walter Page, Jo Jones, Freddie Green and Basie himself — permanently shaped jazz rhythm.

Characteristics

hallmarks include the swung eighth note, call-and-response between brass and reed sections, head arrangements built from repeated riffs, and soloists improvising over the arrangement. Danceability was central: tempos suited the Lindy Hop and other dances of the Savoy Ballroom in Harlem.
