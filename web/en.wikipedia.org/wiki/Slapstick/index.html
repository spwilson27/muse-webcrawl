Slapstick

Slapstick is a style of humor involving exaggerated physical activity that exceeds the boundaries of normal physical comedy. Slapstick may involve both intentional violence and violence by mishap, often resulting from inept use of props such as saws and ladders. The term arises from a double stick which, when wielded by a character in commedia dell'arte, produces a loud whacking noise with little force, and dates to the 16th century in Italy.

History

Physical comedy has roots in ancient Greek and Roman theatre and in the Italian commedia dell'arte, whose stock characters beat each other with the batte or slap stick. The style flourished in 19th-century music hall and vaudeville, then in early cinema: the films of Mack Sennett's Keystone Studios, Charlie Chaplin, Buster Keaton, Harold Lloyd, and the Marx Brothers made slapstick internationally popular, with chase scenes, pie fights, and elaborate falls performed without (or before) dialogue.

Television and later film

Slapstick remained central to the work of The Three Stooges, Lucille Ball, Jerry Lewis, Benny Hill, Rowan Atkinson's Mr. Bean, and Jim Carrey, and it is a staple of cartoons such as Tom and Jerry and Looney Tunes, where characters survive impossible violence. The style also survives in the "gag" traditions of Hong Kong action comedy, notably the films of Jackie Chan.

Characteristics

Slapstick routines typically involve mistaken identity, exaggerated reactions, collisions, pratfalls, and the misuse of everyday objects, with the comedy arising from the performer's timing and apparent — but not real — pain. Critics distinguish slapstick's broad, physical register from verbal wit and situational irony, though many comedians blend all three.
