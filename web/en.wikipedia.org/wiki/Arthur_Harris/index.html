Arthur Harris

Sir Arthur Harris

Born: 13 April 1892, Cheltenham

Died: 5 April 1984, Henley-on-Thames

Nickname: "Bomber" Harris

Command: RAF Bomber Command (1942–1945)

Air Chief Marshal Sir Arthur Travers Harris, 1st Baronet (1892–1984), commonly known as "Bomber" Harris, was Commander-in-Chief of RAF Bomber Command during the height of the Anglo-American strategic bombing campaign against Nazi Germany in the Second World War. A forceful advocate of area bombing of German cities, he remains one of the most controversial British commanders of the war.

Early career

Harris emigrated to Rhodesia as a teenager, served as a bugler and infantryman in the First World War's African campaigns, then joined the Royal Flying Corps, ending the war as a squadron commander. In the interwar RAF he flew in Iraq and India, pioneered night flying and long-range navigation, and rose through air staff posts.

Bomber Command

Appointed to lead Bomber Command in February 1942, Harris inherited a force whose night bombing had been shown to be grossly inaccurate. Acting on the Area Bombing Directive, he concentrated on morale-breaking attacks on industrial cities, beginning with the thousand-bomber raid on Cologne in May 1942. Under his command the offensive expanded through the Battle of the Ruhr, the firestorm at Hamburg (Operation Gomorrah, 1943), the costly Battle of Berlin, and the attack on Dresden in February 1945, which killed an estimated 25,000 people and became a lasting symbol of the bombing war. Harris resisted diverting his force to precision targets such as oil plants and transportation, though Bomber Command played major roles in the pre-D-Day transport plan and other campaigns.

Later life and legacy

Harris retired in 1946 and emigrated to South Africa, returning to Britain in 1953, when Churchill's government granted him a baronetcy. He defended his strategy for the rest of his life, arguing that it shortened the war; critics contend that area bombing was neither decisive nor morally defensible. Bomber Command's aircrew — 55,573 of whom died — received no campaign medal during Harris's lifetime of advocacy, and a national memorial was unveiled only in 2012; a statue of Harris erected in London in 1992 drew protests.
