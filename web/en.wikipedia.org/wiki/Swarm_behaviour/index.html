Swarm behaviour

[Image placeholder: swarm behaviour]

Swarm behaviour, or swarming, is a collective behaviour exhibited by entities, particularly animals, of similar size which aggregate together, perhaps milling about the same spot or moving en masse in a common direction. It is a highly interdisciplinary topic, studied by biologists, physicists, and computer scientists.

Examples in nature

Swarming is seen in bird flocks (murmurations), fish schools, insect swarms such as locusts and honey bees, krill, and herds of ungulates. The collective motion appears coordinated even though there is no central leader; instead, each individual follows simple local rules based on the positions and velocities of its neighbours.

Models

The boids model, proposed by Craig Reynolds in 1986, simulates flocking with three rules: separation (avoid crowding neighbours), alignment (steer toward the average heading of neighbours), and cohesion (steer toward the average position of neighbours). The Vicsek model and related self-propelled particle models from statistical physics reproduce the phase transitions between disordered and ordered collective motion seen in real swarms.

Benefits

Swarming can confuse predators, dilute the risk to any one individual, improve foraging efficiency, and enable tasks — such as the construction of nests by termites or the selection of a new hive site by bees — that no individual could accomplish alone.

Applications

Swarm principles underpin the field of swarm intelligence and swarm robotics, in which groups of simple robots coordinate through local interactions, and optimisation algorithms such as particle swarm optimisation and ant colony optimisation.
