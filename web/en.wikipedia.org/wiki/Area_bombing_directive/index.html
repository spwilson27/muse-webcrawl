Area bombing directive

RAF heavy bombers during a night raid on Germany.

The Area Bombing Directive was a British Air Ministry directive issued on 14 February 1942 to RAF Bomber Command during the Second World War. It formally authorised "area bombing" — the targeting of built-up areas of German cities, particularly their working-class housing — with the stated aim of destroying the morale of the enemy civilian population, "in particular of the industrial workers". The directive was issued after the Butt Report of 1941 had shown that precision bombing at night was largely ineffective, with only a small fraction of bombs falling near their targets.

Content and implementation

The directive named priority targets including Essen, Duisburg, Düsseldorf and the other cities of the Ruhr, and it removed earlier restrictions on attacking civilian areas. It coincided with the appointment of Air Marshal Arthur Harris as Commander-in-Chief of Bomber Command on 22 February 1942 and the introduction of Gee navigation aids and a "bomber stream" tactic. Under Harris, area bombing became the core of Bomber Command's night offensive, culminating in operations such as the Battle of the Ruhr, the firestorm raid on Hamburg (Operation Gomorrah, July 1943) and, near the war's end, the bombing of Dresden in February 1945, which followed a later clarification of the directive in January 1945.

Assessment

The policy remains one of the most debated aspects of the Allied war effort. Critics, including Bishop George Bell, questioned its morality at the time; post-war assessments have debated its effectiveness relative to its cost in aircrew and civilian lives. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey argued that attacks on specific industrial systems were more effective, though area attacks also diverted enormous German resources to air defence.
