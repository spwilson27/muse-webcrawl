Health system

A health system, also sometimes referred to as a health care system, is the organisation of people, institutions, and resources that deliver health care services to meet the health needs of target populations. The World Health Organization defines a health system as all organisations, people and actions whose primary intent is to promote, restore or maintain health.

Goals and functions

The WHO's framework identifies four key functions of a health system: stewardship (governance and policy), financing, resource generation (workforce, facilities, knowledge), and service delivery. Its goals include improving population health, responding to people's expectations, and ensuring fairness in financial contribution — protecting households from catastrophic health costs. The WHO's "building blocks" model describes six components: service delivery; health workforce; information; medical products, vaccines and technologies; health financing; and leadership and governance.

Models of provision and funding

National health systems are commonly grouped into models:

  • Beveridge model — tax-funded, government-provided care, free at the point of use (e.g. the United Kingdom's National Health Service).
  • Bismarck model — care provided by private or non-profit providers and funded through social health insurance contributions (e.g. Germany, France, Japan).
  • National health insurance — a single public payer funding largely private providers (e.g. Canada).
  • Out-of-pocket model — most care paid for directly by patients, common where systems are underdeveloped.

The United States operates a mixed system combining employer-sponsored private insurance, public programmes such as Medicare and Medicaid, and a significant uninsured population.

Performance and comparison

Health systems are compared on measures such as life expectancy, infant and maternal mortality, amenable mortality, coverage, waiting times, equity, and expenditure as a share of GDP. The WHO's World Health Report 2000 controversially ranked 191 member states' systems. Analysts note that the United States spends roughly twice the OECD average per capita while achieving lower life expectancy, whereas many European and East Asian systems achieve universal coverage at lower cost.

Challenges

Common pressures include ageing populations, the rising burden of chronic disease, workforce shortages, medical inflation, antimicrobial resistance, and pandemic preparedness — all highlighted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which strained even well-resourced systems.
