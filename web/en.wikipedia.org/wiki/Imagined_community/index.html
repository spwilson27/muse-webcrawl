Imagined community

An imagined community is a concept in political science and sociology developed by Benedict Anderson in his 1983 book Imagined Communities: Reflections on the Origin and Spread of Nationalism. Anderson depicts a nation as a socially constructed community, imagined by the people who perceive themselves as part of that group. The concept has become highly influential in the study of nationalism.

The concept

Anderson defined a nation as "an imagined political community — and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign". It is imagined because "the members of even the smallest nation will never know most of their fellow-members, meet them, or even hear of them, yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion". It is imagined as limited because even the largest nation has finite, if elastic, boundaries beyond which lie other nations; as sovereign because nations dream of being free under a sovereign state; and as a community because, regardless of actual inequality, the nation is conceived as a deep, horizontal comradeship.

Anderson emphasised that "imagined" does not mean "fabricated" or false, as critics sometimes assumed; communities are to be distinguished "not by their falsity/genuineness, but by the style in which they are imagined".

Origins

Anderson argued that the nation became imaginable through a combination of historical forces: the decline of Latin and of sacred script-languages, the erosion of dynastic realms and divine-right monarchy, the rise of print-capitalism — the mass production of books and newspapers in vernacular languages — and new conceptions of time, what he called "homogeneous, empty time", in which simultaneity is marked by clock and calendar. Print media allowed large numbers of people who would never meet to imagine themselves as part of the same community, moving through the same shared time.

Influence and criticism

The book became one of the most cited works in the social sciences, and the concept has been applied far beyond nationalism — to diasporas, religious movements, and even online communities. Critics have questioned Anderson's emphasis on print, his treatment of the Americas as the birthplace of nationalism, and whether the "imagining" of nations adequately explains the emotional force of nationalist sentiment and violence.
