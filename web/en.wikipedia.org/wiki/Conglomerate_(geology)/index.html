In geology, a conglomerate is a clastic sedimentary rock composed of a substantial fraction of rounded to subangular gravel-size clasts larger than 2 mm in diameter, set in a matrix of finer material such as sand, silt, or clay, and typically cemented by silica, calcite, or iron oxides. Conglomerates form when high-energy currents transport and round coarse fragments and deposit them where the energy drops.

Classification

Conglomerates are classified by clast composition and support fabric. Monomict conglomerates contain clasts of a single rock type, while polymict conglomerates mix many. Clast-supported conglomerates consist mainly of gravel with little matrix, whereas matrix-supported conglomerates (diamictites when poorly sorted and of glacial origin) have clasts floating in mud or sand. The angular counterpart of conglomerate is breccia, whose sharp-edged fragments indicate little transport.

Formation

Rounding of clasts requires abrasion during transport, so conglomerates record energetic environments: steep mountain streams and alluvial fans, braided river channels, wave-worked beaches, and some deep-marine turbidite systems. The rock types preserved as clasts allow geologists to reconstruct vanished source terrains — the foundation of provenance studies. Famous examples include the conglomerates of Death Valley's alluvial fans and the great gold-bearing conglomerates of the Witwatersrand Basin in South Africa, the source of much of the world's mined gold.
