The Advanced Boiling Water Reactor (ABWR) is a Generation III boiling water reactor (BWR) design developed jointly by General Electric, Toshiba and Hitachi. It is the most advanced BWR design to have entered commercial service, with a standard gross electrical output of about 1,350 megawatts.

Design

The ABWR builds on decades of boiling water reactor operating experience, principally in Japan, the United States and Sweden. Its principal innovations include internal recirculation pumps mounted inside the reactor pressure vessel, which eliminate the large external recirculation piping of earlier BWRs and the associated large-break loss-of-coolant accident risk; fine-motion control rod drives combining electric motors with hydraulic insertion; and a cylindrical reinforced-concrete containment vessel integrated with the reactor building.

The design uses a three-division emergency core cooling system, digital instrumentation and control, and a design-basis that targets a core damage frequency an order of magnitude lower than that of earlier plants. The reactor core contains 872 fuel assemblies in the standard design, with an 18- to 24-month refuelling cycle.

Operating units

The first ABWRs were units 6 and 7 of the Kashiwazaki-Kariwa plant in Japan, which entered commercial operation in 1996 and 1997 with construction times of roughly three years, among the fastest ever achieved for large nuclear units. Two further units operate at Hamaoka (unit 5) and Shika (unit 2) in Japan. Units were also constructed at Lungmen in Taiwan, though the plant was mothballed before fuel loading was completed.

In the United States, the ABWR received design certification from the Nuclear Regulatory Commission in 1997, and it was selected for the South Texas Project expansion and for proposed units in the United Kingdom at Wylfa and Oldbury by Horizon Nuclear Power, though both projects were later suspended or cancelled.

Successors

The ABWR forms the basis for the simpler Economic Simplified Boiling Water Reactor (ESBWR) design and for the smaller BWRX-300, both developed by GE Hitachi.
