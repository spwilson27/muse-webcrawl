Tiebreaker

In games and sports, a tiebreaker or tiebreak is any method used to determine a winner from among competitors who are tied at the end of a contest. Tiebreakers take many forms: additional play such as extra time or a shootout, a sudden-death period, comparison of secondary statistics accumulated during the contest, or entirely separate ranking criteria applied in league standings.

In individual sports

The best-known tiebreak is that of tennis, introduced in 1970, in which a set tied at six games all is decided by a tiebreak game: players alternate serves and the first to seven points with a margin of two wins the set. Grand Slam tournaments long allowed final sets to continue indefinitely, producing marathon matches such as the 2010 Wimbledon match between John Isner and Nicolas Mahut, which ended 70–68 in the fifth set; all four majors have since adopted final-set tiebreaks. In golf, tied players contest a playoff, either sudden death or over a set number of holes. In chess, tied matches are decided by rapid and blitz playoffs or by an Armageddon game.

In team sports and leagues

League standings use ordered lists of tiebreaking criteria, commonly head-to-head results, goal difference or points differential, and totals scored. Knockout tournaments that cannot end in a draw use extra time followed, if necessary, by a penalty shootout in association football, or overtime periods in North American sports. Some competitions historically used replays, countbacks, or even coin tosses and drawing of lots as a last resort.

In elections and voting

Tied votes are resolved variously by lot, by a casting vote of a presiding officer, or by a runoff election, depending on the jurisdiction and office.
