Orographic lift

Air forced upward by terrain cools and condenses on the windward side.

Orographic lift occurs when an air mass is forced from a low elevation to a higher elevation as it moves over rising terrain. As the air mass gains altitude, it expands and cools adiabatically, often to the point where its water vapour condenses, producing clouds and precipitation on the windward side of the terrain. Orographic lift is one of the principal mechanisms of precipitation formation in mountainous regions and a major control on regional climate.

Effects

Windward precipitation

Moist air ascending a mountain slope cools first at the dry adiabatic lapse rate and, once saturated, at the slower moist adiabatic rate, wringing out rain or snow on the windward slopes. Some of the wettest places on Earth owe their rainfall to orographic lift: the windward slopes of the Western Ghats during the Indian monsoon, the Olympic Mountains of Washington, and the trade-wind slopes of Hawai'i, where Mount Waiʻaleʻale is among the rainiest spots in the world.

Rain shadow

Having lost much of its moisture, the air descends on the leeward side and warms adiabatically, arriving hot and dry. The resulting rain shadow explains deserts and dry basins lying behind mountain ranges, such as the Great Basin behind the Sierra Nevada, Patagonia behind the Andes, and the interior of Central Asia. Related downslope windstorms include the Föhn of the Alps and the Chinook of the Rockies.

Cloud forms

Orographic lifting generates distinctive cloud types: cap clouds hugging summits, lenticular (standing wave) clouds downwind of ridges, banner clouds streaming from peaks such as the Matterhorn, and persistent fog banks on windward coasts. Such clouds are important cues for glider pilots, who exploit ridge lift and mountain waves to gain altitude.
