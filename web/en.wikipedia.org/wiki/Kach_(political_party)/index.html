Kach (Hebrew: כך, meaning "Thus") was an Orthodox Jewish, ultranationalist political party in Israel, founded in 1971 by Rabbi Meir Kahane. Rooted in the ideology that came to be known as Kahanism, the party advocated a theocratic Jewish state governed by Jewish religious law and the removal of most Arabs from Israel and the territories it controlled.

History

Meir Kahane, who had founded the Jewish Defense League in the United States in 1968, immigrated to Israel in 1971 and soon established Kach, taking its name and symbol — a clenched fist inside a Star of David — from his earlier movement. After several failed attempts, Kahane was elected to the Knesset in the 1984 election, where Kach won a single seat. His tenure was marked by controversy, boycotts by other members, and legislation restricting his parliamentary privileges.

In 1985 the Knesset passed a Basic Law amendment barring parties that incite racism, and in 1988 the Central Elections Committee disqualified Kach from that year's election, a decision upheld by the Supreme Court of Israel. Kahane was assassinated in New York in 1990. The party fragmented into Kach and a splinter group, Kahane Chai ("Kahane Lives").

Banning

Following the 1994 Cave of the Patriarchs massacre, in which Kach supporter Baruch Goldstein murdered 29 Palestinian worshippers in Hebron, the Israeli cabinet declared both Kach and Kahane Chai terrorist organizations under the 1948 Prevention of Terrorism Ordinance, and they were formally outlawed. The United States, Canada, and the European Union also listed the groups as terrorist organizations; the U.S. State Department later delisted them in 2022 on the grounds that they were defunct, while retaining sanctions related to their remnants.

Ideology

Kach advocated the transformation of Israel into a Jewish theocracy governed by Halakha, the annexation of all territory it regarded as part of the biblical Land of Israel, and the transfer of the Arab population. It denied the compatibility of Judaism and Western-style democracy as applied to non-Jews in a Jewish state. Critics and Israeli authorities characterized its platform as racist.

Legacy

Although Kach itself was banned, Kahanist ideas continued to influence Israeli far-right politics through successor movements and figures, including activists who later entered mainstream right-wing parties. Kahane's writings remain in circulation among supporters.
