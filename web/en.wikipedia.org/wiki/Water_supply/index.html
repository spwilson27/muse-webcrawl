Water supply

Reservoirs store raw water before treatment and distribution.

Water supply is the provision of water by public utilities, commercial organizations, community endeavors or individuals, usually via a system of pumps and pipes. Public water supply systems are crucial to properly functioning societies and are among the most important infrastructure for public health. A typical system abstracts raw water from rivers, lakes, reservoirs or aquifers, treats it to drinking-water quality, and distributes it under pressure through a network of mains to homes and businesses, storing it en route in water towers and service reservoirs.

History

Ancient civilizations built aqueducts, qanats and wells; Rome's aqueduct system is the most famous early example. The modern era began in the 19th century, when filtration and chlorination, together with separate sewer systems, dramatically cut waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid. Physician John Snow's 1854 investigation of a London cholera outbreak famously traced it to a contaminated pump on Broad Street. London's intercepting sewers, built under Joseph Bazalgette after the Great Stink of 1858, became a model for other cities.

Access and challenges

Global coverage has improved steadily: most of the world's population now uses at least a basic drinking-water service, but billions still lack safely managed supplies, and roughly half the world's population experiences severe water scarcity during at least part of the year. Sustainable Development Goal 6 targets universal access to safe drinking water and sanitation. Utilities worldwide struggle with aging pipes and non-revenue water — losses through leakage and theft that in some systems exceed 40%. Climate change, groundwater depletion and growing demand add pressure, making conservation and reuse central to supply planning.
