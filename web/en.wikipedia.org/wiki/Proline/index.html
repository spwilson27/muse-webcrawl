Proline (symbol Pro or P) is an organic acid classed as a proteinogenic amino acid, used in the biosynthesis of proteins. It is unique among the standard amino acids because its side chain loops back onto the amino group, forming a five-membered pyrrolidine ring and making the amino group secondary rather than primary; for this reason it is sometimes called an imino acid. L-Proline is non-essential in humans, who can synthesise it from glutamate.

Structure and properties

The cyclic structure of proline gives it exceptional conformational rigidity. In proteins, proline acts as a "helix breaker": it introduces kinks into alpha helices and is abundant in turns and in the fibrous protein collagen, where the repeating sequence Gly–Pro–Hyp (hydroxyproline) stabilises the triple helix. The peptide bond preceding proline can adopt the cis configuration far more readily than other peptide bonds, and the slow cis–trans isomerisation of prolyl bonds is a rate-limiting step in protein folding, catalysed by peptidyl-prolyl isomerase enzymes.

Biosynthesis and metabolism

Proline is synthesised from L-glutamate via glutamate-5-semialdehyde and Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate (P5C), using the enzymes P5C synthase and P5C reductase. Degradation runs in reverse through proline dehydrogenase (PRODH) back to glutamate. Defects in this pathway cause hyperprolinaemia; PRODH lies in the chromosomal region (22q11.2) deleted in DiGeorge syndrome. In plants and microorganisms, proline accumulates as a compatible osmolyte under drought and salt stress.

Uses

Proline and its hydroxylated derivative are used in pharmaceuticals, nutritional supplements and as chiral organocatalysts — L-proline catalyses asymmetric aldol reactions, a discovery that contributed to the 2021 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for asymmetric organocatalysis (awarded to Benjamin List and David MacMillan).
