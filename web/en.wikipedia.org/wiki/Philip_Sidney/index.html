Philip Sidney

Sir Philip Sidney (30 November 1554 – 17 October 1586) was an English poet, courtier, scholar, and soldier who is remembered as one of the most prominent figures of the Elizabethan age. His works include the sonnet sequence Astrophel and Stella, the prose romance The Countess of Pembroke's Arcadia, and The Defence of Poesy, one of the earliest works of English literary criticism. His death in battle against the Spanish at Zutphen made him a Protestant hero and a model of the ideal Renaissance courtier.

Early life

Sidney was born at Penshurst Place, Kent, the eldest son of Sir Henry Sidney, Lord Deputy of Ireland, and Mary Dudley, sister of Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester. Queen Elizabeth I was his godmother. He was educated at Shrewsbury School and Christ Church, Oxford, and from 1572 to 1575 travelled in Europe, meeting scholars and Protestant leaders and studying languages, literature, and statecraft.

Court and politics

At court Sidney became a favourite of Elizabeth, though his career was frustrated by her caution. A famous letter opposing her proposed marriage to the Duke of Anjou put him temporarily out of favour. He served as ambassador to the Holy Roman Emperor in 1577, was knighted in 1583, married Frances Walsingham, daughter of the spymaster Sir Francis Walsingham, and in 1585 became governor of Flushing in the Netherlands.

Literary works

Astrophel and Stella

Written around 1582 and published posthumously in 1591, this sequence of 108 sonnets and 11 songs—England's first great sonnet sequence—dramatises the poet Astrophel's unrequited love for Stella, modelled on Penelope Devereux. It transformed English lyric poetry.

The Arcadia

The Countess of Pembroke's Arcadia (written for his sister Mary, Countess of Pembroke) is a vast prose romance interwoven with verse, combining pastoral, chivalric adventure, and political philosophy. Sidney revised it extensively; the "New Arcadia" remained unfinished at his death.

The Defence of Poesy

Written in response to Stephen Gosson's attack on poetry, An Apology for Poetry (or The Defence of Poesy, c. 1580, published 1595) argues that poetry teaches and delights, moving readers to virtue better than history or philosophy.

Death and legacy

Serving under Leicester in the Dutch revolt against Spain, Sidney was wounded in the thigh at the Battle of Zutphen (22 September 1586) and died of infection 26 days later, aged 31. Legend holds that on the battlefield he gave his water bottle to a wounded soldier, saying "Thy necessity is greater than mine." His state funeral at St Paul's Cathedral was one of the grandest of the age, and his reputation as the perfect courtier—soldier, scholar, and poet—inspired a generation of English writers.
