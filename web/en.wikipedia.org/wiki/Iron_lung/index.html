Iron lung

An iron lung is a type of negative-pressure ventilator: a large cylindrical chamber that encloses a patient's body up to the neck and rhythmically varies the air pressure inside, forcing air into and out of the lungs. Iron lungs enabled people whose breathing muscles were paralysed — most famously by poliomyelitis — to survive respiratory failure.

History

The first widely used iron lung was developed by Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw at Harvard and first used clinically in 1928 at Boston Children's Hospital. Improved designs followed, notably John Haven Emerson's cheaper model of 1931. During the polio epidemics of the 1940s and 1950s, hospital wards were filled with rows of the machines, and the March of Dimes funded their manufacture and patients' long-term care. During the 1952 Copenhagen polio epidemic, manual positive-pressure ventilation through tracheostomies proved more effective for bulbar polio, paving the way for modern intensive care.

Decline and legacy

Mass vaccination with the Salk and Sabin polio vaccines from the mid-1950s virtually eliminated new polio cases in the developed world, and positive-pressure ventilators largely replaced iron lungs. A small number of survivors continued to rely on them for decades; Paul Alexander, who survived in an iron lung from 1952 until his death in 2024, became one of the best-known. The devices remain icons of the polio era and of medical technology's social impact.
