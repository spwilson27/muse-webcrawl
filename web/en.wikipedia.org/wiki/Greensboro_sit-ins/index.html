Greensboro sit-ins

The Greensboro sit-ins were a series of nonviolent protests in Greensboro, North Carolina, in 1960, in which African American students sat at a segregated Woolworth lunch counter and refused to leave after being denied service. The protests sparked a wave of sit-ins across the American South and became a landmark of the civil rights movement.

The first sit-in

On 1 February 1960, four freshmen from North Carolina Agricultural and Technical State University — Ezell Blair Jr. (later Jibreel Khazan), David Richmond, Franklin McCain, and Joseph McNeil, known as the "Greensboro Four" — bought school supplies at the F. W. Woolworth store on South Elm Street, then sat down at the whites-only lunch counter and politely ordered coffee. Refused service, they remained seated until the store closed.

Spread and outcome

Within days the protest grew to hundreds of students, expanded to other Greensboro stores, and was copied by students in dozens of Southern cities, often met by harassment, arrests, and violence. Sustained economic pressure from boycotts pushed Greensboro's stores to relent: on 25 July 1960 the Woolworth counter served its first Black customers. The sit-in movement also led, in April 1960, to the founding of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC).

Legacy

The original lunch counter section is preserved at the Smithsonian's National Museum of American History, and the former Woolworth building now houses the International Civil Rights Center and Museum, opened on the sit-ins' 50th anniversary in 2010.
