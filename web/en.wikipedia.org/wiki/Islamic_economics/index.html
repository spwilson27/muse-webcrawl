Islamic economics is the economic theory and practice derived from Islamic law (sharia) and the economic teachings of the Qur'an and the Sunnah. Its best-known application is Islamic banking and finance, which prohibits the charging of interest (riba) and instead structures transactions around profit-and-loss sharing, asset-backed financing and trade-based contracts, alongside obligatory almsgiving (zakat) and prohibitions on gambling and excessive uncertainty.

Principles

Classical and modern writers ground the system in the idea that wealth belongs ultimately to God and humans hold it in trust, so economic activity carries social obligations: fair dealing, fulfilment of contracts, prohibition of hoarding and exploitation, and redistribution through zakat and charity. Forbidden elements include riba, gharar (excessive uncertainty in contracts), maysir (gambling), and trade in prohibited goods such as alcohol and pork.

History and practice

Medieval Muslim jurists and thinkers such as Ibn Khaldun wrote extensively on markets, taxation and money, and instruments like the hawala transfer network and commenda-like partnerships were widely used. The modern discipline of "Islamic economics" as a distinct system took shape in the mid-20th century, notably in the writings of Abul A'la Maududi and others, and became institutional with the first Islamic banks in the 1970s (Dubai Islamic Bank, 1975), the Islamic Development Bank, and standard-setting bodies such as AAOIFI (1991). Islamic financial assets — banking, sukuk (Islamic bonds), takaful insurance and funds — have grown to several trillion US dollars, concentrated in the Gulf states, Malaysia and Iran. Critics, including economist Timur Kuran, question how far contemporary Islamic finance departs from conventional interest-based practice, while supporters cite its resilience and ethical aims.
