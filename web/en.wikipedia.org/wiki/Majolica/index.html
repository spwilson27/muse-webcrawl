Majolica

Majolica is a term applied to several kinds of pottery, most precisely to the tin-glazed earthenware made in Italy during the Renaissance, painted with scenes and ornament on a white opaque glaze. The technique came to Italy from the Islamic world through Spain — the name is thought to derive from Majorca, a transshipment point for Hispano-Moresque ware — or from a corruption of opus de Mallorca. In the nineteenth century the word was reused in English for a different product: brightly coloured lead-glazed "majolica ware" popular in Victorian Britain and America.


An Italian Renaissance majolica dish painted with a narrative scene (istoriato).

Italian maiolica

Italian potters in centres such as Faenza (whose name gave French faïence), Deruta, Gubbio, Urbino, Siena, and Florence developed tin-glazing to its height between about 1480 and 1600. The porous earthenware body was coated with a glaze made opaque white with tin oxide, providing a ground for painting with metal-oxide pigments that fused into the glaze during a second firing. Products included apothecary jars (albarelli), drug and syrup vessels, pilgrim flasks, and great display dishes painted istoriato — with narrative scenes from classical mythology, the Bible, and contemporary chronicles, often copied from printed engravings. Gubbio and Deruta were famous for metallic lustreware, giving iridescent ruby and gold effects revived from Islamic and Spanish techniques.

Spread of tin-glazed ware

Italian majolica workers carried the technique across Europe, founding the traditions known as faience in France, Delftware in the Netherlands, talavera in Spain and Mexico, and English delftware. Each adapted the white ground and painted decoration to local tastes and, later, to competition with Chinese porcelain.

Victorian majolica

At the Great Exhibition of 1851, the Staffordshire firm of Minton introduced brightly coloured lead-glazed earthenware modelled in naturalistic relief — leaves, shells, animals — and marketed it as "Palissy ware" and later "majolica", borrowing the Renaissance name. Copied widely in Britain, France, and the United States (where firms such as Griffen, Smith and Hill sold "Etruscan majolica"), it became a mass-market fashion for tableware, jardinières, and sardine boxes, before falling from favour around 1900.
