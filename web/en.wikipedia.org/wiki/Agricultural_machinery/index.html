Agricultural machinery is machinery used in the operation of farms and other agricultural enterprises. Mechanisation replaced human and animal labour across nearly every farm task — ploughing, sowing, cultivating, irrigating, harvesting, threshing, transporting and processing — and was a central driver of the enormous rises in agricultural productivity of the 19th and 20th centuries, releasing most of the workforce in industrialised countries from farm labour.

History

Early improvements included Jethro Tull's seed drill (1701) and mechanical reapers and threshers in the 19th century. Steam-powered traction engines hauled ploughs and drove threshing machines from the mid-1800s; the internal-combustion tractor — standardised by machines such as the Fordson (1917) and fitted with the three-point hitch and power take-off in the Ferguson system — became the universal farm prime mover by the mid-20th century. Self-propelled combine harvesters, mechanical cotton pickers and milking machines completed the transformation.

Principal types

Modern machinery includes tractors and implements (ploughs, harrows, seeders, sprayers, mowers, balers), harvesting machines (combines, forage harvesters, pickers), irrigation equipment (centre pivots, pumps), livestock equipment (milking machines, feed mixers) and materials-handling machines (loaders, trailers, grain augers). Implements increasingly incorporate electronics: GPS autosteering, yield mapping, variable-rate application and telematics characterise "precision agriculture", and manufacturers are developing autonomous and electric machines.

Effects

Mechanisation raised yields and cut the farm workforce to a few percent of employment in developed economies, but it also encouraged larger fields and farms, capital-intensive operations, and dependence on fuel and machinery manufacturers — issues debated in discussions of sustainable agriculture and the "right to repair".
