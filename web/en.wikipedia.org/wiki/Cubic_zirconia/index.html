Cubic zirconia (CZ) is the cubic crystalline form of zirconium dioxide (ZrO2), synthesised in laboratories and widely used as an inexpensive diamond simulant. (It is not to be confused with zircon, ZrSiO4, a natural gemstone.) Because zirconia's melting point is extremely high (about 2,758 °C), and the cubic phase is normally unstable at room temperature, CZ is grown by the "skull melting" technique with stabilisers such as yttrium oxide.

History

The mineral baddeleyite is the natural monoclinic form of zirconia; the stabilised cubic form was developed in the 1930s for industrial ceramics, and Soviet scientists at the Lebedev Physical Institute (FIAN) in Moscow perfected skull-crucible growth in the 1970s — the Soviet trade name "Fianit" derives from the institute. Commercial jewellery production followed from about 1976.

Properties versus diamond

CZ is optically flawless and colourless when well made, slightly softer (8–8.5 Mohs vs 10) and much denser than diamond, and shows more "fire" (dispersion) but less brilliance (lower refractive index). It is a thermal insulator where diamond is an excellent conductor, so simple thermal testers readily distinguish them; CZ also tends to scratch and cloud with wear over years.

Uses

Beyond jewellery, zirconia ceramics derived from the same chemistry are used in dental crowns, oxygen sensors, and knife blades, though those are typically tetragonal-phase materials rather than gem CZ.
