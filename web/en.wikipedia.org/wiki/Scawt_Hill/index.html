Scawt Hill

Scawt Hill is a volcanic plug in County Antrim, Northern Ireland, famous in geological literature as the classic locality where contact metamorphism of chalk by an igneous intrusion produced an unusual suite of rare calcium-silicate minerals.

Geology

The hill is the eroded remnant of a Palaeogene volcanic vent associated with the Antrim lava field, part of the North Atlantic Igneous Province. A plug of olivine dolerite intruded Cretaceous chalk (the Ulster White Limestone) about 60 million years ago. Heat from the intrusion baked and recrystallised the surrounding chalk, and chemical exchange between the magma and the limestone produced a skarn-like assemblage of high-temperature minerals.

Mineralogy

Scawt Hill is the type locality for several rare minerals, most notably larnite (β-calcium orthosilicate), first described from here in 1929 by Cecil Tilley, and scawtite, which is named after the hill itself. Other minerals found in the metamorphic aureole include spurrite, rankinite, merwinite, gehlenite, wollastonite and hydrogrossular. The locality remains a standard teaching example of limestone assimilation and contact metamorphism by basaltic magma.
