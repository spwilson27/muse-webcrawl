Battle of Bouvines

The Battle of Bouvines was fought on 27 July 1214 near the village of Bouvines in northern France. It was one of the most decisive battles of the European Middle Ages, in which King Philip II of France defeated a coalition led by Emperor Otto IV, John, King of England, and Count Ferrand of Flanders.

Background

Philip II had seized Normandy and other Angevin lands from King John, who sought to recover them by building a continental alliance. John landed in western France while Otto and the Flemish counts attacked from the north-east, aiming to converge on Paris. Philip intercepted the northern army at Bouvines on a Sunday as it crossed a bridge over the Marque.

Battle

The French formed in three divisions. On the right, French men-at-arms repelled Flemish knights; in the centre Philip himself was unhorsed and nearly killed before his household knights rescued him. Otto fled the field as French forces closed in. On the left, Ferrand and Renaud were captured after hard fighting. The coalition army broke, leaving many prisoners.

Aftermath

Ferrand and Renaud were imprisoned for years. Otto was deposed soon afterwards, leaving Frederick II as emperor. John returned to England weakened, a context for the barons' revolt that produced Magna Carta in 1215. In France the victory greatly strengthened the Capetian monarchy and is still commemorated as a foundational national triumph.
