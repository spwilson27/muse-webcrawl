Overview

A great circle is the largest possible circle that can be drawn on a sphere: it is the intersection of the sphere with a plane passing through the sphere's centre, and it divides the sphere into two equal hemispheres. On Earth, the Equator is a great circle, and every meridian forms one half of a great circle completed by its opposite meridian.

Circles of latitude other than the equator are not great circles, because their planes do not pass through the centre; navigators call them small circles.

Shortest paths

The shortest route between two points on a sphere follows the arc of the great circle through them — which is why long-haul air routes between, say, Europe and Japan curve far north over the Arctic on a flat map, even though they are the straightest possible paths on the globe. Sailors and pilots compute great-circle distances and courses, and radio operators aim antennas along great-circle bearings.

Great-circle geometry is also the basis of spherical trigonometry, used in astronomy for the celestial sphere.
