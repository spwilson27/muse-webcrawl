Student loans in the United States

Most US student debt is held by the federal government

Student loans in the United States are a form of financial aid used to help students pay for post-secondary education and associated costs, including tuition, fees, books and living expenses. Total outstanding student debt in the United States has grown to roughly $1.6–1.8 trillion, owed by more than 40 million borrowers, making it one of the largest categories of household debt after mortgages.

Federal and private loans

The great majority of the debt—over 90 percent—is owed to the federal government through the William D. Ford Federal Direct Loan Program, which offers Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized Loans to undergraduates, PLUS loans to parents and graduate students, and Perkins loans (discontinued in 2017). Earlier loans made under the bank-based FFEL programme remain outstanding. Private student loans, made by banks and non-bank lenders, carry market interest rates, lack most federal borrower protections, and are harder to discharge.

Repayment and policy

Federal borrowers can choose among standard, graduated and extended repayment plans, as well as income-driven repayment plans that cap payments at a percentage of discretionary income and forgive remaining balances after 20–25 years. Public Service Loan Forgiveness cancels balances for borrowers employed full-time in qualifying public service after 120 qualifying payments. Unlike most consumer debt, student loans are exceptionally difficult to discharge in bankruptcy, generally requiring a showing of "undue hardship" under the Brunner test. Delinquency and default rates, debt-to-earnings concerns at for-profit colleges, and proposals for large-scale cancellation by executive action have made student debt a persistent subject of national political debate, intensified by the COVID-19 payment pause of 2020–2023.
