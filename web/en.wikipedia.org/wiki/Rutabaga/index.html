Rutabaga (North American English) or swede (British English), Brassica napus (Napobrassica Group), is a root vegetable that originated as a hybrid between the cabbage (Brassica oleracea) and the turnip (Brassica rapa). The roots are eaten in a variety of ways, and the leaves can be eaten as a leaf vegetable. The plant grows best in cool climates and is known for its hardiness, long storage life, and sweet, yellow flesh.

History

The first printed reference to a cabbage–turnip hybrid vegetable comes from the Swiss botanist Gaspard Bauhin in 1620, who noted it growing wild in Sweden. It is generally believed to have originated in Scandinavia or Russia and was introduced to England in the late 18th century — hence the name "Swedish turnip," shortened to "swede." The word rutabaga derives from the Swedish dialectal rotabagge ("root ram/bag"). Rutabaga became a staple in northern Europe; in Germany the Steckrübenwinter ("rutabaga winter") of 1916–17, when the vegetable became a near-subsistence food amid wartime shortages, left it a lasting association with hardship.

Description and cultivation

Rutabaga is a biennial grown as an annual. Its swollen hypocotyl root is round to elongated, with yellow flesh and skin that is greenish to purple above ground and creamy below. Compared with turnips, rutabagas are larger, smoother, sweeter, and store better, often for months in cool conditions. They are sown in late spring for autumn harvest and tolerate frost, which concentrates sugars and improves flavour.

Culinary use

Rutabaga is eaten mashed (classically with butter and pepper), roasted, in soups and stews, and baked into pasties. In Scotland, mashed swede ("neeps") is the traditional accompaniment to haggis, served with mashed potatoes ("tatties"). In Finland, lanttulaatikko (rutabaga casserole) is a standard Christmas dish. The roots are also an important livestock fodder and are carved into lanterns at Halloween in parts of Scotland and northern England, a tradition predating the pumpkin lantern.

Nutrition

Rutabaga is a good source of vitamin C, potassium, manganese, and dietary fibre, and contains glucosinolates typical of brassicas. A 100-gram serving provides roughly 37 kilocalories.
