Moll Flanders

The Fortunes and Misfortunes of the Famous Moll Flanders is a novel by Daniel Defoe, first published in 1722. It purports to be the true account of the life of the eponymous Moll, detailing her exploits from birth until old age.

By 1721, Defoe had become a recognised novelist, with the success of Robinson Crusoe in 1719. His political work was tapering off, along with the influence of Robert Harley, 1st Earl of Oxford and Earl Mortimer, who had been a major patron and political ally. Defoe wrote this novel at a high level of reportorial skill; he combined his own youthful misadventures with the criminal exploits of the women he had observed in the tradition of "Newgate" literature.

Plot summary

Moll's mother is a convict in Newgate Prison in London who is given a reprieve by "pleading her belly," a reference to the custom of postponing the sentences of pregnant women. Her mother is eventually transported to Virginia, and Moll is raised from the age of three until adolescence by a kind foster mother. She gets attached to a household as a servant where she is loved by both sons, the elder of whom convinces her to "act like they were married" in bed. Unwilling to marry her, he persuades her to marry his younger brother. After five years of marriage, she then is widowed, leaves her children in the care of in-laws, and begins honing the skill of passing herself off as a fortuned widow to attract a man who will marry her and provide her with security.

The first time she does this, her "gentleman-tradesman" husband goes bankrupt and flees to mainland Europe, leaving her on her own with their child. The second time, she makes a match that leads her to Virginia with a kindly man who introduces her to his mother. After three children (one dies), Moll learns that her mother-in-law is actually her biological mother, which makes her husband her half-brother. She dissolves the marriage and after continuing to live with her brother and their children for three years, she leaves, travels back to England, and stays in Bath to seek a new husband.

In Bath, she meets a man whose wife is mentally ill and locked away. They become close friends and eventually cohabit, despite Moll hesitating at first. Their arrangement lasts three years, and eventually in a twist he repents and returns to his wife, leaving Moll without a partner, friend, security, or money. After more years as an itinerant fortune-hunter, she marries a Lancashire man who had been falsely represented to her as wealthy while she represented herself the same to him. They call it quits after realizing neither has any money, but Moll soon becomes pregnant by him and he leaves to be a highwayman, as criminals are often referred to in the novel as gentlemen.

Themes

The novel is often cited as an early example of the English novel and of the picaresque tradition, tracing a woman's survival through economic necessity, crime, and repentance. Defoe's narrative voice adopts Moll's first-person perspective with a framing "editor's" preface that claims to have polished Moll's rougher language.
