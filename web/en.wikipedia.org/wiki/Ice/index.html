Ice is water that is frozen into a solid state, typically forming at or below temperatures of 0 °C (32 °F). Depending on the presence of impurities such as particles of soil or bubbles of air, it can appear transparent or a more or less opaque bluish-white colour.

Ice occurs abundantly in nature: as snow, frost, sea ice, glaciers, ice sheets and icebergs. It plays a central role in Earth's climate and water cycle and in many human activities, from food preservation to winter sports.

Physical properties

Unusually for a solid, ice is less dense than liquid water (about 0.917 g/cm³ versus 1.000 g/cm³), so it floats. This results from the open hydrogen-bonded structure of the common crystalline form, ice Ih, in which each water molecule is bonded to four neighbours in a hexagonal lattice. More than a dozen other crystalline and amorphous phases of ice are known at high pressures or low temperatures.

Ice is slippery because of a thin quasi-liquid layer at its surface, a fact first discussed by Michael Faraday. Its melting point decreases with pressure, which has implications for glacier flow.

Formation and occurrence

On Earth, ice forms wherever temperatures fall below freezing: in polar regions, high mountains, and seasonal winter climates. Sea ice covers large parts of the Arctic and Southern Oceans. Beyond Earth, water ice is common on Mars, on icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus, and in comets.

Uses

Humans have harvested and manufactured ice for cooling and preservation since antiquity; the 19th-century ice trade shipped natural ice worldwide. Modern refrigeration largely replaced it, but ice remains used in food and drink, medicine, sports, and industry.
