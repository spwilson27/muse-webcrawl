Elderflower cordial

Elderflower cordial is a soft drink made largely from a refined sugar and water solution flavoured with the flowers of the European elder (Sambucus nigra L.). Historically, cordials were tonic preparations, often alcoholic, intended to stimulate the heart; the modern elderflower cordial is a non-alcoholic concentrate diluted with water, sparkling water or other drinks before serving.

Production

The creamy-white elderflower heads are gathered in late spring. To make the cordial, the flowers are steeped in a hot sugar syrup, usually with lemon juice and zest — citric acid helps to balance the sweet, fragrant, muscatel flavour and acts as a preservative. The mixture is left to infuse for one to several days, then strained and bottled. Commercial brands are widely sold in the United Kingdom and Scandinavia; homemade versions are a seasonal tradition and keep for only a few weeks without freezing or pasteurisation.

Uses

Elderflower cordial is most commonly diluted as a refreshing summer drink. It is also used as a flavouring in desserts and cakes, folded into whipped cream or icing, and in cocktails — notably with gin, prosecco or champagne. A sparkling elderflower drink ("elderflower pressé") is a ready-diluted carbonated form. Elderflower flavouring appears in sweets, and the same flowers are used to make elderflower fritters and homemade "champagne".

Related drinks

In Romania, a similar drink called socată is made by fermenting elderflowers with water, sugar, lemon and yeast, producing a lightly fizzy, mildly alcoholic beverage. Elderflower drinks are traditional across much of central and northern Europe.
