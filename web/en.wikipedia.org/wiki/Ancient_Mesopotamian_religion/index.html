RegionMesopotamia (Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia, Assyria)
Periodc. 3500 BC – early centuries AD
Chief deitiesAnu, Enlil, Enki/Ea, Inanna/Ishtar, Marduk, Ashur

Ancient Mesopotamian religion refers to the religious beliefs and practices of the peoples of Sumer, Akkad, Babylonia, and Assyria over some three millennia. It was a polytheistic system in which thousands of named deities — gods of sky, storm, water, sun, moon, love, and war — governed the cosmos and each city honoured its patron god, whose temple household stood at the centre of civic life. Humans, in the dominant myths, were created to serve the gods, relieving them of labour by producing food and maintaining temples.

Pantheon and temples

The senior gods included Anu (sky), Enlil (wind and authority, patron of Nippur), and Enki/Ea (fresh water and wisdom, patron of Eridu), alongside Inanna/Ishtar (love and war), Utu/Shamash (sun and justice), and Nanna/Sin (moon). In the first millennium BC, Marduk of Babylon and Ashur of Assyria rose to national supremacy. Temples, often surmounted by a ziggurat, were staffed by priests who fed and clothed the cult statue in daily rituals; great festivals, above all the Babylonian New Year (Akitu), reaffirmed the god's kingship and the human king's legitimacy.

Myth and the afterlife

Major literary works include the Epic of Gilgamesh, the creation epic Enūma Eliš, and the Atrahasis flood story, motifs from which echo in later Near Eastern literature. The afterlife was generally pictured as a dreary netherworld (Kur) where all the dead ate dust, so religious effort concentrated on this life: divination (especially extispicy, reading sheep livers), omens, incantations, and exorcism sought to secure divine favour and avert evil.

Decline

Mesopotamian cults survived the Persian, Hellenistic, and Parthian periods — the last datable cuneiform texts are astronomical tablets of the first century AD — before gradually disappearing as Christianity and later Islam spread through the region.
