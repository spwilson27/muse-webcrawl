Jonas Edward Salk (28 October 1914 – 23 June 1995) was an American virologist and medical researcher who developed one of the first successful polio vaccines — the inactivated poliovirus vaccine (IPV), announced as safe and effective on 12 April 1955 after a field trial involving some 1.8 million children.

Salk, working at the University of Pittsburgh with March of Dimes funding, used virus killed with formaldehyde so it could not cause disease but still provoked immunity. Famously, he did not patent the vaccine — asked who owned the patent, he replied 'the people', likening it to patenting the sun. He later founded the Salk Institute for Biological Studies in La Jolla, California, and worked on an HIV vaccine in his final years. Albert Sabin's live oral vaccine (1961) complemented Salk's; both remain in eradication use, with IPV now favoured in polio-free countries.

Impact

US polio cases collapsed within a few years of the vaccine's licensing; the terror of summer polio seasons, iron lungs and closed swimming pools receded from public life. Salk became a national hero, though relations with Sabin and parts of the scientific establishment were strained by priority disputes.
