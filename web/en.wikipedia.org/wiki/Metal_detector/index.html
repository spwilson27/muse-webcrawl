Metal detector

A hobbyist metal detector in use

A metal detector is an instrument that detects the nearby presence of metal. Metal detectors are useful for finding metal objects on the surface, underground, or underwater. The unit itself consists of a control box and a shaft to which is attached a search coil (head); the operator sweeps the coil over the ground or object. When the coil passes near a piece of metal, it produces an audible tone or a needle movement on an indicator.

Operating principles

Most modern detectors operate on electromagnetic induction. A transmitter coil carries an alternating current that generates an oscillating magnetic field. This field induces eddy currents in nearby conductive objects, which in turn generate their own magnetic field that is picked up by a receiver coil. Common technologies include very low frequency (VLF) detectors, which can discriminate between metals by phase shift, pulse induction (PI) detectors, favored for beaches and underwater use, and beat-frequency oscillation (BFO) designs. Multi-frequency and simultaneous multi-frequency systems have become common in advanced hobby units.

History

Early metal detectors were developed in the late 19th century; Alexander Graham Bell devised an induction balance in 1881 in an attempt to locate the bullet lodged in U.S. President James Garfield. Large-scale development followed during the World Wars for mine detection, notably the work of Polish officer Józef Kosacki, whose design was used at the Second Battle of El Alamein. Postwar advances in transistor electronics led to compact portable detectors and a popular treasure-hunting hobby from the 1960s onward.

Applications

Metal detectors are used in security screening at airports, courthouses, and events (both walk-through arches and hand-held wands), demining and unexploded ordnance clearance, archaeology, treasure hunting and prospecting, construction and utility work, and industrial quality control in food and pharmaceutical production.
