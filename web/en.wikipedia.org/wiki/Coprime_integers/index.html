Coprime integers

In number theory, two integers a and b are coprime, relatively prime, or mutually prime if the only positive integer that divides both of them is 1. Equivalently, their greatest common divisor is 1:

gcd(a, b) = 1

For example, 14 and 15 are coprime, whereas 14 and 21 are not (both share the factor 7). The number 1 is coprime to every integer. Coprimality can be tested with the Euclidean algorithm, and Bézout's identity states that a and b are coprime if and only if there exist integers x and y with ax + by = 1.

Properties

If a and b are coprime, then: any common divisor-related properties follow — a divides bc implies a divides c (Euclid's lemma generalised); a and b have modular inverses modulo each other when the modulus is taken appropriately; and the pair generates the unit group counted by Euler's totient function: among the integers 1..n, exactly φ(n) are coprime to n. Sets of integers may be setwise coprime (gcd of the whole set is 1) or the stronger pairwise coprime, where every pair is coprime — as in 6, 10, 15 (setwise but not pairwise) versus 6, 35 (pairwise).

Probability

For two large randomly chosen integers, the probability that they are coprime tends to 6/π² ≈ 0.6079, a celebrated result connecting elementary number theory with the Basel problem, since the probability generalises to 1/ζ(s) for s-tuples. More generally the probability that k random integers are coprime is 1/ζ(k).

Applications

Coprimality is fundamental in modular arithmetic (invertibility modulo n), RSA cryptography (the exponent must be coprime to φ(n)), gear design (hunting-tooth ratios), and in defining reduced fractions: a fraction a/b is in lowest terms exactly when a and b are coprime.
