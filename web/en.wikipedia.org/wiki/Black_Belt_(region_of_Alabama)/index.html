The Black Belt is a region of the U.S. state of Alabama that stretches across the central part of the state from the Mississippi border to the Georgia border. The term originally referred to the exceptionally dark, fertile topsoil of its chalk prairie, part of the larger Black Belt Prairie physiographic region extending into Mississippi; because this soil was ideal for cotton, the region became heavily worked by enslaved African Americans, and "Black Belt" later also described its majority-Black population and culture.

History

The underlying Selma Chalk, laid down in the Cretaceous period, weathered into rich black prairie soils. After the forced removal of the Creek and Choctaw peoples in the 1830s, white planters established large cotton plantations worked by enslaved labourers; by 1860 some Black Belt counties had enslaved populations exceeding 80 percent. After emancipation, sharecropping and tenancy dominated, and poverty persisted. The region was central to the civil rights movement — Selma was the starting point of the 1965 Selma to Montgomery marches, and Montgomery, on the region's eastern edge, hosted the 1955–56 bus boycott. Booker T. Washington described the region in Up from Slavery (1901), and Tuskegee Institute lies just east of the region.

Today

The Alabama Black Belt, usually defined as between 17 and 23 counties (definitions vary, including those used by the Alabama Tombigbee Regional Commission and the Southeast Crescent Regional Commission), remains predominantly rural, with economies based on agriculture (now including catfish farming and cattle), timber, and heritage tourism. It is noted for persistent poverty and infrastructure challenges — including inadequate wastewater systems — alongside a rich cultural heritage of music, folk art (such as the Gee's Bend quilters) and civil-rights history.
