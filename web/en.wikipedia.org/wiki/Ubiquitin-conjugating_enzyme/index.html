Ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme

Ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes, also known as E2 enzymes, perform the second step in the ubiquitination cascade that tags proteins with ubiquitin, most famously targeting them for degradation by the proteasome. Dozens of E2 enzymes exist in humans, and their interactions with E3 ligases determine substrate specificity.

Mechanism

An E1 ubiquitin-activating enzyme first activates ubiquitin in an ATP-dependent reaction and transfers it to a cysteine residue on an E2, forming a thioester intermediate. The E2 then cooperates with an E3 ubiquitin ligase to attach ubiquitin to a lysine on the target protein. Depending on the E2–E3 pair, ubiquitin chains of different linkages (such as K48 or K63) are built, encoding different cellular outcomes.

Families

E2 enzymes share a conserved ubiquitin-conjugating catalytic (UBC) domain of roughly 150 amino acids. Some, like the UBE2V family members, lack the catalytic cysteine and act as cofactors. Mutations in E2 genes are linked to intellectual disability and other disorders.
