The jansky (symbol Jy, plural janskys) is a non-SI unit of spectral flux density, or spectral irradiance, used especially in radio astronomy. It is equivalent to 10−26 watts per square metre per hertz. The unit is named after the American physicist and radio engineer Karl Guthe Jansky, who in 1932 discovered radio waves emanating from the Milky Way.

Definition

The flux density or monochromatic flux, S, of a source is the integral of its spectral radiance over the source's solid angle. Janskys measure this per-unit-frequency quantity: 1 Jy = 10−26 W m−2 Hz−1 = 10−23 erg s−1 cm−2 Hz−1 in cgs units. Because astronomical radio sources are extremely faint, the small size of the jansky makes it convenient: bright sources such as the Sun, Cassiopeia A, and Cygnus A reach thousands of janskys at metre wavelengths, while the faint sources studied by modern interferometers are measured in millijanskys and microjanskys.

Usage

The jansky is used for continuous radio, infrared, and other electromagnetic emission measurements across astronomy, though it applies strictly to flux per unit frequency; per-unit-wavelength quantities require conversion. Care is also needed with polarization: catalogues may quote total intensity (Stokes I) or a single polarization component. In the AB magnitude system, a flux density of 3631 Jy corresponds to magnitude zero.

Karl Jansky

Karl Guthe Jansky (1905–1950), working at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Holmdel, New Jersey, identified a steady hiss in his recordings that tracked sidereal time and peaked toward the centre of the Milky Way — the first detection of extraterrestrial radio emission, effectively founding radio astronomy. The unit was adopted in his honour after his death.
