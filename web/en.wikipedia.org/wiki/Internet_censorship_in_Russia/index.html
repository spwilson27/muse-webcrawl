Internet censorship in Russia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Roskomnadzor, the federal service supervising communications and media

Internet censorship in Russia is enforced on the basis of several laws and through a variety of mechanisms. The federal executive body responsible for overseeing media and communications is Roskomnadzor, which maintains a centralised register of banned content and orders internet service providers to block access to listed websites, pages and IP addresses.

Legal framework

A 2012 law created a unified blacklist ostensibly aimed at child pornography, material promoting drug use and content encouraging suicide, but critics argued it could be applied far more broadly. Subsequent legislation banned "extremist" content, material deemed to offend religious feelings, and information about unsanctioned protests. The 2016 "Yarovaya law" required providers to store users' communications and metadata and to hand them to security services on request.

The 2019 "sovereign internet" law required traffic exchange points to be registered, obliged providers to install state-supplied deep packet inspection equipment (known as TSPU), and provided for a national domain name system, giving authorities the technical capacity to throttle or isolate the Russian internet (RuNet) from the global network.

Blocked services and escalation

Over the 2010s and 2020s Russia blocked or restricted numerous platforms, including LinkedIn (2016, for refusing to localise data), the messaging service Telegram (blocked unsuccessfully from 2018 to 2020), and, after the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Facebook, Instagram and Twitter/X, alongside independent media outlets and the websites of foreign news organisations. Laws criminalising the dissemination of "false information" about the armed forces, punishable by long prison terms, drove widespread self-censorship and the closure or exile of independent newsrooms.

Authorities have also throttled services, restricted VPNs and ordered app stores to remove applications including those of opposition organisations and independent media. Rights groups such as Roskomsvoboda document thousands of blocked resources, and Freedom House rates Russia "not free" in its Freedom on the Net reports.
