Asherah (Ugaritic: Athirat) was a major goddess in ancient Semitic religion. She appears in Akkadian, Hittite and Ugaritic sources, and in the Hebrew Bible the word asherah (plural asherim) most often denotes a cult object — a sacred wooden pole or stylised tree erected beside an altar — associated with her worship.

In Ugaritic religion

In the texts from Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra, Syria), Athirat is the consort of the high god El and the mother of the gods, styled "creatress of the gods" and "Lady Athirat of the Sea". She intercedes with El on behalf of the storm god Baal and is portrayed as a wise, maternal figure.

In the Hebrew Bible

The Bible mentions Asherah and her cult objects some forty times, usually in condemnation: Israelite and Judahite kings are criticised for tolerating asherim on the "high places", while reformers such as Hezekiah and Josiah are praised for cutting them down. A much-debated inscription from Kuntillet Ajrud blesses a worshipper by "Yahweh and his asherah", which some scholars take as evidence that Asherah was once venerated as Yahweh's consort in popular Israelite religion, though others read it as referring to her cult symbol.

Archaeology

Hundreds of small female pillar figurines found in Judahite homes of the 8th–6th centuries BCE are frequently linked with Asherah or with popular goddess devotion more generally, though the identification remains contested.
