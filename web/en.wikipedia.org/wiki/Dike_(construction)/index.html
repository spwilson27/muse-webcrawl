Dike (construction)

Dike
PurposeFlood protection; land reclamation
MaterialsEarth, clay, sand, rock, concrete

A dike (also spelled dyke) is an elongated, artificially constructed embankment or wall that regulates water levels and protects adjacent land from flooding. Dikes are typically built from compacted earth, clay, or rock along the edges of rivers, lakes, or seas. In the United States, similar structures are more commonly called levees.

History

Dike building is ancient. Mesopotamian and Egyptian farmers raised earthen banks along the Tigris, Euphrates, and Nile to control seasonal floods. In China, dikes along the Yellow River date back more than two thousand years. The Netherlands is the country most associated with dikes: roughly a third of its land lies below sea level, protected by an extensive system of dikes, dunes, and pumping stations. The catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953, which killed over 1,800 people in the Netherlands, led to the Delta Works, one of the largest flood-protection projects in the world.

Design

A dike's cross-section is usually trapezoidal, with a broad base and gentle slopes to resist erosion and seepage. A clay core or facing reduces permeability, while the crest may carry a road or maintenance path. Engineers must account for wave action, storm surge, settlement, and the risk of piping — internal erosion that can cause sudden failure.

Failure and maintenance

Dikes fail by overtopping, erosion of the inner or outer slope, or undermining. Failures during Hurricane Katrina in 2005 flooded much of New Orleans. Regular inspection, vegetation management, and reinforcement are essential to dike safety.
