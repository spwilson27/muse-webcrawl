Vestigiality

Vestigiality is the retention, during the process of evolution, of genetically determined structures or attributes that have lost some or all of the ancestral function in a given species. Assessment of the vestigiality must generally rely on comparison with homologous features in related species. The emergence of vestigiality occurs by normal evolutionary processes, typically by loss of function of a feature that is no longer subject to positive selection pressures, or when the trait's function is selected against, or when the feature is no longer needed in a changed environment.

Classic examples include the pelvic bones of whales and snakes, the wings of flightless birds such as the ostrich and kiwi, the eyes of blind cave fish, and in humans the appendix, the coccyx (tailbone), wisdom teeth, and the muscles that move the ears. Vestigial structures have long been cited as evidence for evolution, because they are most readily explained as remnants of functional structures in ancestors.

History

Vestigial structures were noted by ancient Greek philosophers, and were discussed by naturalists before Darwin. Charles Darwin listed several putative vestigial features in humans in The Descent of Man (1871), including the muscles of the ear, wisdom teeth, the appendix, the tailbone, body hair, and the semilunar fold in the corner of the eye. In 1893, Robert Wiedersheim published a list of 86 human vestigial features.

Characteristics

Vestigial traits can take various forms: anatomical structures, behaviours, biochemical pathways, and genetic remnants such as pseudogenes. A vestigial trait need not be entirely functionless; many retain secondary functions or partial utility. For example, the human appendix serves as a reservoir for gut bacteria, and ostrich wings are used in mating displays and balance. Vestigiality is therefore defined relative to the original function, not to any function whatsoever.
