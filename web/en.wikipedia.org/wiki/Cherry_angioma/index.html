Cherry angioma

A cherry angioma on the skin (image placeholder)

Cherry angiomas, also known as Campbell de Morgan spots or senile angiomas, are small, benign skin growths made up of blood vessels. They appear as bright red, round or oval spots, typically 1–5 mm in diameter, and are among the most common skin findings in adults.

Characteristics

The lesions arise from a proliferation of capillaries in the dermis. They are usually asymptomatic, though they may bleed if traumatised. Cherry angiomas tend to appear from the third or fourth decade of life onwards and increase in number with age; by age 75 the large majority of people have at least some. They are most common on the trunk but can occur almost anywhere on the body.

Cause and treatment

The cause is not fully understood, but ageing, genetic factors, and hormonal changes are thought to contribute. Somatic mutations in genes such as GNAQ and GNA11 have been identified in some lesions. No treatment is medically necessary; lesions may be removed for cosmetic reasons or if they bleed repeatedly, using methods such as electrocautery, laser therapy (e.g. pulsed dye or Nd:YAG laser), cryotherapy, or shave excision.
