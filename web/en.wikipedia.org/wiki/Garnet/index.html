Garnet

CategoryNesosilicate minerals
FormulaX3Y2(SiO4)3
Crystal systemCubic (isometric)
Hardness6.5–7.5 (Mohs)
Colourred, orange, yellow, green, purple, brown, black

Garnets are a group of silicate minerals that share a common crystal structure but vary in chemical composition, with the general formula X3Y2(SiO4)3. They have been used since the Bronze Age as gemstones and abrasives. The name derives from the Latin granatus, a reference to the pomegranate, whose red seeds resemble garnet crystals.

Species

The principal end-member species are divided into two series. The pyralspite series (aluminium garnets) includes pyrope (Mg), almandine (Fe) and spessartine (Mn). The ugrandite series (calcium garnets) includes uvarovite (Cr), grossular (Al) and andradite (Fe). Well-known gem varieties include the green demantoid (andradite), orange hessonite (grossular) and vivid green tsavorite. Most natural garnets are solid solutions between end members.

Properties and occurrence

Garnets crystallise in the cubic system, commonly as dodecahedra and trapezohedra. They have a hardness of 6.5–7.5 on the Mohs scale, no cleavage, and a vitreous to resinous lustre. Garnets form in metamorphic rocks such as schist and gneiss, in igneous rocks and in skarns; resistant grains accumulate in placer deposits. Important sources include India, Sri Lanka, Madagascar, Tanzania, Russia and the United States.

Uses

Red garnets are the traditional January birthstone and have been prized in jewellery for millennia; Bohemian pyrope jewellery was especially fashionable in the 18th and 19th centuries. Industrially, garnet is widely used as an abrasive in waterjet cutting, sandblasting and abrasive paper, and as a filter medium in water treatment. Yttrium aluminium garnet (YAG) is a synthetic garnet used as a laser medium (Nd:YAG) and, doped with cerium, as a phosphor in white LEDs.
