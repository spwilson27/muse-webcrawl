Ancient Libya refers to the lands of North Africa west of Egypt in antiquity. The Greeks used the name Libya for the whole of Africa known to them, but in narrower usage it denoted the territory of the indigenous peoples — ancestral Berbers known to Egyptian records as the Libu, Meshwesh, and others — and the Phoenician, Greek, and Roman colonies planted along its Mediterranean coast.

Indigenous peoples

Egyptian sources from the New Kingdom onward describe Libyan tribes pressing on Egypt's western border; Libyan chiefs of the Meshwesh eventually seized power in Egypt itself, founding the Twenty-second Dynasty (the "Libyan" or Bubastite dynasty, from c. 943 BC). In the interior, the Garamantes of the Fezzan built an oasis civilisation with underground irrigation channels (foggaras), chariots — depicted in Saharan rock art — and trade across the desert. Herodotus's Histories (Book 4) is the richest early source on the Libyan tribes, from the coastal Nasamones to the cave-dwelling Troglodytes.

Phoenician and Greek colonisation

Phoenician traders founded emporia along the western coast, of which Leptis Magna, Sabratha, and Oea — the "three cities" (Tripolis) of Tripolitania — became the greatest; they fell under Carthaginian influence. In the east, Greek settlers from Thera founded Cyrene around 631 BC, and the Pentapolis ("five cities") of Cyrenaica prospered on grain, horses, and the medicinal plant silphium, exported so intensively that it became extinct. Cyrenaica passed to the Ptolemies, and Cyrene was the birthplace of the philosopher Aristippus and the mathematician Eratosthenes.

Roman Libya

Rome annexed Cyrenaica in 96–74 BC and took Tripolitania after the fall of Carthage and Numidia. Under the emperors, and especially the Severan dynasty — Septimius Severus was a native of Leptis Magna — the coastal cities were adorned with forums, theatres, and baths whose ruins are among the best preserved in the Roman world. The frontier zone, the Limes Tripolitanus, was guarded by forts and fortified farms against desert raiders. Libyan grain and olive oil supplied Rome; in late antiquity the region was contested by Vandals (from 439 AD), reconquered by the Byzantine general Belisarius (533–34), and finally absorbed into the Islamic world by the Arab conquests of the 640s AD.
