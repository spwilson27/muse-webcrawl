Law of agency

The law of agency is an area of commercial law dealing with a set of contractual, quasi-contractual, and non-contractual fiduciary relationships that involve a person, called the agent, who is authorised to act on behalf of another, called the principal, to create legal relations with a third party. In short, it regulates relationships in which one person acts for another.

The agency relationship

An agency relationship arises when the principal manifests assent that the agent shall act on the principal's behalf and subject to the principal's control, and the agent consents so to act. The agent owes the principal fiduciary duties, including loyalty, obedience, reasonable care, and a duty to account. In return, the principal owes duties to compensate and indemnify the agent and to deal fairly and in good faith.

Authority

An agent's power to bind the principal depends on authority, which may take several forms:

  • Actual authority — express or implied authority actually granted by the principal.
  • Apparent (ostensible) authority — where the principal's conduct leads a third party reasonably to believe the agent is authorised; the principal may be estopped from denying it.
  • Authority by ratification — where the principal later approves an unauthorised act.
  • Agency of necessity — arising in emergencies where an agent must act to protect the principal's property.

Liability

Where an agent acts within authority, the contract is generally between the principal and the third party, and the agent drops out. An agent who acts without authority may be liable for breach of warranty of authority. Principals may also be vicariously liable for torts committed by agents in the course of their agency. The doctrine of the undisclosed principal allows, in defined circumstances, a third party who did not know of the principal's existence to sue or be sued by that principal.

Termination

Agency terminates by agreement, expiry, revocation by the principal, renunciation by the agent, or by operation of law, such as the death or incapacity of either party. Some forms of agency, such as a power of attorney coupled with an interest, may be irrevocable.
