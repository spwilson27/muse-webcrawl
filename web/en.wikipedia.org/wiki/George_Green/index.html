George Green (14 July 1793 – 31 May 1841) was a British mathematical physicist who wrote An Essay on the Application of Mathematical Analysis to the Theories of Electricity and Magnetism in 1828. The essay introduced the term "potential" and the concept now known as Green's function, together with Green's theorem, and laid foundations for the mathematical theory of electricity and magnetism later developed by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) and James Clerk Maxwell.

Life

Green was born in Sneinton, Nottingham, the son of a baker, and worked in his father's milling business — Green's Windmill in Sneinton still stands. He had very little formal schooling, attending school for only about a year, and was largely self-taught in mathematics, possibly with help from the Nottingham Subscription Library. In 1828 he published his famous essay at his own expense, selling it by subscription to 51 people.

Through the patronage of Sir Edward Bromhead, Green entered Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, in 1833 as an undergraduate at the age of 40, graduating fourth wrangler in 1837. He was elected a fellow of the college in 1839 but died in Nottingham only two years later.

The 1828 essay

Green's essay introduced what are now called Green's identities and Green's theorem, relating volume and surface integrals, and developed the method of Green's functions for solving partial differential equations — a technique central to modern theoretical physics. The obscure publication was almost lost until Lord Kelvin obtained a copy in 1845 and arranged its republication, after which Green's work became enormously influential. The George Green Institute for Electromagnetics at the University of Nottingham and a Westminster Abbey memorial commemorate him.
