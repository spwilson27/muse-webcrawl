Renewal theory

Renewal theory is the branch of probability theory that generalizes the Poisson process for arbitrary holding times, i.e. arbitrary inter-arrival times. Essentially, the renewal process is a counting process in which the gaps between successive events are independent and identically distributed random variables with a general distribution. Instead of exponentially distributed holding times, a renewal process may have any independent and identically distributed (iid) holding times that have finite expectation.

A renewal process has the Markov-like property that the process restarts probabilistically at each event time: the future evolution after an arrival is independent of the past and statistically identical.

Key results

The central object of study is the renewal function m(t), the expected number of events in the interval (0, t]. The elementary renewal theorem states that m(t)/t converges to the reciprocal of the mean holding time as t grows large. The key renewal theorem, developed by David Blackwell and others, describes the asymptotic behaviour of the expected number of events in a sliding window and underlies results in queueing theory, reliability, inventory control, and actuarial science (such as modelling claim arrivals and equipment replacement).

Renewal-reward processes

A renewal-reward process attaches a random reward (or cost) to each cycle between renewals. The renewal reward theorem states that the long-run average reward per unit time equals the expected reward per cycle divided by the expected cycle length, a result widely used in operations research and engineering economics.
