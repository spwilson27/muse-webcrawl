The Trabant is a series of small cars produced from 1957 to 1991 by former East German car manufacturer VEB Sachsenring Automobilwerke Zwickau. It is often regarded as a symbol of the defunct East Germany and of the collapse of the Eastern Bloc in general.

History

The name "Trabant" means "satellite" or "companion" in German and was inspired by the Soviet Sputnik satellite launched in 1957, the year production began. The main models were the P50 (1957–1962), the P60 and, from 1964, the long-lived Trabant 601, which remained in production with minimal changes until 1990.

Design

The Trabant had a body made of Duroplast, a hard plastic reinforced with recycled cotton or wool fibres, mounted on a steel unibody chassis. Most models used a small air-cooled two-stroke, two-cylinder engine notable for its noise and smoky exhaust. The car had front-wheel drive and a four-speed column-shift gearbox with a freewheel device.

Legacy

Despite waiting lists of more than a decade in East Germany, the Trabant became an object of ridicule in the West after reunification, but it has since acquired a cult following; "Trabi safaris" and preservation clubs remain popular in Germany. Nearly 3.7 million Trabants were built in total.
