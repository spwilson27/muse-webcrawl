Capital control

Capital controls are residency-based measures such as transaction taxes, quantitative limits, or outright prohibitions designed to limit the flow of foreign capital in and out of a country's economy. They may target inflows, outflows, or both, and are applied to asset classes including equities, bonds, and foreign exchange transactions.

History

Capital controls were a standard feature of the post-war Bretton Woods system, endorsed by Keynes as a means of preserving domestic monetary autonomy. From the 1970s onward, most advanced economies dismantled them in favour of capital account liberalisation, but many emerging markets retained or reimposed controls during crises — notably Malaysia in 1998, Iceland after its 2008 banking collapse, and Cyprus and Greece during the euro-area crisis.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argue that controls can dampen destabilising speculative flows, reduce crisis risk, and preserve policy independence (the "impossible trinity" trade-off). Critics contend that controls invite evasion and corruption, raise the cost of capital, and are often ineffective over time. The IMF, long an advocate of liberalisation, adopted in 2012 an "institutional view" accepting capital flow management measures in certain circumstances.
