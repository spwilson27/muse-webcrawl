Customary international law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Customary international law is one of the principal sources of international law, designated in Article 38(1)(b) of the ICJ Statute as "international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law". It consists of rules arising from two elements: a general and consistent practice of states, and opinio juris — the belief that the practice is legally required or permitted (as distinct from mere habit, courtesy or comity).

Formation and identification

The ICJ's cases frame the method: the North Sea Continental Shelf cases (1969) required extensive and virtually uniform practice including that of specially affected states, with time not decisive; Nicaragua (1986) allowed opinio juris to be drawn from resolutions and treaty adherence, and imperfect practice to be treated as breach rather than disproof (a move critics find too permissive). The International Law Commission's 2018 conclusions systematised the two-element approach and the persistent-objector rule (a state that objects consistently during formation is not bound — except by jus cogens). Custom governs where treaties do not reach (non-parties, gaps, new problems such as cyberspace operations, whose customary analysis dominates current debate) and supplies law binding on new states automatically — its democratic deficit (practice of the powerful weighs more) being the standing critique.
