Agora

The agora (Ancient Greek: ἀγορά, romanized: agorá, meaning "market" or "assembly") was a central public space in ancient Greek city-states. It was the centre of the athletic, artistic, business, social, spiritual, and political life of the city. The word appears in early works such as the Iliad and the Odyssey, where it denotes a gathering place; only later did it come to mean primarily a marketplace.

Functions

The agora served as the marketplace where merchants sold their goods in stalls or shops, often arranged around the edges in covered colonnades called stoas. Citizens gathered there daily to conduct business, hear announcements, gossip, and discuss philosophy — Socrates famously debated in the Athenian agora. Public buildings such as council houses (bouleuteria), law courts, temples, and altars typically stood around its perimeter, and political assemblies and ostracism votes could be held there.

The Athenian agora

The best-known example lies northwest of the Acropolis in Athens. Developed from the 6th century BC onward, it contains the exceptionally well-preserved Temple of Hephaestus and the reconstructed Stoa of Attalos, now a museum. Excavations by the American School of Classical Studies at Athens, begun in 1931, uncovered temples, a concert hall (Odeon of Agrippa), mint, and civic offices.

Legacy

The agora's combination of commerce, politics, and public debate made it a model for later European squares and a lasting symbol of democratic civic life. The modern fear of open spaces, agoraphobia, derives its name from the word.
