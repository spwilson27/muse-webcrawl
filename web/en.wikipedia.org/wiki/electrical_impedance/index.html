Electrical impedance

Electrical impedance is the measure of the opposition that a circuit presents to a current when a voltage is applied. Quantitatively, impedance is the complex ratio of the voltage to the current in an alternating current (AC) circuit. Impedance extends the concept of resistance to AC circuits and possesses both magnitude and phase, unlike resistance, which has only magnitude.

Definition

Impedance is denoted by the symbol Z and is expressed in ohms (Ω). It can be written in complex form as Z = R + jX, where R is the resistance (the real part) and X is the reactance (the imaginary part). The concept was introduced by Oliver Heaviside in 1886.

Complex impedance

The impedance of a resistor is purely real and equal to its resistance. An ideal inductor has impedance jωL and an ideal capacitor has impedance 1/(jωC), where ω is the angular frequency. Combining elements in series adds their impedances; elements in parallel combine by the reciprocal rule, just as with resistors.

Phase

Because the voltage and current in reactive components are not in phase, impedance carries a phase angle θ. In a purely resistive circuit the phase angle is zero; in an inductive circuit current lags voltage, and in a capacitive circuit current leads voltage.

Applications

Impedance is fundamental to AC power systems, audio engineering, radio-frequency design, and impedance matching, where maximum power transfer occurs when the load impedance is the complex conjugate of the source impedance.
