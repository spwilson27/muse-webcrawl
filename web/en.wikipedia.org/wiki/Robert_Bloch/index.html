Robert Albert Bloch (April 5, 1917 – September 23, 1994) was an American fiction writer, primarily of crime, psychological horror, and fantasy, much of which has been dramatized for radio, cinema and television. He also wrote a relatively small amount of science fiction. His writing career lasted over 60 years, including more than 30 years in television and film.

Early career

Born in Chicago and raised in Milwaukee, Bloch was a youthful correspondent and protégé of H. P. Lovecraft, who encouraged his writing. He sold his first stories to Weird Tales in 1934, at seventeen, and contributed tales to the Cthulhu Mythos before turning toward crime and psychological suspense. His story "Yours Truly, Jack the Ripper" (1943) became a classic, and "That Hell-Bound Train" won the Hugo Award for Best Short Story in 1959.

Psycho and later work

Bloch's 1959 novel Psycho, inspired in part by the crimes of Ed Gein, was adapted by Alfred Hitchcock into the landmark 1960 film of the same name; Bloch later wrote two sequel novels. He wrote extensively for television, including episodes of Alfred Hitchcock Presents, Thriller, and three episodes of Star Trek, as well as screenplays for films produced by Amicus and William Castle. His autobiography, Once Around the Bloch, appeared in 1993.

Bloch won the World Fantasy Award for Life Achievement and the Horror Writers Association's Bram Stoker Award for Lifetime Achievement, and remains one of the most influential American horror writers of the twentieth century. He was a mentor to younger writers and famous in fandom for his punning wit.
