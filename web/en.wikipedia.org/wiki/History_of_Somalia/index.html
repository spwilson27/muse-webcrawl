History of Somalia

The history of Somalia reaches from some of the earliest evidence of human habitation in the Horn of Africa, through ancient trading kingdoms linked to Egypt and Arabia, medieval Islamic sultanates, European colonization, independence in 1960, the military regime of Siad Barre, the civil war and state collapse of 1991, and the slow rebuilding of federal institutions in the 21st century.

Ancient history

The region of modern Somalia was known to the ancient Egyptians as part of the Land of Punt, a source of myrrh, frankincense, gold, and exotic animals, famously visited by the expedition of Queen Hatshepsut around 1493 BCE. The Laas Geel cave paintings near Hargeisa, dating to roughly 9,000–3,000 BCE, are among the finest prehistoric rock art in Africa. City-states of the Somali coast, such as Opone and Mosylon, traded with Greeks, Romans, and Indians; the 1st-century CE Periplus of the Erythraean Sea describes their commerce in cinnamon, spices, and tortoiseshell.

Medieval sultanates

Islam arrived on the northern coast in the earliest centuries of the faith, and Mogadishu became a flourishing sultanate and trading city visited by Ibn Battuta in 1331. Inland and coastal states included the Sultanate of Ifat and its successor, the Adal Sultanate, whose ruler Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi ("Ahmed Gurey") led a celebrated invasion of the Ethiopian highlands in the 16th century. Other important states included the Ajuran Sultanate, noted for hydraulic engineering and centralised rule, and later the Geledi and Majeerteen sultanates.

Colonial period

In the late 19th century, Britain established the Somaliland Protectorate in the north, Italy colonized the south (Italian Somaliland), and France held Djibouti. The Dervish movement of Mohammed Abdullah Hassan resisted colonial rule for two decades (1899–1920). During the Second World War, Italy occupied British Somaliland briefly before British forces took control of both territories. The south became a UN trust territory under Italian administration in 1950, preparing for independence.

Independence and the Somali Republic

On 26 June 1960, British Somaliland gained independence, followed on 1 July 1960 by the Trust Territory of Somaliland; the two united as the Somali Republic, with Aden Abdullah Osman Daar as its first president. The parliamentary period was marked by clan-based party politics and irredentist ambitions toward Somali-inhabited regions of Ethiopia, Kenya, and Djibouti — the "Greater Somalia" idea symbolised by the five-pointed star of the national flag.

Barre regime

In 1969, Major General Siad Barre seized power in a coup and established the Somali Democratic Republic, pursuing "scientific socialism", adopting a written script for the Somali language (1972), and launching mass literacy campaigns. In 1977–78 Somalia invaded Ethiopia's Ogaden region; after the Soviet Union switched support to Ethiopia, Somalia was defeated and turned to the United States. Repression, clan favouritism, and economic decline fuelled armed opposition in the 1980s.

Civil war and state collapse

Barre was overthrown in January 1991, but the victorious clan-based factions fought among themselves, and central government collapsed. The northwest declared independence as Somaliland (unrecognized internationally). Famine in 1992 prompted US-led and UN interventions (UNITAF and UNOSOM), ending after the 1993 Battle of Mogadishu depicted in Black Hawk Down. The Puntland region declared autonomy in 1998. Warlords, the Islamic Courts Union (which briefly held Mogadishu in 2006), Ethiopian intervention, and the rise of the al-Qaeda-linked al-Shabaab insurgency prolonged the conflict.

Rebuilding

A Transitional Federal Government was formed in 2004, and in 2012 a Federal Government of Somalia was established in Mogadishu with international recognition, supported by African Union peacekeeping missions (AMISOM, later ATMIS/AUSSOM) against al-Shabaab. Federal member states were formed across the country. Somalia has held indirect elections, adopted a provisional constitution, and regained access to international finance, though al-Shabaab attacks, clan politics, drought, and disputes between Mogadishu, the federal states, and Somaliland continue to challenge the state.
