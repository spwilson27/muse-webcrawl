Refracting telescope

Refracting telescope (refractor)
Objective: Lens
First described: Netherlands, 1608
Largest: Yerkes Observatory 40-inch (1.02 m), 1897
Key designs: Galilean, Keplerian, achromatic, apochromatic

A refracting telescope (also called a refractor) is a type of optical telescope that uses a lens as its objective to form an image. The refracting telescope design was originally used in spyglasses and astronomical telescopes and is the design of the first known practical telescopes, patented in the Netherlands in 1608 and famously turned on the sky by Galileo Galilei in 1609.

Designs

Galilean and Keplerian

Galileo's telescope combined a convex objective with a concave eyepiece, giving an upright but tiny field of view. Johannes Kepler's design substituted a convex eyepiece, inverting the image but greatly widening the field and allowing high magnification — the form adopted by astronomers ever since.

Achromatic and apochromatic

Single lenses suffer chromatic aberration: different colours focus at different distances. The achromatic doublet (patented by John Dollond in 1758) pairs crown and flint glass to bring two colours to a common focus; modern apochromats use extra-low-dispersion glass to correct three colours.

Limits of size

A lens can be supported only at its edges, so gravity makes large lenses sag, and all light must pass through the glass. The largest practical refractor remains the 40-inch (1.02 m) Yerkes Observatory telescope of 1897, built by Alvan Clark & Sons — after which professional astronomy moved to reflectors. Refractors remain prized by amateur astronomers and for astrographs for their sharp, high-contrast images and sealed, low-maintenance tubes.
