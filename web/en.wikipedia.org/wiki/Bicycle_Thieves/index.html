Bicycle Thieves

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Antonio and his son Bruno searching Rome's markets for the stolen bicycle, the boy dwarfed by crowds of indifferent adults]

Bicycle Thieves (Italian: Ladri di biciclette) is a 1948 Italian neorealist film directed by Vittorio De Sica, from Cesare Zavattini's screenplay after Luigi Bartolini's novel. It is universally counted among the greatest films ever made — the founding masterpiece of Italian neorealism and a permanent fixture of critics' polls since it topped early lists in the 1950s.

In post-war Rome, the unemployed Antonio Ricci finally lands a job pasting posters — on condition he has a bicycle. The family pawns their bedsheets to redeem it from hock; on his first day it is stolen. The film follows father and small son Bruno through a Sunday of searching: markets, a church mission, a fortune-teller, false leads, and at last Antonio's own despairing attempt at theft before his son's eyes. Non-professional actors (a factory worker, Lamberto Maggiorani; a street boy, Enzo Staiola) and real locations give it documentary force.

Neorealism

Zavattini's theory — follow a man, find the world; no actors, no studio, no plot machinery — reached its purest form here. Shot on scavenged film stock with money raised piecemeal (including, by legend, from selling the rights of another project), it made neorealism the conscience of post-war cinema from Satyajit Ray to the Iranian New Wave.

Reception

An international triumph on release (honorary Academy Award, 1950), the film slipped in fashion as neorealism was absorbed, then rose again: it placed in the top ranks of the 2012 Sight and Sound directors' poll discussions and remains the first film in most curricula of world cinema — the measure of what simplicity of means can achieve.
