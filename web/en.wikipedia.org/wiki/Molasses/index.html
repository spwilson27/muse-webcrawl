Molasses

Molasses
TypeSweetener, syrup
SourceSugarcane or sugar beet refining
VarietiesLight, dark, blackstrap; sulfured and unsulfured
UsesBaking, rum production, animal feed, fermentation

Molasses (from Portuguese melaço, from Latin mel, "honey") is a viscous by-product of the refining of sugarcane or sugar beets into sugar. It is a thick, dark syrup whose flavour and colour deepen with each successive boiling of the sugar syrup from which crystals are extracted.

Types

The first boiling yields light molasses, sweetest and mildest; the second gives dark molasses, used in gingerbread and baked beans; the third produces blackstrap molasses, very dark, bitter, and rich in minerals including iron, calcium, potassium, and magnesium. Molasses may be "sulfured" if sulfur dioxide was used in processing. Cane molasses is distinct from sorghum syrup, which is made from sorghum juice.

History and uses

Molasses was central to the colonial Atlantic economy: heavy British taxation under the Molasses Act of 1733 (widely evaded) contributed to American revolutionary grievances, and molasses imported to New England was distilled into rum within the "triangular trade." In January 1919, the Boston Molasses Disaster — the collapse of a huge storage tank — released a wave of molasses that killed 21 people. Today molasses is used in baking (gingerbread, shoofly pie, parkin), sauces and marinades, as a feedstock for rum and industrial fermentation (including citric acid and yeast production), in animal feed, and as a soil amendment.

Nutrition

Unlike refined sugar, molasses retains minerals from the cane; blackstrap molasses is a notable source of iron and calcium, though it remains predominantly sugar (about 55–75% sugars by weight) and is consumed in small quantities.
