Objective (optics)

In optics, an objective is the optical element of an instrument that is closest to the object being observed and that first collects its light to form an image. Objectives are the primary light-gathering and image-forming components of telescopes, microscopes, cameras, and binoculars; in microscopes and cameras they are often simply called objective lenses.

Telescopes

In a refracting telescope the objective is the large lens at the front of the tube; in a reflector it is the primary mirror. Its diameter (aperture) determines the instrument's light-gathering power and resolution, and its focal length, divided by that of the eyepiece, sets the magnification. Historical objectives were simple lenses plagued by chromatic aberration, leading to achromatic doublets and later apochromatic designs.

Microscopes

A microscope objective produces a magnified real image that the eyepiece enlarges further. Objectives are characterised by their magnification (commonly 4× to 100×), numerical aperture — the true measure of their resolving power — working distance, and degree of correction (achromat, plan, fluorite, apochromat). High-power objectives are designed to be used with immersion oil between lens and specimen. Modern objectives built to the infinity-corrected standard project the image to infinity and are standardised (for example by the DIN thread and parfocal distance) so several can share a revolving nosepiece.

Cameras

In photography the term objective is used especially in technical and European usage for the camera lens as a whole — the complex multi-element assembly that focuses the image onto film or sensor — as distinct from the camera body.
