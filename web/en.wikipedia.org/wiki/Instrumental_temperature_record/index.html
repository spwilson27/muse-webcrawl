Instrumental temperature record

The instrumental temperature record is the record of temperatures measured with thermometers and other instruments, providing global and regional surface temperature data from the mid-19th century to the present. It shows that the Earth's surface has warmed by roughly 1.1–1.3 °C since the late 19th century, with the rate of warming since about 1970 being substantially faster than in the preceding century. The record is one of the principal lines of evidence for recent climate change.

Coverage and datasets

Systematic thermometer measurements began at a small number of European stations in the 17th and 18th centuries, but a quasi-global record is generally considered possible only from about 1850, when coverage of land stations and ship-based sea surface temperature observations became reasonably widespread. Major datasets include HadCRUT (Met Office Hadley Centre and Climatic Research Unit), the NASA Goddard Institute's GISTEMP, NOAA's GlobalTemp, and the independent Berkeley Earth record, all of which agree closely on the overall warming trend.

Methods

Raw observations are quality-controlled and adjusted for known non-climatic influences, including changes in station location and instrumentation, changes in the time of day of observation, the transition from canvas buckets to engine-intake measurements of sea surface temperature, and the growth of cities around stations (the urban heat island effect). Temperatures are usually expressed as anomalies relative to a reference period such as 1951–1980 or 1850–1900, which reduces the influence of local factors and missing data. Gridded fields are then averaged, with area weighting, to produce hemispheric and global means.

Findings

The record shows relative coolness in the mid-19th century, warming to the 1940s, a slight flattening or cooling into the 1970s (attributed in part to aerosol pollution), and rapid warming thereafter. The years since 2015 include the warmest in the record, and each of the last four decades has been successively warmer than any decade since 1850. Warming is greater over land than ocean and is amplified in the Arctic.

Relation to other records

For periods before about 1850, temperatures must be inferred from proxy records such as tree rings, ice cores, corals and sediments, which reconstruct past climate at lower temporal resolution. Satellite measurements of the lower troposphere, available since 1978, and ocean heat content measurements provide independent confirmation of warming in recent decades.
