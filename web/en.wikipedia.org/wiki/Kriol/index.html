Kriol

Kriol is an Australian creole language that developed from a pidgin used in the early 19th century between European and Chinese settlers and Aboriginal Australians in northern Australia. It is now the first language of many Aboriginal communities across the Kimberley, the Top End and the Gulf Country, with estimates of speakers ranging from several thousand to around 30,000 depending on how speakers are counted.


Kriol is spoken across Australia's north, from the Kimberley to Cape York.

History

Kriol developed from New South Wales Pidgin English brought north by settlers, stockmen and mission workers, and became a full creole at the Roper River Mission (now Ngukurr) in the early 20th century, where children of mixed-language backgrounds acquired it as a first language. It subsequently spread along stock routes and through missions and government settlements.

Dialects

Regional varieties include Roper River Kriol (Ngukurr and surrounding areas), Barunga Kriol, Kimberley Kriol (Fitzroy Valley) and varieties spoken around Katherine, Tennant Creek and in the Gulf Country. The varieties form a continuum influenced by the traditional languages of each area.

Features

Kriol vocabulary derives mainly from English, but its grammar reflects Aboriginal language structures: it has a dual and trial number distinction in pronouns, inclusive/exclusive "we" forms (e.g. yunmi "you and I", mela "we, not including you"), and transitive marking with the suffix -im. A Bible translation into Kriol, the Holi Baibul, was completed in 2007 — the first complete Bible translation into an Australian Indigenous language.
