Pitch (music)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pitch is a perceptual property that allows sounds to be ordered on a frequency-related scale, or more commonly, pitch is the quality that makes it possible to judge sounds as "higher" and "lower" in the sense associated with musical melodies. Pitch is a major auditory attribute of musical tones, along with duration, loudness, and timbre.

Pitch may be quantified as a frequency, but pitch is not a purely objective physical property; it is a subjective psychoacoustical attribute of sound. Historically, the study of pitch and pitch perception has been a central problem in psychoacoustics and has been instrumental in forming and testing theories of sound representation, processing, and perception in the auditory system.

Perception

The perceived pitch of a tone is closely related to the frequency of its fundamental, though the relationship is not purely linear: doubling the frequency raises the pitch by an octave, and equal ratios of frequency are perceived as equal intervals. For complex tones, the auditory system can infer the pitch of a "missing fundamental" that is not physically present. Pitch perception is most acute in the range of roughly 100 Hz to 4,000 Hz, and trained listeners can discriminate frequency differences of a few cents (hundredths of a semitone).

Pitch standards and notation

Throughout history, the absolute pitch assigned to a given note name varied widely by place and era. The modern international standard, adopted in the twentieth century, sets A4 (the A above middle C) at 440 Hz, known as concert pitch (ISO 16). Historical pitch standards were often lower — around A = 415 Hz in the Baroque era — while some modern orchestras tune slightly higher, to 442 Hz or above.

In Western notation, pitch is represented by the vertical position of noteheads on the staff, modified by clefs, key signatures and accidentals. Scientific pitch notation identifies notes by letter name and octave number (e.g. C4 for middle C). Pitches that sound the same but are written differently — such as C♯ and D♭ — are called enharmonic equivalents.

Definite and indefinite pitch

Instruments such as the piano, violin and flute produce sounds of definite pitch: a listener can match them to a note on a keyboard. Many percussion instruments — drums, cymbals, and the like — produce sounds of indefinite pitch, dominated by noise components without a clear fundamental. Some instruments, such as timpani and tubular bells, fall between these extremes.

Pitch and culture

Musical systems divide the pitch continuum differently: Western music predominantly uses twelve-tone equal temperament, while other traditions employ microtonal divisions, such as the 22 śrutis of Indian music or the quarter tones of Arabic maqam. Individuals with absolute pitch (perfect pitch) can identify or produce a pitch without a reference tone; the ability is rare and appears to depend on both genetic predisposition and early musical training.
