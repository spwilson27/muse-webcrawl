Microdot

A microdot is text or an image substantially reduced in size onto a small disc — typically about one millimetre in diameter, the size and shape of a typographic dot — to prevent detection by unintended recipients. Microdots are a form of steganography: the concealment of a message within something else.

History

Photographic reduction techniques date to the Franco-Prussian War era, when pigeons carried microfilmed dispatches (the pigeon post). The practical microdot technique was developed in Germany in the 1920s and 1930s, associated with Professor Emanuel Goldberg and the Zapp process. During the Second World War, German agents used microdots to transmit espionage information hidden as punctuation on ordinary letters; Allied counterintelligence, alerted by a tip, learned to search for them, and J. Edgar Hoover called the microdot "the enemy's masterpiece of espionage."

Technique

Using specialized optics and ultra-fine-grained film, a full page of text could be photographed down to a dot of roughly 1 mm diameter. The dot was then attached to a letter, disguised as a full stop or the dot of an "i", sealed under collodion in a slit in a postcard, or hidden under a postage stamp. The recipient read it with a microscope or special viewer.

Later use

Microdots continued in use through the Cold War. In a different application, microdot identification technology sprays thousands of microscopic discs bearing a unique code onto vehicles and valuable equipment, as an anti-theft marking system readable under ultraviolet light and magnification.
