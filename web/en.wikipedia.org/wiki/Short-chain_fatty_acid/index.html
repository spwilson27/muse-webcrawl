Short-chain fatty acid

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are fatty acids with fewer than six carbon atoms. They are produced when gut bacteria in the colon ferment dietary fibre and resistant starch. The three most abundant SCFAs are acetate (C2), propionate (C3), and butyrate (C4), which together account for roughly 90–95% of the SCFAs in the gut, typically in a molar ratio of about 60:20:20.

Production and absorption

SCFAs are the main products of microbial fermentation of indigestible carbohydrates in the large intestine. They are absorbed by colonocytes via diffusion and monocarboxylate transporters. Butyrate is the preferred energy source of the colonic epithelium, supplying much of its energy needs; propionate is largely taken up by the liver, where it contributes to gluconeogenesis; acetate reaches the peripheral circulation and is used by muscle and other tissues.

Biological roles

SCFAs act as signalling molecules through G-protein-coupled receptors (GPR41/FFAR3, GPR43/FFAR2, and GPR109A) and as histone deacetylase inhibitors, especially butyrate and propionate. Through these mechanisms they influence gut barrier integrity, mucus production, regulatory T-cell development, inflammation, appetite regulation via gut hormones such as GLP-1 and PYY, and energy metabolism. Research links altered SCFA production with obesity, type 2 diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, and colorectal cancer, though causal roles in humans remain under study.
