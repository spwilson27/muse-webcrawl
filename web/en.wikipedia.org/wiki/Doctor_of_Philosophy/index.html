A Doctor of Philosophy (PhD; Latin: philosophiae doctor) is the most common degree at the highest academic level, awarded following a course of study and original research. Despite the name, PhDs are awarded in nearly all academic disciplines, not only philosophy — the word here retains its older sense of systematic learning. In most systems the degree requires the candidate to produce a substantial thesis or dissertation presenting original contributions to knowledge, and to defend it before examiners.

History

The research doctorate in its modern form developed in early 19th-century Germany, notably at the University of Berlin founded on Wilhelm von Humboldt's model, and spread to the United States (the first American PhD was awarded at Yale in 1861) and to Britain in the early 20th century, where Oxford introduced the DPhil in 1917. The degree became the standard qualification for university teaching and academic research careers.

Requirements

Admission typically requires a bachelor's or master's degree. Candidates work under one or more supervisors, complete coursework or training as required by the institution, and write a thesis — in some countries a collection of published papers may substitute. The final assessment is usually an oral examination (the viva voce or defence). Holders are entitled to the title "Doctor" and the post-nominal letters PhD (or DPhil at Oxford and some other universities).

Variations

Professional and practice-based doctorates (such as the DrPH, EngD or DBA) coexist with the PhD in some countries, while other research doctorates, like the Doctor of Science (DSc) or habilitation, may rank above it in certain European systems.
