Misdirection (magic)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Misdirection is a form of deception in magic performance in which the performer directs the audience's attention toward one thing while carrying out a secret action — a "sleight" or move — elsewhere. It is often called the fundamental principle of magic: the audience is not so much fooled by what it sees as by what it fails to notice. A common formulation, attributed to the magician's tradition, is that "the hand is not quicker than the eye" — rather, the eye is led to be in the wrong place.

Principles

Misdirection exploits known features of human attention and perception. Performers use gaze direction, body language, gesture, questions and conversation, and moments of laughter or surprise ("off-beats") to relax the audience's attention at the critical instant. Large motions cover small ones; a natural action provides cover for an unnatural one; and the performer's own apparent focus convinces spectators where the interesting event is happening. Theorists such as Nevil Maskelyne, Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin and Dai Vernon emphasised that misdirection is psychological as much as physical — managing what the audience thinks, remembers and expects, not merely what it looks at.

Research

Cognitive psychologists and neuroscientists have studied misdirection to understand attention, change blindness and inattentional blindness, collaborating with magicians such as Apollo Robbins. Studies using eye tracking show that spectators often look directly at a secret move yet fail to perceive it when their attention is engaged elsewhere.
