Sundiata Keita

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A griot performing with the kora, keeper of the Sundiata epic tradition]

Sundiata Keita (c. 1217 – c. 1255), also called Mari Djata I, was the founder of the Mali Empire in West Africa. Prince of the Mandinka Keita clan of Kangaba, he defeated the Sosso king Sumanguru Kanté at the Battle of Kirina (c. 1235) and united the Mandinka and their neighbours into an empire controlling the Niger bend's trade and goldfields. His life is preserved in the Epic of Sundiata, the oral history still performed by griots (jeliw) of the Keita tradition — a founding charter of Manding identity in which the sickly child prince, exiled by a jealous stepmother, returns to destroy the sorcerer-king and divide the offices of the empire among his allies.

The epic and the history

The epic — recorded in versions from the 20th century (notably D. T. Niane's from the griot Mamoudou Kouyaté) — tells of Sundiata's miraculous childhood (unable to walk until seven, then uprooting a baobab), his exile, and the gathering of allies: the smiths who forged his weapons, the general Fakoli, his sister's spying that stole Sumanguru's magical secret (a cock's spur arrow that broke the king's power). Beneath the magic lies plausible history: the collapse of Ghana's successor order, the Sosso interregnum, and the Mandinka unification that birthed Mali. Sundiata's constitutional settlement (the Kouroukan Fouga, sometimes styled a charter of rights) apportioned clans, trades and territories.

Legacy

Sundiata's successors made Mali the gold-trading empire of Mansa Musa's fame; his capital at Niani and his clan remained the imperial core until Mali's late decline. The epic made him the culture-hero of Mali, Guinea, Senegal and the Gambia — and a figure through whom West African oral historiography's methods and meanings have been debated worldwide (UNESCO proclaimed the Manden Charter a heritage of humanity in 2009).
