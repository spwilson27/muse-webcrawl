Reaganomics

Reaganomics (a portmanteau of Reagan and economics attributed to Paul Harvey) was the set of economic policies promoted by United States President Ronald Reagan during the 1980s. These policies are commonly associated with supply-side economics, referred to as "trickle-down economics" or "voodoo economics" by political opponents, and "free-market economics" by political advocates.

The pillars of Reagan's economic policy included increasing defense spending, balancing the federal budget and slowing the growth of government spending, reducing the federal income tax and the capital gains tax, reducing government regulation, and tightening the money supply in order to reduce inflation.

Historical background

Prior to the Reagan administration, the United States economy experienced a decade of high unemployment and persistently high inflation, a combination known as stagflation. Political pressure favored stimulus resulting from increased deficits. Reagan, influenced by the work of economists such as Arthur Laffer and the writings of Jude Wanniski, favored a policy of making marginal tax rate reductions in order to encourage economic growth and reduce inflation.

Policies

Reagan's first term saw the passage of the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, which lowered the top marginal income tax rate from 70 percent to 50 percent over three years and accelerated depreciation deductions for businesses. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 further lowered the top rate to 28 percent while broadening the tax base. At the same time, military spending increased substantially as part of the administration's Cold War strategy.

Results and legacy

Results of Reaganomics are still debated. Supporters point to the end of stagflation, the long economic expansion of the 1980s, and reductions in inflation and unemployment. Critics point to increased federal budget deficits, a tripling of the national debt, and widening income inequality. Federal revenue as a share of GDP fell during the first years of the program before partially recovering, while federal spending as a share of GDP remained elevated due to defense increases.
