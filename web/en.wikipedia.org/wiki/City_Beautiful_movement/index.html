City Beautiful movement

The City Beautiful movement was a reform philosophy of North American architecture and urban planning that flourished during the 1890s and 1900s with the intent of introducing beautification and monumental grandeur into cities. Its proponents held that such beautification could promote social harmony, civic virtue, and a harmonious social order.

Monumental civic architecture and landscaped boulevards were hallmarks of the movement.

Origins

The movement grew out of the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893 in Chicago, whose gleaming neoclassical "White City", planned under architect Daniel Burnham, demonstrated the visual power of coordinated civic design. The McMillan Plan of 1901–1902 applied the ideas to Washington, D.C., restoring and extending L'Enfant's plan for the National Mall. Burnham's 1909 Plan of Chicago — "Make no little plans" — became the movement's most famous comprehensive city plan.

Characteristics and legacy

City Beautiful schemes favored Beaux-Arts neoclassicism, grand axial boulevards, civic centers grouping public buildings, park systems, and tree-lined parkways. Cities including Cleveland, Denver, Kansas City, San Francisco, and Indianapolis adopted such plans. Critics charged the movement with neglecting housing, sanitation, and the everyday needs of the poor in favor of aesthetics; by the 1910s it was eclipsed by the more pragmatic "City Practical" approach of professional city planning, though its parks, civic centers, and boulevards remain defining features of many American cities.
