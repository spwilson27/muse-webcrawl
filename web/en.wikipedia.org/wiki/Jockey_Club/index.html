The Jockey Club is a British organisation associated with horse racing. Founded in 1750 at Newmarket, long the headquarters of English racing, it governed the sport in Britain for more than two centuries, setting the rules of racing, licensing participants and maintaining the General Stud Book of Thoroughbred breeding, before handing regulatory functions to successor bodies in the 1990s and 2000s.

History

The club emerged from a group of racing gentlemen who met at the Star and Garter in Pall Mall and later established themselves at Newmarket, where the subscription rooms became the centre of the turf. Its members were drawn from the aristocracy and landed gentry, and its authority rested on custom rather than statute: owners who defied the club were "warned off" every racecourse in the land. The club introduced rules on handicapping, colours registration and the settlement of betting disputes, and under figures such as Admiral Rous, the great nineteenth-century handicapper, shaped the pattern of the British racing calendar.

Loss of regulatory role

From the 1990s the club progressively surrendered its governing powers: the British Horseracing Board took over governance in 1993, and regulation and integrity passed to the Horseracing Regulatory Authority and then the British Horseracing Authority in 2007. The club retains the General Stud Book through its Weatherbys connection in the public mind, and remains one of British racing's largest commercial operators.

Modern activities

Today the Jockey Club owns and operates fifteen racecourses, including Cheltenham, Aintree, Epsom Downs and Newmarket's two courses, home of the Grand National, the Cheltenham Festival and the Derby. Through Jockey Club Estates it manages the training grounds at Newmarket and Lambourn and the National Stud. The name "Jockey Club" has been adopted by racing authorities in many other countries.
