Circumcision and HIV

Voluntary medical male circumcision services in Africa

Research on circumcision and HIV has investigated the effect of male circumcision on the transmission of the human immunodeficiency virus. Three randomised controlled trials conducted in sub-Saharan Africa in the 2000s — in South Africa, Kenya, and Uganda — found that medical circumcision of adult men reduced the risk of acquiring HIV through heterosexual intercourse by approximately 50 to 60 percent. On the basis of these results, the World Health Organization and UNAIDS recommended in 2007 that voluntary medical male circumcision (VMMC) be promoted as an additional HIV prevention strategy in settings with high HIV prevalence and low circumcision rates.

Proposed mechanism

The foreskin's inner mucosal surface contains cells targeted by HIV, including Langerhans cells, and is thought to be more susceptible to microtears and viral entry than the keratinised skin of the circumcised penis. Removing the foreskin also reduces genital ulcer disease and some other sexually transmitted infections that facilitate HIV transmission.

Public health programmes

Following the WHO recommendation, large-scale VMMC programmes were implemented across eastern and southern Africa, performing tens of millions of circumcisions in priority countries; modelling studies attribute substantial numbers of averted infections to the intervention. Circumcision is promoted as a complement to, not a replacement for, condoms, antiretroviral treatment as prevention, and pre-exposure prophylaxis, since its protection is partial and applies to female-to-male transmission; evidence for direct protection of female partners or for men who have sex with men is inconclusive.

Debate

The findings and their policy translation have been debated. Critics question the generalisability of the African trial results to other epidemics and social settings, raise concerns about risk compensation, and situate the programmes within the broader ethics controversies surrounding non-therapeutic circumcision. Public-health bodies generally maintain that adult voluntary circumcision in high-prevalence settings is cost-effective, while emphasising informed consent and surgical safety, including the management of adverse events.
