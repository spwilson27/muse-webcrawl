Intellectual disability (ID), also called intellectual developmental disorder, is a neurodevelopmental condition characterised by significant limitations in intellectual functioning and in adaptive behaviour (the everyday practical, conceptual and social skills needed to live independently). Both limitations must be present before age 18. The term has largely replaced "mental retardation" in professional and legal usage following Rosa's Law (2010) in the United States, since the older term had become offensive.

Diagnosis and classification

Diagnosis requires standardised testing: an IQ score below approximately 70 (two standard deviations below the mean) and deficits in adaptive functioning. Severity is classified as mild, moderate, severe, or profound; the majority of affected people fall in the mild range and can live and work largely independently with support. ICD-11 groups these conditions under "disorders of intellectual development" (6A00), and DSM-5-TR calls them "intellectual developmental disorder".

Causes

Causes are diverse: genetic syndromes (notably Down syndrome and fragile X syndrome), prenatal factors (fetal alcohol exposure, maternal infections, iodine deficiency), perinatal injury, childhood infections and malnutrition, and head trauma. In many cases no single cause is identified. Some causes are preventable: universal salt iodisation and lead abatement are among the most cost-effective public-health measures known.

Management and rights

There is no cure, but early intervention, special education, and supported employment substantially improve outcomes. The disability rights movement and frameworks such as the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities have shifted policy from institutionalisation — dominant in much of the twentieth century — towards inclusion, community living, and supported decision-making. Organisations such as the Special Olympics promote participation in sport.
