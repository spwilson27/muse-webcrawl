Anal cleansing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anal cleansing is the hygienic practice of cleaning the anus after defecation. Methods vary widely by culture and region. In many Western countries toilet paper is the standard; in much of the Islamic world, South and Southeast Asia, and parts of southern Europe, water washing — with the left hand, a bidet, a bidet shower ("health faucet"), or a lota vessel — is customary, often reflecting religious requirements for ritual purity such as istinja in Islam.

Methods and history

Historical materials included sponges on sticks (the Roman tersorium), corn cobs, leaves, stones, and cloth. Commercial toilet paper became widespread in the late 19th and 20th centuries. In Japan, washlet toilets with integrated bidet functions are common. Health considerations include reducing skin irritation and the spread of pathogens through handwashing after cleansing; dermatologists sometimes recommend water washing for conditions such as haemorrhoids or anal fissures.

Cultural variation

Preferences are strongly cultural: surveys show water-based cleansing dominates in Muslim-majority countries and much of Asia, while dry paper dominates in North America and northern Europe. Environmental debates contrast the water consumption of washing with the paper, energy, and forestry footprint of toilet paper.
