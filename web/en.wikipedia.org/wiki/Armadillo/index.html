Armadillo

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The nine-banded armadillo (Dasypus novemcinctus), the most widespread species.

Armadillos (Spanish for "little armoured ones") are New World placental mammals in the order Cingulata, characterised by a leathery armour shell formed of bony plates (osteoderms) covered in overlapping keratin scales. There are about 21 extant species, ranging in size from the pink fairy armadillo (about 125 g) to the giant armadillo (up to about 54 kg). They are native to the Americas, with most species in South America; the nine-banded armadillo has expanded its range into the southern United States and Mexico.

Description

Armadillos have short legs, long claws for digging, poor eyesight but a keen sense of smell, and a low body temperature (around 34 °C). The shell consists of a shoulder shield, a pelvic shield, and, in many species, a series of movable bands in between, allowing some species—but only the three-banded armadillos—to roll into a complete ball. They are prolific diggers and mostly nocturnal, feeding on insects, grubs, and other invertebrates.

Evolution and classification

Armadillos belong to the superorder Xenarthra, along with anteaters and sloths. Their extinct relatives include the huge glyptodonts, whose domed shells could exceed three metres in length, and the horned pampatheres. Genetic studies place the origin of the cingulate lineage in South America over 60 million years ago.

Reproduction and behaviour

The nine-banded armadillo is famous for polyembryony: a single fertilised egg splits into four genetically identical quadruplets. Most armadillos are solitary and dig burrows for shelter. When startled, nine-banded armadillos can leap vertically into the air—a trait that often proves fatal in encounters with vehicles.

Armadillos and humans

Armadillos are hunted for food and their shells in parts of Latin America, and the charango, an Andean instrument, was traditionally made from an armadillo shell. The nine-banded armadillo is the only other known animal besides humans naturally susceptible to leprosy, making it an important model organism and a rare zoonotic source of the disease. The giant armadillo and several other species are threatened by habitat loss and hunting.
