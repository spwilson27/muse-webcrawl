The European badger (Meles meles), also known as the Eurasian badger, is a badger species in the family Mustelidae native to Europe and parts of Asia. It is classified as least concern, with a wide distribution and large total population.

Description

European badgers are powerfully built, black, white, brown and grey animals with short legs and long, strong claws adapted for digging. The head is marked with distinctive black stripes passing over the eyes against a white face. Adults weigh 8–12 kg, varying seasonally, and measure 60–90 cm in body length.

Behaviour and ecology

Badgers are nocturnal and social, living in family groups of up to about twenty individuals in large underground burrow systems called setts, which may be used for many generations. They are omnivorous: earthworms form the bulk of the diet in much of Europe, supplemented by insects, small mammals, carrion, cereals and fruit. Cubs are born in early spring after delayed implantation.

Humans and disease

Badgers are protected in many countries, though they have historically been persecuted through badger baiting and digging. In the United Kingdom and Ireland, badgers have been implicated in the transmission of bovine tuberculosis to cattle, and licensed culling as part of disease-control policy has been a subject of sustained scientific and public controversy.
