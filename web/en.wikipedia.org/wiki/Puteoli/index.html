Puteoli


The Macellum of Puteoli, long misidentified as a temple of Serapis.

Puteoli was an ancient Roman port city on the northern shore of the Gulf of Naples in Campania, at the site of modern Pozzuoli. Founded as the Greek colony of Dikaiarcheia around 530 BC and refounded as a Roman colony in 194 BC, it became the principal port of Rome and Italy's greatest commercial harbour until the emperor Claudius built the harbour at Portus near Ostia.

History

Through Puteoli passed the grain ships of Alexandria, the trade of the eastern Mediterranean, and passengers such as the Apostle Paul, who landed there on his way to Rome (Acts 28). The city was famous for its commerce, its wealthy merchants, and its volcanic surroundings—the Phlegraean Fields—whose pozzolana (volcanic ash, named after the city) made Roman hydraulic concrete possible. Cicero owned a villa nearby, and the area was lined with the resorts of the Roman elite. Puteoli declined after the growth of Portus and suffered from barbarian invasions and bradyseism—the slow rising and sinking of the ground due to volcanic activity.

Bradyseism and archaeology

The columns of the city's Macellum (market building), long called the "Serapeum," bear marine borings up to several metres high, proving the ground sank below the sea and rose again; Charles Lyell used them as a frontispiece to his Principles of Geology (1830) as evidence of gradual geological change. The Flavian Amphitheatre at Puteoli, the third-largest in Italy with a capacity of about 50,000, and a network of subterranean structures survive. Renewed ground uplift in the 1980s forced the evacuation of Pozzuoli's old town.
