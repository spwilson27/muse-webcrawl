Checkpoint inhibitor

Checkpoint blockade releases the "brakes" on T cells

A checkpoint inhibitor is a drug — usually a monoclonal antibody — that blocks immune checkpoint proteins used by cancers to suppress the immune system. By releasing these "brakes", checkpoint inhibitors allow T cells to attack tumours. They are a major class of cancer immunotherapy and have produced durable remissions in some previously incurable cancers.

Mechanism and targets

  • CTLA-4 — cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4, an early checkpoint on T cells. Ipilimumab, approved in 2011 for melanoma, was the first checkpoint inhibitor.
  • PD-1 — programmed cell death protein 1 on T cells. Inhibitors include pembrolizumab and nivolumab.
  • PD-L1 — the ligand for PD-1, often expressed by tumour cells. Inhibitors include atezolizumab, durvalumab and avelumab.
  • LAG-3 — targeted by relatlimab, approved in combination with nivolumab in 2022.

Clinical use

Checkpoint inhibitors are approved for many cancers, including melanoma, non-small cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, head and neck cancers, and tumours with mismatch-repair deficiency (a tissue-agnostic approval based on the MSI-H/dMMR biomarker). Response rates vary widely; combinations such as nivolumab plus ipilimumab are more effective but more toxic. PD-L1 expression and tumour mutational burden are used as imperfect predictive biomarkers.

Adverse effects

Because they unleash the immune system, checkpoint inhibitors cause distinctive immune-related adverse events: inflammation of the colon, lungs, liver, skin and endocrine glands, among others. These are usually managed by pausing treatment and giving corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants; endocrine damage may be permanent and require hormone replacement.

History

The field grew from the work of James P. Allison, who showed that blocking CTLA-4 could cure tumours in mice in the 1990s, and Tasuku Honjo, who discovered PD-1. Allison and Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discovery of cancer therapy by inhibition of negative immune regulation".
