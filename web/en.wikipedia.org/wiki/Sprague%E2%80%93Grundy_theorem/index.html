Sprague–Grundy theorem

The Sprague–Grundy theorem is a fundamental result in combinatorial game theory, discovered independently by Roland Sprague (1935) and Patrick Grundy (1939). It states that every finite impartial game under the normal play convention — where the last move wins — is equivalent to a Nim heap of a certain size, called its Grundy value or nimber.

Grundy values

The Grundy value of a position is the mex (minimum excluded value) of the Grundy values of the positions reachable from it. Terminal positions, from which no move is possible, have value 0. A position is losing for the player to move exactly when its Grundy value is 0.

Disjunctive sums

The theorem's power lies in analysing sums of games — such as a collection of subgames where a move consists of moving in one component. The Grundy value of a sum is the bitwise XOR (the "nim-sum") of the Grundy values of the components, exactly as in the game of Nim analysed by Charles Bouton in 1901. A player can therefore compute a winning strategy for complicated take-away and turning-turtles games by reducing them to Nim.

Applications

The theorem applies to games including Green Hackenbush, Treblecross, Dawson's Kayles, and many subtraction games. It founded the modern theory of impartial combinatorial games later developed in works such as Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays.
