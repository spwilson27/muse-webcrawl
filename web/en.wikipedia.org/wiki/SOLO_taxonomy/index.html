DomainEducational psychology, assessment

The SOLO taxonomy (Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes) is a model of learning and assessment developed by Australian psychologist John Biggs and his collaborator Kevin Collis, first set out in their 1982 book Evaluating the Quality of Learning. Rather than classifying a learner's overall ability, the taxonomy classifies the structural complexity of a student's response to a particular task, making it a tool for designing assessments and setting learning outcomes at increasing levels of sophistication.

Levels

The taxonomy describes five levels of increasing complexity. At the prestructural level, the student's response misses the point of the task entirely, using irrelevant information or simply restating the question. At the unistructural level, the response identifies one relevant aspect of the task. At the multistructural level, several relevant aspects are identified but treated independently, without integration. At the relational level, the aspects are integrated into a coherent whole, allowing the student to draw conclusions and see the overall structure. Finally, at the extended abstract level, the integrated structure is generalised to a new domain or applied hypothetically, going beyond the given information.

Relation to other models

The SOLO taxonomy is frequently compared with Bloom's taxonomy, but whereas Bloom's model classifies intended learning objectives and cognitive processes, SOLO focuses on the observable structure of actual student responses. Biggs later built the taxonomy into his theory of constructive alignment, in which intended learning outcomes, teaching activities and assessment tasks are aligned so that assessment criteria are expressed in SOLO terms.

Applications

Teachers and curriculum designers use the taxonomy to write outcome statements with verbs matched to target levels (for example, "identify" for unistructural outcomes and "theorise" or "generalise" for extended abstract outcomes), to construct marking rubrics, and to diagnose where students' understanding is breaking down.
