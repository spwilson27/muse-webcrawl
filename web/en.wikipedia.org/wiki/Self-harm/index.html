Self-harm

Self-harm is intentional behaviour that causes injury to one's own body, most commonly cutting, burning, scratching or hitting oneself. It is usually not undertaken as a suicide attempt; rather, it is most often used as a way of coping with overwhelming emotional pain, intense anger or distress. Self-harm occurs most frequently among adolescents and young adults.

Terminology

Terms include deliberate self-harm, non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI), self-mutilation and parasuicide. The DSM-5 lists non-suicidal self-injury as a condition for further study, defined by intentional self-inflicted damage on five or more days in the past year without suicidal intent.

Causes and functions

Self-harm is associated with mental disorders including depression, anxiety, eating disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder and borderline personality disorder, as well as with experiences of trauma and abuse. Functions reported include regulating emotions, expressing distress, self-punishment, and relieving feelings of numbness. Temporary relief followed by shame or guilt can reinforce the behaviour as a coping cycle.

Prevalence

Lifetime prevalence in community samples of adolescents is commonly estimated between 13% and 23%, with lower rates among adults. Rates of hospital presentation have risen in many countries since the early 2000s, particularly among adolescent girls.

Treatment

No medication is specifically approved for self-harm; treatment focuses on underlying conditions and on psychological therapies. Dialectical behaviour therapy and mentalisation-based treatment have evidence for reducing repetition, as do structured problem-solving and safety planning. Compassionate, non-judgemental responses from health services are considered essential to effective care.
