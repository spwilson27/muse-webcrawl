Sugar tax

A sugar tax (also called a soda tax or sugar-sweetened beverage tax) is a levy or surcharge applied to drinks — and occasionally foods — containing added sugar. It is a "sin tax" designed as a public-health intervention: by raising prices it aims to reduce consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, which are associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes and dental decay, and in some designs it incentivises manufacturers to reformulate products with less sugar.

Implementations

Mexico's 2014 tax of one peso per litre was followed by a fall in purchases of taxed drinks of about 5.5% in the first year and 9.7% in the second. The United Kingdom's Soft Drinks Industry Levy (2018) is tiered by sugar concentration — higher rates at 8 g and 5 g per 100 ml — and drove widespread reformulation, cutting the total sugar sold in soft drinks substantially even before it took effect. Other jurisdictions with such taxes include France, Hungary, South Africa, Chile, Saudi Arabia, several US cities including Berkeley and Philadelphia, and Catalonia.

Evidence and debate

Studies generally find that sugary drink purchases fall after taxation, with the largest effects in lower-income households. Critics argue the taxes are regressive, harm small businesses, and that consumers may substitute other calorific products; supporters respond that the health benefits are also concentrated among poorer groups, whose burden of disease is highest. The World Health Organization recommends taxing sugar-sweetened beverages as part of its strategy against non-communicable diseases, suggesting a price rise of at least 20% to achieve significant health effects.
