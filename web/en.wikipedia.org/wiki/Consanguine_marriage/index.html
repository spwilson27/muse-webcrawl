Consanguine marriage

Prevalence of consanguineous marriages (second cousins or closer) by country

Consanguine marriage is marriage between individuals who are closely related by blood (consanguinity). Though it may involve incestuous unions outside socially accepted norms, a consanguineous marriage is usually understood as a union between persons who are related as second cousins or closer. It is estimated that more than 10 per cent of marriages worldwide are consanguineous, and the practice is particularly prevalent in North Africa, the Middle East, and West, Central and South Asia, where in some countries 20–50 per cent or more of marriages are between relatives, most commonly first cousins.

Social and cultural aspects

Consanguineous marriage has deep historical roots: it was practised in ancient Egypt (including among royalty and occasionally siblings), the Inca and Hawaiian royal lines, and widely among propertied families seeking to keep land and wealth within the group. Preferred patterns vary: patrilateral parallel-cousin marriage (a man marrying his father's brother's daughter) is characteristic of much of the Arab world; cross-cousin marriage is traditional in South India; double first-cousin and uncle–niece unions occur in some communities. Perceived social advantages include strengthening family ties, greater compatibility between the bride and her in-laws, keeping property within the family, and lower dowry or bridewealth costs. The Roman Catholic Church historically restricted cousin marriage by canon law but grants dispensations; the Church of England has permitted first-cousin marriage since the 16th century, and it is legal in most of Europe and in about half of US states.

Genetic aspects

Children of related parents have a higher proportion of homozygous genes and thus an increased risk of autosomal recessive disorders. For first cousins, the risk of a child having a serious congenital anomaly or genetic disease is roughly doubled compared with the general population — on the order of an additional 1.7–2.8 percentage points above a baseline of about 2–3 per cent — and repeated consanguinity over generations compounds the risk. Genetic counselling is recommended for consanguineous couples in many health systems. Population studies in highly consanguineous communities have also been scientifically valuable in identifying recessive disease genes.

Legal status

Laws vary widely. First-cousin marriage is legal in most of the world, including the United Kingdom, most of Europe, Japan and Brazil; it is prohibited in China and in roughly half of US states, a legacy of 19th- and early 20th-century American legislation influenced by early (and partly eugenic) concerns. Marriages between closer relatives are almost universally prohibited as incest.
