Seed drill

A seed drill is a device used in agriculture that sows seeds for crops by positioning them in the soil and burying them to a specific depth. This ensures that seeds will be distributed evenly. The seed drill sows the seeds at the proper seeding rate and depth, ensuring that the seeds are covered by soil, which protects them from being eaten by birds and animals, or being dried up due to exposure to the sun. This differs from a broadcast seeder, which scatters seed over the surface of the field.

History

Seed drills were used in ancient China, where multi-tube iron seed drills appeared by the Han dynasty, and in earlier Mesopotamian agriculture. In Europe, the Venetian Senate granted a patent for a seed drill to Camillo Torello in 1566. The machine is popularly associated with the English agricultural pioneer Jethro Tull, who developed a horse-drawn seed drill around 1701 and described it in Horse-hoeing Husbandry (1731). Tull's drill metered seed from a hopper through a rotating cylinder into furrows cut by coulters — a key technology of the British Agricultural Revolution.

Design and use

A modern drill consists of a seed hopper, a metering mechanism (fluted rollers, pneumatic systems or precision discs), seed tubes, and furrow openers — discs, tines or coulters — followed by press wheels or harrows that firm soil over the seed. Drills allow precise control of seeding rate, depth and row spacing, conserving expensive seed, improving germination and making later operations such as hoeing and harvesting easier. Specialised forms include air seeders for broadacre farming and no-till drills that sow directly into untilled stubble.
