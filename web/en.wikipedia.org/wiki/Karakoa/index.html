Karakoa

TypeOutrigger warship (lashed-lug plank boat)
RegionVisayas and Mindanao, Philippines
EraPre-colonial to 19th century
PropulsionSail (tanja / crab-claw) and paddlers/rowers
RoleSea raiding (mangayaw), warfare, prestige vessel

Karakoa (also spelled caracoa) were large outrigger warships of the Visayan and other peoples of the Philippines, used for centuries before and after Spanish colonisation. Built with lashed-lug plank construction and twin outriggers, they were fast, manoeuvrable vessels mounting raised fighting platforms (burulan) for warriors, and were rowed by ranks of paddlers in addition to their sails.

Description and use

Large karakoa could exceed 25 metres in length and carry more than a hundred rowers and warriors. They served as the flagships of datus (chiefs) in the seasonal sea-raiding expeditions known as mangayaw or pangayaw, central to prestige and warfare among coastal polities of the Visayas, Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago. Spanish accounts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries describe them in detail and employed captured or allied karakoa in colonial campaigns.

Decline

Spanish restrictions on raiding and shipbuilding, and the shift to European-style vessels, drove the karakoa into decline by the nineteenth century, though related outrigger craft such as the balangay and vinta persist in Philippine maritime culture.
