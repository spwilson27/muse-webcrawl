Indusium

An indusium (plural: indusia) is a thin membranous covering that protects a developing sorus — a cluster of sporangia — in many ferns. The presence, shape and attachment of the indusium are important characters used in fern identification and classification.

Sori covered by flap-like indusia on a fern frond.

Form and function

The indusium is an outgrowth of the leaf tissue (a "true" indusium, as in many members of the Dryopteridaceae) or is formed by the inrolled leaf margin (a "false" indusium, as in bracken and maidenhair ferns). Shapes vary widely between groups: round or kidney-shaped (reniform), linear along a vein, cup-shaped, or hood-like. As the sporangia ripen, the indusium typically shrivels or lifts, exposing the mature sporangia so the spores can be released.

In other organisms

The term is also used in other contexts, including for a protective case or covering in some invertebrates and for the amnion-like membrane in certain insect development, though its botanical sense — the covering of a fern sorus — is the most familiar.
