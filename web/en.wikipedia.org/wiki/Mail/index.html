The mail or post is a system for physically transporting postcards, letters, and parcels. A postal service can be private or public, though many governments place restrictions on private systems. Since the mid-19th century, national postal systems have generally been established as a government monopoly, with a fee on the article prepaid, often in the form of adhesive postage stamps.

History

The practice of communication by written documents carried by an intermediary from one person or place to another dates back almost to the invention of writing, but formal postal systems developed in the empires of Persia, China, Rome and Egypt. Modern postal systems emerged in early modern Europe; the introduction of the postage stamp in Britain in 1840, with the Penny Black, and uniform penny postage, greatly increased use of the mails.

The Universal Postal Union, founded in 1874, coordinates postal policies between member nations and maintains the worldwide postal system.

How mail works

Mail is collected from posting boxes, post offices and business premises, then sorted — increasingly by automated machinery reading addresses and barcodes — and transported by road, rail, air and sea to the destination area, where it is sorted again for delivery rounds. Delivery staff, commonly called mail carriers or postmen/postwomen, deliver to homes and businesses, typically to a letter box or mailbox.

Types of mail

  • Letters and postcards — the classic personal and business correspondence.
  • Parcels and packages — goods, including the enormous volumes generated by e-commerce.
  • Registered and recorded mail — services providing proof of posting and delivery.
  • Express and courier services — time-guaranteed delivery.

Decline of letters and growth of parcels

The rise of email and electronic billing has greatly reduced personal letter volumes since the early 2000s, while parcel traffic has grown steeply with online shopping. Many postal operators have restructured around logistics and parcels in response.
