Caesium

Caesium (IUPAC spelling; also spelled cesium in American English) is a chemical element; it has symbol Cs and atomic number 55. It is a soft, silvery-golden alkali metal with a melting point of 28.4 °C (83.2 °F), which makes it one of only five elemental metals that are liquid at or near room temperature. Caesium has physical and chemical properties similar to those of rubidium and potassium.

History

Caesium was the first element discovered by spectroscopy: in 1860 Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff found its bright blue spectral lines in mineral water from Dürkheim and named it from the Latin caesius ("sky blue"). It is the most electropositive and most alkaline stable element; its only stable isotope is caesium-133.

Applications

The second is defined by 9,192,631,770 periods of the radiation from the hyperfine transition of the caesium-133 atom, making caesium atomic clocks the basis of international timekeeping (and of GPS). Caesium compounds are used in photoelectric cells, drilling fluids (caesium formate), organic chemistry and as catalysts. The radioactive isotope caesium-137, a fission product, is used in industrial gauges and radiotherapy, and is notorious as an environmental contaminant of the Chernobyl and Fukushima accidents and of the 1987 Goiânia accident in Brazil.

Handling

Metallic caesium reacts explosively with water and is pyrophoric, so it is stored under inert gas or vacuum in sealed ampoules.
