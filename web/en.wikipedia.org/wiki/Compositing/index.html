Compositing

Compositing is the process or technique of combining visual elements from separate sources into single images, often to create the illusion that all those elements are parts of the same scene. Live-action shooting for compositing is variously called "chroma key", "blue screen", "green screen" and similar terms. Today, most, though not all, compositing is achieved through digital image manipulation. Pre-digital compositing techniques, however, go back as far as the trick films of Georges Méliès in the late 19th century, and some are still in use.

Basic procedure

All compositing involves the replacement of selected parts of an image with other material, usually (but not always) from another image. In the digital method of compositing, software commands designate a narrowly defined range of colour in the digital image that is to be replaced. Then the software replaces every pixel within the designated colour range with a pixel from another image, aligned to appear as part of the original. For example, one could record a television weather presenter positioned in front of a plain blue or green background, while compositing software replaces only this designated colour with the chosen weather maps.

Typical applications

  • Film and television – combining actors shot against blue or green screens with computer-generated or separately filmed backgrounds, set extensions, and visual effects elements.
  • Matte painting – painted or digital environments merged with live-action plates; traditionally done with glass paintings and optical printers.
  • Multiple exposures – photographic and in-camera compositing techniques historically used for trick photography and spirit photography.
  • Photomontage – combining elements of several photographs into a new image.

Digital compositing

Modern compositing software, such as Adobe After Effects, Nuke, and Blackmagic Fusion, is typically node-based or layer-based and builds on the mathematics of the alpha channel and compositing algebra formalized by Thomas Porter and Tom Duff in 1984 ("Compositing Digital Images"). The "over" operator places a foreground element over a background according to the foreground's alpha (opacity) values. Related operations include tracking, rotoscoping, keying, colour correction, and 3D compositing in which rendered CGI is combined with live action.

Physical compositing

Physical compositing techniques include in-camera effects, rear and front projection, miniature photography, and the use of matte paintings. Some contemporary filmmakers continue to prefer practical composites for their realism and the constraints they impose on staging.
