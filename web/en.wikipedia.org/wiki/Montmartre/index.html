Montmartre is a large hill in Paris's northern 18th arrondissement, 130 metres high, giving its name to the surrounding neighbourhood. It is one of the most visited districts of Paris, famous for the white-domed Basilica of the Sacré-Cœur on its summit and for its history as the home of the artists, cabarets, and bohemian nightlife of the Belle Époque. The name is traditionally derived from mons Martyrum, "mount of martyrs," commemorating the legendary beheading of Saint Denis, the first bishop of Paris, around 250 CE; an alternative Roman etymology is mons Martis, "mount of Mars."

History

A village outside Paris for most of its history, Montmartre was known for its gypsum quarries (the source of "plaster of Paris"), its windmills, and its vineyards — one small vineyard, the Clos Montmartre, survives today. It was annexed to Paris in 1860. Its cheap rents and position outside the city's wine-tax wall (before annexation) made it a magnet for cabarets and dance halls such as the Moulin Rouge (opened 1889) and Le Chat Noir.

Artists' quarter

From the 1880s to the First World War, Montmartre was the centre of modern art. Renoir, Toulouse-Lautrec, Van Gogh, Utrillo, Modigliani, and Picasso (who lived at the Bateau-Lavoir studio building and painted Les Demoiselles d'Avignon there in 1907) were among the artists and writers who lived and worked in the quarter. After the war, the artistic centre of Paris shifted to Montparnasse, but Montmartre retained its picturesque reputation, celebrated in films such as Amélie (2001).

Landmarks

The Sacré-Cœur Basilica, built between 1875 and 1914 as a national votive offering after the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune, crowns the hill; the Commune itself began on Montmartre in March 1871, when crowds prevented the army from removing the National Guard's cannons stored there. Other landmarks include the Place du Tertre, filled with portraitists; the Moulin de la Galette, immortalised by Renoir; the Lapin Agile cabaret; the Espace Dalí; and the Montmartre Cemetery.
