Ross's paradox

Ross's paradox is a puzzle in deontic logic — the logic of obligation and permission — identified by the Danish philosopher Alf Ross in 1941. It arises from the principle that if an action is obligatory, then a disjunction of that action with any other action is also obligatory.

The paradox

In standard deontic logic, from "You ought to mail the letter" (Op) one may infer by disjunction introduction "You ought to mail the letter or burn it" (O(p ∨ q)). Formally the inference is valid, yet the conclusion seems false or even absurd: the obligation to mail the letter gives one no licence to be told that burning it would equally satisfy what one ought to do. The paradox is more precisely about imperatives: from the imperative "Mail the letter!" it seems invalid to derive "Mail the letter or burn it!", since the second command could be obeyed by burning the letter.

Responses

Proposed resolutions include denying that imperatives and obligations are truth-apt in the way the formalism assumes; distinguishing the logical content of an obligation from what an utterance of it pragmatically conveys (compare Gricean implicature and the related "free choice permission" puzzle); restricting disjunction introduction in normative contexts; and revising the semantics of deontic logic so that an obligation's content concerns the best or required outcomes rather than arbitrary weakenings of them. The paradox remains a standard test case for systems of deontic logic and the logic of imperatives.
