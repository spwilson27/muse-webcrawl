Haze

Haze is traditionally an atmospheric phenomenon in which dust, smoke, and other dry particulates suspended in air obscure the clarity of the sky.

Haze is an atmospheric phenomenon in which dust, smoke, and other dry particulate matter suspended in the air obscure the clarity of the sky and reduce visibility. The World Meteorological Organization's code distinguishes haze from fog and mist by the dryness of the particles involved: haze particles are dry, while fog and mist consist of water droplets. When humid air is involved, the phenomenon is often a blend of mist and haze.

Causes

Haze arises from numerous sources, including agricultural burning, traffic and industrial emissions, forest fires, and wind-blown dust. Secondary particles form in the atmosphere when gases such as sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and ammonia react to produce sulphates, nitrates and ammonium salts. Seasonal transboundary haze from land-clearing fires in Southeast Asia has repeatedly blanketed Malaysia, Singapore and Indonesia, prompting the ASEAN Agreement on Transboundary Haze Pollution (2002).

Effects

Haze reduces visibility, affecting aviation and road transport, and the fine particles responsible (notably PM2.5) penetrate deep into the lungs, aggravating respiratory and cardiovascular disease. Haze also scatters sunlight, slightly cooling the surface while warming the layer of air in which it sits. In polar regions, "Arctic haze" results from long-range transport of industrial pollutants from Eurasia.
