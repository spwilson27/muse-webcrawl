Hovercraft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A large hovercraft on its air cushion crossing the sea near a beach]
An SR.N4-style hovercraft at sea.

A hovercraft (air-cushion vehicle, ACV) is a craft that travels over land, water, mud and ice on a cushion of air trapped beneath it by a flexible skirt. Christopher Cockerell\'s experiments in 1950s England led to the SR.N1 crossing the English Channel in 1959; giant SR.N4 car ferries ran the Channel route for 32 years until 2000, and military LCAC hovercraft serve the US and other navies for amphibious landings.

Principle

A lift fan blows air under the hull into a plenum chamber contained by a skirt of rubberised fabric \'fingers\'; the craft floats on this cushion with little friction, propelled by air propellers and steered by rudders and differential thrust. Having no contact with the surface, hovercraft are truly amphibious but struggle with steep slopes and side winds.

Service history

Cross-Channel SR.N4s carried up to 418 passengers and 60 cars at 60+ knots, but fuel costs, the Channel Tunnel and duty-free abolition ended them in 2000. Smaller Griffon Hoverwork craft still link Portsmouth with the Isle of Wight (the world\'s only remaining scheduled passenger hovercraft service, as often described), and coastguards use ACVs on mudflats and ice.

Military use

The US Navy\'s LCAC (and its successor SSC) lands vehicles and troops over 70% of the world\'s coastlines unreachable by landing craft; Russia\'s Zubr class is the largest military hovercraft, and Iran and China operate their own types.
