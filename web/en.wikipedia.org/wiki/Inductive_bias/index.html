Inductive bias

The inductive bias (also known as learning bias) of a learning algorithm is the set of assumptions that the learner uses to predict outputs of given inputs that it has not encountered. Without such assumptions, generalisation from finite data would be impossible: for any training set there are infinitely many functions consistent with it that disagree on unseen inputs.

A learner's bias restricts the hypotheses it will consider.

Role in learning theory

Inductive bias is formalised in notions such as the VC dimension and in the no free lunch theorems, which show that averaged over all possible problems, no learner outperforms any other — success depends on matching the learner's bias to the structure of the actual problem. A classic example of strong inductive bias is Occam's razor: preferring the simplest hypothesis consistent with the data.

Examples

Different algorithms embody different biases. Linear regression assumes the target is (approximately) a linear function of the inputs. Decision trees prefer short trees with high-information-gain splits near the root. Convolutional neural networks build in translation equivariance and locality, biases well suited to images. In Bayesian learning, the bias is expressed explicitly as a prior distribution over hypotheses.

Bias and variance

Inductive bias interacts with the bias–variance tradeoff: stronger bias generally lowers variance (less sensitivity to the particular training sample) at the cost of systematic error when the bias is wrong, while weaker bias risks overfitting.
