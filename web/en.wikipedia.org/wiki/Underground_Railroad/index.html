Underground Railroad
Activec. 1780s–1865
RegionUnited States, Canada, Mexico, Caribbean

The Underground Railroad was a network of secret routes and safe houses used by enslaved African Americans, mainly in the decades before the American Civil War, to escape to free states and Canada. It was neither underground nor a railroad; the terms "stations", "conductors" and "passengers" were code.

Operation

White abolitionists, free Black people and formerly enslaved people sheltered fugitives, moved them by night — often following the North Star — and forwarded them station to station. Harriet Tubman, herself escaped, returned south about 13 times and rescued some 70 people; Levi Coffin in Indiana and Ohio was called its "president"; William Still in Philadelphia documented hundreds of arrivals. Estimates suggest around 100,000 people escaped via the network. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which compelled northern complicity in recapture, pushed the terminus to Canada, where slavery had been abolished empire-wide in 1834.

Legacy

The Railroad became a central symbol of resistance to slavery and interracial cooperation. Its work ended with emancipation and the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865, though escapes also ran south to Mexico and to Florida and the Caribbean.
