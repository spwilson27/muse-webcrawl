Post-transition metal

Post-transition metals are the metallic elements in the periodic table located between the transition metals (to their left) and the metalloids (to their right). Depending on the definition used, the group usually includes aluminium, gallium, indium, thallium, tin, lead, and bismuth, and sometimes also zinc, cadmium, mercury, and polonium. These elements are generally softer, weaker, and lower-melting than transition metals, with poorer mechanical strength and electrical conductivity, and they tend to form more covalent compounds.

Terminology

There is no IUPAC-sanctioned definition of "post-transition metal," and alternative names include "poor metals," "other metals," and "p-block metals." The boundary with the metalloids is conventionally drawn near silicon, germanium, arsenic, antimony, and tellurium, so membership lists differ among textbooks. IUPAC's 2021 provisional report on the composition of group 3 and related classifications noted the term's informal status.

Properties

Compared with transition metals, post-transition metals have filled d-subshells and form ions by losing their p electrons (and often s electrons, as in the inert pair effect seen in thallium, lead, and bismuth). Consequences include lower melting and boiling points (gallium melts at 29.8 °C), greater electronegativity, more covalent character in compounds, colored-free ions in many cases, and amphoteric oxides for aluminium, zinc, tin, and lead. Bismuth's rainbow iridescent oxide and lead's historical toxicity are characteristic features of the group.

Applications

Aluminium is the most abundant metal in Earth's crust and a mainstay of transport and packaging; tin and lead are classic solder components; indium is essential to indium tin oxide touchscreens; gallium underpins gallium arsenide semiconductors; bismuth is used in cosmetics and pharmaceuticals such as bismuth subsalicylate.
