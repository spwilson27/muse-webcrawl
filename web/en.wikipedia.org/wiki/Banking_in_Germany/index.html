Banking in Germany

The skyline of Frankfurt am Main, Germany's financial centre.

Banking in Germany is characterised by a distinctive three-pillar structure (Drei-Säulen-System) comprising private commercial banks, public-sector banks, and cooperative banks. Germany has historically had a large number of small, locally rooted institutions, and bank-based finance — long-term lending relationships between firms and their Hausbank ("house bank") — has played a greater role in funding industry than capital markets.

The three pillars

Private banks include the big universal banks Deutsche Bank and Commerzbank, along with regional and foreign banks and private bankers. Public-sector banks consist of the local savings banks (Sparkassen), owned by municipalities and districts, and their regional Landesbanken, together with promotional institutions such as KfW. The Sparkassen are confined largely to their home territories and follow a public-welfare mandate. Cooperative banks (Volksbanken and Raiffeisenbanken) are member-owned institutions descended from the nineteenth-century cooperative movement of Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen and Hermann Schulze-Delitzsch.

Central bank and supervision

The Deutsche Bundesbank is Germany's central bank and a member of the Eurosystem; monetary policy is set by the European Central Bank in Frankfurt. Prudential supervision is shared between the Bundesbank and the Federal Financial Supervisory Authority (BaFin), with large banks supervised directly by the ECB's Single Supervisory Mechanism since 2014.

History

German universal banking developed in the nineteenth century, when joint-stock banks such as Deutsche Bank (1870), Dresdner Bank (1872) and Commerzbank (1870) financed railways and heavy industry. The system survived hyperinflation, the 1931 banking crisis, division after 1945, and reunification. The 2008 financial crisis badly hit several Landesbanken and required state rescues, prompting restructuring of the public pillar. The sector has since consolidated steadily: the number of credit institutions has fallen from several thousand in the 1990s to roughly 1,400–1,500, while low interest rates and digital competition have pressured traditional branch banking.
