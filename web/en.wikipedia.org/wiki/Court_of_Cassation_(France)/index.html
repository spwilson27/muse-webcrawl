The Court of Cassation (French: Cour de cassation) is the highest court in the French judicial order, with jurisdiction over civil and criminal matters. It is one of France's two supreme courts, alongside the Conseil d'État, which heads the administrative order. The court sits in the Palais de Justice on the Île de la Cité in Paris.

The Court of Cassation sits in the Palais de Justice, Paris.

Role

The Court of Cassation is not a third instance on the facts: it reviews decisions of lower courts only for errors of law (pourvoi en cassation). If the court finds that the law was misapplied, it "quashes" (casse) the judgment and ordinarily remits the case to another court of the same level for retrial (renvoi); if the law was correctly applied, the appeal is rejected (rejet). Its function is to secure the uniform interpretation of the law across France rather than to do justice in the individual facts of a case.

Organisation

The court is divided into six chambers: three civil chambers, a commercial chamber, a social chamber and a criminal chamber. A chambre mixte or a plenary assembly (assemblée plénière) hears cases raising questions of principle or conflicting interpretations between chambers. The court is headed by the First President, and its public prosecution service is led by the Prosecutor General (procureur général). Advocates who plead before it belong to a special bar (avocats aux Conseils).

The court can also give advisory opinions (avis) to lower courts on novel questions of law, and it refers priority questions of constitutionality (question prioritaire de constitutionnalité, QPC) to the Constitutional Council after filtering.

History

The court descends from the Tribunal de cassation created in 1790 during the French Revolution, itself heir to the practice of the royal Conseil du roi of annulling judgments that contravened ordinances. It received the name Cour de cassation in 1804 under Napoleon. Its model — a supreme court reviewing law rather than facts — has been widely copied in civil-law jurisdictions, including Belgium, Italy and Spain, and in many francophone states.
