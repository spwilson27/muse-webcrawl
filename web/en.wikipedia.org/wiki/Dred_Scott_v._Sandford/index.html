Dred Scott v. Sandford

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dred Scott v. Sandford, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 393 (1857), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that held that the US Constitution was not meant to include American citizenship for people of African descent, enslaved or free, so the rights and privileges the Constitution confers on American citizens could not apply to them; and that Congress had no authority to prohibit slavery in the federal territories, voiding the Missouri Compromise of 1820.

Dred Scott, enslaved in Missouri, had been taken by his owner to free Illinois and to Wisconsin Territory, and sued for his freedom on the basis of that residence (his wife Harriet litigated in parallel; the family was ultimately freed by manumission after the case). Chief Justice Roger Taney's majority opinion — asserting that at the founding Black people were regarded as beings of an inferior order with “no rights which the white man was bound to respect” (a passage the decision's defenders then and since have read in context, and history has not) — settled nothing: it inflamed the sectional crisis, helped propel the Republican Party and Lincoln (whose debates with Douglas centred on it) to power, and is ranked with Plessy among the Court's most condemned decisions. It was repudiated by the Civil War and nullified by the Thirteenth (1865) and Fourteenth (1868) Amendments, the latter expressly granting birthright citizenship to all persons born in the United States. Scott himself died in 1858, a free man for the last year of his life.
