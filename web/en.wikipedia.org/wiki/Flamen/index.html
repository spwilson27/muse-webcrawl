Flamen

A flamen (plural: flamines) was a priest of the ancient Roman religion devoted to the service of a particular god or goddess. The most famous was the Flamen Dialis, the priest of Jupiter. There were fifteen flamines in total: three major flamines (for Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus) drawn from the patricians, and twelve minor flamines drawn from the plebeians, serving deities such as Vulcan, Flora, and Pomona.

Office and restrictions

Flamines were appointed for life, though they could be removed for breaches of ritual. The Flamen Dialis was subject to an extraordinary set of taboos recorded by Aulus Gellius: he could not ride a horse, look upon an army, wear a closed ring, have knots in his clothing, touch a she-goat, a dog, raw meat, beans, or ivy, or spend three nights away from his bed in Rome. He wore distinctive dress, including the conical cap known as the apex. His wife, the flaminica Dialis, was also bound by ritual restrictions, and if she died, he was obliged to resign his office.

History

The institution was traditionally credited to King Numa Pompilius. The flamines were part of the College of Pontiffs. Under the Empire, additional flamines served the cult of deified emperors. The office faded with the Christianization of Rome in the 4th century AD.
