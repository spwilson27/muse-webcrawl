Jules Gabriel Verne (8 February 1828 – 24 March 1905) was a French novelist, poet, and playwright. His collaboration with publisher Pierre-Jules Hetzel produced the Voyages extraordinaires, a series of bestselling adventure novels including Journey to the Centre of the Earth (1864), Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas (1870), and Around the World in Eighty Days (1872).

Life

Born in the port city of Nantes, Verne trained for a legal career in Paris but devoted himself to writing. After early work for the theatre, his first novel Five Weeks in a Balloon (1863) launched his partnership with Hetzel. He later settled in Amiens, travelled widely on his yachts, and served as a town councillor. In 1886 he was shot by his nephew Gaston, leaving him lame; he died of diabetes in 1905.

Works and influence

Verne is regarded, with H. G. Wells, as a founding figure of science fiction. His novels combined adventure with contemporary scientific knowledge, anticipating submarines, space travel — From the Earth to the Moon (1865) — and videoconferencing. According to UNESCO, he is the second most-translated author in the world, between Agatha Christie and William Shakespeare.

Early English translations often abridged and altered his texts, contributing to his reputation in the Anglophone world as a children's author, a view revised by modern scholarly translations and criticism.
