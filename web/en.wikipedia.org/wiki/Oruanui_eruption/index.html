The Oruanui eruption of the Taupo Volcano in New Zealand, about 26,500 years ago, was the world's most recent super-eruption, attaining a Volcanic Explosivity Index of 8. The eruption produced about 430 km³ of pyroclastic fall deposits, 320 km³ of pyroclastic density current (ignimbrite) deposits, and 420 km³ of primary intracaldera material — equivalent to roughly 530 km³ of magma.

Geology

The eruption formed much of the northern part of the modern caldera of Lake Taupo, New Zealand's largest lake. Ignimbrite from the eruption covered much of the central North Island with material up to 200 metres deep in places, and ash fell as far away as the Chatham Islands, some 1,000 km distant. The eruption occurred near the height of the Last Glacial Maximum and is thought to have had significant effects on the regional landscape and climate.

Phases

Geologists have distinguished multiple phases of the eruption, from an initial Plinian phase through the main ignimbrite-emplacing phase, lasting in total from several days to weeks. The Oruanui ignimbrite is a distinctive, largely non-welded deposit that underlies much of the central North Island.
