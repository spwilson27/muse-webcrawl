Fire dancing

Fire dancing is a group of performance arts or skills that involve the manipulation of fire. Fire dancing typically incorporates skills based on juggling, baton twirling, poi spinning, and other forms of object manipulation. It also involves the art of manipulating other objects, such as fire staffs. Fire dancing has been practiced by many cultures throughout the world, including the Aztecs, Balinese, and Borneo's Dayak people, and has become a popular feature of modern festivals, street performance, and events such as Burning Man.


A fire performer spins poi, tracing arcs of flame.

Types of fire dance

  • Poi — balls of wicking material on chains, spun around the body in rhythmic patterns. Poi originates with the Māori people of New Zealand, where it was traditionally performed with flax balls; the fire version is a modern adaptation.
  • Fire staff — a staff with wicks at one or both ends, spun and manipulated like a martial-arts staff.
  • Fire fans, hoops, and ropes — props with multiple wicks arranged for visual effect.
  • Fire breathing and fire eating — closely related disciplines, generally treated as separate arts because of their distinct techniques and risks.
  • Palm torches and fire fingers — small wicks worn on the hands, used in belly-dance-influenced performances.

History

Fire performance has ancient antecedents. The Aztecs performed a fire dance dedicated to Xiuhtecuhtli, the god of fire. In Bali, the sanghyang dedari and fire dances formed part of ritual practice, and the Balinese fire dance in which performers trance-dance over hot coals became famous among visitors. In Polynesia, the Samoan fire knife dance (siva afi) — originally performed with a machete, later with fire added — became a celebrated competitive art.

Modern fire dancing emerged as a recreational and professional art in the late twentieth century, growing out of juggling conventions, the Hawaii and West Coast festival scenes, and events such as Burning Man in Nevada's Black Rock Desert. Dedicated gatherings such as fire-spinning festivals now teach technique and safety to thousands of practitioners.

Equipment and fuels

Fire props are usually built with Kevlar wicking, which absorbs liquid fuel and burns with a durable flame without itself being consumed quickly. Common fuels include white gas (Coleman fuel), lamp oil, and paraffin/kerosene blends, chosen for flame size, burn time, and smoke. Performers typically wear natural-fibre clothing (cotton, leather, wool) that resists melting, and keep a dedicated safety assistant (a "fire safety") with a wet towel or fire blanket and an extinguisher nearby.

Safety

Fire dancing carries risks of burns, fuel spills, and uncontrolled fire. Standard safety practice includes a pre-performance fuel-area inspection, keeping fuel containers far from the performance space, spinning off excess fuel before lighting ("spin-off"), and never performing under the influence of alcohol or near flammable scenery. Many jurisdictions require permits for open-flame performance.
