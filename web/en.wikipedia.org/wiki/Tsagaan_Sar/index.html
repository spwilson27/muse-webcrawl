Tsagaan Sar

Tsagaan Sar
Observed byMongols (Mongolia and Inner Mongolia)
TypeCultural, national holiday
SignificanceBeginning of the lunar new year; end of winter, start of spring
DateFirst new moon between 21 January and 20 February
DurationThree official days; celebrations up to a month

Tsagaan Sar (Mongolian: Цагаан сар, "White Moon") is the Mongolian Lunar New Year festival, celebrated at the first new moon of spring, usually between late January and February. It is the most important traditional holiday in Mongolia, marking the end of winter and the beginning of spring, and is a time for family reunions, paying respect to elders, and ceremonial feasting. It is closely related to the Tibetan Losar.

Customs

Preparations begin weeks ahead: homes are thoroughly cleaned, debts are settled, quarrels resolved, and large quantities of food are prepared, including hundreds or thousands of buuz (steamed meat dumplings). On the eve of the festival, bituun, families gather for a final meal of the old year.

On the morning of the new year, people perform the traditional greeting, zolgokh, in which a younger person supports an elder's outstretched arms from beneath at the elbows, while the elder rests their hands on the younger person's head or shoulders and they exchange snuff bottles and good wishes. Traditional costume (deel) is worn, and guests visiting a home are offered dairy products — the "white foods" that give the festival its name — along with milk tea, airag, sweets arranged in a layered tower (ul boov pastry stacked with curds and candies), and a whole cooked sheep's back or rump displayed as the centrepiece of the table.

History

The festival's origins lie in the shamanic traditions of the Mongol steppe; it was reshaped by Tibetan Buddhism, which spread in Mongolia from the sixteenth century and whose calendrical system fixes the date. Under the socialist Mongolian People's Republic the celebration was officially suppressed for periods (notably from the 1960s) as a religious and feudal relic, and a collectivised "herders' new year" was promoted in its place. After the democratic revolution of 1990, Tsagaan Sar was fully restored as a public holiday and a symbol of national identity.

Similar festivals

Tsagaan Sar coincides with, and shares customs with, the Tibetan Losar, and falls near the Chinese New Year and Korean Seollal, though the Mongolian date sometimes differs from the Chinese calendar by a month because of differences in the lunisolar calculations.
