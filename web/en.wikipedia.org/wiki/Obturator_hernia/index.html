Obturator hernia

Specialty: General surgery

Symptoms: Bowel obstruction, pain along the medial thigh

Risk factors: Female sex, advanced age, low body weight, multiparity

Diagnosis: CT imaging

Treatment: Surgery

An obturator hernia is a rare type of abdominal wall hernia in which abdominal contents, usually a loop of small intestine, protrude through the obturator canal of the pelvis. It accounts for well under one percent of all hernias but is notable for a high rate of strangulation and a historically high mortality, largely because the diagnosis is difficult and often delayed.

Signs and symptoms

Obturator hernias occur predominantly in elderly, thin, multiparous women, in whom loss of the fat pad that normally protects the obturator canal, combined with a wider pelvis, predisposes to herniation. The typical presentation is small bowel obstruction without an obvious external hernia. A classic finding is the Howship–Romberg sign: pain and paresthesia radiating down the inner thigh to the knee, caused by compression of the obturator nerve, relieved by flexion of the thigh. A mass is only rarely palpable.

Diagnosis

Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis is the investigation of choice and can show the hernia sac and its contents between the pectineus and obturator muscles. Because the condition is rare and the symptoms nonspecific, it is frequently diagnosed only at laparotomy for unexplained bowel obstruction.

Treatment

Treatment is surgical, usually urgently, because a large proportion of cases involve strangulated or gangrenous bowel requiring resection. The hernia sac is reduced and the defect repaired, by open or laparoscopic approaches; in contemporary series laparoscopy allows inspection of both obturator canals and the rest of the abdomen.
