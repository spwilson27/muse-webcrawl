Thermoregulation

Thermoregulation
DefinitionMaintenance of internal body temperature within a viable range
Control centreHypothalamus (in vertebrates)
StrategiesEndothermy, ectothermy; physiological and behavioural mechanisms
Human set point≈ 37 °C (98.6 °F) core temperature

Thermoregulation is the ability of an organism to keep its body temperature within certain boundaries, even when the surrounding temperature is very different. It is an aspect of homeostasis. Animals are classified as endotherms, which generate most of their heat metabolically (mammals and birds), and ectotherms, which rely mainly on external heat sources (most reptiles, amphibians, fish and invertebrates); a parallel classification distinguishes homeotherms, which hold a nearly constant temperature, from poikilotherms, whose temperature varies.

Mechanisms in endotherms

In mammals and birds the hypothalamus acts as a thermostat, comparing core temperature with a set point and triggering corrective responses. To shed heat, animals dilate skin blood vessels, sweat or pant, and seek shade. To conserve or produce heat, they constrict skin vessels, erect fur or feathers (piloerection) to trap air, shiver, and activate non-shivering thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue. Behaviour — basking, burrowing, huddling, migration — complements these physiological controls.

Ectotherms and plants

Ectotherms regulate temperature behaviourally, moving between sun and shade, altering body orientation and posture, or using thermal inertia of water. Some insects warm their flight muscles by shivering before takeoff, and a few plants — such as the skunk cabbage — generate heat metabolically to melt snow or volatilize pollinator attractants.

Disorders and limits

Failure of thermoregulation produces hypothermia and hyperthermia (heat stroke), both potentially fatal. Fever is a regulated raising of the hypothalamic set point, typically in response to infection. Extreme environments, exertion, dehydration, age and illness all strain the system; human technology — clothing, shelter, heating and air conditioning — is in large part an extension of thermoregulatory behaviour.
