Audio mixing

Audio mixing is the process of combining multiple sounds into one or more channels, adjusting their relative levels, frequency content, dynamics and spatial placement to achieve a desired result. Mixing is practised in many contexts: in live sound reinforcement, broadcasting, film and television post-production, theatre, and in the production of recorded music — the last of which is treated in detail at audio mixing (recorded music).

Equipment

Mixing is performed on a mixing console (mixer), which may be an analogue or digital hardware desk or a software mixer within a digital audio workstation. Each input channel typically provides gain control, equalisation, panning, auxiliary sends for effects and monitoring, and a fader controlling its contribution to the mix bus. Signal processors — compressors, limiters, gates, equalisers and time-based effects such as reverb and delay — are used to shape individual sources or the overall mix.

Contexts

Live sound

In live sound, the front-of-house engineer mixes the performance for the audience in real time, while monitor engineers mix separate feeds for performers. Live mixing must contend with feedback, room acoustics and the unrepeatable nature of the performance.

Broadcast

Broadcast mixing balances speech, music and effects against loudness standards (such as EBU R128 or ATSC A/85), with intelligibility of dialogue as the priority.

Film and television

In post-production, dialogue, music and effects ("stems") are mixed by a re-recording mixer into final formats from stereo to immersive surround, such as Dolby Atmos.

Process

Although workflows differ by context, mixing generally involves balancing levels so that important elements are clearly heard, carving frequency space with equalisation so sources do not mask one another, controlling dynamics, placing sources in the stereo or surround field, adding effects for depth or character, and checking the result on multiple playback systems.
