Independent politician

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Independents sit in legislatures without party affiliation, though some caucus with parties.

An independent politician or non-affiliated politician is a politician not affiliated with any political party or bureaucratic association. There are numerous reasons why someone may stand for office as an independent: they may support policies that differ from those of the major parties in their district, they may be former party members expelled or resigned, or the electoral system in their area may reward personal followings more than party labels.

Independent officeholders

George Washington, the only U.S. president elected as an independent, warned against parties in his Farewell Address. In the modern U.S. Congress, Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont — an independent since his career began — caucuses with the Democrats. In Ireland, independent TDs have regularly held the balance of power and entered coalition arrangements. Icelandic president Halla Tómasdóttir and many local mayors worldwide hold office without party ties.

Advantages and disadvantages

Independents can vote their conscience or district interest without party discipline and often campaign on anti-establishment platforms. Against this, they usually lack party fundraising machinery, committee influence, and a clear brand for voters; in party-list proportional systems they may be effectively excluded altogether.

Independent voters

The term also describes voters who decline party registration. In the United States, self-identified independents have formed a plurality of the electorate in many surveys, though most "lean" toward a party and vote accordingly.
