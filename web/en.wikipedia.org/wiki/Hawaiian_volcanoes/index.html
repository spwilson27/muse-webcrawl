Hawaiian volcanoes

The Hawaiian volcanoes are a chain of shield volcanoes formed by the Hawaiian hotspot, a mantle plume beneath the Pacific Plate. As the plate moves northwestward, the hotspot generates new volcanoes, producing the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain, which stretches some 6,200 kilometres from the Aleutian Trench to the island of Hawaii.

Formation

Hawaiian volcanoes pass through stages: a submarine preshield stage, a shield-building stage of fluid tholeiitic lava flows, a postshield stage with more viscous alkalic lavas, and finally erosion and rejuvenated volcanism. Their eruptions are typically effusive, producing pāhoehoe and ʻaʻā lava flows.

Major volcanoes

The island of Hawaii is composed of five volcanoes: Kohala (extinct), Hualālai, Mauna Kea (the tallest, at 4,207 m above sea level and over 10,000 m from base), Mauna Loa (the largest shield volcano on Earth), and Kīlauea, which erupted nearly continuously from 1983 to 2018. The next volcano in the chain, Kamaʻehuakanaloa (formerly Lōʻihi), is still submarine, southeast of the island.

Monitoring

The Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, founded in 1912, monitors the active volcanoes for hazards, including lava flows, vog (volcanic smog), and earthquakes.
