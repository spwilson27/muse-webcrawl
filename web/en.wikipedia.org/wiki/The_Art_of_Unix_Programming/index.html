The Art of Unix Programming

The Art of Unix Programming is a book by Eric S. Raymond, published by Addison-Wesley in 2003, about the history, culture, and design philosophy of the Unix operating system and of Unix-derived systems such as Linux. Drawing on contributions and review from members of the Unix and open-source communities, it sets out the engineering norms — often collectively called the Unix philosophy — that the author argues explain Unix's longevity.

Contents

The book opens with a history of Unix and a comparison of its design culture with other operating systems, then works through design topics: modularity, orthogonality, text protocols and file formats, transparency, robustness, interface design, program generation, configuration, and community practices. It is available in print and was also released online under a Creative Commons licence.

The Unix philosophy as summarised

Raymond condenses the philosophy into a set of rules, including: the Rule of Modularity (build simple parts connected by clean interfaces); the Rule of Clarity (clarity is better than cleverness); the Rule of Composition (design programs to connect with other programs); the Rule of Separation (separate policy from mechanism); the Rule of Simplicity; the Rule of Parsimony (write a big program only when it is clear nothing smaller will do); the Rule of Transparency; the Rule of Robustness; the Rule of Repair (fail loudly and early); the Rule of Economy (programmer time is expensive); and the Rule of Least Surprise. These echo the earlier formulation by Doug McIlroy: write programs that do one thing and do it well, that work together, and that handle text streams.

Reception

The book was widely reviewed in the technical press and remains a standard statement of Unix design culture, though some critics questioned whether the philosophy described is uniquely Unix's or universally practised even there. Raymond's earlier work, The Cathedral and the Bazaar, treats the social side of the same culture.
