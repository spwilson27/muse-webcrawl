Fractal art

Fractal art is a form of algorithmic art created by calculating fractal objects and representing the results as still images, animations and media. It developed from the mid-1980s, as Benoit Mandelbrot's work on fractal geometry and the advent of accessible computing made it possible to render the intricate, self-similar structures of sets such as the Mandelbrot set and Julia sets in rich colour.

Techniques

Fractal artists use software such as Ultra Fractal, Apophysis and Mandelbulb3D to explore parameter space: choosing formulas, zooming into promising regions, mapping colours to iteration counts, and layering multiple fractal "flames" or escape-time renderings. Three-dimensional fractals, notably the Mandelbulb (described in 2009), extended the form into volumetric sculpture-like imagery. Although generated mathematically, fractal art involves substantial human judgement in composition, colouring and selection.

Reception

Fractal images became ubiquitous in 1990s poster and calendar art and screen-saver culture, and have been exhibited in galleries and science museums; notable practitioners include Carlos Ginzburg, who coined "fractal art" exhibitions in the 1980s, and the artists of the fractal flame community. Critics debate how far the artist, rather than the algorithm, authors the final image.
