Inheritance

Inheritance is the practice of receiving private property, titles, debts, entitlements, privileges, rights, and obligations upon the death of an individual. The recipient of an inheritance is called an heir or beneficiary, and the property transferred is called an estate. The rules of inheritance differ between societies and have changed over time; they are governed by succession law, wills, and cultural and religious norms. Inheritance plays a major role in the transmission of wealth across generations and is a recurring subject of debate regarding equality of opportunity and taxation.

Testate and intestate succession

When a person dies leaving a valid will, their property is distributed according to its terms, a process called testate succession, usually administered through probate. When there is no valid will, the estate passes under the statutory rules of intestacy, which typically order entitlement among the surviving spouse, children, parents, and more distant relatives. Historically, many systems practised primogeniture, concentrating the estate in the eldest son, whereas modern systems generally divide property more equally among children.

Historical and religious systems

Roman law developed elaborate rules on heirs and legacies that shaped European civil law. Islamic inheritance law (ʿilm al-farāʾiḍ) prescribes fixed shares for specified relatives in the Qur'an, and Jewish and Hindu traditions likewise codified succession rules. Anthropologists note wide variation across cultures, including matrilineal systems in which property passes through the female line.

Inheritance tax

Many jurisdictions levy inheritance or estate taxes on transfers at death. Supporters argue that such taxes reduce inherited inequality; critics argue they amount to double taxation and can force the sale of family businesses or farms. Rates and exemptions vary widely, and some countries have abolished the tax entirely.

Biological inheritance

In genetics, inheritance refers to the passing of traits from parents to offspring through DNA, a mechanism first systematically described by Gregor Mendel. This biological sense is distinct from the legal practice described here, though both concern transmission between generations.
