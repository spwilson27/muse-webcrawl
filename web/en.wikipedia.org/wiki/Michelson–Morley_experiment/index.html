Michelson–Morley experiment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of the Michelson interferometer: a light beam split into two perpendicular arms and recombined]
The Michelson interferometer used in the 1887 experiment.

The Michelson–Morley experiment was an attempt to measure the relative motion of Earth through the luminiferous aether — the medium then believed to carry light waves — by comparing the speed of light in perpendicular directions. Performed in 1887 by Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley in Cleveland, it is the most famous failed experiment in physics: it found no significant difference, no 'aether wind', where about 0.4 of a fringe shift in the interference pattern was expected.

The null result undermined the aether theories of light and, after the FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction hypothesis (an ad hoc rescue), helped clear the ground for Albert Einstein's Special relativity (1905), which explained it by principle: the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames. Michelson received the 1907 Nobel Prize (the first American in the sciences) largely for his interferometric precision work. Repetitions with ever-greater precision — modern versions using cryogenic optical resonators constrain any anisotropy of light speed to parts in 10¹⁸ — continue to test relativity's foundations. Einstein's own account of the experiment's influence on him was modest and shifting; historians regard it as necessary background rather than his direct inspiration.

The apparatus

Light from one source was split by a half-silvered mirror into two beams travelling equal perpendicular paths (lengthened to 11 m by multiple reflections), recombined into interference fringes, and the whole instrument — floating on mercury to rotate smoothly — was turned through 90°: any aether wind should shift the fringes as the arms exchanged roles. None appeared, day or night, season to season.
