Orphism (more rarely Orphicism; Ancient Greek: Ὀρφικά, Orphiká) is the name given to a set of religious beliefs and practices originating in the ancient Greek and Hellenistic world, associated with literature ascribed to the mythical poet Orpheus, who descended into the Greek underworld and returned. This type of journey is called a katabasis and is a popular theme in Greek mythology. Orphics believed that a person's soul would receive judgment upon their death, and that they could improve their chances of a positive fate through knowledge of certain practices and truths. Key characteristics of Orphism include a pursuit of purity, reincarnation based on living an "Orphic life" — which often included abstention from certain kinds of consumption (such as meat) — and the capacity for the soul to ascend to the divine.

Beliefs

Central Orphic myth tells of the god Dionysus (as Zagreus), child of Zeus and Persephone, who was dismembered and eaten by the Titans; Zeus blasted the Titans with lightning, and humankind arose from their ashes — hence humans bear a dual nature, a Titanic body imprisoning a divine, Dionysiac soul. This original sin could be expiated through ritual purification and a succession of lives, until the soul escaped the "sorrowful wheel" of reincarnation and rejoined the gods. Orphic gold tablets placed in graves instructed the soul to avoid the spring of Lethe (forgetfulness) and drink instead from the lake of Mnemosyne (memory), and to proclaim: "I am a child of Earth and starry Heaven, but my race is of Heaven."

Sources and influence

Evidence for Orphism includes the Derveni papyrus (a commentary on an Orphic poem, the oldest surviving Greek literary papyrus), the Gurôb papyrus, bone tablets from Olbia, and gold funerary tablets from Magna Graecia, Thessaly and Crete; the Orphic Hymns and the Orphic Argonautica survive from late antiquity. Orphic ideas influenced Pythagoreanism and Plato's doctrines of the soul, and were treated with suspicion by some ancient authors who associated "Orpheotelestae" — itinerant initiators selling purifications — with religious fraud.
