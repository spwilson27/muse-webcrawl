The Priestly Blessing (Hebrew: ברכת כהנים, Birkat Kohanim), also known in rabbinic literature as the lifting of the hands (Nesiat kapayim) and by the Yiddish term Dukhening (from the platform, dukhan, on which it was performed), is a Hebrew prayer recited by Kohanim, the Jewish priests — descendants of Aaron — who bless the congregation in the name of God. Its text is set out in the Book of Numbers (6:23–27):

The LORD bless you and keep you;
the LORD make his face to shine upon you and be gracious to you;
the LORD lift up his countenance upon you and give you peace.

The three verses contain 3, 5 and 7 Hebrew words respectively, and the blessing culminates in God's promise: "So shall they put my name upon the people of Israel, and I will bless them."

Performance of the ritual

During the repetition of the Amidah prayer, the Kohanim remove their shoes, have their hands ritually washed by Levites, ascend the platform before the Torah ark, cover their heads and hands with their prayer shawls (tallitot), and raise their hands with fingers arranged so that the two hands form five gaps — traditionally explained as the configuration through which the Divine Presence (Shekhinah) peers. The prayer leader prompts each word, which the Kohanim chant in a distinctive melody. Congregants traditionally avert their eyes from the Kohanim's hands during the blessing. In Israel the blessing is recited daily in many communities; in most Diaspora Orthodox synagogues it is performed only on festivals. A mass Priestly Blessing (Birkat Kohanim) held at the Western Wall during Passover and Sukkot draws tens of thousands of worshippers.

The hand gesture

The split-hand formation of the blessing — thumb to thumb, with a gap between the ring/middle fingers of each hand — became widely known in popular culture because Leonard Nimoy, who had seen the ritual as a child, adopted it as the Vulcan salute in Star Trek.

Archaeological significance

The text of the blessing is the oldest known biblical passage preserved on an artefact: two silver scroll amulets excavated at Ketef Hinnom near Jerusalem, dating to roughly the late 7th or early 6th century BC (the First Temple period), bear versions of the blessing in paleo-Hebrew script — predating the Dead Sea Scrolls by centuries.

Use in Christianity

The blessing is also used as a benediction in Christian liturgy, particularly in Lutheran, Anglican and other traditions, where it is known as the Aaronic blessing and often closes services of worship.
