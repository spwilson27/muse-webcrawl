White cube

White cube
ConceptNeutral gallery exhibition space
Key textBrian O'Doherty, Inside the White Cube (1976)
FeaturesWhite walls, artificial light, polished floors, no windows

The white cube is a style of art gallery space characterised by square or oblong rooms with plain white walls, an even artificial light source (usually from the ceiling), and unadorned floors of polished wood or concrete. Emerging with early modernist exhibition design in the early 20th century, it became the dominant mode of displaying contemporary art, presenting works in a supposedly neutral setting detached from context.

Critique

The term was popularised by artist and critic Brian O'Doherty in a series of essays in Artforum in 1976, later collected as Inside the White Cube: The Ideology of the Gallery Space. O'Doherty argued that the white cube is far from neutral: it sacralises the objects within it, removes them from the world, and imposes its own ideology of timeless, disembodied viewing. The model has since been challenged by installation art, site-specific practice, and alternative exhibition formats, though it remains the default for commercial galleries and many museums.
