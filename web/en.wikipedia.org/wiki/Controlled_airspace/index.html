Controlled airspace

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Controlled airspace is airspace of defined dimensions within which air traffic control (ATC) services are provided to flights in accordance with the airspace classification. In controlled airspace, aircraft must obtain ATC clearance, maintain two-way radio communication, and comply with controller instructions, in exchange for which ATC provides separation between instrument flight rules (IFR) traffic and traffic information or separation for visual flight rules (VFR) traffic, depending on the class.

Airspace classes

Under the ICAO scheme adopted by most countries, airspace is divided into classes A through G; classes A–E are controlled (with variations), while F (where used) and G are uncontrolled. Class A, typically high-altitude en-route airspace, admits IFR flights only. Classes B, C, and D surround progressively smaller busy airports and impose equipment and communication requirements on all users; Class E covers most other controlled airspace, including that above general aviation airports and low-altitude airways, where VFR flights may operate without a clearance but IFR flights are controlled. Structures within the system include control zones (CTR) around airports, terminal control areas (TMA), airways, and upper control areas, as well as special-use airspace such as restricted, prohibited, and danger areas.

Rules and services

Requirements for entry typically include a filed flight plan (for IFR), a transponder (often Mode C or ADS-B Out), and an ATC clearance. In uncontrolled airspace, by contrast, no clearance is needed and pilots are responsible for their own separation, subject to flight rules and visibility minima. The boundaries and rules differ somewhat between jurisdictions: the United States (FAA) and Europe (EASA/Eurocontrol) both use the ICAO letter system with national variations.
