The Bronze Horseman (poem)


The monument that gives the poem its name

The Bronze Horseman: A Petersburg Tale (Russian: Медный всадник, Medny vsadnik) is a narrative poem written by Alexander Pushkin in 1833 about the equestrian statue of Peter the Great in Saint Petersburg and the great flood of 1824. It is widely considered Pushkin's finest work and one of the greatest poems in Russian literature; its title, and the phrase "Bronze Horseman" for the statue itself, have become permanent fixtures of Russian culture.

Synopsis

The poem opens with a famous prologue in which Peter the Great stands on the shore of the Neva and resolves to found a city "as a window cut into Europe", and the poet praises the beauty and grandeur of Saint Petersburg. The narrative then follows Evgenii, a poor clerk whose fiancée Parasha is killed in the catastrophic flood of 1824. Driven mad by grief, Evgenii wanders the city and, confronting the statue of Peter, shakes his fist and mutters a threat at the founder whose city destroyed his happiness. In a hallucination, the Bronze Horseman comes alive and gallops after him through the streets. Evgenii dies shortly afterwards.

Themes

The poem stages the conflict between the imperial state — embodied by Peter and his city, built by compulsion on a swamp — and the happiness of the "little man". Pushkin holds both sides in balance: the prologue celebrates Peter's achievement, while the narrative exposes its human cost. Debates over which side Pushkin favours have continued for nearly two centuries.

History

The poem was written at Boldino in the autumn of 1833 but was banned from publication by Nicholas I's censors; it was published posthumously in 1837, in a censored version, and in full only in the twentieth century. Its statue subject is Étienne Falconet's monument (unveiled 1782) in Senate Square, Saint Petersburg.
