East Germany, officially the German Democratic Republic (GDR; German: Deutsche Demokratische Republik), was a country in Central Europe that existed from its creation on 7 October 1949 until its dissolution and reunification with West Germany on 3 October 1990. It was created as a socialist state from the Soviet occupation zone of Germany after the Second World War, in response to the founding of the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) from the Western zones.

History

The GDR was governed by the Socialist Unity Party (SED), formed in 1946 from a forced merger of communists and social democrats in the Soviet zone. An uprising on 17 June 1953, sparked by increased work quotas, was suppressed by Soviet tanks. Persistent emigration to the West — some 2.7 million people by 1961 — led the regime to seal the border and build the Berlin Wall in August 1961. Under Walter Ulbricht and, from 1971, Erich Honecker, the GDR developed the most industrialised economy in the Eastern Bloc, joined the Warsaw Pact and the United Nations (1973), and achieved wide diplomatic recognition after the Basic Treaty with West Germany (1972).

The state maintained an extensive surveillance apparatus through the Ministry for State Security (the Stasi), which by 1989 employed some 91,000 staff and perhaps 189,000 informal collaborators. In 1989, mass emigration via Hungary and growing Monday demonstrations in Leipzig and other cities forced Honecker's resignation; the Wall fell on 9 November 1989. Free elections in March 1990 produced a government committed to rapid unification, and the GDR acceded to the Federal Republic on 3 October 1990.

Society and legacy

East Germany guaranteed full employment, cheap rents and basic goods, extensive childcare, and near-complete female participation in the workforce, alongside shortages of consumer goods, long waiting lists for cars such as the Trabant, and pervasive political control. After 1990 the Treuhand agency privatised or closed most state industry, causing mass unemployment in the east; the cultural and economic afterlife of the GDR — sometimes discussed under the term Ostalgie — remains a subject of debate in unified Germany.
