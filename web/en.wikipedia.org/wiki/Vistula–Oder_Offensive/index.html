Vistula–Oder Offensive

The Vistula–Oder Offensive was a Red Army operation on the Eastern Front of the Second World War, fought from 12 January to 2 February 1945. In one of the fastest major advances of the war, Soviet forces under Marshals Georgy Zhukov (1st Belorussian Front) and Ivan Konev (1st Ukrainian Front) broke out of their bridgeheads on the Vistula River in Poland, destroyed the German Army Group A, liberated Warsaw and Kraków, overran Silesia's industrial region, and advanced some 500 kilometres (300 miles) in three weeks to reach the Oder River — within about 70 kilometres of Berlin.

Background

By January 1945 the Red Army stood on the Vistula, holding bridgeheads at Magnuszew, Puławy, and Baranów (Sandomierz). The offensive had been brought forward from 20 January to 12 January, partly at Western Allied request following the German Ardennes offensive. The Soviets enjoyed overwhelming superiority in men, artillery, and armour.

The offensive

Konev attacked first from the Baranów bridgehead on 12 January, shattering the German front and taking Kielce and Kraków (19 January, largely intact). Zhukov attacked on 14 January; Warsaw was liberated on 17 January. Poznań was encircled and Łódź taken as the tank armies raced west. Konev's forces seized the Upper Silesian industrial district — deliberately encircled rather than stormed, preserving its factories — and crossed the Oder in late January, while Zhukov's spearheads reached the Oder near Küstrin by the end of the month and established bridgeheads on its west bank. During the advance, Soviet troops liberated the Auschwitz concentration camp on 27 January 1945.

Aftermath

The offensive destroyed Army Group A and carried the front to Germany's doorstep, but the advance halted at the Oder while the flanks in Pomerania and East Prussia were cleared — setting the stage for the Battle of Berlin in April. The operation triggered mass flight and expulsions of German civilians from the eastern territories and stands as one of the largest and fastest offensives in military history.
