Trastevere (Italian: [traˈsteːvere]) is the 13th rione of Rome, Italy, on the west bank of the River Tiber, south of Vatican City. Its name derives from the Latin trans Tiberim, "across the Tiber". Historically the district of fishermen, sailors, immigrants and the Roman working class, it retains a village-like maze of narrow cobbled streets, medieval houses and lively piazzas.

History and sights

In antiquity the area lay outside the city proper and hosted communities of Jews and early Christians, as well as Syrian and Egyptian merchants. Its great churches include Santa Maria in Trastevere — among Rome's oldest, famed for its 12th- and 13th-century golden mosaics of the Coronation of the Virgin — Santa Cecilia in Trastevere and San Pietro in Montorio, whose courtyard holds Bramante's Tempietto (1502), a jewel of High Renaissance architecture.

The Villa Farnesina preserves frescoes by Raphael, including the Triumph of Galatea. Rising above the district, the Janiculum hill offers panoramic views and monuments to Garibaldi. Trastevere's patronal festival, the Festa de' Noantri in July, honours Our Lady of Mount Carmel. Today the quarter is celebrated for its trattorias and nightlife, centred on Piazza di Santa Maria in Trastevere, while remaining a byword for authentic Roman character — its residents traditionally calling themselves noantri, "we others", as distinct from Romans across the river.
