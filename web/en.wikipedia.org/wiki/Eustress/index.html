Eustress

Eustress is a term for positive, beneficial stress — psychological, physical, or biochemical — that is perceived as being within one's coping abilities and that motivates, focuses energy, and improves performance. The term was coined by endocrinologist Hans Selye in 1974, combining the Greek prefix eu-, meaning "good", with "stress", to contrast with distress, the harmful form of stress.

Definition

Selye defined eustress as "positive stress" that results from stressors experienced as challenges rather than threats. Unlike distress, which causes anxiety, withdrawal and impaired performance, eustress is characterised by feelings of fulfilment, hope and vigour. Whether a given stressor produces eustress or distress depends less on the event itself than on the individual's perception of it, their sense of control, and the desirability of the outcome.

Characteristics

Eustress is typically short-term, is perceived as motivating, improves focus and performance, and is experienced as exciting. Common sources include starting a new job, marriage, having a child, exercising, competing in sport, learning a new skill, travelling, and other voluntary challenges that are demanding but manageable.

Relation to arousal and performance

The concept is closely related to the Yerkes–Dodson law, which describes an inverted-U relationship between arousal and performance: too little arousal produces apathy, a moderate level produces optimal performance — the zone of eustress — and excessive arousal produces anxiety and breakdown, i.e. distress. It also fits within Richard Lazarus's transactional model of stress, in which primary appraisal determines whether a demand is judged a challenge or a threat.

Occupational and health research

In occupational psychology, eustress is studied as a counterpart to workplace distress; researchers distinguish "challenge stressors" such as workload and responsibility, which can be energising, from "hindrance stressors" such as bureaucracy and role conflict, which are mainly harmful. Moderate, well-managed stress is associated with enhanced immune readiness and psychological growth, whereas chronic distress is linked to cardiovascular disease, depression and burnout.

Criticism

Some researchers caution that the eustress/distress distinction can be vague, since the physiological stress response itself is largely the same in both cases; the difference lies in duration, appraisal and recovery rather than in the body's immediate reaction.
