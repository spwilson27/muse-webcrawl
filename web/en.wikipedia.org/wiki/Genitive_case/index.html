Genitive case

In grammar, the genitive case (abbreviated GEN) is the grammatical case that marks a word, usually a noun, as modifying another word, also usually a noun—thus indicating an attributive relationship of one noun to the other noun. A genitive can also serve purposes indicating other relationships. For example, some verbs may feature arguments in the genitive case, and the genitive case may also have adverbial uses (see adverbial genitive).

Functions

The genitive most typically expresses possession: in "the student's book", the genitive marks the possessor. Beyond possession, languages use the genitive for relations of origin, composition, partitivity (part of a whole), and as the object of certain verbs and prepositions. In Latin, for example, the genitive appears in expressions of description, value, and remembering or forgetting.

Forms in different languages

In English, the genitive is marked by the clitic "'s" (e.g., "Mary's coat") and by the preposition "of", as well as by possessive determiners and pronouns such as "my", "your", and "his". In German, nouns in the genitive take distinct endings, such as des Tages ("of the day"). Latin forms the genitive with endings such as -ī, -ae, and -is depending on declension, and Ancient Greek, Sanskrit, Russian, Finnish, and many other inflected languages have rich systems of genitive forms.

Possessive constructions

Grammarians distinguish the genitive case from the possessive construction more broadly: many languages express possession without a genitive case, using prepositions, possessive affixes, or word order, while genitive cases often cover more than possession. In English linguistic literature, the "'s" form is frequently called the possessive case or Saxon genitive.
