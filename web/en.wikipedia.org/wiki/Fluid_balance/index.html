Fluid balance

Fluid balance is an aspect of the homeostasis of organisms in which the amount of water in the body is controlled through osmoregulation and behaviour, so that the concentrations of electrolytes (salts in solution) in the various body fluids remain within healthy ranges. The core principle of fluid balance is that the amount of water lost from the body must equal the amount taken in; if this equilibrium is disrupted — for example by illness, exercise or climate — body function can be seriously disturbed.

Fluid balance charts are used on hospital wards to track intake and output.

Intake and output

Water enters the body mainly through drinking and food, with a small amount produced by metabolism (metabolic water). Water is lost through urine, faeces, sweat, and insensible losses from the skin and exhaled breath. A typical sedentary adult in a temperate climate takes in and loses roughly 2.5 litres per day. The kidneys, under the control of antidiuretic hormone (ADH/vasopressin) and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, adjust urine output, while thirst drives intake.

Body fluid compartments

About 60% of the adult human body is water. Two thirds of this is intracellular fluid; the remaining third is extracellular fluid, divided between interstitial fluid and blood plasma. Distribution between compartments is governed by osmotic and hydrostatic pressures across capillary walls and cell membranes.

Clinical significance

Disorders of fluid balance include dehydration (water deficit), overhydration and water intoxication, oedema (excess interstitial fluid), and disturbances associated with heart failure, kidney disease, burns and sepsis. In clinical practice, fluid balance charts record measured intake (oral and intravenous) and output (urine, drains, vomit) to guide fluid therapy. Plasma sodium concentration is a key indicator, since sodium is the dominant extracellular cation.
