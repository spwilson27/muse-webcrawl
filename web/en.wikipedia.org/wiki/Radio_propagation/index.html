Radio propagation
The principal modes of radio wave propagation
Principal modesGround wave, sky wave, line-of-sight

Radio propagation is the behavior of radio waves as they travel, or are propagated, from one point to another in vacuum or into various parts of the atmosphere. As a form of electromagnetic radiation, radio waves are affected by the phenomena of reflection, refraction, diffraction, absorption, polarization, and scattering.

Understanding propagation is essential to designing radio systems, from long-distance shortwave communication to local FM broadcasting and mobile telephone networks.

Modes of propagation

Line-of-sight propagation

At VHF and UHF frequencies and above, radio waves travel predominantly in straight lines, limited by the horizon. This mode serves FM and television broadcasting, mobile communications, and microwave links.

Ground wave propagation

At lower frequencies, especially medium wave and long wave, radio waves follow the curvature of the Earth as ground waves, guided between the surface and the ionosphere. Ground conductivity strongly affects range.

Sky wave propagation

Shortwave (HF) signals can be refracted by layers of the ionosphere — the D, E, and F layers — and returned to Earth at great distances, enabling intercontinental communication. Ionospheric conditions vary with time of day, season, and the solar cycle, so usable frequencies change correspondingly. The maximum usable frequency (MUF) and lowest usable frequency (LUF) bound the workable range.

Other effects

Sporadic E propagation, tropospheric ducting, meteor scatter, and auroral reflections can all produce unusual long-distance propagation on VHF bands. Multipath propagation, in which signals arrive by several paths, causes fading and is a major concern in urban mobile radio. Free-space path loss increases with the square of distance and frequency.
