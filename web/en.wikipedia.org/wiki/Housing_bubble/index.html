Housing bubble

A housing bubble (or real estate bubble) is a run-up in housing prices fuelled by demand, speculation and exuberant expectations, in which prices rise far beyond levels justified by incomes, rents and other fundamentals, and which eventually bursts, causing prices to fall rapidly. Housing bubbles are a type of economic bubble and, because housing is commonly bought with borrowed money, their collapse can destabilise the wider financial system.

Characteristics and causes

Common features include rapid price growth outpacing incomes, high price-to-rent ratios, abundant and cheap mortgage credit, speculative buying and "flipping", overbuilding, and a widespread belief that prices will only rise. Contributing causes debated by economists include low interest rates, lax lending standards, financial deregulation and securitisation, tax treatment, land-use restrictions limiting supply, and herd behaviour among buyers, lenders and appraisers.

Notable episodes

The Japanese asset price bubble of the late 1980s, whose collapse ushered in the "Lost Decades", included extreme urban land values. In the 2000s, synchronised housing booms occurred in the United States, Ireland, Spain, the United Kingdom and elsewhere; the bursting of the United States housing bubble, together with subprime mortgage defaults, triggered the financial crisis of 2007–2008 and the Great Recession. House prices in Ireland and Spain fell by roughly half from their peaks, leaving widespread negative equity and banking crises.

Identification and policy

Bubbles are notoriously difficult to identify in real time, though economists monitor price-to-income and price-to-rent ratios against long-run trends. Macroprudential tools such as loan-to-value limits, countercyclical capital buffers and tighter underwriting standards are used to lean against housing booms.
