Hugh Miller.

Hugh Miller (10 October 1802 – 24 December 1856) was a Scottish geologist, writer and folklorist, celebrated both for his contributions to palaeontology and for his vivid prose. Self-educated while working as a stonemason in his native Cromarty, he became one of the best-known popularisers of geology in the Victorian era.

Life and work

Miller's father, a shipmaster, drowned when Hugh was five. Apprenticed as a stonemason, Miller read voraciously and began publishing poetry, then studies of local folklore and natural history. His account of the Old Red Sandstone fish fossils of the Cromarty shore, serialised and then published as The Old Red Sandstone (1841), made his reputation, combining meticulous description of Devonian fossil fish — including genera such as Pterichthyodes — with theological reflection. Other works include Foot-prints of the Creator (1849), arguing against the evolutionary theory of Vestiges of the Natural History of Creation; My Schools and Schoolmasters (1854), a classic autobiography; and the posthumous The Testimony of the Rocks (1857), in which he reconciled Genesis with geology through a day-age reading of creation.

From 1840 he edited The Witness, the influential Edinburgh newspaper of the Free Church party, and was a prominent figure in the Disruption of the Church of Scotland (1843). Suffering from overwork and mental illness, he took his own life on Christmas Eve 1856. His Cromarty cottage is now a museum, and he remains a canonical figure in the history of geology and Scottish letters.
