Baryon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Chart of the baryon octet and decuplet: protons, neutrons, lambda, sigma, xi, delta and omega particles]
The light baryons in their SU(3) multiplets.

In particle physics, a baryon is a type of composite subatomic particle that contains an odd number of valence quarks, most commonly three. Baryons belong to the Hadron family of particles; they are fermions (half-integer spin) and participate in the residual strong force that binds atomic nuclei. The most familiar baryons are the protons and neutrons of which atomic nuclei are made.

The name (from Greek barys, 'heavy') dates from when baryons were the heaviest known particles. Baryons carry baryon number B = 1 (antibaryons −1), a quantum number conserved in all Standard Model processes; its near-perfect conservation — the proton's lifetime exceeds 10³⁴ years — combined with its violation in most grand unified theories, is central to explaining the universe's matter–Antimatter asymmetry. The baryon family includes the Λ, Σ, Ξ and Ω⁻ hyperons whose discovery pattern (the Eightfold Way) led Murray Gell-Mann to quarks.

Matter versus antimatter

Every baryon has an antibaryon of opposite charges and baryon number. The observable universe contains essentially no primordial antibaryons — the 'baryon asymmetry problem': the Big Bang should have produced nearly equal numbers. Andrei Sakharov's conditions (1967) — baryon-number violation, C and CP violation, and departure from thermal equilibrium — define what any explanation must supply; the Standard Model supplies all three too feebly, so the asymmetry is evidence for physics beyond it.
