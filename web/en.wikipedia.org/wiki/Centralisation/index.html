Centralisation

[Image: Diagram of a centralised network, with all lines radiating from a single central hub, contrasted with decentralised and distributed networks]

Centralisation is the process by which the activities of an organisation or state — especially planning and decision-making — become concentrated within a particular location or group. Its opposite is decentralisation. The concept applies to governments, businesses, and technical systems.

In government

Centralisation in politics describes the concentration of authority in a central government, as in a unitary state, as distinct from federal systems that divide power between levels. Historically, centralisation accompanied state-building in early modern Europe, and regimes such as France's Fifth Republic have been associated with strong central administration. Supporters cite coherence, uniformity and economies of scale; critics cite remoteness, inflexibility and the suppression of local knowledge and identity.

In organisations and economics

In management, centralisation refers to decision rights retained at the top of a hierarchy; franchises, holding companies and platform businesses strike varying balances. In monetary economics, centralisation is embodied in the central bank's monopoly of money issuance, exercised in functions such as the lender of last resort.

In computing

Computing has oscillated between centralised architectures (mainframes, cloud services) and decentralised ones (personal computers, peer-to-peer networks, distributed ledgers), with ongoing debate about the resilience, privacy and power implications of each.
