Corporate media

Corporate media is a term used to refer to mass media and news outlets that are owned and controlled by large corporations, as distinct from independent, alternative, public or community media. The term is frequently used in media criticism to argue that commercial ownership shapes news selection, framing and editorial priorities.

Ownership and concentration

In the United States and many other countries, television, radio, newspapers, publishing and online media are concentrated in a relatively small number of conglomerates — historically including companies such as Disney, Comcast, Warner Bros. Discovery, Paramount, News Corp and Bertelsmann. Deregulation, notably the U.S. Telecommunications Act of 1996, accelerated consolidation, reducing the number of owners of local stations and newspapers.

Criticism

Critics such as Edward S. Herman and Noam Chomsky, in their propaganda model, argue that corporate media systematically filter news through ownership, advertising dependence, sourcing from official institutions, organised "flak," and ideology, thereby serving elite interests without direct censorship. Others contend that commercial imperatives primarily drive sensationalism and entertainment values rather than any unified political line, and that professional norms and market competition preserve substantial pluralism. The term is often contrasted with independent media and alternative media, which position themselves outside corporate ownership structures.
