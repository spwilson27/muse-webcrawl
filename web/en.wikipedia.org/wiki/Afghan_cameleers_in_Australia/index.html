The Afghan cameleers in Australia, commonly (though inaccurately) called "Afghans" or "Ghans", were camel drivers who arrived in Australia from the mid-19th century, chiefly from Afghanistan and the western provinces of British India. Recruited for their skill in handling camels in arid country, they became essential to the exploration, settlement and pastoral development of the Australian outback, carrying supplies, mail and building materials across deserts that horses and bullock teams could not cross reliably.

History

Camels were first used in Australian exploration by the Burke and Wills expedition of 1860–61, whose animals were imported from Karachi along with their handlers. From the 1860s, shipping agents and merchants recruited cameleers mainly from Baluchistan and the North-West Frontier region. The Overland Telegraph Line, completed in 1872, depended on camel transport, and cameleers supported major infrastructure projects including railways, the Rabbit-proof fence and mines in Western Australia's goldfields.

By the 1890s there were an estimated 600 cameleers and some 15,000–20,000 camels in Australia. "Ghantowns" grew up on the edges of inland settlements such as Marree, Farina, Oodnadatta and Alice Springs, with mosques, halal butchers and stores. The Marree mosque, built around 1882, is among the oldest mosques in Australia.

Decline

From the early 1900s the Immigration Restriction Act 1901 (the White Australia policy) made it difficult for cameleers to bring families or return after travelling overseas, and the motor truck progressively displaced camel transport during the 1920s and 1930s. The last commercial camel strings had largely ceased by the 1940s, and many released camels formed the basis of Australia's large feral camel population.

Legacy

The cameleers are commemorated in the name of The Ghan passenger train, which runs between Adelaide, Alice Springs and Darwin, and in landmarks such as the Afghan mosque in Adelaide. Descendants of the cameleers live throughout Australia, and their contribution to inland development is increasingly recognised in museums and public history.
