Sheaf (mathematics)

In mathematics, a sheaf is a tool for systematically tracking data — such as functions, sets or algebraic structures — attached to the open sets of a topological space and defined "locally". The data assigned to smaller open sets must be consistent restrictions of the data on larger ones, and locally consistent data must glue uniquely into global data.

Definition

A presheaf on a space X assigns to each open set U a set (or abelian group, ring, etc.) F(U) of sections over U, together with restriction maps to every smaller open subset, satisfying identity and composition axioms. A presheaf is a sheaf if it satisfies two further conditions: locality — a section that restricts to zero on every set of an open cover is zero — and gluing — sections on the members of a cover that agree on overlaps arise from a unique section on the union. The stalk of F at a point x is the direct limit of sections over all neighbourhoods of x, capturing infinitesimally local behaviour; the étalé space construction turns a sheaf into a single topological space over X.

Examples and history

Canonical examples include the sheaf of continuous real-valued functions, of smooth functions on a manifold, of holomorphic functions, and of differential forms. Sheaf theory was founded by Jean Leray in the 1940s (in work begun in a prisoner-of-war camp), developed by Henri Cartan and Jean-Pierre Serre, and recast in far greater generality by Alexander Grothendieck, whose reformulation underlies modern algebraic geometry: schemes are glued from affine pieces precisely in sheaf-theoretic fashion. Sheaf cohomology measures the obstruction to passing from local to global data.
