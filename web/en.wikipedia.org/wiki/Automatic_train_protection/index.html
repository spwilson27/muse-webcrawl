Automatic train protection (ATP) is a type of railway safety system that continually checks that the speed of a train does not exceed the maximum allowed for the track and signals ahead, and automatically applies the brakes if the driver fails to respond. ATP systems protect against trains passing signals at danger (SPADs), overspeeding on curves and through junctions, and other driver errors, forming the technical backbone of modern railway signalling safety.

Principles

An ATP system obtains information about the line ahead — signal aspects, permitted speeds and the position of other trains — from trackside devices such as balises, track circuits or radio. On-board equipment computes a braking curve to the next restriction and supervises the train's speed against it. If the driver exceeds the permitted speed or fails to acknowledge a warning, the system intervenes, first with alarms and then by cutting traction and applying the service or emergency brake.

Systems worldwide

In Britain, the Automatic Warning System (AWS) dates from the 1950s and the Train Protection & Warning System (TPWS) was fitted across the network after the Southall (1997) and Ladbroke Grove (1999) crashes; full ATP had been piloted on the Great Western and Chiltern lines. Germany uses the intermittent PZB/Indusi system and the continuous LZB on high-speed lines. The European Train Control System (ETCS), part of ERTMS, is being deployed across Europe to provide interoperable, standardised ATP. In the United States, Positive Train Control (PTC), mandated by Congress in 2008 after the Chatsworth collision, provides ATP functions using GPS and radio data links. Japan's ATS and ATC systems and China's CTCS are analogous developments.

Levels of protection

Simpler systems warn the driver or trip the brakes only at signals; full ATP supervises speed continuously over the whole route, including temporary speed restrictions. ATP is a component of automatic train operation (ATO) and automatic train control (ATC) hierarchies, and its reliability requirements are among the most stringent in safety-critical engineering.
