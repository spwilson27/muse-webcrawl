Oven

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A modern domestic oven with its door open, racks visible inside]

An oven is a thermally insulated chamber used for heating, baking and roasting food. Ovens range from earth pits and clay structures to the modern domestic gas or electric oven, usually combined with a hob (cooktop) in a cooker or range. Because an oven surrounds food with hot, dry air, it cooks evenly and can brown surfaces — the basis of bread baking, roasting and casseroles.

History

The earliest ovens were pits and clay domes used in the ancient Middle East and Indus Valley; Greek and Roman bakers built beehive ovens heated by a fire inside, the ashes raked out before bread went in — a design still used for pizza in Naples. Cast-iron stoves spread in the 18th–19th centuries, gas ovens followed in the 19th, and electric ovens in the 20th. Microwave ovens, which heat by a completely different mechanism, arrived after the Second World War.

Types

Conventional ovens heat by radiation and natural convection; convection (fan) ovens circulate air for faster, more even cooking, typically allowing a 20 °C lower setting. Other types include wood-fired ovens, tandoors (clay ovens of South and Central Asia), steam ovens, toaster ovens, and industrial tunnel ovens for bakeries. Self-cleaning (pyrolytic) models burn off residues at very high temperature.

Temperatures

Domestic ovens typically run from about 50 °C to 260–290 °C. Recipes describe settings in words — slow, moderate, hot — alongside Celsius, Fahrenheit and the British Gas Mark scale. Oven thermostats are often inaccurate, so keen bakers use a separate oven thermometer.
