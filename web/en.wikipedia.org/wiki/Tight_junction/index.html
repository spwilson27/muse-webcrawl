Tight junction

Tight junction
LocationApical region of epithelial and endothelial cells
Key proteinsClaudins, occludin, junctional adhesion molecules, zonula occludens proteins (ZO-1, ZO-2, ZO-3)

Tight junctions, also known as occluding junctions or zonulae occludentes (singular, zonula occludens), are multiprotein junctional complexes between epithelial cells. Their primary functions are to limit the passage of molecules and ions through the space between cells (the paracellular pathway) and to block the movement of membrane proteins between the apical and basolateral surfaces of the cell, thereby preserving cell polarity.

Structure

Tight junctions are composed of a branching network of sealing strands, each strand formed by a row of transmembrane proteins embedded in both plasma membranes, with their extracellular domains joining one another directly. The principal protein families are the claudins, which are the main determinants of the barrier's selectivity, and occludin. On the cytoplasmic side, the transmembrane proteins associate with scaffold proteins such as ZO-1, which connect the junction to the actin cytoskeleton.

Functions

Tight junctions perform two classical functions:

  • Barrier function: they regulate the paracellular transport of ions, solutes, and water. Different tissues express different combinations of claudins, giving junctions with widely varying permeability; the junctions of the blood–brain barrier are among the tightest, while those of the proximal tubule of the kidney are comparatively leaky.
  • Fence function: they prevent lipids and proteins in the apical membrane from diffusing into the basolateral membrane, maintaining the distinct compositions of the two surfaces.

The tightness of a junction is commonly measured by its transepithelial electrical resistance (TER). Tight junctions also participate in intracellular signaling, cell proliferation, and the regulation of gene expression.

Physiological and clinical relevance

Defects in tight-junction proteins underlie several human diseases. Mutations in claudin-16 and claudin-19 cause familial hypomagnesemia with hypercalciuria and nephrocalcinosis, a kidney disorder of magnesium wasting. Disruption of intestinal tight junctions is implicated in inflammatory bowel disease and coeliac disease, and many pathogens — including Clostridioides difficile toxins and the Helicobacter pylori protein CagA — target junctional complexes to breach epithelial barriers.
