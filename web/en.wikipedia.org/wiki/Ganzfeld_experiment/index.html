Ganzfeld experiment

A ganzfeld experiment (from German ganzes Feld, "whole field") is an experimental protocol used in parapsychology to test individuals for extrasensory perception (ESP), specifically telepathy. Developed in the 1970s from a technique of Gestalt psychology, it places a "receiver" in a state of mild, homogeneous sensory deprivation — uniform red light seen through halved ping-pong balls over the eyes and white or pink noise played through headphones — while a "sender" in another room views a randomly selected target (a picture or video clip) and attempts to transmit it mentally. The receiver then describes their impressions and ranks four candidate targets for similarity.

Results and controversy

Proponents Charles Honorton and Daryl Bem reported hit rates around 32–35% against a 25% chance expectation across series of studies, and Honorton's computerised "autoganzfeld" experiments were designed to answer criticisms of earlier manual procedures. A celebrated 1986 exchange between Honorton and sceptic Ray Hyman produced a joint communiqué agreeing on stricter standards while disagreeing on whether an effect had been shown. Subsequent meta-analyses have reached conflicting conclusions: a 1999 analysis by Milton and Wiseman found results consistent with chance, while later analyses by Bem and others reported significant effects. Independent replications under pre-registered, tightly controlled conditions have generally failed to find evidence of telepathy.

Scientific status

The mainstream scientific consensus is that ganzfeld experiments have not provided reproducible evidence for ESP; reported effects are attributed to methodological flaws, sensory leakage, selective reporting and publication bias, and the decline effect seen across replications. The ganzfeld remains the most extensively debated paradigm in parapsychology and is frequently cited in discussions of replication and meta-analysis in psychology more generally.
