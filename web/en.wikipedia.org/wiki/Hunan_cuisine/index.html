Hunan cuisine, also known as Xiang cuisine, is one of the Eight Great Traditions of Chinese cuisine, originating from the Xiang River region of Hunan province in southern China. It is renowned for its hot and spicy flavours, fresh aroma and deep colours, and is often considered even spicier than the better-known Sichuan cuisine. Whereas Sichuan heat is typically "numbing" (málà) from Sichuan pepper, Hunan heat is a pure, dry chilli heat, often combined with sourness from pickled chillies.

History

The use of chilli peppers in Hunan cooking dates to their introduction from the Americas in the late Ming and early Qing dynasties; chillies were initially used as a salt substitute in poorer inland districts and became central to the regional palate. Hunan cooking was further shaped by the province's humid climate — hot food was believed to dispel dampness — and by traditions of smoking and curing meats for preservation.

Regional styles

Hunan cuisine comprises three main regional styles: the Xiang River style centred on Changsha (rich, with braised and cured dishes), the Dongting Lake style around Yueyang and Changde (noted for fish and game), and the mountainous western Hunan style of Xiangxi (known for smoked and pickled foods).

Notable dishes

  • Mao's braised pork (hóngshāo ròu) — red-braised pork belly, associated with Mao Zedong, a Hunan native
  • Steamed fish head with chopped red chillies (duòjiāo yútóu)
  • Stir-fried pork with chillies (nóngjiā xiǎo chǎoròu)
  • Dong'an chicken
  • Changsha stinky tofu
  • Fire-roasted peppers with preserved egg

Techniques

Common techniques include stir-frying, steaming, braising, smoking and pickling. Hunan cooks make extensive use of chopped pickled chillies (duò làjiāo), fermented black beans, and cured smoked pork (làròu).
