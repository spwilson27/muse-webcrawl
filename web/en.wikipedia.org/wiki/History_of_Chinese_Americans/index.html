History of Chinese Americans

The history of Chinese Americans begins with significant immigration in the mid-19th century. Chinese migrants, mostly from Guangdong province, arrived in California during the Gold Rush (from 1849) and later formed a major part of the workforce that built the western portion of the First Transcontinental Railroad, completed in 1869.

Exclusion era

Economic competition produced anti-Chinese hostility, violence and discriminatory taxes. The Page Act (1875) and especially the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 — the first U.S. law to bar immigration of a specific nationality — severely restricted Chinese immigration and naturalisation, and created a largely male "bachelor society" in America's Chinatowns. Exclusion was extended by the Geary Act (1892) and made permanent in 1902; the 1906 San Francisco earthquake's destruction of records enabled some migrants to claim U.S. citizenship as "paper sons." Exclusion was repealed by the Magnuson Act in 1943, when China was a wartime ally, though with a token quota.

Post-1965 era

The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 opened large-scale immigration again; subsequent decades brought professionals, students and families from mainland China, Taiwan and Hong Kong, alongside ethnic Chinese from Southeast Asia. Chinese Americans became one of the largest Asian American groups, with major communities in California, New York and other metropolitan areas, and have played prominent roles in science, business, politics and culture while continuing to confront issues from discrimination to debates over U.S.–China relations.
