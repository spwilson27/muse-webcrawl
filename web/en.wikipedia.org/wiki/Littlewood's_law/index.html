Littlewood's law

Given enough events, remarkable coincidences become inevitable

Littlewood's law, formulated by the British mathematician John Edensor Littlewood, states that individuals can expect "miracles" — strikingly improbable events — to happen to them at a rate of roughly one per month. It is an illustration of the law of large numbers applied to everyday experience, and it appears in Littlewood's A Mathematician's Miscellany (1953).

Formulation

Littlewood defined a miracle as an event with odds of about one in a million. He supposed that a person is alert for roughly eight hours a day and experiences events at a rate of about one per second — sights, sounds, thoughts, encounters. That gives about 28,800 events per day, and therefore around one million events every 35 days. Consequently, a one-in-a-million event should occur, purely by chance, roughly once every 35 days — about once a month.

Interpretation

The law is often invoked to demystify coincidence: with billions of people each experiencing millions of events, extraordinarily unlikely coincidences are guaranteed to happen to someone every day, and their occurrence to any particular person is unremarkable. It is related to the "law of truly large numbers" described by Persi Diaconis and Frederick Mosteller, and is a standard reference in skeptical discussions of the paranormal, premonition, and synchronicity.

Critics note that the argument is informal — the assumed event rate and the definition of a miracle are arbitrary — but its qualitative conclusion, that surprising coincidences are far more common than intuition suggests, is widely accepted.
