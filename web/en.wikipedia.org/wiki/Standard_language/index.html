Standard language

A standard language (also standard variety or standard dialect) is a variety of a language that has undergone substantial codification of its grammar, vocabulary, and orthography and is used as a public model of correct usage — in education, government, publishing, and broadcasting. Standardisation typically involves selecting a prestige variety, codifying it in dictionaries and grammars, and elaborating its functions across all domains of public life.

Development

Linguist Einar Haugen modelled standardisation in four stages: selection of a norm, codification of its form, elaboration of its functions, and acceptance by the community. Classic examples include Standard French, shaped by the Académie française from 1635; Standard German, unified largely through print and Luther's Bible translation; and Standard English, which emerged from the London-based Chancery standard and the printing press. Some languages are pluricentric, with several national standards (English, Spanish, Arabic, Portuguese).

Standard and dialect

Sociolinguists stress that a standard is one dialect among others, distinguished by social and institutional support rather than by intrinsic linguistic superiority. The contrast between "language" and "dialect" is famously summed up in the quip that a language is "a dialect with an army and a navy". Standard varieties coexist with regional and social dialects, and their prestige shapes attitudes and sometimes discrimination in education and employment.
