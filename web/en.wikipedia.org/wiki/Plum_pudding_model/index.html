Plum pudding model

The plum pudding model is an obsolete scientific model of the atom, proposed by J. J. Thomson in 1904, shortly after his discovery of the electron. In the model, the atom is composed of electrons (which Thomson called "corpuscles") embedded in a diffuse sphere of positive charge, like plums in a plum pudding (or raisins in a cake), with the total positive charge balancing the electrons' negative charge so the atom is electrically neutral.

Background

In 1897 Thomson demonstrated that cathode rays consisted of negatively charged particles much lighter than atoms. This raised the question of how an atom's mass and neutrality could be explained. Thomson proposed that the atom was a sphere of uniform positive electrification — a kind of "pudding" — with the corpuscles arranged inside it in rotating rings, an arrangement he showed could be stable and could reproduce some periodic patterns among the elements.

Refutation

The model began to fail against experiment. In 1909, Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, working under Ernest Rutherford, fired alpha particles at thin gold foil. While most passed through, roughly 1 in 8,000 was deflected by more than 90 degrees — impossible if positive charge were spread diffusely through the atom. In 1911 Rutherford concluded that the atom's positive charge and most of its mass are concentrated in a tiny central nucleus, proposing the Rutherford model in place of the plum pudding.

Legacy

Though wrong, the plum pudding model was the first model of the atom to incorporate subatomic particles, and it launched the modern programme of modelling atomic structure. The name itself, which Thomson never used, became a popular shorthand for a superseded scientific theory.
