Jacques Derrida (1930–2004) was an Algerian-born French philosopher best known for developing deconstruction, a method of reading texts that seeks to expose their dependence on unstable oppositions and suppressed terms. Among the most influential — and most contested — thinkers of the late 20th century, he taught at the École Normale Supérieure and the École des Hautes Études in Paris and held visiting posts at American universities including Yale and UC Irvine.

Thought

In three books of 1967, including Of Grammatology, Derrida argued that Western philosophy exhibits a "logocentrism" — a privileging of speech over writing and of presence over absence — that its own texts undermine. His term différance (with an 'a') condenses differing and deferring: meaning in language arises from differences between signs and is endlessly deferred along chains of signification. Deconstructive readings of Plato, Rousseau, Saussure, Austin and others became a method taken up across literary studies, law, architecture and political theory. Later work turned to ethics and politics: hospitality, forgiveness, the "democracy to come", animal ethics, and the spectres of Marx.

Reception

Derrida's dense, playful prose made him a polarising figure: celebrated as a liberating critic of metaphysics, attacked by analytic philosophers (notably in the 1992 Cambridge honorary-degree affair and John Searle's earlier exchange with him) as obscure or relativistic. He consistently denied that deconstruction means texts can mean anything at all, insisting on rigorous, double reading. Since his death, publication of his seminars has continued to reshape interpretation of his work.
