St Albans Cathedral

St Albans Cathedral, formally the Cathedral and Abbey Church of St Alban, is a Church of England cathedral in St Albans, Hertfordshire, England. Standing on the hill above the Roman city of Verulamium, it occupies the site of the martyrdom of Saint Alban, Britain's first Christian martyr, and houses his shrine.

History

A Benedictine abbey was founded on the site by Offa of Mercia around 793. The present building was begun in 1077 under the Norman abbot Paul of Caen, using brick and tile salvaged from Roman Verulamium; its great crossing tower survives from this period. The abbey became one of the richest and most powerful in England — its school produced the chronicler Matthew Paris — before being surrendered at the Dissolution in 1539. The townspeople bought the church in 1553, and it became a cathedral in 1877 when the Diocese of St Albans was created.

Architecture

The cathedral has the longest nave of any English cathedral, at about 85 metres, reflecting its monastic origins. The building mixes Norman, Early English and later Gothic work; the west front was heavily restored in the nineteenth century by Lord Grimthorpe, whose designs remain controversial. The shrine of St Alban, destroyed at the Dissolution, was reconstructed and rededicated in 2015, and the cathedral also contains the shrine of Saint Amphibalus and rare medieval wall paintings.
