Experience curve effects

The experience curve describes the relationship between cumulative production volume and unit cost: as a firm produces more of a product over time, its costs per unit tend to fall by a characteristic percentage each time cumulative output doubles. The concept generalises the older learning curve, first quantified by Theodore Paul Wright in 1936 for aircraft manufacturing, where direct labour hours fell by a constant percentage with each doubling of output.

Development

In 1968, Bruce Henderson and the Boston Consulting Group (BCG) extended the idea to all costs — not only labour but also marketing, distribution, and overhead — and made the experience curve a central tool of corporate strategy. Observed "slopes" typically range from about 70% to 90%: with an 80% curve, unit costs fall to 80% of their previous level each time cumulative volume doubles. Causes include worker learning, process improvements, product redesign, and economies of scale.

Strategic implications

BCG argued that the producer with the largest cumulative volume — usually the market-share leader — should enjoy the lowest costs and highest margins, a logic that underpinned the growth-share matrix. The idea influenced pricing strategies in which firms price below current cost to gain volume and ride the curve down faster than competitors. Critics note that experience can be shared, bought, or leapfrogged, that the curve can flatten or be disrupted by new technology, and that an exclusive focus on cost and share contributed to some strategic failures. Related phenomena include Wright's law for technology costs, observed in industries from solar panels to lithium-ion batteries.
