Xen is a free and open-source type-1 hypervisor — software that runs directly on computer hardware and allows multiple operating systems ("guests") to run concurrently in isolated virtual machines. Originating in the XenoServers research project at the University of Cambridge, Xen was first released in 2003 and became one of the most influential virtualisation platforms, underpinning early large-scale cloud computing services including Amazon Web Services.

Architecture

The Xen hypervisor itself is a small privileged layer. On boot it launches a special management guest, dom0, typically running Linux or NetBSD, which hosts device drivers and the administrative toolstack; unprivileged guests are called domU domains. Xen originally relied on paravirtualisation, in which the guest operating system is modified to make efficient hypercalls instead of using privileged instructions. With the spread of hardware virtualisation extensions (Intel VT-x and AMD-V), Xen also supports unmodified guests in hardware virtual machines (HVM), and hybrid PVH modes. Device access in paravirtual domains is provided through split drivers — frontends in the guest and backends in dom0 or driver domains — communicating over shared-memory ring buffers and event channels.

History and use

Xen was commercialised by XenSource, acquired by Citrix in 2007, which built the XenServer (now Citrix Hypervisor) product line on it. Amazon used Xen for EC2 instances from its launch in 2006 until the gradual introduction of the Nitro system from 2017. In 2013 the Xen Project became a collaborative project of the Linux Foundation. Xen's strong isolation model also made it the basis of security-focused systems such as Qubes OS, and it is used in automotive and embedded virtualisation.
