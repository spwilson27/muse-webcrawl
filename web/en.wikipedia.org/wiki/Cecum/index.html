Cecum

The cecum and appendix in the human digestive system.

The cecum (also spelled caecum) is a pouch-like structure at the beginning of the large intestine, located at the junction of the small intestine and the colon. It receives chyme from the ileum through the ileocecal valve. In humans the vermiform appendix projects from the posteromedial aspect of the cecum. The name derives from the Latin caecus, meaning "blind", referring to its blind-ended pouch shape.

Structure

In humans the cecum is an intraperitoneal structure about 6 cm long, situated in the right iliac fossa of the abdomen. The ileum opens into it through the ileocecal valve, which limits reflux of colonic contents into the small intestine. The appendix arises from the cecum below this junction, at the convergence of the three taeniae coli — a landmark used by surgeons to locate the appendix.

Function

The cecum absorbs fluids and salts remaining after intestinal digestion. In many herbivorous mammals it is greatly enlarged and houses symbiotic bacteria that ferment cellulose, making the cecum central to digestion; hindgut fermenters such as horses and rabbits depend on it. In humans its digestive role is modest, though gut-associated lymphoid tissue in the cecum and appendix contributes to immune function and to maintaining the gut microbiome.

Comparative anatomy

Most amniotes possess a cecum. Birds typically have two (paired ceca). Some species, such as bears, lack a cecum entirely. In ruminants the fermentation role is taken over by the rumen, and the cecum is relatively small.

Clinical significance

The cecum may be affected by appendicitis (at its appendiceal orifice), cecal volvulus (twisting of the cecum), tumours such as carcinoid and adenocarcinoma, and infections including typhlitis in immunocompromised patients.
