Spy fiction

Spy fiction is a genre of literature involving espionage as a central theme, typically featuring a spy, intelligence agent, or secret agent engaged in clandestine operations on behalf of a government or organisation. The genre emerged in the early 20th century, influenced by the development of modern intelligence services and the rivalries of the great powers, and flourished during the Cold War.

History

Early examples include Rudyard Kipling's Kim (1901) and Joseph Conrad's The Secret Agent (1907). William Le Queux and E. Phillips Oppenheim popularised invasion-scare and espionage stories before the First World War. In the interwar years, authors such as W. Somerset Maugham (Ashenden, 1928) and Eric Ambler brought greater realism and moral ambiguity to the genre.

The Cold War produced the genre's best-known works: Ian Fleming's James Bond novels, beginning with Casino Royale (1953), presented a glamorous, action-oriented style, while John le Carré, beginning with The Spy Who Came in from the Cold (1963), offered a bleak, realistic counterpoint rooted in bureaucratic intrigue and betrayal. Len Deighton's unnamed agent in The IPCRESS File (1962) occupied a similar realist vein.

Themes and conventions

Common elements include tradecraft, surveillance and countersurveillance, double agents, dead drops, cryptography, and the tension between loyalty and morality. Spy fiction often explores the psychological cost of deception and the difficulty of distinguishing friend from enemy.

In other media

The genre has been extensively adapted for film and television, from the long-running James Bond film series to television series such as The Americans and adaptations of le Carré's novels.
