The Tangier International Zone (French: Zone internationale de Tanger; Spanish: Zona Internacional de Tánger) was an international zone of about 382 km² centred on the Moroccan city of Tangier, administered jointly by several European powers and the United States between 1925 and 1956, when it was reintegrated into the newly independent Kingdom of Morocco.

Creation

Tangier's strategic position at the entrance to the Mediterranean made it a focus of great-power rivalry. After Morocco was partitioned into French and Spanish protectorates in 1912, the city's status was left unresolved. The Tangier Protocol of December 1923, signed by France, Spain, and the United Kingdom, declared Tangier and its surroundings a permanently neutral, demilitarised international zone, governed by an administrator and a legislative assembly representing the various national communities and the Sultan of Morocco. The statute took effect in 1925 and was later adhered to by Italy, the United States, and others.

Life in the Zone

With minimal taxation, no exchange controls, and dozens of banks, the Zone became a flourishing free port and a notorious haven for smuggling, espionage, and currency speculation. Its permissive, cosmopolitan atmosphere drew a large expatriate colony of writers, artists, and adventurers; figures associated with interwar and postwar Tangier include Paul Bowles, William S. Burroughs, and the characters of the "Interzone" in Naked Lunch. During the Second World War Spain occupied the Zone (1940–1945), after which international administration was restored with American and Soviet participation.

Dissolution

On Moroccan independence in 1956, the signatory powers agreed at the Fedala conference to abolish the international statute, and on 29 October 1956 Tangier was formally reunited with Morocco. A transitional charter preserved some economic privileges until 1960, when the free-port regime ended, causing an exodus of banks and foreign residents and a sharp economic decline from which the city later recovered.
