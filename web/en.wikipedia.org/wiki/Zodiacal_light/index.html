Zodiacal light

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The zodiacal light is a faint, diffuse, roughly triangular white glow visible in the night sky extending up from the vicinity of the Sun along the ecliptic — the plane of the zodiac. It is caused by sunlight reflecting off interplanetary dust particles in the inner Solar System. Best seen from dark sites just after evening twilight in spring, or before dawn in autumn, it was once mistaken for the first light of dawn — sometimes poetically called "false dawn".

Observation

Because the ecliptic stands steeply to the horizon at those seasons, the luminous cone — sometimes brighter than the Milky Way — reaches high into the sky. In the tropics it can be observed year-round. A much fainter oval brightening at the antisolar point, the gegenschein, and a connecting zodiacal band complete the phenomenon.

Explanation

Giovanni Domenico Cassini studied the light in 1683–85 and proposed that it came from a cloud of small particles orbiting the Sun. The modern zodiacal cloud consists of dust shed by comets and produced by asteroid collisions; grains slowly spiral into the Sun under Poynting–Robertson drag and are continually replenished. Spacecraft measurements, from the Helios probes to Parker Solar Probe's imaging of the dust near the Sun, have mapped the cloud's extent and composition.

Cultural significance

The "false dawn" (Arabic: al-fajr al-kadhib) is traditionally distinguished from the "true dawn" in Islamic practice because it marks no change in the rules for fasting and prayer. The phenomenon features in Omar Khayyam's poetry and in modern skywatching lore. Brian May, later of Queen, completed his PhD thesis (published 2007) on radial velocities in the zodiacal dust cloud.
