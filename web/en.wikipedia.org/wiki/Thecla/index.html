Thecla

Thecla (Greek: Θέκλα, Thékla) was a saint of the early Christian Church and a reported follower of Paul the Apostle. Her story is told in the 2nd-century apocryphal Acts of Paul and Thecla. She is venerated as a protomartyr and "equal to the apostles" in the Eastern Orthodox tradition, and is one of the most prominent women saints of early Christianity — though modern scholarship considers her largely or entirely legendary.

Story

According to the Acts, Thecla was a young noblewoman of Iconium who heard Paul preaching on chastity from a neighbouring window, renounced her fiancé Thamyris, and followed Paul. Condemned by her own family and the authorities, she miraculously survived execution by fire (a rainstorm quenched the flames) and later, in Antioch, survived being thrown to wild beasts, baptising herself in a tank of seals. She travelled dressed as a man, preached, and finally settled near Seleucia in Syria, living as an ascetic healer in a cave; the narrative ends with her disappearing into the rock. Tertullian already complained that the Acts were used to justify women teaching and baptising.

Veneration

Thecla's shrine at Seleucia (Ayatekla, near Silifke in modern Turkey) became a major pilgrimage centre described by the pilgrim Egeria; the nun Marthana presided over its community. Her cult spread widely in East and West — she is patron of Tarragona in Spain and of the dying. Her feast day is September 23 in the Roman calendar and September 24 in the Orthodox. Churches, icons and frescoes of Thecla are found from Syria and Egypt to Italy and Spain.
