1939 White Paper

The 1939 White Paper, also known as the MacDonald White Paper, was a policy statement issued by the British government, led by Prime Minister Neville Chamberlain and Colonial Secretary Malcolm MacDonald, in May 1939. It radically revised British policy in Mandatory Palestine, severely restricting Jewish immigration and land purchases and envisioning an independent binational Palestinian state — a reversal that the Zionist movement denounced as a betrayal of the Balfour Declaration.

Provisions

The White Paper proposed that Palestine become an independent state within ten years, in which Arabs and Jews would share government in proportion to their populations. Jewish immigration was limited to 75,000 over five years (10,000 per year plus up to 25,000 refugees at the High Commissioner's discretion), after which no further Jewish immigration would be permitted without Arab consent. Regulations were introduced dividing Palestine into zones where transfers of land from Arabs to Jews were prohibited, restricted, or left free.

Context and reaction

The policy followed the Arab Revolt of 1936–39 and the failed St. James Conference, and came as Britain sought Arab goodwill on the eve of the Second World War. The Zionist leadership under Chaim Weizmann rejected the White Paper as a breach of the Mandate; the League of Nations Mandates Commission found it inconsistent with Britain's obligations, but war intervened before the League Council ruled. Arab reaction was divided: the Mufti of Jerusalem, Haj Amin al-Husseini, rejected it for not granting immediate independence, though many Palestinian leaders regarded it as a significant achievement.

Consequences

Enforced throughout the war, the immigration restrictions barred most Jewish refugees fleeing the Holocaust, producing tragedies such as the Struma disaster and driving the Zionist movement into open revolt against Britain after 1945, including illegal immigration (Aliyah Bet) and armed resistance. The policy effectively governed Palestine until Britain referred the Mandate to the United Nations in 1947.
