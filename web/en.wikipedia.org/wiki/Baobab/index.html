Baobab

Baobab (Adansonia)
FamilyMalvaceae
SubfamilyBombacoideae
SpeciesNine recognised
DistributionMadagascar, mainland Africa, Australia

Baobab is the common name for the nine species of trees in the genus Adansonia, named after the French naturalist Michel Adanson. Six species are endemic to Madagascar, one — the African baobab, Adansonia digitata — is native to the African mainland, one (A. gregorii) to northwestern Australia, and a further species was described from Madagascar in 2012. Baobabs are icons of the dry tropics, famous for their enormous bottle-shaped trunks, stark root-like branches, and extraordinary longevity.

Description and ecology

Baobabs store tens of thousands of litres of water in soft, fibrous trunk tissue to survive long dry seasons, shedding their leaves for much of the year. The largest individuals reach 25 metres in height with trunks over 10 metres in diameter; radiocarbon dating has shown that the oldest African baobabs exceed 2,000 years in age. The large white flowers open at night and are pollinated by fruit bats and hawk moths. The hard, velvety pods contain tart, vitamin-rich pulp — eaten fresh or dried into powder — and the seeds, bark fibre, and leaves are all used locally, giving rise to the name "tree of life."

Culture and conservation

Baobabs figure centrally in African and Malagasy folklore and in literature, most famously in Antoine de Saint-Exupéry's The Little Prince. Madagascar's Avenue of the Baobabs, lined with A. grandidieri, is a celebrated landmark. In the 2010s researchers documented the sudden deaths of many of the oldest and largest African baobabs, a pattern tentatively linked to climate change, prompting concern for the genus's future.
