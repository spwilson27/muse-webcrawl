Capital budgeting

Capital budgeting evaluates whether long-term investments, such as new plant or machinery, are worth funding.

Capital budgeting, also known as investment appraisal, is the planning process used by organizations to determine whether long-term investments — such as new machinery, replacement of equipment, new plants, or research and development projects — are worth pursuing. Because such projects require large outlays and generate returns over many years, capital budgeting techniques assess projected cash flows against the cost of capital.

Methods

Net present value

Net present value (NPV) discounts all expected future cash flows of a project, including the initial outlay, to their present value using a discount rate reflecting the project's risk, usually the weighted average cost of capital. Projects with positive NPV are expected to add value and are accepted; mutually exclusive projects are ranked by NPV.

Internal rate of return

The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate at which a project's NPV equals zero. A project is accepted if its IRR exceeds the required rate of return (hurdle rate). IRR can be misleading for non-conventional cash flows, which may produce multiple rates.

Payback period

The payback period measures how long it takes for cumulative cash inflows to repay the initial investment. It is simple and emphasizes liquidity, but ignores the time value of money (unless discounted payback is used) and all cash flows after payback.

Other techniques

Other approaches include the profitability index, the accounting rate of return, and — for projects with managerial flexibility — real options analysis, which values the option to defer, expand or abandon a project.

Process

A typical capital budgeting process involves generating proposals, estimating incremental after-tax cash flows, evaluating them with the techniques above, selecting projects subject to the available budget, and conducting post-audits comparing actual results with forecasts.
