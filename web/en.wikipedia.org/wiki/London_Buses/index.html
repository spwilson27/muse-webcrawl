London Buses

London Buses is the subsidiary of Transport for London (TfL) that manages most bus services in London, England. It specifies routes, fares and service levels, while buses themselves are operated under contract by private companies. The red double-decker bus is an iconic symbol of the city.

Red buses on Oxford Street; London's bus network is one of the largest in the world.

The network

The network has roughly 675 routes served by around 8,600 buses (prior to recent reductions), with some 19,000 bus stops across Greater London. Services are tendered by TfL to operators such as Arriva, Stagecoach, Go-Ahead, RATP and Abellio (now Transport UK), which employ drivers and own or lease vehicles built to TfL specification — invariably painted in the corporate red. Cash fares were abolished in 2014; passengers pay by Oyster card, contactless bank card or phone, and a "Hopper" fare allows unlimited bus journeys within an hour for a single fare.

History

London's buses descend from the horse omnibuses introduced by George Shillibeer in 1829. From 1933 the London Passenger Transport Board unified services; its successor London Transport introduced the Routemaster double-decker in 1956, which remained in front-line service until 2005. Deregulation in the 1980s left London with a franchised model — unlike the rest of Great Britain, where buses were fully deregulated.

Modern developments

Articulated "bendy buses" were introduced in the 2000s and withdrawn by 2011. The New Routemaster, a hybrid double-decker designed by Thomas Heatherwick, entered service from 2012. TfL has been converting the fleet to hybrid and battery-electric operation, with a commitment to a zero-emission bus fleet.
