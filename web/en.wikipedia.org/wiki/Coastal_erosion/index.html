Coastal erosion is the loss or displacement of land, or the long-term removal of sediment and rocks along the coastline, due to the action of waves, currents, tides, wind-driven water, waterborne ice, or other storm impacts. The landward retreat of the shoreline can be measured over timescales from days (storms) to millennia.

Processes

Waves erode rock by hydraulic action (compressed air in cracks), abrasion by suspended sediment, attrition between particles, and chemical solution of rocks such as limestone and chalk. Soft cliffs of clay or sand may retreat metres per year, while hard rock coasts erode slowly. Erosion is worsened where sediment supply is cut off by dams or coastal structures, and by sea-level rise.

Management

"Hard" defences — sea walls, revetments, groynes and breakwaters — protect specific frontages but may worsen erosion elsewhere. "Soft" approaches include beach nourishment (adding sand), dune restoration, and managed realignment, in which defences are moved inland and the sea is allowed to reclaim low-value land, often creating valuable salt-marsh habitat.
