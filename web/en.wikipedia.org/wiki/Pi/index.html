Pi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The symbol π large in the centre, surrounded by its first hundred decimal digits]

The number π (pi) is a mathematical constant that is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, approximately equal to 3.14159. It appears in many formulae across mathematics and physics and is an irrational number — it cannot be expressed exactly as a common fraction — and a transcendental number, as proved in 1882.

π has been known in approximation since antiquity: Babylonian and Egyptian estimates date back nearly 4,000 years, and Archimedes bounded it between 223/71 and 22/7 using polygons. Its decimal digits have been computed to trillions of places, though only a few dozen are ever needed in practice.

Notation and celebration

The Greek letter π was popularised for this ratio by Leonhard Euler in the 18th century (following William Jones, 1706). Pi Day is celebrated on 14 March (3/14 in month/day format), especially in the United States.

Where it appears

Beyond geometry, π appears in trigonometry, complex analysis (Euler's identity e^{iπ} + 1 = 0), probability (the normal distribution), Fourier analysis and general relativity.
