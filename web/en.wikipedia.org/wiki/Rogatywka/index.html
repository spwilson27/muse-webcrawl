The rogatywka (Polish: [rɔɡaˈtɨfka]) is a distinctive Polish four-cornered peaked cap, traditionally worn with the uniform of the Polish military and today a national symbol of Polish soldiery. Its name comes from the Polish word róg ("horn" or "corner"). It exists in a hard, ceremonial version and a soft field-cap version, usually in the colour of the service branch and bearing the Polish crowned eagle badge.

History

Four-cornered caps appeared in Polish military dress in the 18th century, with roots in the square-topped caps (konfederatka) associated with the Bar Confederation (1768) — indeed konfederatka remains an alternative name. The rogatywka was worn by Polish legionaries and uhlans in the Napoleonic era and by insurgents in the November (1830) and January (1863) uprisings, making it an emblem of the struggle for independence. It was standardized in the reborn Polish Army after 1919.

Second World War and after

Both the Polish Armed Forces in the West and the Polish People's Army in the East wore versions of the cap during and after the Second World War. The soft field rogatywka (pattern 1943 and later) became the standard field cap of the communist-era army. After 1989 the hard ceremonial rogatywka was fully restored, including the traditional crowned eagle; the cap is also worn by Polish police, border guards, firefighters, scouts, and railway staff.

Description

The cap is characterized by its square, four-pointed crown with a short peak (visor), a band bearing corps colours in ceremonial use, and the metal eagle cap badge. In the field version the crown is soft and the peak often stiffened only lightly, so the cap can be folded; historically, soldiers wore it tilted at characteristic angles.
