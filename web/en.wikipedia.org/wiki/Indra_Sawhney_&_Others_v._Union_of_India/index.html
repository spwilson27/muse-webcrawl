Indra Sawhney & Others v. Union of India

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Indra Sawhney & Others v. Union of India (1992) is a landmark judgment of the Supreme Court of India, popularly known as the Mandal Commission case. A nine-judge Constitution Bench, by a 6–3 majority, upheld the Union Government's decision to reserve 27 per cent of posts in central government services and public-sector undertakings for Other Backward Classes (OBCs), while imposing important limits on the reservation system. It remains the foundational authority on affirmative action ("reservations") in India.

Background

The Second Backward Classes Commission, chaired by B. P. Mandal and appointed in 1979, identified 3,743 castes as socially and educationally backward and recommended 27 per cent reservation for OBCs in government jobs, in addition to the existing 22.5 per cent for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. In August 1990, Prime Minister V. P. Singh announced implementation of the recommendation, triggering widespread protests, including self-immolations by upper-caste students, as well as strong support from backward-caste movements. Petitions led by advocate Indra Sawhney challenged the order.

Key holdings

The majority held that: (1) caste can be a valid, though not the sole, criterion for identifying a backward class under Article 16(4); (2) total reservations should ordinarily not exceed 50 per cent, following the rule in M. R. Balaji; (3) the socially and economically advanced "creamy layer" within backward classes must be excluded from the benefit; (4) reservations apply only to initial appointments and not to promotions (for OBCs); and (5) the 27 per cent OBC quota was constitutionally valid, while a separate 10 per cent quota for economically weaker sections of the forward classes, introduced by the succeeding government, was struck down at that time.

Legacy

The judgment reshaped Indian politics and administration, consolidating OBC reservation in central services and later in central educational institutions (upheld, with creamy-layer exclusion, in Ashoka Kumar Thakur in 2008). Its 50 per cent ceiling and creamy-layer doctrine continue to frame litigation over reservations, including later disputes over state quotas exceeding 50 per cent and the 10 per cent EWS reservation introduced by the 103rd Constitutional Amendment and upheld in 2022.
