Lollardy was a proto-Protestant Christian religious movement in England between the late 14th century and the English Reformation. Its principal ideas were derived from the teachings of John Wycliffe, the Oxford theologian who questioned the authority of the Roman Catholic Church. The name "Lollard" was a derogatory term probably derived from the Dutch lollaerd ("mumbler") or the Latin lolium (tare, a weed among wheat).

Beliefs

Lollards emphasised the authority of Scripture over church tradition, supported the translation of the Bible into English (the Wycliffe Bible), denied transubstantiation, rejected pilgrimages, image veneration and clerical celibacy, and criticised the wealth and political power of the church. Their views were summarised in documents such as the Twelve Conclusions of 1395, nailed to the doors of Westminster Abbey and St Paul's Cathedral.

Persecution

The statute De heretico comburendo (1401) authorised the burning of heretics, and Archbishop Thomas Arundel's Constitutions (1409) restricted Bible translation and lay preaching. Sir John Oldcastle's Lollard revolt of 1414 failed and drove the movement underground, where it survived in artisan and rural communities until it merged with the currents of the English Reformation in the 16th century.

Legacy

Lollardy is regarded as an important precursor of the Protestant Reformation, and its Bible-centred piety influenced later English religious life.
