Tape recorder

An audio tape recorder, also commonly known as a tape machine, tape player or tape deck, is a device that records and plays back sounds, usually using magnetic tape. In its various forms the tape recorder developed from the 1930s onward, revolutionising music production, broadcasting and home entertainment before being largely displaced by digital recording in the late 20th and early 21st centuries.

History

The first practical tape recorder, the Magnetophon, was developed in Germany in the 1930s by AEG in cooperation with BASF, building on the magnetic wire recorders pioneered by Valdemar Poulsen. After the Second World War the technology spread to the United States, where companies such as Ampex built recorders that transformed radio broadcasting — Bing Crosby famously invested in Ampex machines so he could pre-record his shows.

Formats

Professional studios used reel-to-reel machines running quarter-inch tape at speeds such as 7½, 15 and 30 inches per second, often with multiple tracks for multitrack recording. Consumer formats included the 8-track cartridge and, most successfully, Philips' Compact Cassette (1963), which became the dominant home audio format of the 1970s and 1980s, boosted by portable players such as the Sony Walkman.

How it works

A tape recorder moves plastic tape coated with a magnetic layer past a recording head. An electrical audio signal drives the head, which magnetises the tape in a pattern corresponding to the waveform; on playback, the moving magnetised tape induces a corresponding signal in the playback head. An erase head, fed with a high-frequency signal, clears previous recordings.

Decline and revival

Compact discs, MiniDiscs, MP3 players and streaming displaced tape in the consumer market, and professional studios moved to digital audio workstations. Nevertheless, cassettes and reel-to-reel machines have enjoyed a modest revival among enthusiasts who value their sound character and tactile operation.
