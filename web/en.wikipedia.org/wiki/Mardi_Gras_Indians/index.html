Mardi Gras Indians

A Big Chief in full regalia, New Orleans

Mardi Gras Indians (also known as Black Masking Indians) are Black carnival revelers in New Orleans, Louisiana, who dress in elaborate, hand-sewn suits influenced by Native American ceremonial apparel. Organized into "tribes" or "gangs", they parade on Mardi Gras Day, St. Joseph's Day (Super Sunday), and at jazz funerals, honouring the Native Americans who sheltered escaped enslaved people and celebrating African and Caribbean heritage.

History

The tradition is commonly traced to the 1880s, when Becate Batiste is said to have founded the first tribe, the Creole Wild West. Excluded from the white Mardi Gras krewes under segregation, Black New Orleanians created their own carnival. In earlier decades, encounters between rival tribes could turn violent; under leaders such as Allison "Tootie" Montana of the Yellow Pocahontas — the "Chief of Chiefs" — the competition shifted to artistry, with prestige going to the "prettiest" suit rather than to fighting.

Suits and roles

A new suit is made each year, taking months of sewing: intricate beadwork pictures, rhinestones, and crowns of dyed ostrich and marabou feathers, sometimes weighing over 100 pounds. Tribal positions include the Big Chief, Big Queen, Spy Boy (who scouts ahead), Flag Boy, and Wild Man. Tribes use a distinctive chant language and songs such as "Iko Iko" and "Indian Red", and the funk band The Wild Magnolias, led by Bo Dollis, carried the music to wider audiences in the 1970s.

Today

Around forty tribes parade in New Orleans. The Backstreet Cultural Museum and other institutions preserve suits and document the tradition, which is now recognised as a treasured expression of the city's Black culture.
