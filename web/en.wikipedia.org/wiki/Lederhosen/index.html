Lederhosen

Lederhosen (German: Lederhose, plural Lederhosen, "leather breeches") are short or knee-length leather breeches worn traditionally in the Alpine regions of Germany, Austria, and South Tyrol, particularly Bavaria. Once the practical workwear of Alpine peasants, hunters, and woodsmen, they are now worn chiefly as festive traditional dress (Tracht) at folk festivals such as Oktoberfest.

Description

Lederhosen are traditionally made of deerskin or goatskin, tanned and often embroidered with regional motifs. Common features include a front flap (rather than a fly), side seams with knife pocket, and braces (suspenders) joined by a decorated chest strap, or alternatively a belt. Short styles are called kurze; knee-length styles, worn with woollen stockings, are Kniebundhosen. The outfit is typically completed with a checked or plain shirt, a loden jacket, and sturdy Haferl shoes. Women's counterpart in traditional dress is the dirndl.

History

Leather breeches were common workwear across Europe for centuries; in the Alpine region they survived longest because the rugged material withstood hard physical labour and lasted for decades, sometimes being passed down through generations. In the nineteenth century, clubs dedicated to preserving traditional costume (Trachtenvereine), encouraged by the Bavarian court, romanticised and standardised lederhosen as a symbol of regional identity. Their modern popularity surged with the growth of Oktoberfest tourism in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, when sales of lederhosen and dirndls — including inexpensive mass-produced versions — rose sharply.
