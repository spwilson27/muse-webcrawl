The Algonquian languages (also spelled Algonkian) are a subfamily of the Algic language family, spoken by Indigenous peoples across a vast area of North America from the Atlantic coast to the Rocky Mountains. They include some of the most widely spoken Indigenous languages of Canada, notably Cree and Ojibwe.

Classification

Linguists traditionally divide the family into three areal groupings, though these do not all represent genetic subgroups: Plains Algonquian (Blackfoot, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Gros Ventre); Central Algonquian (Cree–Montagnais–Naskapi, Ojibwe–Potawatomi, Menominee, Shawnee, Miami–Illinois, Sauk–Fox–Kickapoo); and Eastern Algonquian (Mi'kmaq, Maliseet–Passamaquoddy, Abenaki, Massachusett, Delaware languages, Powhatan, and others), the last being a genuine genetic subgroup. All descend from Proto-Algonquian, spoken perhaps 2,500 to 3,000 years ago, whose homeland is debated but often placed between Lake Huron and the upper Great Lakes.

Features

Algonquian languages are polysynthetic, with complex verb morphology encoding subject, object, tense and evidentiality. Nouns are classified as animate or inanimate; verbs distinguish proximate and obviative third persons and direct and inverse alignment. The family gave English many loanwords, including moose, skunk, tomahawk, wigwam, toboggan and Mississippi.

Status

Many Algonquian languages are endangered following centuries of colonisation and forced assimilation, but Cree and Ojibwe retain tens of thousands of speakers, and revitalisation programmes — including Wampanoag (Wôpanâak), revived from extinction — are under way for several others.
