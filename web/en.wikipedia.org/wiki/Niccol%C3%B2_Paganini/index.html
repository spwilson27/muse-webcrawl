Niccolò Paganini (October 27, 1782 – May 27, 1840) was an Italian violinist and composer whose extraordinary virtuosity made him the most celebrated instrumental performer of his age. His 24 Caprices for Solo Violin, Op. 1, transformed violin technique with their demands in double stops, harmonics, left-hand pizzicato and flying staccato, and remain benchmarks of the instrument's repertoire.

Life and career

Born in Genoa, Paganini was taught violin by his father and studied with leading local teachers before touring Italy as a teenager. He served as court violinist in Lucca under Elisa Bonaparte Baciocchi, Napoleon's sister, before embarking on independent tours that from 1828 carried him to Vienna, Paris, London and other European capitals to sensational acclaim.

His gaunt appearance, remarkable flexibility of joint and apparently effortless command of impossible feats fed rumours that he had made a pact with the Devil, legends he sometimes cultivated for publicity.

Music and legacy

Besides the Caprices, Paganini composed six violin concertos, numerous sonatas and chamber works, and music for guitar, an instrument he also played to a high standard. His playing inspired composers including Franz Liszt, who sought a pianistic equivalent of his virtuosity, as well as Robert Schumann, Johannes Brahms and Sergei Rachmaninoff, whose Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini draws on the 24th Caprice.

The international Paganini Competition in Genoa, founded in 1954, is named in his honour. Paganini suffered from chronic ill health and died in Nice in 1840; controversy over the Devil rumours delayed his burial in consecrated ground for decades.
