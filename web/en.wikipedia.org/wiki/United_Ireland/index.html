United Ireland

A United Ireland, also called Irish reunification, is the proposed political unification of the Republic of Ireland with Northern Ireland, which is currently part of the United Kingdom, into a single independent Irish state. The idea is a central aim of Irish nationalism and republicanism and is opposed by unionists who wish Northern Ireland to remain in the United Kingdom.

Partition

The island was partitioned under the Government of Ireland Act 1920. Following the Anglo-Irish Treaty, the Irish Free State was established in 1922, while Northern Ireland remained within the United Kingdom. Partition divided a largely nationalist and Catholic south and west from a northeastern area with a Protestant and unionist majority. It became a source of prolonged political conflict, culminating in the Troubles of the late twentieth century.

Good Friday Agreement

The 1998 Good Friday Agreement affirmed the principle of consent: Northern Ireland's constitutional status cannot change without the support of a majority of its people in a referendum. The agreement also allows the Secretary of State for Northern Ireland to call a border poll if it appears likely that a majority would vote for a united Ireland. A corresponding referendum would be required in the Republic of Ireland, whose constitution was amended to define a united Ireland as achievable only by peaceful means and consent.

Contemporary debate

Supporters argue that reunification would complete Irish self-determination, simplify all-island economic planning and remove the border emphasized by the United Kingdom's departure from the European Union. Opponents stress Northern Ireland's British identity, public spending, institutional differences, healthcare and the risk of renewed instability. Recent discussion focuses on demographics, referendum timing, possible constitutional arrangements, taxation, public services and whether a future state would be unitary, federal or confederal. No border poll has yet been scheduled.
