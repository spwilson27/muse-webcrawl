Mooring

A mooring is any permanent structure to which a seagoing vessel may be secured. Examples include quays, wharves, jetties, piers, anchor buoys, and mooring buoys. A ship is secured to a mooring to forestall free movement of the ship on the water. An anchor mooring fixes a vessel's position relative to a point on the bottom of a waterway without connecting the vessel to shore.

As a verb, mooring refers to the act of attaching a vessel to a mooring. The term likely stems from the Dutch verb mereren (to moor), used since the end of the medieval period.

A mooring system typically consists of an anchor or block on the seabed, a chain or rope (the mooring line or rode) running to the surface, and a buoy to which a vessel ties up. Permanent moorings are common in harbours and marinas where space for swinging at anchor is limited, and are used by both commercial and recreational craft. Mooring buoys are also used over environmentally sensitive seabeds, such as coral reefs and seagrass beds, to prevent anchor damage; these are often fitted with elastic rodes that reduce chain drag on the bottom.

The design of a mooring depends on vessel size, water depth, tidal range, currents, wind exposure and the holding characteristics of the seabed. Modern moorings may use screw anchors (helical anchors) embedded in the seabed, mushroom anchors, or large concrete blocks. Failure of a mooring can result in a vessel breaking free, causing damage to itself and other craft.
