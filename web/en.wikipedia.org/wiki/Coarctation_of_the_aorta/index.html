Coarctation of the aorta

Coarctation of the aorta
SpecialtyCardiology, paediatrics
SymptomsHigh blood pressure in the arms, weak pulses in the legs; heart failure in infants
OnsetCongenital (present at birth)
TreatmentSurgical repair, balloon angioplasty, stenting
Frequency~1 in 2,900 births

Coarctation of the aorta (CoA) is a congenital narrowing of the aorta, the body's main artery. The narrowing most often occurs just beyond the branches supplying the head and arms, at the site of the ductus arteriosus (the "juxtaductal" position), and obstructs blood flow to the lower body. It accounts for roughly 5–8% of congenital heart defects and is about twice as common in males as in females. Coarctation is strongly associated with Turner syndrome and with bicuspid aortic valve.

Signs and symptoms

In severe cases, newborns present in the first days of life, when the ductus arteriosus closes, with poor feeding, breathlessness, and signs of heart failure or shock. Milder coarctation may go undetected until later childhood or adulthood, when it is found through investigation of high blood pressure. The classic physical signs are hypertension in the arms with diminished or delayed femoral pulses in the legs (radiofemoral delay), a blood pressure difference between the arms and legs, and a systolic murmur. Over time, enlarged collateral vessels develop around the obstruction; dilation of the intercostal arteries can erode the lower borders of the ribs, producing "rib notching" visible on a chest X-ray.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made by echocardiography, which can visualise the narrowing and measure the pressure gradient across it. CT angiography and MRI give detailed anatomical images for planning treatment. Measurement of blood pressure in all four limbs is a key clinical step.

Treatment

Significant coarctation requires repair. Options include surgical resection of the narrowed segment with end-to-end anastomosis (first performed by Clarence Crafoord in 1944), balloon angioplasty, and — in older children and adults — placement of a covered stent to hold the aorta open. Lifelong follow-up is needed because hypertension, re-narrowing (recoarctation), and aneurysm formation at the repair site can occur even after successful treatment.
