Deviance (sociology)

In sociology, deviance describes an action or behavior that violates social norms, including a formally-enacted rule (e.g., crime), as well as informal violations of social norms (e.g., rejecting folkways and mores). Although deviance may have a negative connotation, the violation of social norms is not always a negative action; positive deviation exists in some situations. For example, a person who risks their life to save a stranger violates expectations of self-preservation yet may be praised. What counts as deviant depends on the society, the situation and the time period.

Theoretical perspectives

  • Structural functionalism: Émile Durkheim argued deviance is a normal and even necessary part of society, clarifying moral boundaries and promoting unity and change. Robert Merton's strain theory holds that deviance arises when society promotes goals without equal access to legitimate means.
  • Labelling theory: Associated with Howard Becker (Outsiders, 1963), it holds that deviance is created by the social groups that make rules and apply labels; being labelled can produce secondary deviance through a deviant career.
  • Conflict theory: Focuses on how power shapes which acts are criminalised and how laws are enforced.
  • Social control theories: Ask not why people deviate but why they conform, pointing to bonds and stakes in conformity.

Forms and social control

Societies respond to deviance through informal sanctions (gossip, embarrassment, ostracism) and formal sanctions (fines, imprisonment, medicalisation). Sociologists distinguish primary deviance (initial rule-breaking) from secondary deviance (deviance following from being labelled).
