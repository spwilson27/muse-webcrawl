Usher syndrome


Tunnel vision in retinitis pigmentosa

Usher syndrome is a genetic disorder characterised by the combination of sensorineural hearing loss and progressive vision loss from retinitis pigmentosa. It is the most common cause of deafblindness, affecting roughly 3–10 people per 100,000, and is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. It is named after the Scottish ophthalmologist Charles Usher, who recognised its hereditary nature in 1914.

Types

Type 1 presents with profound deafness at birth, balance problems from vestibular dysfunction, and vision loss beginning in childhood; genes include MYO7A and CDH23. Type 2, the most common, involves moderate-to-severe hearing loss from birth, normal balance, and vision loss starting in adolescence (genes USH2A, ADGRV1). Type 3, frequent in Finland, features progressive post-lingual hearing loss and variable vision loss (gene CLRN1). At least ten genes are implicated, many encoding proteins of the hair-cell stereocilia and the photoreceptor connecting cilium.

Management

There is no cure. Cochlear implants work particularly well in type 1 if given early; hearing aids, orientation and mobility training, low-vision aids and communication methods such as tactile signing support independence. Vitamin A palmitate may slow retinal degeneration in some patients, and gene-therapy trials are under way.
