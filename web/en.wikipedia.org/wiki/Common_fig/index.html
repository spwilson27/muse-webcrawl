The common fig (Ficus carica) is an Asian species of flowering plant in the mulberry family, Moraceae, native to the Mediterranean region and western Asia. It has been cultivated since ancient times and is now widely grown throughout the world for its fruit, also called the fig. Ficus carica is the type species of the genus Ficus, which contains over 800 species of trees, shrubs, and vines.

Description

The common fig is a deciduous tree or large shrub growing to 7–10 metres, with smooth white bark and large, deeply lobed leaves. Its "fruit" is a syconium — a hollow, fleshy receptacle whose inner surface bears hundreds of tiny true flowers. Wild trees are pollinated by the fig wasp Blastophaga psenes in a celebrated obligate mutualism, though most cultivated varieties are parthenocarpic and set fruit without pollination.

Cultivation and history

Figs were among the earliest fruits domesticated: charred remains from Neolithic sites in the Jordan Valley date to about 9400–9200 BCE, possibly preceding cereal domestication. The fruit is eaten fresh or dried, and dried figs travel and store well, making them a staple in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern cuisines. Major producers include Turkey, Egypt, Morocco, Iran, and Algeria. Popular cultivars include 'Black Mission', 'Brown Turkey', 'Kadota', and 'Adriatic'.

Cultural significance

The fig appears in the Book of Genesis, where Adam and Eve cover themselves with fig leaves, and the fig tree is a recurrent symbol of fertility and peace ("every man under his vine and under his fig tree"). The sacred fig (Ficus religiosa) under which the Buddha attained enlightenment is a different species.
