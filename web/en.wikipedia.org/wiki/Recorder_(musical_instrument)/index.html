Recorder (musical instrument)

The recorder is a family of woodwind musical instruments in the group known as internal duct flutes: flutes with a whistle mouthpiece, also known as fipple flutes. A recorder can be distinguished from other duct flutes by the presence of a thumb-hole for the upper hand and seven finger-holes: three for the upper hand and four for the lower. It is the most prominent duct flute in the Western classical tradition.

Recorders are made in a variety of sizes, from the garklein (a hand's length) to contrabass instruments over two metres tall. The most common today are the soprano (descant) and alto (treble) recorders. The instrument was popular in medieval times and enjoyed wide use in the Renaissance and Baroque, with a large repertoire by composers including Handel, Telemann, Vivaldi and Bach; it fell from favour as transverse flutes and clarinets grew louder and more expressive, and was revived in the twentieth century by pioneers such as Arnold Dolmetsch and Frans Brüggen, both as a serious early-music instrument and as a school instrument worldwide.

Construction and sound production

A recorder consists of a cylindrical or slightly conical tube whose mouthpiece contains a plug (block or fipple) that leaves a narrow windway. Air strikes the sharp edge (labium) of a window cut in the tube, setting the air column vibrating. Eight holes — seven in front, one thumb-hole behind — are stopped by the fingers; the thumb-hole is partially uncovered ("pinched") to produce the upper register. Traditional instruments are made of close-grained hardwoods such as maple, pear, boxwood and ebony; school instruments are commonly moulded in ABS resin.

Sizes and pitch

The recorder family is built in F and C at successive sizes: garklein and sopranino in F and C, soprano in C, alto in F, tenor in C, bass in F, and great bass and contrabass below. In consorts, music is voiced like a choir. In modern British school usage the soprano is called the "descant" and the alto the "treble". The pitch standard for modern instruments is A = 442 Hz, while Baroque reproductions often use A = 415 Hz.

History

Duct flutes are ancient, but the earliest surviving medieval recorder dates from the fourteenth century (the Dordrecht recorder). Renaissance recorders, with wide bores and a soft, blending tone, were played in whole consorts and depicted in the hands of angels and shepherds. The Baroque recorder — narrower, in three joints, with a wider range and a more penetrating sound — was a favourite solo instrument at the courts of England and France; Henry Purcell, Handel and Telemann wrote extensively for it. Virtuosi such as Frans Brüggen and Michala Petri led a modern revival, commissioning new works from composers including Luciano Berio (Gesti, 1966) and extending the instrument's technique with multiphonics and contemporary effects.

In education

Because it is inexpensive, robust and quick to give a first musical result, the soprano recorder became a staple of primary-school music education in many countries during the twentieth century, notably in Germany, Japan and the United Kingdom.
