Diesel-electric locomotive

Image: Diesel-electric freight locomotive

A diesel-electric locomotive is a type of railway locomotive in which the diesel engine drives an electrical generator that supplies power to traction motors, which in turn drive the wheels. It is the most common form of diesel locomotive worldwide, having displaced steam traction on most non-electrified railways during the mid-twentieth century.

Principle of operation

The diesel prime mover is mechanically decoupled from the wheels: it turns a main generator or alternator whose electrical output is fed to traction motors mounted on the axles. This arrangement acts as a continuously variable transmission, allowing the engine to run at efficient speeds regardless of train speed, and gives high tractive effort at starting. Modern locomotives typically use AC alternators with rectifiers and, increasingly, AC traction motors controlled by inverters.

History

Experimental diesel-electric locomotives appeared in the 1910s and 1920s, including units built by General Electric and the first commercially successful switchers of the 1920s. The introduction of lightweight streamliners in the 1930s and of standardised road locomotives such as EMD's FT in 1939 drove the "dieselisation" of North American railroads, largely complete by 1960; British Rail and other systems followed similar transitions.

Advantages

Compared with steam, diesel-electric locomotives offer higher thermal efficiency, lower maintenance, rapid starting, and the ability to operate multiple units under one crew. Compared with straight electric locomotives, they require no fixed electrification infrastructure, at the cost of carrying their own fuel and power plant.
