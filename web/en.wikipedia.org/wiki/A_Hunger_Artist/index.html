A Hunger Artist

"A Hunger Artist" (German: "Ein Hungerkünstler") is a short story by Franz Kafka first published in the periodical Die neue Rundschau in 1922. It was later included in the collection A Hunger Artist, published by Verlag Die Schmiede in 1924, shortly after Kafka's death. The story describes a Hungerkünstler — a professional faster who performs in a cage for paying audiences — whose art falls out of fashion and who ends his career as a neglected circus attraction.

Plot

The hunger artist fasts for periods of up to forty days in a cage strewn with straw, watched by teams of observers (usually butchers, to the artist's annoyance) who ensure he does not eat in secret. Crowds initially flock to see him, but many suspect cheating. The artist longs to fast indefinitely, yet his impresario limits fasts to forty days because public interest wanes after that point. As tastes change, professional fasting ceases to draw crowds. The artist joins a circus, where his cage is placed near the menagerie and gradually forgotten. When an overseer finally discovers him, nearly dead, the artist confesses that he was never worthy of admiration: he fasted only because he could never find food he liked — "if I had found it, I would have made no fuss and stuffed myself like you or anyone else." He is buried with his straw, and a young panther, exulting in its appetite for life, takes his place in the cage.

Themes and interpretation

The story is commonly read as an allegory of the artist's alienation from an uncomprehending public, of ascetic striving toward an impossible perfection, and of the conflict between spirit and flesh. The panther's joyful vitality contrasts with the artist's self-destructive idealism. Kafka corrected the proofs of the collection on his deathbed, while himself unable to swallow because of tuberculosis of the larynx — a biographical parallel often noted by critics.
