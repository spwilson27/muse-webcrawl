Overview

A time zone is a region that observes a uniform standard time. Time zones are generally centred on meridians of longitude spaced 15° apart — the distance the Sun appears to move in one hour — and are expressed as offsets from Coordinated Universal Time, such as UTC+1 or UTC−5.

Before standard time, towns kept their own local solar time; railway timetables made that impractical in the 19th century, and national standard zones spread from Britain and North America around the world.

Variations

Real time-zone boundaries follow political borders rather than meridians, producing famous irregularities: China uses a single zone across five geographical zones, Spain keeps Central European Time far west of Greenwich, and offsets include half- and quarter-hour steps such as India's UTC+5:30 and Nepal's UTC+5:45. Many countries add a further hour of daylight saving time in summer. The count of distinct offsets in use changes as governments legislate, so it is given here only approximately.
