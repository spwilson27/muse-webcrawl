Pledge (law)

A pledge is a bailment that conveys possessory title to property owned by a debtor (the pledgor) to a creditor (the pledgee) to secure repayment for some debt or obligation, and to the mutual benefit of both parties. The term is also used to denote the property which constitutes the security. A pledge is a type of security interest.

Characteristics

The pledge arises when possession of the asset is transferred to the creditor, and it typically relates only to movable (personal) property. It is to be distinguished from a mortgage or hypothecation, where the debtor retains possession, and from a lien, which arises by operation of law. On repayment of the debt, the pledgee must return the property; on default, the pledgee may sell it, subject to the rules of the applicable jurisdiction.

Pawnbroking

The best-known modern example is the pawnbroker's transaction: the pawner pledges goods as security for a small loan, redeemable within a stated period, and the pawnbroker may sell unredeemed pledges. In modern finance, pledging is common in securities lending and margin lending, where shares or bonds serve as collateral.

History

The pledge has roots in Roman law (pignus) and was developed in English common law as one of the classic forms of bailment recognised in cases such as Coggs v Bernard (1703).
