Flag semaphore

Flag semaphore (from Greek sema, "sign", and phoros, "bearer") is a telegraphy system conveying information at a distance by means of visual signals with hand-held flags, rods, disks, paddles, or occasionally bare or gloved hands. Information is encoded by the positions of the flags; it is read when the flags are held in a fixed position. Semaphore flags are usually divided diagonally and coloured red and yellow in the maritime version.

System

In the modern two-flag system, the signaller holds a flag in each hand and places them in one of eight positions at 45-degree increments around a circle, giving combinations that encode the letters of the alphabet, numerals and a few control signals (such as "error", "attention" and numerals/letters shift). The system was developed in the Royal Navy in the early nineteenth century, adapting the idea of earlier optical telegraphs and mechanical semaphore lines, and was widely used for ship-to-ship and ship-to-shore communication before radio became universal. Signalling by flag semaphore is still taught in navies and in Scouting, and the flags remain part of the International Code of Signals era equipment.

Cultural references

The semaphore positions for the letters N and D, superimposed, form the basis of the well-known peace symbol designed by Gerald Holtom in 1958 for the British nuclear disarmament movement. The cover of the Beatles' album Help! (1965) shows the band in semaphore-like poses (which, famously, do not spell "HELP" but approximations chosen for appearance).
