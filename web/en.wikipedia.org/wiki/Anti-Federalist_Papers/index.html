Anti-Federalist Papers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Anti-Federalist Papers are the body of essays, speeches and pamphlets written in opposition to the United States Constitution during the ratification debates of 1787–88. Their pseudonymous authors — “Brutus” (probably Robert Yates), “Cato”, the “Federal Farmer” (attributed variously to Richard Henry Lee or Melancton Smith), “Centinel” — together with Patrick Henry, George Mason (whose refusal to sign helped force the issue) and Mercy Otis Warren, argued that the Constitution created a consolidated national government too distant, too powerful and too aristocratic to be safe: a large republic would extinguish liberty; the necessary-and-proper and supremacy clauses would swallow the states; taxation, standing armies and a federal judiciary without juries threatened the people's rights; and the absence of a bill of rights was disqualifying.

The Anti-Federalists lost the ratification fight but won the amendment agenda: the United States Bill of Rights (1791) — including the Tenth Amendment's reservation of powers — is substantially their legacy, and their suspicion of consolidated power recurs in American politics from nullification to modern federalism and originalist critiques of national administration. Unlike the Federalist Papers they were not collected as a canon in their time (modern editions, notably Storing's The Complete Anti-Federalist, 1981, assembled them), and scholarship treats them as the founding era's great “losers' history”, recovering a democratic, small-republic tradition within American constitutionalism.
