Wankel engine

The Wankel engine is a type of internal combustion engine using an eccentric rotary design to convert pressure into rotating motion. Invented by German engineer Felix Wankel and developed with NSU in the 1950s, it replaces pistons with a single triangular rotor turning inside an oval (epitrochoid) housing.

Operation

The rotor's three faces form moving combustion chambers that pass intake, compression, power and exhaust phases as the rotor turns — completing one power stroke per face per revolution of the eccentric shaft, which rotates three times for each rotor revolution. The design is compact, mechanically simple, has few moving parts and runs smoothly, producing high power for its size and weight.

Applications and drawbacks

NSU launched the first Wankel-powered car, the Spider, in 1964, and Mazda became the engine's most committed maker, using rotaries in the Cosmo, the RX-7 and the RX-8, and winning the 1991 Le Mans 24 Hours with the 787B — the only victory by a non-piston-engined car. Persistent drawbacks included apex-seal wear, high fuel consumption and hydrocarbon emissions, and poor low-speed torque. Mazda ended RX-8 production in 2012 and later revived the rotary as a range-extender generator in the MX-30 e-Skyactiv R-EV. Rotary engines also saw use in motorcycles, aircraft and unmanned aerial vehicles.
