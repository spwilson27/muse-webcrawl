Google Books

Google Books (previously known as Google Book Search and Google Print) is a service from Google Inc. that searches the full text of books and magazines that Google has scanned, converted to text using optical character recognition (OCR), and stored in its digital database. Books are provided either by publishers and authors through the Books Partner Program, or by library partners through the Library Project. Launched in October 2004 at the Frankfurt Book Fair, the service has scanned tens of millions of volumes from major research libraries including those of Stanford, Harvard, Oxford, Michigan and the New York Public Library.

Access levels

Depending on copyright status and publisher permissions, results display as full view (public-domain works), limited preview (a fixed portion of pages), snippet view (a few lines around the search term) or no preview. Users can build personal libraries, and the underlying corpus powers the Google Ngram Viewer, which charts word frequencies across the scanned literature.

Copyright litigation

The Library Project provoked major lawsuits. In 2005 the Authors Guild and several publishers sued Google for copyright infringement; a proposed settlement in 2008 would have created a Book Rights Registry and a commercial e-book service, but Judge Denny Chin rejected it in 2011. In November 2013 Judge Chin ruled that Google Books was fair use, praising its "transformative" public benefits, and the Second Circuit affirmed in 2015; the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the Authors Guild's appeal in 2016, ending the litigation.

Legacy

Google Books remains a principal tool for discovering out-of-print and scholarly literature; many library scans were also contributed to the HathiTrust digital repository. Critics have noted OCR errors, metadata problems and the gradual slowing of new scanning, but the corpus endures as one of the largest digital book collections ever assembled.
