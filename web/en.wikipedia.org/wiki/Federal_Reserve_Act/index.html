The Federal Reserve Act is a United States federal law, signed by President Woodrow Wilson on 23 December 1913, which created the Federal Reserve System, the central banking system of the United States. It authorised the issue of Federal Reserve notes as the national currency and established a structure of regional reserve banks supervised by a federal board in Washington, a compromise between advocates of centralised public control and those who feared a single central bank dominated by either Wall Street or the federal government.

Background

The Act was the culmination of decades of debate over American banking, sharpened by the Panic of 1907, when a liquidity crisis was contained only by the private intervention of J. P. Morgan. The National Monetary Commission, chaired by Senator Nelson Aldrich, produced the bankers'-oriented Aldrich Plan; after the Democratic victory of 1912, Representative Carter Glass and Senator Robert Owen reshaped the proposal into a decentralised system with a publicly appointed oversight board, enacted as the Federal Reserve Act.

Provisions

The Act divided the country into twelve Federal Reserve districts, each with a Federal Reserve Bank — in Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas and San Francisco — owned by the member banks in its district. National banks were required to join the system and subscribe to the stock of their reserve bank; state banks could elect to join. The Act created the Federal Reserve Board in Washington, authorised the reserve banks to rediscount commercial paper and thereby supply an "elastic currency", required reserves against deposits, and established a nationwide system of cheque clearing.

Amendments

The system the Act created has been repeatedly reshaped. The Banking Act of 1935 centralised authority in the Board of Governors and created the modern Federal Open Market Committee. The Federal Reserve Reform Act of 1977 and the Humphrey–Hawkins Act of 1978 codified the "dual mandate" of maximum employment and stable prices. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010, following the financial crisis of 2007–2008, curtailed the Fed's emergency lending powers and imposed additional reporting and audit requirements.
