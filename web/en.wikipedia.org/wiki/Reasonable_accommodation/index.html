Reasonable accommodation

A reasonable accommodation is an adjustment or modification to a job, work environment, or process that enables a qualified person with a disability to perform the essential functions of a position, or to enjoy equal access to a place, program, or service. The concept is a cornerstone of disability discrimination law in many jurisdictions.

United States law

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA), employers with 15 or more employees must provide reasonable accommodations to qualified individuals with disabilities unless doing so would cause "undue hardship" — significant difficulty or expense. Examples include making facilities wheelchair accessible, modifying work schedules, providing sign language interpreters or screen-reading software, restructuring non-essential job duties, and allowing service animals. The employer and employee are expected to engage in an informal "interactive process" to identify an effective accommodation.

Related duties arise under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, the Fair Housing Act (for housing modifications), and education laws such as IDEA and Section 504 plans, which require accommodations for students with disabilities. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act separately requires reasonable accommodation of sincerely held religious practices absent undue hardship — a standard the Supreme Court revisited in Groff v. DeJoy (2023), holding that employers must show substantial increased costs.

International law

The UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities defines reasonable accommodation and treats its denial as a form of discrimination; similar duties exist in EU directives, the UK Equality Act 2010 ("reasonable adjustments"), and Canadian human rights law ("duty to accommodate" short of undue hardship).
