Dissociative identity disorder

Dissociative identity disorder (DID), previously known as multiple personality disorder, is a dissociative disorder characterised by the presence of two or more distinct personality states (commonly called alters) that recurrently take control of a person's behaviour, accompanied by memory gaps beyond ordinary forgetting. The condition is generally attributed to severe and prolonged trauma during early childhood, typically extreme, repetitive physical, sexual, or emotional abuse.

Signs and symptoms

According to the DSM-5, diagnosis requires disruption of identity characterised by two or more distinct personality states, recurrent gaps in recall of everyday events, personal information, or traumatic events, and clinically significant distress or impairment. Other symptoms commonly reported include depersonalisation, derealisation, headaches, and co-occurring depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress.

Causes

The prevailing trauma model holds that DID develops as a response to overwhelming childhood trauma before the age at which a unified sense of self consolidates. A sociocognitive model, by contrast, proposes that the phenomenon arises through social expectations, media influence, and suggestive therapeutic techniques; the disorder remains the subject of scientific debate.

History

Cases of apparent multiple personality were described in the nineteenth century, and the diagnosis rose to prominence in the 1970s and 1980s following popular accounts such as Sybil. The name was changed from multiple personality disorder to dissociative identity disorder in DSM-IV (1994) to emphasise dissociation rather than separate "personalities".

Treatment

Treatment is chiefly long-term, phase-based psychotherapy aimed at safety, stabilisation, trauma processing, and — where desired — integration of identity states. Guidelines are published by the International Society for the Study of Trauma and Dissociation.
