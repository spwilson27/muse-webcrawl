Relative volatility

Relative volatility is a measure in chemical engineering of the difference in the volatility between two components in a liquid mixture. It is widely used in the design of large industrial distillation processes, because it indicates how easy or difficult a particular separation by distillation is likely to be.

Definition

Relative volatility is defined as the ratio of the vapour pressure of the more volatile component to the vapour pressure of the less volatile component at a given temperature, or more generally in terms of the distribution coefficients (K-values) of the two components:

αAB = KA / KB = (yA/xA) / (yB/xB)

where y is the mole fraction in the vapour phase and x is the mole fraction in the liquid phase. For an ideal mixture obeying Raoult's law, the relative volatility reduces to the ratio of the pure-component vapour pressures.

Significance

If the relative volatility is close to 1, the two components have very similar vapour pressures and boiling points, and separation by ordinary distillation requires many equilibrium stages and a high reflux ratio. If α is large, the separation is easy. Relative volatility is used in the Fenske equation to estimate the minimum number of theoretical plates required for a separation, and in the McCabe–Thiele method for column design.
