Speech-generating device

Speech-generating devices (SGDs), also known as voice output communication aids, are electronic augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) systems used to supplement or replace speech or writing for individuals with severe speech impairments, enabling them to verbally communicate. Users select letters, words, pictures or symbols on the device, which then speaks the message aloud using digitised (recorded) or synthesised speech.

An SGD with a grid of symbols that speak when selected.

Users and access methods

SGDs are used by people with conditions including cerebral palsy, ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis), aphasia after stroke, traumatic brain injury, and autism. Access methods are adapted to motor abilities: direct touch, head pointers, switches with scanning, and eye-gaze tracking. The physicist Stephen Hawking was the device's most famous user, communicating from the 1980s with a wheelchair-mounted system operated first by a hand switch and later by a cheek muscle sensor, speaking with a distinctive synthesised voice.

Design

Interfaces range from simple single-message buttons to dynamic-display tablets with thousands of vocabulary items organised by core-word layouts, symbol sets (such as PCS or SymbolStix), spelling keyboards with word prediction, and increasingly personalised synthetic voices — including "voice banking," in which a person records their own voice before losing it, so the device can later speak in a version of it.

Clinical practice

SGDs are prescribed with assessment and training by speech-language pathologists, since successful adoption depends on matching the device to the user's language, motor and cognitive profile. Research indicates AAC does not hinder natural speech development and may support it. Modern consumer tablets and apps (e.g., Proloquo2Go) have sharply reduced costs compared with dedicated medical devices, expanding access since around 2010.
