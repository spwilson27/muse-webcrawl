A shareholder (or stockholder) is a person, company or institution that owns at least one share of a company's stock — a share being a unit of equity ownership in the corporation. Shareholders are the legal owners of the company in proportion to their holdings, though in most jurisdictions the corporation is a separate legal person and shareholders enjoy limited liability for its debts.

Rights

Common shareholders typically have the right to vote at general meetings on matters such as electing directors, approving mergers and amending the charter; to receive dividends when declared; to a share of residual assets on liquidation; and to inspect certain corporate records and sue directors for breach of duty. Rights vary by share class: preferred shareholders usually have priority in dividends and liquidation but limited or no voting rights.

Types

Shareholders range from individual retail investors to institutional investors such as pension funds, mutual funds and sovereign wealth funds. A majority or controlling shareholder owns enough voting stock to direct corporate policy, while minority shareholders hold smaller stakes and rely on legal protections against oppression or unfair prejudice.

Governance and activism

Through annual general meetings, proxy voting and engagement, shareholders exercise oversight of management. Shareholder activism — campaigns by investors to change strategy, capital structure or board composition — has become a significant force in corporate governance, alongside debates over whether managers should maximise shareholder value alone or consider wider stakeholder interests.
