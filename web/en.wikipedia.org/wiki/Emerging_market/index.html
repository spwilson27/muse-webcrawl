Emerging market

An emerging market (or an emerging country or an emerging economy) is a market that has some characteristics of a developed market, but does not fully meet its standards. This includes markets that may become developed markets in the future or were in the past. The term was coined in 1981 by Antoine Van Agtmael of the International Finance Corporation.

Characteristics

Emerging markets generally feature a lower-than-average per capita income, rapid economic growth, industrialization, a growing middle class, increasing integration into the global economy, and developing financial market infrastructure. They are often characterized by higher volatility and political risk than developed markets, alongside the potential for higher investment returns.

Classification

Index providers such as MSCI, FTSE Russell, and S&P Dow Jones classify countries as developed, emerging, frontier, or standalone markets based on economic development, size and liquidity requirements, and market accessibility. The BRICS grouping—Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa—is a prominent association of major emerging economies. China and India are the largest emerging markets by GDP.

Criticism of the term

Some economists argue that the term has become too broad to be useful, grouping countries with very different levels of income, institutions, and growth prospects. Others note that several countries classified as emerging have per capita incomes or financial systems comparable to developed economies.
