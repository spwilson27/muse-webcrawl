The hustle is a catch-all name for several disco dances that became popular in the 1970s. Today it mostly refers to a unique partner dance associated with the disco era. The hustle has some features in common with swing dance: it is danced in a slotted pattern, with turns, spins, and dips, and its basic footwork is counted in syncopated "&1 2 3" patterns to music in 4/4 time.

History

The hustle emerged around 1973 in Latino and African-American social clubs in the South Bronx of New York City, evolving from earlier dances such as the mambo-derived Latin dances and the "Spanish hustle." By 1975 it had spread to Manhattan discotheques and gone mainstream. Van McCoy's 1975 instrumental hit "The Hustle" — which won a Grammy Award and reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 — cemented the name, and the dance featured prominently in the 1977 film Saturday Night Fever.

Forms

Several variants circulated during the disco era:

  • Line hustle — a line dance performed without partners, popularized by "The Hustle" recordings.
  • Partner (Latin) hustle — the touch-disco partner dance with three-count syncopated basic steps, rope spins, and wraps.
  • Rope hustle — danced using a rope or scarf connecting the partners.
  • New York hustle — a faster, more theatrical club style with lifts and dips.

After the decline of disco, the partner hustle survived within the ballroom and swing-dance communities, where it is still taught and danced socially to contemporary pop and dance music.
