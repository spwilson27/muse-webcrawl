Capital punishment in Europe

Image placeholder: Europe is almost entirely abolitionist.

Capital punishment has been abolished in almost all European countries. Europe is the only region in the world where the death penalty is effectively eliminated: Belarus is the only European state that still carries out executions, and Russia maintains a moratorium.

Abolition movement

Abolition gathered pace after the Second World War as part of a broader human rights framework. The Council of Europe made abolition a condition of membership, and Protocol No. 6 to the European Convention on Human Rights (1983) abolished the death penalty in peacetime, while Protocol No. 13 (2002) abolished it in all circumstances, including wartime. The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union likewise prohibits capital punishment, and no EU member state may practise it.

Status by country

Countries such as the United Kingdom (last execution 1964, full abolition for murder 1965–1969), France (abolished 1981), and Germany (abolished in the Federal Republic's Basic Law of 1949) led the trend. The last execution in the European Union took place in Latvia's predecessor era and in France in 1977; most states have also removed capital offences from military law. Belarus, outside the Council of Europe, continues to execute prisoners, typically by shooting, and is regularly condemned by European institutions.
