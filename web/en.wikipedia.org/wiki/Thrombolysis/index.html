Thrombolysis

Thrombolysis, also called fibrinolytic therapy, is the pharmacological breakdown of blood clots formed in blood vessels, using drugs that activate plasminogen to plasmin, which dissolves the fibrin meshwork of the clot. It is used to treat conditions caused by thrombosis, notably myocardial infarction (heart attack), ischaemic stroke, and massive pulmonary embolism, where rapidly restoring blood flow limits tissue damage.

Mechanism

Thrombolytic drugs are plasminogen activators: they convert the inactive proenzyme plasminogen, which is incorporated into clots, into the protease plasmin. Plasmin cleaves fibrin strands and dissolves the clot. Agents differ in fibrin specificity: alteplase (recombinant tissue plasminogen activator, tPA) and its variants tenecteplase and reteplase are relatively clot-selective, whereas the bacterial protein streptokinase, the oldest agent, produces a more systemic fibrinolytic state.

Uses

In ST-elevation myocardial infarction, thrombolysis is given when timely primary angioplasty is unavailable, ideally within 12 hours of symptom onset. In acute ischaemic stroke, intravenous alteplase or tenecteplase may be given within a narrow window (generally up to 4.5 hours) after excluding haemorrhage by imaging. In high-risk pulmonary embolism with haemodynamic instability, thrombolysis can rapidly reduce clot burden. Catheter-directed thrombolysis delivers drug directly into a clot, for example in extensive deep vein thrombosis.

Risks and contraindications

The principal hazard is bleeding, including a small but serious risk of intracranial haemorrhage; recent surgery, active bleeding, uncontrolled hypertension and recent stroke are among the contraindications that must be screened for before treatment.
