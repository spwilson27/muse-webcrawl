Coin clipping

Coin clipping is the illegal practice of shaving a small amount of precious metal from the edge of a coin in circulation, so that the clipped coin can still be passed at its full face value while the clippings are collected and sold or melted down as bullion. It was a persistent fraud in the centuries when coins were made of gold or silver and struck with irregular, hand-made flans, and it was typically punishable as a form of treason or counterfeiting.

History

Because hammered coins were irregular in shape, modest clipping was hard to detect, and by the mid-seventeenth century English silver coins had lost a large fraction of their proper weight. The Great Recoinage of 1696 under William III replaced the hammered silver with machine-made coins. From the reign of Charles II, milled coinage produced at the Royal Mint — under Isaac Newton as Warden and then Master of the Mint, an energetic prosecutor of clippers and counterfeiters — carried lettered or grained edges precisely to make clipping evident. The Latin edge inscription DECVS ET TVTAMEN ("an ornament and a safeguard"), introduced in 1662, advertised the feature.

Countermeasures

Reeded (milled) edges, edge lettering, and the shift to base-metal token coinage whose face value exceeds its metal value have effectively eliminated clipping in modern currencies.
