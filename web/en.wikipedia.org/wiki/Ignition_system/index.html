Ignition system

An ignition system generates a spark or heats an electrode to a high temperature to ignite a fuel–air mixture in spark-ignition internal combustion engines — in road vehicles, aircraft piston engines, marine outboards, and oil- and gas-fired boilers and furnaces.

Types

Early systems used magneto ignition, generating pulses without a battery. Battery-and-coil (Kettering) ignition, introduced by Charles Kettering in the 1910s, used contact breaker points and a distributor to deliver a high-voltage pulse from an ignition coil to each spark plug in sequence. Electronic ignition replaced points with transistors from the 1970s, improving reliability; modern engines use distributorless systems with coil-on-plug units controlled by the engine control unit, which sets timing precisely for each cylinder.

Operation

The coil acts as a step-up transformer: interrupting current in its low-turn primary winding induces a pulse of tens of thousands of volts in the secondary, which jumps the spark-plug gap. Correct ignition timing — advancing the spark with engine speed and load — is essential to power, economy and emissions.
