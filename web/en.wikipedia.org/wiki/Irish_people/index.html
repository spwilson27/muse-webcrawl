The Irish people (Irish: Na Gaeil or Muintir na hÉireann) are an ethnic group and nation native to the island of Ireland, sharing a common history, culture and ancestry. The population of Ireland is about 6.9 million, but between 70 and 80 million people worldwide claim Irish descent — a legacy of centuries of emigration driven by poverty, famine and economic opportunity — making the Irish diaspora many times larger than the population of the island itself.

Origins and history

Ireland was settled by Mesolithic hunter-gatherers around 10,000 years ago, followed by Neolithic farmers and Bronze Age metalworkers. By the Iron Age the island was Celtic-speaking and divided among Gaelic kingdoms (túatha) owing allegiance to provincial kings and a nominal High King. Christianity arrived in the 5th century, traditionally with Saint Patrick, and Irish monasteries became famous centres of learning. Viking towns (Dublin, Waterford, Cork, Limerick) were founded from the 9th century; the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169 began eight centuries of English involvement, followed by Tudor conquest, the Plantations, the Penal Laws, the Act of Union of 1801 and the catastrophic Great Famine (1845–1852), which killed about a million people and drove another million to emigrate. The independence struggle produced the Irish Free State in 1922; the Republic of Ireland left the Commonwealth in 1949, while Northern Ireland remained in the United Kingdom, experiencing the Troubles (c. 1968–1998) before the Good Friday Agreement.

Culture

Irish culture is known worldwide for its literature — Ireland has produced four Nobel laureates in literature (Yeats, Shaw, Beckett and Heaney) — its traditional music and dance, its mythology and folklore, and the social institution of the pub. Gaelic games (hurling and Gaelic football), administered by the Gaelic Athletic Association founded in 1884, are central to community identity. Saint Patrick's Day (17 March) is celebrated globally, a measure of the reach of the diaspora.

Language

Irish (Gaeilge), a Goidelic Celtic language, is the first official language of the Republic and is spoken daily by a minority, chiefly in the Gaeltacht districts of the west and south, though large numbers learn it in school. English, in the form of Hiberno-English, is the everyday language of the great majority. In Northern Ireland both Irish and Ulster Scots have official recognition under the Good Friday Agreement.

Diaspora

The largest Irish-descended populations are in the United States (about 31 million reporting Irish ancestry), Great Britain, Canada, Australia and Argentina. Chain migration patterns tied emigrants to particular counties and neighbourhoods, and remittances sustained families at home for generations. Emigration resumed in the 1950s and 1980s before reversing during the Celtic Tiger boom; Ireland has in recent decades become a country of net immigration and notable diversity.
