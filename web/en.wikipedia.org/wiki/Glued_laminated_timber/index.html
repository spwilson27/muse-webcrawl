Glued laminated timber

Glued laminated timber, commonly called glulam, is a type of structural engineered wood product made by bonding together layers (laminations) of dimension lumber with durable, moisture-resistant structural adhesives, with the grain running parallel. The technique was patented in Germany by Otto Hetzer in the early 1900s and is now used worldwide.

Properties and uses

Glulam members can be manufactured in very large sizes and in curved or arched shapes that are impossible in solid timber. Pound for pound, glulam is stronger than steel and can span long distances, making it popular for sports halls, bridges, churches and commercial roofs. Because it is made from smaller trees and stores carbon, it is promoted as a sustainable alternative to steel and concrete in mass timber construction.

Manufacture

Laminations are kiln-dried, finger-jointed into long lengths, planed, coated with adhesive (often melamine-urea-formaldehyde or polyurethane), stacked in a jig of the desired shape, and cured under pressure.
