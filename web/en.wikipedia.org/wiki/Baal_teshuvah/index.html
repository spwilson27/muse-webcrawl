In Judaism, a baal teshuvah (Hebrew: בעל תשובה, literally "master of return" or "master of repentance") is a Jew from a secular or non-observant background who becomes religiously observant, typically within Orthodox Judaism. The term derives from teshuvah, the Jewish concept of repentance and return to God and to observance of the commandments (mitzvot). The wider phenomenon of Jews adopting Orthodoxy later in life is often called the baal teshuvah movement.

The movement

The movement grew markedly from the late 1960s and 1970s, paralleling the broader countercultural search for meaning and, in the Soviet Union, the underground religious revival among Jews denied religious education. Outreach (kiruv) organisations — most prominently Chabad-Lubavitch, as well as Aish HaTorah and Ohr Somayach — established yeshivas and seminaries designed specifically for beginners, such as Machon Meir and the yeshivas of the Ohr Somayach network in Jerusalem, teaching students with little or no Hebrew or Talmudic background. In the former Soviet Union and among its emigrants, the movement drew large numbers of highly educated Jews raised under state atheism.

Religious status and culture

Classical sources esteem the baal teshuvah highly: the Talmud teaches that "in the place where baalei teshuvah stand, even the completely righteous cannot stand". Socially, however, baalei teshuvah may face practical challenges integrating into communities of the "frum from birth" (FFB), including differences in custom, dress codes and matchmaking (shidduchim). Popular culture has taken up the theme in memoirs, novels and films about the transition from secular to Orthodox life.
