Peace and conflict studies is a social science field that identifies and analyses violent and nonviolent behaviours and the structural mechanisms attending conflicts, with a view towards understanding those processes which lead to a more desirable human condition. Also called peace studies or irenology, it is an interdisciplinary field drawing on political science, sociology, psychology, geography, economics, history, and anthropology.

History

The field took institutional form after the Second World War. Manchester College in Indiana established an early undergraduate peace studies program in 1948. In 1959 Johan Galtung founded the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO), and the Journal of Peace Research began publication in 1964. University programs multiplied during and after the Vietnam War era, and departments such as the University of Bradford's Peace Studies department (founded 1973) became major centres.

Key concepts

Galtung distinguished negative peace (the absence of direct violence) from positive peace (the presence of social justice and the absence of structural violence — harm built into social, political, and economic arrangements). Later scholars such as John Paul Lederach developed conflict transformation, which treats conflict as a normal feature of social life that can be channelled constructively, and multi-track diplomacy, which involves non-state actors in peacebuilding alongside official negotiations.

Applications

Research and teaching in the field inform mediation, peacekeeping, post-conflict reconstruction, transitional justice, nonviolent resistance movements, and disarmament. Critics have debated whether peace studies functions as an objective science or an activist, value-committed enterprise, and whether its methods privilege Western liberal assumptions about peace.
