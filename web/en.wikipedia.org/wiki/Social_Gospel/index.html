Social Gospel

The Social Gospel was a Protestant movement that developed in the United States and Canada in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It applied Christian ethics to social problems, especially issues of social justice such as economic inequality, poverty, alcoholism, crime, racial tensions, slums, child labour, and the danger of war. Its theologians held that the Kingdom of God required collective as well as individual salvation, and that Christians had an obligation to reform society, not merely to save souls one at a time.

History

The movement emerged amid the social upheavals of the Gilded Age, as industrialisation and urbanisation created vast slums in American cities. Ministers such as Washington Gladden began preaching that Christianity must address labour conditions and poverty. The movement reached its theological maturity with Walter Rauschenbusch, a Baptist pastor who worked for eleven years in New York's Hell's Kitchen neighbourhood and later systematised the Social Gospel in Christianity and the Social Crisis (1907) and A Theology for the Social Gospel (1917).

Charles Sheldon's 1896 novel In His Steps, in which a congregation pledges to ask "What would Jesus do?" before every action, popularised Social Gospel ideas for a mass audience; the phrase "What would Jesus do?" (WWJD) derives from it. Social Gospel leaders supported labour unions, public health reform, prohibition, and the settlement house movement exemplified by Jane Addams's Hull House in Chicago.

The movement declined after the First World War, which Rauschenbusch's later writings grappled with, but it left a lasting legacy: it helped inspire the Social Creed of the Churches adopted by the Federal Council of Churches in 1908, influenced the development of the welfare state, and shaped later theologians including Reinhold Niebuhr and Martin Luther King Jr.

Theology

Social Gospel theologians reinterpreted doctrines such as sin and salvation in social terms: sin was embodied in unjust institutions ("social sin"), and salvation required transforming the social order toward the Kingdom of God on earth. Critics, including later neo-orthodox theologians, charged the movement with reducing Christianity to ethics and with an overly optimistic view of human nature and historical progress.
