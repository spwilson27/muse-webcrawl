Galilean transformation


Galilean transformations relate coordinates between inertial frames in Newtonian mechanics.

In physics, a Galilean transformation is used to transform between the coordinates of two reference frames which differ only by constant relative motion within the constructs of Newtonian physics. These transformations, together with spatial rotations and translations in space and time, form the Galilean group. The transformations are named after Galileo Galilei, who articulated the principle of relativity for uniform motion.

Formulation

For two frames S and S′ with S′ moving at velocity v along the x-axis of S, with origins coinciding at t = t′ = 0, the transformation is:

x′ = x − vt,   y′ = y,   z′ = z,   t′ = t

Differentiating gives the Galilean velocity-addition rule, u′ = u − v, and accelerations are identical in both frames, so Newton's laws are invariant under the transformation. Time and simultaneity are absolute: all observers agree on the time interval between events.

Contrast with Lorentz transformations

Galilean transformations fail for electromagnetism: Maxwell's equations predict a constant speed of light, contradicting Galilean velocity addition. Einstein's theory of special relativity replaced them with the Lorentz transformations, in which time and simultaneity are frame-dependent. At speeds much smaller than the speed of light, the Lorentz transformations reduce approximately to the Galilean ones, which is why Galilean relativity remains accurate for everyday phenomena.
