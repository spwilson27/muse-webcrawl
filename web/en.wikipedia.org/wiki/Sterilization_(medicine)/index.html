Sterilization (medicine)

Sterilization (or sterilisation) is any of a number of medical methods of permanent contraception that intentionally leaves a person unable to reproduce. Sterilization procedures are intended to be permanent; reversal is difficult and often unsuccessful. It is one of the most widely used methods of family planning worldwide, with female sterilization the single most common contraceptive method globally.

Methods

For men, vasectomy involves cutting and sealing the vasa deferentia so that sperm cannot enter the semen; it is a brief outpatient procedure performed under local anaesthetic and does not affect hormone production or sexual function. For women, tubal ligation (or occlusion) blocks or cuts the fallopian tubes, preventing eggs from reaching the uterus; it is usually performed laparoscopically or at the time of a caesarean delivery. Removal of the tubes (salpingectomy) has also become more common because it reduces the risk of ovarian cancer.

History and compulsory sterilization

Sterilization has a controversial history as a tool of eugenics. In the 20th century many countries operated compulsory sterilization programmes targeting people with disabilities, ethnic minorities, and the poor: the United States sterilised some 60,000–70,000 people under state laws upheld in Buck v. Bell (1927); Sweden about 63,000 between 1934 and 1975; and Nazi Germany roughly 400,000. Japan's Eugenic Protection Law authorised about 25,000 sterilisations, many without consent, until 1996. Mass campaigns in India (notably during the 1975–77 Emergency) and Peru (1990s) also involved coercion. Forced sterilization is now condemned in international law and prosecuted in some contexts as a crime against humanity.

Effectiveness and risks

Both vasectomy and tubal methods are over 99% effective. Risks are low but include surgical complications and, for tubal procedures, a small chance of ectopic pregnancy if failure occurs. Sterilization does not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
