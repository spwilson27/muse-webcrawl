Psychological hedonism

Psychological hedonism is the descriptive theory of human motivation which holds that all human action is ultimately aimed at increasing one's own pleasure and avoiding one's own pain. It is to be distinguished from ethical hedonism, the normative claim that people ought to pursue pleasure. Psychological hedonism makes a claim about how people actually are, not about how they should behave.

History

Versions of the doctrine appear in ancient philosophy, notably in Epicurus, and in early modern writers such as Thomas Hobbes and John Locke. It was a working assumption of classical utilitarians: Jeremy Bentham's "felicific calculus" presupposed that pleasure and pain govern human conduct, and Henry Sidgwick discussed the theory at length. Psychological egoism — the broader thesis that all action is self-interested — is a close relative, psychological hedonism being the specifically pleasure-centred form of it.

Criticism

The classic objection, pressed by Bishop Joseph Butler in his Fifteen Sermons (1726), is that pleasure normally results from satisfying a desire for something other than pleasure itself — food, knowledge, another's welfare — so those desires cannot all be desires for pleasure. Critics also charge that the theory can be rendered unfalsifiable: if every apparently self-sacrificing act is redescribed as secretly pleasure-seeking, the claim becomes empty. Joel Feinberg's twentieth-century analysis of the concept remains a standard treatment. Empirical psychology offers mixed evidence, finding genuinely other-regarding motivation alongside hedonic motives.
