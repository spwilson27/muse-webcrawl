Populism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Populism is a contested political concept describing a range of approaches that emphasise the role of "the people" and often juxtapose them against "the elite". In the most widely used scholarly definition (Cas Mudde), populism is a "thin-centred ideology" that considers society to be separated into two homogeneous and antagonistic camps, "the pure people" versus "the corrupt elite", and argues that politics should be an expression of the general will of the people. Because it is "thin", populism attaches to fuller ideologies of left or right — hence left-wing, right-wing and centrist populisms.

History

The term derives from the Russian Narodniks and the American People's Party of the 1890s, which mobilised farmers against banks and railroads. Twentieth-century Latin American leaders such as Juan Perón built durable populist movements combining mass mobilisation with charismatic leadership. Since the 1980s–2000s, populist parties have grown across Europe, the Americas and Asia, fuelled by economic insecurity, immigration debates, distrust of institutions and media change.

Features and debates

Common features include claims to speak for the "real" people, hostility to intermediary institutions (parties, courts, media, experts), plebiscitary styles of leadership, and simple solutions framed as common sense. Scholars debate whether populism is a pathology of democracy or a corrective to unresponsive elites; critics warn that populist governments may erode checks and balances and minority rights, while other analysts note that populists have also expanded political participation and forced neglected issues onto the agenda. Ernesto Laclau treated populism as the very logic of politics; Jan-Werner Müller emphasises anti-pluralism — the claim to exclusive moral representation of the people — as its defining danger to democracy.
