Signifier (linguistics)

In linguistics and semiotics, a signifier (French: signifiant) is the form of a sign — the sound pattern, written marks or other perceptible vehicle — as opposed to the signified (signifié), the concept it represents. The pairing of signifier and signified constitutes the linguistic sign in the theory of Ferdinand de Saussure, set out in his posthumously published Course in General Linguistics (1916). Saussure insisted that the link between the two is in principle arbitrary: nothing in the sound sequence of, say, the English word tree naturally connects it to the concept of a tree, as the diversity of languages shows.

Saussure's model

Saussure compared the sign to a sheet of paper: thought is the front and sound the back; one cannot cut the front without cutting the back. Importantly, the signifier for Saussure is not the physical sound itself but the psychological "sound-image" (the mental impression it leaves), and the signified is likewise a concept, not the object in the world. Because signs are arbitrary, their value within a language comes from their differences from other signs — the famous claim that "in language there are only differences". Onomatopoeia and interjections are marginal partial exceptions that do not overturn the principle of arbitrariness.

Later developments

The signifier/signified distinction became foundational for structuralism and semiotics. Louis Hjelmslev reformulated it as the planes of expression and content. Jacques Lacan radically elevated the signifier in psychoanalysis, arguing that the signifier has primacy over the signified, that meaning "slides" beneath the signifying chain, and that "the unconscious is structured like a language". Jacques Derrida criticised the stability of the pairing, coining différance to stress that meaning is endlessly deferred through chains of signifiers. The term "floating signifier" (from Lévi-Strauss) denotes a signifier whose signified is indeterminate or overloaded, such as the concept of mana.
