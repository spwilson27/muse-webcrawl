Viceroy of India

The Viceroy of India, formally the Governor-General of India, was the representative of the monarch of the United Kingdom in British India from 1858 to 1947. The office was created in 1773 as the Governor-General of Bengal, and its holder controlled British India as the representative of the British Crown after the Government of India Act 1858 transferred administration from the East India Company to the Crown.

The title "Viceroy" was not used in statutes but was the customary style of the Governor-General when acting as the Crown's direct representative to the princely states. The first Viceroy was Lord Canning, and the last was Lord Mountbatten, who oversaw the partition of British India in 1947 and briefly served as Governor-General of the independent Dominion of India.

History

Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General of Bengal in 1774 under the Regulating Act 1773. The Charter Act 1833 made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India, a title first held by Lord William Bentinck. Following the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the East India Company's territories were placed under the direct control of the Crown, and the Governor-General additionally received the title of Viceroy.

Powers and role

The Viceroy governed with the assistance of an Executive Council, administered British provinces, and conducted relations with more than 500 princely states. He was advised by the Secretary of State for India in London and accountable to the British Parliament. The Viceroy's residences included Government House in Calcutta, the Viceregal Lodge in Shimla, and from 1929 the Viceroy's House in New Delhi, now the Rashtrapati Bhavan.

Notable viceroys

Notable holders include Lord Dalhousie, architect of expansion and the Doctrine of Lapse; Lord Curzon, who partitioned Bengal in 1905; Lord Irwin, who negotiated the Gandhi–Irwin Pact; and Lord Mountbatten, the last Viceroy, who presided over the transfer of power in August 1947.
