Regular graph

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Regular graphs of degrees 0, 1, 2 and 3

In graph theory, a regular graph is a graph where each vertex has the same number of neighbours; that is, every vertex has the same degree. A regular graph with vertices of degree k is called a k-regular graph or regular graph of degree k. Regular graphs of degree at most 2 are easy to classify: a 0-regular graph consists of disconnected vertices, a 1-regular graph consists of disconnected edges, and a 2-regular graph consists of a disjoint union of cycles.

Examples

3-regular graphs are called cubic graphs; famous examples include the utility graph K3,3, the Petersen graph, the cube graph and the complete graph K4. Complete graphs Kn are (n−1)-regular, and complete bipartite graphs Kn,n are n-regular. The platonic solids all correspond to regular graphs: the tetrahedron (K4), cube and octahedron (3- and 4-regular), and the dodecahedron and icosahedron (3- and 5-regular).

Properties

By the handshaking lemma, a k-regular graph with k odd must have an even number of vertices. A regular graph is necessarily connected if it is to be studied as an expander; a family of k-regular graphs whose spectral gap remains bounded away from zero forms an expander family, with applications in computer science. A strongly regular graph is a regular graph in which the number of common neighbours of two vertices depends only on whether they are adjacent — examples include the Petersen graph and the Shrikhande graph. Algebraic graph theory relates regularity to the spectrum of the adjacency matrix: a connected graph is regular if and only if its largest eigenvalue equals its average degree. Random regular graphs are a standard model in combinatorics and network theory, and almost all k-regular graphs (for fixed k ≥ 3) are connected and have good expansion properties.
