Doomsday cult

A doomsday cult is a cult that believes in apocalypticism and millenarianism, and can refer both to groups that prophesy catastrophe and destruction, and to those that attempt to bring it about. The term, first used in the mid-20th century, gained currency after studies of millenarian movements and after mass-casualty events such as the 1978 Jonestown mass murder-suicide. Scholars of religion often prefer more neutral language such as "millenarian movement," since "doomsday cult" is frequently used pejoratively.

Characteristics

Doomsday groups typically centre on a charismatic leader who claims special revelation of an impending transformation of the world — through divine judgment, extraterrestrial intervention, or cosmic catastrophe — and teach that salvation comes only to committed members. Common features include date-setting for the end, communal and totalistic living, and reinterpretation of prophecy when predicted dates pass.

Notable groups

  • Millerites — followers of William Miller who expected Christ's return in 1844; the failure became known as the Great Disappointment, and the movement gave rise to Seventh-day Adventism.
  • Peoples Temple — led by Jim Jones; more than 900 members died at Jonestown, Guyana, in 1978.
  • Branch Davidians — the 1993 Waco siege ended in fire and the deaths of leader David Koresh and 75 others.
  • Aum Shinrikyo — Japanese movement led by Shoko Asahara that carried out the 1995 Tokyo subway sarin attack.
  • Heaven's Gate — 39 members died by suicide in California in 1997, believing they would ascend to a spacecraft following Comet Hale–Bopp.
  • Order of the Solar Temple — deaths in Switzerland, France, and Canada in 1994–1997.
  • Movement for the Restoration of the Ten Commandments of God — mass deaths in Uganda in 2000.

Academic study

Leon Festinger's When Prophecy Fails (1956) studied a small UFO millenarian group and introduced the concept of cognitive dissonance to explain how disconfirmation can intensify belief. Sociologists caution that most apocalyptic groups are not violent, and that violence often emerges from interaction between insular groups and outside pressure.
