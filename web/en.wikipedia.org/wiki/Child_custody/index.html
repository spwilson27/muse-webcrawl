Child custody

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Child custody is a legal term regarding guardianship: the rights and duties of parents (or others) to care for a child — divided analytically into legal custody (decision-making over schooling, health and religion) and physical custody (with whom the child lives). Courts award custody — sole or joint/shared in various combinations — on the best interests of the child standard, applying statutory factor lists (the child's wishes by age and maturity, continuity of care, each parent's capacity, any history of violence or abuse).

Doctrine has cycled: the nineteenth-century paternal presumption gave way to the “tender years” maternal presumption, then to gender-neutral best-interests analysis and, in many jurisdictions, a presumption favouring joint legal custody and substantial shared time. International abduction by a parent is governed by the Hague Child Abduction Convention (1980, more than 100 parties), which orders prompt return to the child's habitual residence for custody to be decided there, subject to narrow exceptions (grave risk). Terminology is shifting from “custody/access” to “parenting time/parenting orders” (Canada, Australia) to reduce win-lose framing; enforcement across borders and the voice of the child (UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, Article 12) remain live reform fronts.
