Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952), also known as the Steel Seizure Case, is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision limiting presidential power. The Court held that President Harry S. Truman's executive order seizing the nation's steel mills during the Korean War exceeded his constitutional authority.

Background

Facing a nationwide steelworkers' strike in April 1952 that he argued would jeopardise war production, Truman issued Executive Order 10340 directing Secretary of Commerce Charles Sawyer to take possession of the mills and keep them operating. The steel companies sued, and the case reached the Supreme Court within weeks.

Decision

Justice Hugo Black's majority opinion held that presidential power must stem from an act of Congress or the Constitution itself, and neither authorised the seizure. Justice Robert H. Jackson's concurrence — classifying presidential action into three categories according to its relation to Congress's will — became the case's most influential legacy and the standard framework for analysing executive power.

Aftermath

The mills were returned; a strike followed and was settled after seven weeks. The case remains a cornerstone precedent on separation of powers, cited in later disputes over executive authority.
