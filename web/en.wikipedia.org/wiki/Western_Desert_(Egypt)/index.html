Western Desert (Egypt)

The Western Desert of Egypt is the portion of the Sahara lying west of the Nile, extending from the Mediterranean coast south to the Sudan border and west to Libya. It covers roughly 680,000 square kilometres — about two-thirds of Egypt's land area — yet is mostly uninhabited except for a chain of oases.

Geography

The desert is a vast plateau of sandstone and limestone, broken by depressions that hold the oases of Siwa, Bahariya, Farafra, Dakhla and Kharga, watered by the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer. Features include the Qattara Depression, one of the lowest points in Africa at about 133 metres below sea level, the Great Sand Sea near the Libyan border, and the chalk formations of the White Desert and the darker volcanic hills of the Black Desert.

History

The oases were inhabited from prehistoric times and were important under the pharaohs and Romans. In the Second World War the Western Desert was the theatre of the North Africa campaign, with battles at El Alamein in 1942 marking a turning point. Today the government promotes land reclamation and settlement in projects such as the New Valley.
