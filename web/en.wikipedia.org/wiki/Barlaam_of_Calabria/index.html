Barlaam of Calabria

Barlaam of Calabria
Born: c. 1290, Seminara, Calabria
Died: c. 1348, Gerace or Avignon
Known for: opponent of hesychasm; teacher of Petrarch and Boccaccio

Barlaam of Calabria (c. 1290 – c. 1348), also known as Barlaam the Calabrian, was a Greek-Italian monk, theologian, and scholar of the Basilian Order. He is best known as the principal opponent of Gregory Palamas in the hesychast controversy that divided the Byzantine Empire in the 1330s and 1340s, and as an early conduit of Greek learning to Renaissance Italy, teaching Greek to Petrarch and Boccaccio.

Life and theology

Born Bernardo Massari in Seminara in Calabria, in the Greek-speaking south of Italy, Barlaam traveled to Constantinople around 1330 and quickly won a reputation as a philosopher and astronomer, writing treatises on logic, mathematics, and the calendar. He first came to prominence defending Orthodox positions against Latin theology, writing against the Filioque, but his rationalist, scholastic method led him into conflict with the hesychast monks of Mount Athos. Barlaam mocked their prayer practice—including the claim that practitioners could see the uncreated light of Tabor—and argued that knowledge of God could be attained through philosophical demonstration. Gregory Palamas replied defending the distinction between God's essence and energies, and synods at Constantinople in 1341 initially favored a compromise before later councils (1347, 1351) vindicated Palamas and condemned Barlaam's positions.

Later life

Discredited in Byzantium, Barlaam returned to Italy, converted to Roman Catholicism, and was made bishop of Gerace in 1342. At Avignon and in Italy he taught Greek to Petrarch and later instructed Boccaccio, earning a place in the prehistory of Renaissance humanism; Petrarch's Greek studies with him were limited, but the contact symbolized the revival of Greek letters in the West. He died around 1348, possibly of plague.
