Virgin birth of Jesus

The virgin birth of Jesus is the Christian doctrine that Jesus was conceived by his mother, Mary, through the power of the Holy Spirit and without sexual intercourse. It is taught in the gospels of Matthew and Luke and is affirmed in the early Christian creeds — the Apostles' Creed professes Jesus "conceived by the Holy Spirit, born of the Virgin Mary" — and it remains doctrine in Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and most Protestant churches. The corresponding Islamic teaching also affirms the miraculous conception of Jesus (ʿĪsā) by Maryam (Mary), as related in the Qur'an.

Biblical accounts

Matthew 1 relates that Mary was found to be pregnant "through the Holy Spirit" before she and Joseph came together, citing Isaiah 7:14 (in its Greek Septuagint form, where Hebrew ʿalmāh "young woman" is rendered parthenos, "virgin") as fulfilled prophecy. Luke 1 describes the Annunciation: the angel Gabriel tells Mary she will conceive, and when she asks how, since she "knows no man", he answers that the Holy Spirit will come upon her. The doctrine is absent from Mark, John, and the letters of Paul, and most critical scholars regard the infancy narratives as theological rather than historical accounts; Christians differ over how the doctrine should be interpreted, though it is a formal dogma for Catholics, who additionally teach Mary's perpetual virginity.

Theological significance

Traditionally, the virgin birth signifies Jesus's divine sonship and freedom from original sin, and marks his entry into the world as an act of God's initiative rather than human generation. It is distinct from the doctrine of the Immaculate Conception (which concerns Mary's own conception) — a common confusion. In Islam, the miraculous birth affirms Jesus's status as a prophet and a sign from God, while denying his divinity.

Historical and scholarly views

Historians note parallels with other miraculous birth traditions of the ancient world, and debate whether the doctrine developed from scriptural interpretation of Isaiah 7:14. Opinion among scholars ranges across acceptance of the narratives as legend, midrash, or theological biography; churches continue to confess the doctrine liturgically at Christmas and in the creeds.
