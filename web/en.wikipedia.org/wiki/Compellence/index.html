Compellence

Compellence is a form of coercion that attempts to get an actor (such as a state) to change its behavior through threats of force, the limited use of force, or both. It is the strategic counterpart of deterrence: whereas deterrence seeks to prevent an action from being taken, compellence seeks to force an actor to take an action, to stop an action already under way, or to undo an action already completed.

The concept was developed most influentially by the American strategist and Nobel laureate Thomas Schelling in his works The Strategy of Conflict (1960) and Arms and Influence (1966). Schelling distinguished compellence from deterrence by its timing and initiative: the compeller takes the initiative and sets a deadline, and the punishment continues until the target complies, so the target must visibly act.

Characteristics

Compellent threats are generally considered harder to make credible and successful than deterrent threats. The target of compellence must publicly change its behaviour, which can involve loss of face, and the compeller must convince the target both that punishment will continue or escalate until compliance, and that compliance will end the punishment. Alexander George developed the related concept of "coercive diplomacy", identifying variants such as explicit ultimatums, gradual turning of the screw, and "try-and-see" approaches.

Examples

Historical cases often analysed as compellence include the American demand during the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 that the Soviet Union withdraw its nuclear missiles from Cuba, backed by a naval quarantine and threats of escalation, and NATO's 1999 air campaign against Yugoslavia during the Kosovo War, intended to force Slobodan Milošević to accept terms regarding Kosovo.

Success and failure

Empirical research on coercive strategies finds that compellence succeeds only in a minority of cases, particularly when demands are clear and limited, the threat of escalation is credible, and the target is offered assurances and a face-saving way out. Robert Pape's work on air power argued that compellence by punishment alone rarely works, while strategies of denial – undermining the target's ability to achieve its military aims – are more effective.
