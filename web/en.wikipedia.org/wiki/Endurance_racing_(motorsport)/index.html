Endurance racing (motorsport)

Endurance racing is a form of motorsport racing in which teams of drivers compete in long-distance races intended to test both the durability of the equipment and the stamina of the participants. Races last from several hours to a full day, and teams typically consist of two to four drivers per car who take turns behind the wheel during pit stops. The most famous events are the 24 Hours of Le Mans (first held in 1923), the 24 Hours of Daytona, the 12 Hours of Sebring and the 24 Hours Nürburgring, which together anchor championships such as the FIA World Endurance Championship (WEC) and the IMSA SportsCar Championship.

Multi-class racing through the night is a defining feature of endurance events.

Format

Unlike most circuit racing, which is run over a fixed distance, endurance races are usually run for a set length of time, with victory going to the car that covers the greatest distance. Multiple classes — in top-level competition, prototypes (such as Le Mans Hypercars and LMDh cars) alongside production-based GT cars — share the circuit simultaneously, so managing traffic is a core skill. Pit stops involve refuelling, tyre changes and driver swaps, and strategy must balance pace against reliability over many hours, including long periods of night running.

History

Long-distance racing dates to the earliest days of the automobile: city-to-city races such as Paris–Rouen (1894) and endurance tests on the Brooklands and Monza circuits. Le Mans, organised by the Automobile Club de l'Ouest from 1923, became the discipline's flagship. The mid-twentieth century saw the World Sportscar Championship and legendary machinery from Ferrari, Ford (whose GT40 won Le Mans four straight years, 1966–69), and Porsche, whose 917 and 935 dominated the 1970s. The Group C era of the 1980s and the diesel and hybrid prototype eras of the 2000s–2010s, dominated by Audi, Porsche and Toyota, emphasised fuel efficiency and advanced hybrid technology.

Triple Crown

Winning Le Mans, Daytona and Sebring in a career is informally known as the Triple Crown of endurance racing; only a handful of drivers, including Phil Hill, have won all three overall. Hurley Haywood, with five Daytona wins, three Le Mans wins and two Sebring wins, is among the most successful endurance drivers in history, while Tom Kristensen holds the record of nine overall Le Mans victories.
