Heinrich Friedrich Emil Lenz (12 February 1804 – 10 February 1865) was a Baltic German physicist working in the Russian Empire, best known for formulating Lenz's law in electrodynamics in 1834.

Life

Lenz was born in Dorpat (now Tartu) and studied at the University of Dorpat. As a young man he served as a physicist on Otto von Kotzebue's third circumnavigation of the globe (1823–1826), studying the physical properties of seawater. In 1828 he moved to St Petersburg, where he became a member of the Imperial Academy of Sciences and later professor of physics at the University of St Petersburg, serving also as its dean and rector.

Scientific work

In 1833–34 Lenz published his rule determining the direction of induced currents: an induced current flows in a direction such that it opposes the change that produced it — a statement of the conservation of energy for electromagnetic induction, now known as Lenz's law and represented by the negative sign in Faraday's law of induction. Working independently of James Prescott Joule, he also established in 1842 that the heat produced by an electric current is proportional to the square of the current, the resistance and the time (the Joule–Lenz law). His experiments on the temperature dependence of electrical resistance and on electromagnetic machines were among the most precise of their era.
