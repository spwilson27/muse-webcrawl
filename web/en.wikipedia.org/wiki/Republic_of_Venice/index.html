The Republic of Venice, traditionally known as La Serenìssima ("the Most Serene"), was a sovereign state and maritime republic in northeastern Italy, centred on the lagoon city of Venice. It existed for roughly eleven centuries, from the late 7th century — when tradition dates the election of the first Doge, Paolo Lucio Anafesto, in 697 — until 1797, when it was dissolved by Napoleon Bonaparte during the French Revolutionary Wars. For much of the medieval and Renaissance periods Venice was one of the great powers of Europe: a leading naval and commercial empire, a hinge of trade between Western Europe and the Byzantine and Islamic worlds, and a famously stable oligarchic republic governed by its hereditary patriciate.

History

Origins and early growth

Venetian tradition traced the city's origins to refugees fleeing the Lombard invasions of mainland Italy in the 6th and 7th centuries, who settled on the islands of the Venetian Lagoon. Nominally subject to the Byzantine exarchate of Ravenna, the lagoon communities elected their own leader, the Doge. In 810 the seat of government moved to the Rialto islands — the nucleus of modern Venice — after a Frankish attack demonstrated the lagoon's defensive strength. Over the following centuries Venice exploited its position to win trading privileges in the Byzantine Empire, notably the chrysobull of 1082, and grew rich carrying goods — and crusaders — between Europe and the Levant.

The maritime empire

The republic's power peaked after the Fourth Crusade (1202–1204), which Venetian ships transported and Venetian diplomacy diverted against Constantinople. In the partition of the Byzantine Empire Venice acquired a string of ports and islands — including, eventually, Crete and later Cyprus — the Stato da Màr, which guarded its trade routes across the Adriatic and eastern Mediterranean. Rivalry with Genoa produced a long series of wars, ending in effective Venetian ascendancy after the War of Chioggia (1378–1381). In the 15th century Venice also built a mainland dominion, the Terraferma, reaching toward Milan and encompassing cities such as Padua, Verona and Brescia.

Decline and fall

From the late 15th century the republic's fortunes waned. Portugal's sea route to India undermined Venice's spice monopoly; the expanding Ottoman Empire stripped away its eastern possessions in a succession of wars, Crete falling in 1669 and the Morea finally in 1718; and Atlantic powers — Spain, the Netherlands, England — eclipsed its carrying trade. The republic survived the 18th century as a wealthy but militarily secondary state, celebrated for its art, music and carnival. In 1797, invaded by Napoleon's armies, the Great Council voted the republic out of existence; the last Doge, Ludovico Manin, abdicated, and by the Treaty of Campo-Formio Venice was handed to Austria.

Government

Venice's constitution was a carefully balanced oligarchy designed to prevent any individual or family from seizing power. The Doge was elected for life by an extraordinarily elaborate procedure of alternating sortition and election, but his powers were tightly circumscribed by his coronation oath. Sovereignty formally resided in the Great Council (Maggior Consiglio), the assembly of all adult male patricians — membership of which was closed and made hereditary in the Serrata ("Locking") of 1297. The Great Council elected the Senate, which directed foreign policy, war and finance; the Council of Ten, created in 1310 after a conspiracy, became a powerful committee for state security; and a dense web of magistracies administered justice, trade, health and the fleet. Contemporaries across Europe admired the system's stability: Venice passed eleven centuries without successful revolution or civil war.

Economy and trade

Trade was the republic's lifeblood. Venetian galleys, sailing in state-organised convoys (mude), linked the Rialto's markets with Constantinople, Alexandria, Beirut, Flanders and England. The city imported spices, silk, grain and slaves, and exported manufactures of its own — Murano glass, woollen and silk cloth, soap, and printed books. The state Arsenal, among the largest industrial enterprises of the pre-modern world, could assemble a war galley in a day using standardised parts, and the gold ducat (or sequin), minted unchanged in weight and purity from 1284 to 1797, was a byword for monetary reliability across the Mediterranean.

Culture

Renaissance Venice was a centre of painting — Giovanni Bellini, Titian, Tintoretto, Veronese — of architecture, from the Byzantine domes of St Mark's Basilica to the classical villas of Palladio on the mainland, and of music, printing and scholarship. Its symbols remain instantly recognisable: the winged lion of St Mark, the Doge's Palace, the gondola, and the carnival mask.
