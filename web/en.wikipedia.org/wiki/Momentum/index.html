Momentum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of two colliding billiard balls exchanging momentum vectors]
Momentum is transferred, never created or destroyed, in collisions.

In Newtonian mechanics, momentum (more specifically linear momentum or translational momentum) is the product of the mass and velocity of an object, p = mv. It is a vector quantity, possessing a magnitude and a direction, measured in kilogram-metres per second. Newton's second law of motion states that the rate of change of momentum of a body is equal to the net force acting on it.

Momentum is conserved in any isolated system — a consequence (by Noether's theorem) of the homogeneity of space — making it one of physics' great bookkeeping quantities: collisions, rocket propulsion (the Tsiolkovsky equation), recoil and orbital mechanics are all momentum accounts. In relativity, momentum becomes part of the four-momentum (E/c, p), conserved as a whole with energy; in quantum mechanics, momentum is an operator (−iħ∇) whose relation to position gives the Uncertainty principle, and the de Broglie wavelength of a particle is λ = h/p (Matter wave). Photons, though massless, carry momentum E/c — measured in radiation pressure and exploited by solar sails.
