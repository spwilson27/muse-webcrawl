A converso (Spanish: [komˈbeɾso]; plural conversos) was a Jew in Spain or Portugal (or a descendant of such people) who converted to Catholicism, under compulsion or for survival, during the 14th and 15th centuries.

History

Anti-Jewish pogroms in 1391 swept Castile and Aragon, forcing mass conversions. Further waves followed the Alhambra Decree of 1492 expelling Jews from Spain, and Portugal's forced conversion of 1497. Converts were called "New Christians" to distinguish them from "Old Christians."

Persecution

Conversos faced discrimination under limpieza de sangre ("purity of blood") statutes and investigation by the Spanish Inquisition (established 1478), which suspected many of secretly practising Judaism.

Crypto-Judaism

Some conversos — called marranos by contemporaries — maintained Jewish practices in secret. Crypto-Jewish communities survived for centuries in Spain, Portugal, and Latin America.

Legacy

Conversos and their descendants included prominent officials, merchants, and mystics, among them Saint Teresa of Ávila, whose grandfather was a converso penanced by the Inquisition.
