Charles Goodyear

Charles Goodyear (December 29, 1800 – July 1, 1860) was an American self-taught chemist and manufacturing engineer who developed vulcanized rubber, for which he received patent number 3633 from the United States Patent Office on June 15, 1844. His discovery made rubber a practical industrial material and laid the foundation of the modern rubber industry.

Early life

Goodyear was born in New Haven, Connecticut, and joined his father's hardware business, which failed in 1830. Fascinated by the commercial potential of natural rubber — a material that melted in summer heat and cracked in winter cold — he spent years experimenting to make it stable, enduring poverty and spells in debtors' prison.

Vulcanization

In 1839, at Woburn, Massachusetts, Goodyear discovered that heating rubber mixed with sulfur produced a material that retained its elasticity across temperatures; according to tradition the discovery followed an accidental spill onto a hot stove. He patented the process in 1844. In Britain, Thomas Hancock obtained a similar patent weeks before Goodyear's process was filed there, having examined samples of Goodyear's rubber; litigation over priority and infringement consumed much of Goodyear's later life and fortune.

Later life and legacy

Despite the enormous value of his invention, Goodyear died heavily in debt in 1860. The Goodyear Tire and Rubber Company, founded in 1898 in Akron, Ohio, was named in his honour but had no connection with him or his family. Goodyear was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1976.
