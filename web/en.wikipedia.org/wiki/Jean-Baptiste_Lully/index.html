Jean-Baptiste Lully (born Giovanni Battista Lulli; 28 November 1632 – 22 March 1687) was an Italian-born French composer, instrumentalist, and dancer who is considered a master of the French Baroque style. Renowned in his time as a ballet dancer, he became famous as the founder of French opera and a central figure in the musical life of the court of Louis XIV.

Life and career

Born in Florence, Lully moved to France in 1646 as a servant to Roger du Plessis, entering the service of the young Louis XIV. A talented violinist and dancer, he joined the king's grande bande of 24 violins and composed music for the elaborate court ballets in which the king himself danced.

In 1653 Louis XIV named him composer of instrumental music. Lully collaborated with Molière on a series of comédies-ballets, including Le Bourgeois gentilhomme (1670). In 1672 he purchased the opera privilege from Pierre Perrin and founded the Académie Royale de Musique, the institution that became the Paris Opera.

Operas

Lully created the tragédie en musique, the distinctively French form of opera, setting librettos by Philippe Quinault. Major works include Cadmus et Hermione (1673), Alceste (1674), Atys (1676), Isis (1677), Proserpine (1680), Persée (1682), Phaëton (1683), Amadis (1684), Roland (1685), and Armide (1686).

Death and legacy

Lully died in Paris on 22 March 1687 of gangrene, the result of striking his foot with his conducting staff while beating time during a performance of his Te Deum. His French overture form and orchestral discipline influenced composers across Europe, and his operas dominated the Paris stage for a century.
