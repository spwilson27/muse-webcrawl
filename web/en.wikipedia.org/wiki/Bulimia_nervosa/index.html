Bulimia nervosa, also known simply as bulimia, is an eating disorder characterised by binge eating followed by purging or other methods intended to avoid weight gain. Binge eating refers to consuming a large amount of food in a short period with a sense of loss of control; purging most often means self-induced vomiting, but compensatory behaviours also include misuse of laxatives or diuretics, fasting, and excessive exercise. The condition was described and named by the British psychiatrist Gerald Russell in 1979.

Signs and symptoms

Unlike anorexia nervosa, people with bulimia are usually of normal weight or overweight, which can make the disorder harder to detect. Physical signs include erosion of tooth enamel from stomach acid, swollen salivary (parotid) glands, calluses on the knuckles from inducing vomiting (Russell's sign), and sore throat. Repeated vomiting can cause dangerous electrolyte disturbances — especially low potassium — which may lead to cardiac arrhythmia and, rarely, death. Gastrointestinal problems, dehydration, and kidney injury may also occur.

Causes

No single cause is known. Twin studies indicate a substantial genetic contribution, and risk is increased by a family history of eating disorders, dieting, body dissatisfaction, low self-esteem, perfectionism, and cultural pressures idealising thinness. Bulimia frequently co-occurs with anxiety disorders, depression, and substance use disorders.

Diagnosis

Under DSM-5 criteria, diagnosis requires recurrent episodes of binge eating and recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviours, both occurring on average at least once a week for three months, with self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight, and not occurring exclusively during episodes of anorexia nervosa. Medical assessment typically includes blood tests for electrolytes and dental examination.

Treatment

The first-line treatment is a form of cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for eating disorders (CBT-E), which addresses the cycle of dietary restraint, bingeing, and purging and the overvaluation of shape and weight. Interpersonal psychotherapy is an alternative. The antidepressant fluoxetine, at higher doses than used for depression, is approved in some countries as an adjunct and can reduce binge-purge frequency. Nutritional rehabilitation and monitoring of physical health are integral parts of care.

Prognosis and epidemiology

Bulimia most often begins in adolescence or young adulthood and is more commonly diagnosed in women, though it occurs in men as well. Lifetime prevalence estimates are around 1 percent in many Western populations, with higher rates of subthreshold symptoms. With treatment, many people recover fully; the course can be relapsing, and mortality, while lower than in anorexia nervosa, is elevated compared with the general population.
