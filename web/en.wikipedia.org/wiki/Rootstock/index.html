Rootstock

A rootstock is the lower portion of a grafted plant — the root system and lower stem — onto which the bud or shoot (the scion) of a chosen variety is joined. Grafting separates the functions of the plant: the scion determines the fruit or flower characteristics, while the rootstock is selected for disease resistance, vigour control, soil and climate adaptation, and precocity.

Uses

Rootstocks are fundamental to modern fruit growing. In viticulture, most of the world's wine and table grapes are grafted onto rootstocks derived from American Vitis species resistant to the root aphid phylloxera, which devastated European vineyards in the nineteenth century. Apple growers choose from series such as the Malling (M) and Malling-Merton rootstocks, which range from extremely dwarfing (M27) to vigorous (M25), controlling orchard density and tree size; citrus, stone fruit, roses and tomatoes are likewise grafted onto selected stocks.

Selection criteria

Breeders evaluate rootstocks for compatibility with scions, resistance to viruses, bacteria, fungi and nematodes, tolerance of drought, salinity, lime or waterlogging, and effects on fruit quality and yield. A rootstock can also influence flowering time and cold hardiness. Incompatible combinations may fail at the union years after planting.
