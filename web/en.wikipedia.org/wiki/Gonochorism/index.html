Gonochorism

In biology, gonochorism (from Greek gone, "offspring" + chorizein, "to separate, sever") is the state of having just one of at least two distinct sexes in any one individual organism. The term is most often used with animals, in which the individuals are of distinct sexes — that is, each individual is either male or female for its entire life. The equivalent botanical term is dioecy (dioecious plants). Gonochorism contrasts with hermaphroditism, in which an individual produces both male and female gametes, either simultaneously or sequentially.

Distribution

Gonochorism is the ancestral and most common reproductive condition in animals: the great majority of animal species, including most vertebrates, arthropods, molluscs and echinoderms, have separate sexes. It is thought to have evolved in multicellular organisms as a way of avoiding the costs of self-fertilisation and of enabling specialisation of the two sexes. In gonochoric species an individual's sex may be fixed genetically — for example by XY or ZW sex-determination systems — or, in some fishes and reptiles, influenced or determined by environmental factors such as temperature.

Terminology

The term was originally introduced in the 19th century (it is associated with Ernst Haeckel) for unisexual species in which each individual belongs to one sex. Zoologists use "gonochoristic" (or "gonochoric") and "dioecious" interchangeably for animals, while botanists reserve "dioecious" for plants and "monoecious" for plants with separate male and female flowers on the same individual.

Evolution

Transitions between gonochorism and hermaphroditism have occurred many times in animal evolution. Hermaphroditism is favoured where mates are scarce or mobility is low, while gonochorism can be favoured where inbreeding depression is severe or where the sexes benefit from divergent specialisation. Some species depart from strict gonochorism, for example through sequential hermaphroditism (protandry and protogyny) in certain fishes, or occasional intersex individuals.
