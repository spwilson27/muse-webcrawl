Defensor pacis

[Image: Marsilius of Padua]
Marsilius of Padua, author of the Defensor pacis (1324)

The Defensor pacis (Defender of the Peace) is a treatise on political philosophy by Marsilius of Padua, completed in Paris in 1324. It is one of the most original political works of the Middle Ages, arguing that the source of all political authority is the people (the legislator humanus) and that the universal claims of the papacy — in particular the doctrine of plenitude of power — were the chief cause of strife in Christian society.

Contents

The work is divided into three discourses. The first, based on Aristotle's Politics, argues that the state exists for the sake of the good life and that laws derive their coercive authority from the whole body of citizens or its weightier part. The second, based on Scripture, denies that the clergy possess any coercive power: Christ excluded the apostles from worldly rule, priests may teach and administer the sacraments but may not compel; excommunication and the interpretation of Scripture belong to the whole community of the faithful, acting through a general council in which laymen may sit. The third discourse summarizes the conclusions. Marsilius also held that clerics should be subject to secular courts and that church property could be taxed by the state.

Context and influence

The treatise served the cause of the emperor Louis IV of Bavaria in his conflict with Pope John XXII, who condemned the work in 1327; Marsilius fled to Louis's court and accompanied him to Italy. The Defensor pacis became a foundational text for conciliarism, was used by later critics of papal power, was translated into English in 1535 under Thomas Cromwell to support the Royal Supremacy of Henry VIII, and is read today as an early statement of popular sovereignty and the separation of spiritual from coercive authority.
