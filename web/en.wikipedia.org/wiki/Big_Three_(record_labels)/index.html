The "Big Three" record labels are the three major music recording and distribution companies that dominate the global recorded music industry: Universal Music Group (UMG), Sony Music Entertainment, and Warner Music Group (WMG). Together they control roughly 70 percent of the world market for recorded music and a comparable share of music publishing through their publishing arms.

History

For most of the late twentieth century the industry was dominated by a larger set of majors — at various times called the "Big Six" — including EMI, PolyGram, CBS Records, MCA, and WEA. Consolidation reduced the group: Sony acquired CBS Records in 1988, PolyGram was absorbed into Universal in 1998, and Sony and BMG merged their recorded-music operations in 2004. The result was a "Big Four": Universal, Sony, Warner, and EMI.

The Big Four became the Big Three in 2012, when Universal acquired EMI's recorded-music division for £1.2 billion, following regulatory approvals that required UMG to divest certain assets, including the Parlophone label group. Sony separately acquired EMI's music publishing business. Since then the structure of the industry has remained stable, with the three groups signing most internationally successful artists and controlling the largest catalogues of master recordings.

Market position

Universal is the largest of the three, typically holding a global market share above 30 percent, followed by Sony and then Warner. The rise of streaming in the 2010s initially threatened the majors' position, but licensing agreements with services such as Spotify and Apple Music entrenched their importance, since the platforms require access to the majors' catalogues. The Big Three also collectively hold significant equity and licensing influence within the streaming economy.

Criticism

The concentration of the industry in three companies has drawn criticism over artist contract terms, the treatment of back catalogues, and the majors' influence over playlisting and chart rules. Independent label organizations have repeatedly challenged further mergers before competition authorities in the United States and the European Union.
