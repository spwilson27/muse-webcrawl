Mal de débarquement

Mal de débarquement syndrome
SpecialtyNeurology, otolaryngology
SymptomsPersistent sensation of rocking, swaying or bobbing
Usual onsetAfter disembarking from a boat or other passive motion
AcronymMdDS

Mal de débarquement syndrome (or syndrome du mal de débarquement, MdDS, literally "sickness of disembarkation") is a neurological condition usually occurring after a cruise, aircraft flight, or other sustained motion event. It is characterized by a persistent false sensation of motion — rocking, swaying or bobbing — that continues after the person has returned to solid ground. The phrase was originally used for the brief, normal phenomenon of "sea legs"; the syndrome refers to cases in which the sensation persists for a month or more.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin shortly after disembarking and characteristically ease, temporarily, when the person is again in passive motion, such as driving a car. Many patients report associated cognitive slowing ("brain fog"), fatigue, anxiety and difficulty concentrating. The condition most often affects women in their 40s and 50s and frequently follows a cruise. Onset can also be spontaneous, without a motion trigger, a form sometimes associated with migraine and hormonal events.

Cause and treatment

The leading hypothesis attributes MdDS to maladaptation of the vestibular system's velocity-storage mechanism, in which the brain fails to recalibrate after adapting to sustained passive motion. There is no universally effective treatment; vestibular rehabilitation, readaptation protocols using optokinetic stimulation, and certain medications used for migraine or anxiety have shown benefit in some patients. Diagnostic criteria were published by the Bárány Society in 2020.
