Peace treaty

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A peace treaty is an agreement between two or more hostile parties, usually countries or governments, to formally end a state of war. It differs from an armistice, which stops hostilities without settling the conflict, and from a surrender, which ends fighting by one side's capitulation. Peace treaties settle boundaries, reparations, prisoners, responsibility, amnesties and future relations, and are governed by the law of treaties (with the caveat that coercion of a state by threat or use of force voids a treaty under the modern Vienna Convention).

History

Notable peace settlements include Westphalia (1648), Utrecht (1713), Vienna (1815), Versailles (1919 — whose terms and "war guilt" clause fed interwar revisionism), the Paris treaties after 1945, San Francisco with Japan (1951 — whose non-signatories left disputes, such as Russia–Japan's absent peace treaty, unresolved), Camp David/Egypt–Israel (1979), the Dayton Accords for Bosnia (1995, a peace agreement ending a civil war) and Colombia's 2016 accord with the FARC. Many modern wars end without peace treaties at all — by ceasefire, victory, or exhaustion — leaving legal states of war and frozen conflicts in their place.
