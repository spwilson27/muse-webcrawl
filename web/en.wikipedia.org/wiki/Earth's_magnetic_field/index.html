Earth's magnetic field, also known as the geomagnetic field, is the magnetic field that extends from Earth's interior out into space, where it interacts with the solar wind to form the magnetosphere. At the surface its strength ranges from about 25 to 65 microtesla. The field is approximately that of a magnetic dipole tilted about 9–11 degrees from the rotational axis, as if a giant bar magnet lay near the planet's centre; the north magnetic pole currently lies in the Canadian Arctic and is drifting toward Siberia at tens of kilometres per year.

Origin: the geodynamo

The field is generated by a self-sustaining dynamo in the liquid iron–nickel outer core. Convection driven by cooling and by the growth of the solid inner core moves electrically conducting fluid, and Earth's rotation organises the motion via the Coriolis effect, producing electric currents that generate the magnetic field. Numerical dynamo models reproduce many observed features, including spontaneous polarity reversals.

Structure and variation

Beyond the dipole, the field contains smaller-scale non-dipole features, including the South Atlantic Anomaly, a region of weak field where radiation-belt particles approach unusually low altitudes. The field changes over years to centuries (secular variation): it has weakened by roughly 9% over the last two centuries. In space, the field is compressed by the solar wind on the day side and stretched into a long tail on the night side; disturbances produce geomagnetic storms and auroras.

Reversals and history

Rocks record that the field has reversed polarity hundreds of times, most recently about 780,000 years ago (the Brunhes–Matuyama reversal). Measurements of magnetism in ancient rocks (paleomagnetism) underpin reconstructions of plate tectonics. The field shields the surface from much of the solar wind and cosmic radiation; its study began with William Gilbert's De Magnete (1600), which first proposed that Earth itself is a magnet, and Gauss later established that the field originates inside the planet.
