George Ellery Hale (29 June 1868 – 21 February 1938) was an American solar astronomer and one of the great institution-builders of modern science. He invented the spectroheliograph, discovered magnetic fields in sunspots, and founded or drove the creation of the Yerkes, Mount Wilson and Palomar observatories and the California Institute of Technology.

Early work

A wealthy Chicagoan's son, Hale built his own observatory as a teenager and studied at MIT. While an undergraduate he invented the spectroheliograph, an instrument for photographing the Sun in the light of a single spectral line, enabling images of prominences and other solar features without an eclipse.

Observatory builder

Hale founded the Yerkes Observatory (1897) with its 40-inch refractor, then moved to California and established the Mount Wilson Observatory, where its 60-inch (1908) and 100-inch Hooker (1917) telescopes revolutionised astronomy — the latter used by Edwin Hubble to show the expansion of the universe. Hale's last great project was the 200-inch reflector at Palomar Observatory, completed after his death and named the Hale Telescope in his honour.

Science and influence

In 1908 Hale discovered that sunspots harbour intense magnetic fields — the first detection of a magnetic field beyond the Earth — and later worked out the 22-year solar magnetic cycle (Hale's law). He founded The Astrophysical Journal, helped organise the National Academy of Sciences' research council, and was central to transforming the Throop Polytechnic Institute into Caltech.
