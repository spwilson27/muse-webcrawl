Apse

In architecture, an apse (from Latin apsis, from Greek ἁψίς, "arch, vault") is a semicircular or polygonal recess in a building, covered by a hemispherical or semi-dome or "semi-vault", usually at the eastern end of a church. The apse is the part of a church that constitutes the main location for the altar; in many Christian traditions it contains the sanctuary and the seats of the clergy (the synthronon in Byzantine churches). In secular Roman architecture, apses served as the location of judges' seats in basilicas and as exedrae in baths and palaces.

History

The apse developed in Roman civic and imperial architecture, where a semicircular exedra terminated basilicas, temples (such as the Temple of Venus and Roma), and audience halls; the niche often held a statue of the emperor or deity. Early Christian basilicas adopted the form for the raised platform (bema) where the bishop and presbyters sat behind the altar, facing the congregation. In Byzantine architecture, churches typically had a central apse flanked by two smaller apses (the prothesis and diaconicon). Romanesque churches elaborated the eastern arm with ambulatory and radiating chapels around the apse — the chevet of French Gothic cathedrals such as Chartres — while Gothic apses became polygonal, ringed with tall windows and flying buttresses. In Orthodox churches the apse is separated from the nave by the iconostasis.

Terminology and features

The semi-dome over an apse is called a conch. Decorated apses frequently carry mosaics or frescoes of Christ Pantocrator, the Virgin, or patron saints in the semi-dome — famous examples include the apse mosaics of Monreale and Cefalù in Sicily and of San Vitale in Ravenna (on the side walls). An apsidiole is a small secondary apse. Not to be confused with the astronomical term apsis, despite the shared Greek origin of the words.
