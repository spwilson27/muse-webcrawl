Broadcast seeding is a method of sowing in which seeds are scattered — "broadcast" — over the surface of a prepared plot of land, rather than planted in rows or at measured intervals. It is one of the oldest agricultural techniques: the sower scattering seed by hand from a bag or basket, as in the biblical Parable of the Sower, remained the standard method of planting cereals for thousands of years.

Method

Seed may be broadcast by hand, by hand-cranked or wheeled spreaders, by tractor-mounted or pneumatic spreaders, or by aircraft (aerial seeding, used for rice, cover crops and reforestation). The seed is usually then lightly covered by harrowing or raking, and rolled to ensure seed-to-soil contact; in wet rice cultivation, pre-germinated seed is broadcast into flooded paddy fields.

Advantages and disadvantages

Broadcasting is fast, requires simple equipment and is well suited to small-seeded crops such as grasses, clover and cover crops, to irregularly shaped plots, and to overseeding existing pasture. Its disadvantages are uneven distribution and plant spacing, higher seed rates (more seed is needed to compensate for losses to birds, poor contact and uneven burial), variable planting depth and germination, and difficulty in mechanically weeding between rows. For most large-scale cereal production it has been superseded by the seed drill, pioneered by Jethro Tull in 1701, which places seed at uniform depth and spacing.

Uses today

Broadcast seeding remains common for lawns, wildflower meadows, pasture renovation, cover cropping, rice in some regions, mine-site reclamation and aerial reforestation after fires.
