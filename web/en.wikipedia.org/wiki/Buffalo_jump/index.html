Buffalo jump

A buffalo jump (also bison jump) is a cliff formation used by Indigenous peoples of the North American Plains to hunt and kill plains bison in mass quantities. Hunters herded the animals and drove them over the cliff, where the fall broke their legs and rendered them immobile, after which they were butchered for meat, hides, and bones.

Method

Skilled runners, sometimes disguised in bison or wolf skins, positioned the herd while lines of people and cairns — "drive lanes" sometimes kilometers long — funneled the animals toward the cliff edge. Once stampeding, the bison at the front could not stop and were pushed over by those behind. Hunters waiting below finished off injured animals with spears, and later bows.

Processing

The kill yielded enormous quantities of meat, much of it dried or made into pemmican. Bones were rendered for grease, hides became clothing and tipis, and nearly every part of the animal was used. Deep bone deposits accumulated at long-used sites.

Major sites

Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump in Alberta, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, was used for over 5,500 years. Other notable jumps include the Madison Buffalo Jump in Montana, the Vore Buffalo Jump in Wyoming, and the Ulm Pishkun (First Peoples Buffalo Jump) near Great Falls, Montana.

Decline

The practice declined in the 19th century as horses and firearms changed hunting methods and bison herds were destroyed by commercial hunting.
