Benzodiazepine

Benzodiazepines (BZDs), colloquially called "benzos", are a class of depressant drugs whose core chemical structure is the fusion of a benzene ring and a diazepine ring. They are prescribed for anxiety, insomnia, seizures, muscle spasms, and alcohol withdrawal. The first benzodiazepine, chlordiazepoxide (Librium), was discovered accidentally by Leo Sternbach in 1955 and marketed by Hoffmann–La Roche from 1960; diazepam (Valium) followed in 1963.

Mechanism of action

Benzodiazepines enhance the effect of the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) at the GABAA receptor, increasing the frequency of chloride channel opening and thereby reducing neuronal excitability throughout the nervous system. This produces sedation, anxiolysis, anticonvulsant and muscle-relaxant effects, and anterograde amnesia.

Medical uses

Benzodiazepines are used for generalised anxiety and panic disorders, insomnia (short-term), status epilepticus and other seizure emergencies, pre-procedural sedation, acute agitation, muscle spasm, and the management of alcohol withdrawal syndrome. They differ mainly in potency, onset, and half-life, from short-acting agents such as midazolam and triazolam to long-acting ones such as diazepam and clonazepam.

Adverse effects and dependence

Common adverse effects include drowsiness, impaired coordination, confusion, and memory problems; risks are greater in older people, in whom benzodiazepines increase falls and fractures. Long-term use leads to tolerance and physical dependence, and abrupt cessation can itself cause a withdrawal syndrome with anxiety, insomnia, and seizures. Overdose alone is rarely fatal but becomes dangerous combined with alcohol or opioids; the antagonist flumazenil can reverse benzodiazepine effects but is used cautiously because it can precipitate withdrawal seizures.
