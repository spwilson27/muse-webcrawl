Ely Cathedral, formally the Cathedral Church of the Holy and Undivided Trinity, is an Anglican cathedral in the city of Ely, Cambridgeshire, England. Rising dramatically from the flat Fenland landscape — it is nicknamed the "Ship of the Fens" — it is celebrated for its unique central Octagon tower and lantern, and for one of the finest Romanesque naves in England.

History

The site began as an abbey founded in 672 by St Æthelthryth (St Etheldreda), an East Anglian princess who became its first abbess and whose shrine made Ely a pilgrimage centre. The monastery was refounded on Benedictine lines in 970 after the Danish invasions. Building of the present church began in 1083 under Abbot Simeon, a kinsman of William the Conqueror. In 1109 Ely became a cathedral with its own diocese, carved out of Lincoln. The Galilee porch, the elegant Lady Chapel — the largest in England — and the famous Octagon followed in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries.

The Octagon

When the central crossing tower collapsed in 1322, the sacrist Alan of Walsingham replaced it with a masterpiece: a wide octagonal opening crowned by a timber lantern, engineered with a hidden framework of massive oak beams. Painted bosses at its centre depict Christ in Majesty. The Octagon, unique in English Gothic architecture, floods the crossing with light and remains the cathedral's defining feature.

Architecture and features

The cathedral is 161 metres (537 feet) long, among the longest in Britain. Its painted nave ceiling, the Prior Crauden's Chapel, and the Stained Glass Museum housed in the south triforium are notable. The Lady Chapel, built 1321–1349, was devastated at the Reformation when Etheldreda's shrine was destroyed. Oliver Cromwell lived in Ely for a time, and his house stands nearby. Major restorations took place under Dean Peacock and George Gilbert Scott in the Victorian period and again in 1986–2000.
