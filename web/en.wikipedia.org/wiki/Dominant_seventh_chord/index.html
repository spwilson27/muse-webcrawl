Dominant seventh chord

In music theory, a dominant seventh chord, or major minor seventh chord, is a seventh chord composed of a root, a major third, a perfect fifth, and a minor seventh. It is frequently built on the fifth scale degree (dominant) of a major scale and is notated V7. In C major, the dominant seventh chord is G–B–D–F.

ComponentsRoot, major third, perfect fifth, minor seventh
QualityMajor-minor seventh
Common notationV7, or by root + 7 (e.g., G7)

Structure

The chord combines a major triad with a minor seventh above the root, giving the intervals (from the root) of a major third (4 semitones), perfect fifth (7), and minor seventh (10). It contains a tritone between the third and seventh of the chord, whose instability drives its strong tendency to resolve.

Resolution

In common-practice harmony the dominant seventh resolves to the tonic chord (I or i): the leading tone (chordal third) rises to the tonic, the chordal seventh falls by step to the third of the tonic chord, and the tritone contracts inward (in major) to the tonic's root and third. Deceptive resolutions to vi are also common. The chord is the engine of the perfect authentic cadence, V7–I.

Secondary dominants and extensions

Dominant sevenths built on other scale degrees act as secondary dominants, tonicizing chords other than the tonic (e.g., V7/V). In jazz harmony, the dominant seventh is among the most heavily extended and altered chord types — ♭9, ♯9, ♯11, and ♭13 alterations are routine — and it forms the V of the ubiquitous ii–V–I progression.

In blues and popular music

In blues, dominant seventh chords are used as tonic and subdominant chords as well (I7 and IV7 in the twelve-bar blues), functioning more as stable colours than as chords requiring resolution. Rock, country, and R&B similarly use "7" chords decoratively.
