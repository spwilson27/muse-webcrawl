Ascaris lumbricoides

Ascaris lumbricoides is a large parasitic roundworm (nematode) that infects the human gastrointestinal tract, causing the disease ascariasis. It is the most common helminth infection of humans worldwide, with estimates of around a billion people infected, mostly in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation.

Morphology

Adult worms are pale pinkish-white and cylindrical. Females measure 20–35 cm in length and males 15–30 cm, making the species one of the largest nematodes parasitising humans. A mature female can produce on the order of 200,000 eggs per day, which are passed in the host's faeces.

Life cycle

Eggs embryonate in soil over two to four weeks in warm, moist conditions and can remain infective for years. When swallowed with contaminated food, water or soil, the larvae hatch in the small intestine, penetrate the gut wall and migrate via the bloodstream through the liver and heart to the lungs. There they break into the alveoli, ascend the trachea, are swallowed, and return to the intestine, where they mature into adults that may live for one to two years.

Clinical effects

Most infections are light and asymptomatic. The larval migration through the lungs can cause a transient pneumonitis (Löffler syndrome). Heavy intestinal burdens may cause malnutrition, growth impairment in children, abdominal pain, and potentially life-threatening complications such as intestinal obstruction, or blockage of the bile or pancreatic ducts. Diagnosis is usually by identifying eggs in stool samples. Treatment uses anthelmintic drugs such as albendazole or mebendazole; prevention depends on sanitation and hygiene, and mass deworming programmes are conducted in endemic areas.
