First Vatican Council

The First Vatican Council (Latin: Concilium Vaticanum I), commonly Vatican I, was the 20th ecumenical council of the Catholic Church, convened by Pope Pius IX in 1869–1870 in St. Peter's Basilica. It is best known for defining the dogma of papal infallibility.

Proceedings

Nearly 800 bishops attended, the first council with bishops from the Americas, Asia and Oceania in large numbers. The constitution Dei Filius (April 1870) affirmed God's knowability by reason against rationalism and fideism. Pastor Aeternus (18 July 1870) defined the primacy and infallibility of the Pope when speaking ex cathedra on faith and morals, despite opposition from a minority who deemed the definition inopportune; historian Lord Acton and theologian Ignaz von Döllinger were prominent critics.

Interruption and aftermath

The Franco-Prussian War and the Italian capture of Rome (September 1870) ended the Papal States; Pius IX suspended the council indefinitely in October 1870 — it never reconvened and was formally closed only in 1960 before Vatican II. Dissenters who rejected infallibility formed the Old Catholic churches. The definition remains a major obstacle in ecumenical dialogue.
