Sheet mulching

Sheet mulching
Also known asSheet composting, lasagna gardening
MethodLayered organic materials over soil or lawn
Used inPermaculture, organic gardening

Sheet mulching is a gardening technique for building new planting beds and suppressing weeds without digging or tilling. Layers of organic material — typically a biodegradable weed barrier such as cardboard or newspaper, followed by compost and mulch — are laid directly over lawn or existing soil. The layers smother vegetation beneath, then decompose in place, improving soil structure and fertility. The method is a cornerstone of permaculture and is also called sheet composting or, when layers of "green" and "brown" materials alternate, lasagna gardening.

Method

A typical sheet mulch begins with mowing the site short and watering it. A nitrogen-rich layer such as manure or fresh grass clippings is spread, then overlapping sheets of cardboard (with tape and staples removed) are laid down and soaked. Above this come alternating layers of carbon-rich materials (straw, leaves, wood chips) and nitrogen-rich materials (compost, manure, kitchen scraps), finishing with a thick top mulch. The total depth is often 30–60 cm (12–24 in), which settles substantially as it decomposes.

Uses

Sheet mulching is used to convert lawns to garden beds, restore compacted or contaminated urban soils, establish food forests, and prepare ground for planting without herbicides or rototilling. Beds can be planted immediately by cutting holes through the cardboard for transplants, or left for a season to mature.

Advantages and limitations

Advocates cite improved soil biology, moisture retention, carbon sequestration and the avoidance of soil disturbance. Limitations include the large volumes of material required, the time needed for decomposition, possible attraction of slugs in wet climates, and temporary nitrogen tie-up if carbon-rich layers dominate.
