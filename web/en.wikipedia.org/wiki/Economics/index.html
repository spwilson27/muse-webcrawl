Economics

[Image: a busy stock-exchange trading floor with screens of rising and falling charts, photographed from above]

Economics is a social science that studies the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services. It focuses on the behaviour and interactions of economic agents (individuals, firms, governments) and how economies work — above all, how scarce resources are allocated among competing uses, and with what consequences for welfare, growth, employment and prices.

Economics divides into microeconomics, the study of individual agents and markets (supply and demand, competition, pricing), and macroeconomics, the study of whole economies (growth, inflation, unemployment, money and fiscal policy).

Development

Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) is conventionally taken as the founding work of modern economics, arguing that markets coordinate self-interest to public benefit (the 'invisible hand') and that the division of labour drives prosperity. Nineteenth-century classical and then marginalist economics formalised value and prices; the twentieth century added Keynesian macroeconomics after the Great Depression, monetarism, game theory and behavioural economics.

Economists' methods range from mathematical theory to statistical econometrics and experiments; the field's forecasts and advice are influential and contested in equal measure, especially after crises such as that of 2008.

Schools and measures

Key indicators — gross domestic product (GDP), inflation indices, unemployment rates — are themselves economic constructions with known limits, prompting alternatives such as Bhutan's Gross National Happiness and broader well-being measures. Schools of thought (classical, Keynesian, neoclassical, Marxian, Austrian, institutional, feminist, ecological) differ in assumptions about rationality, markets and the state.
