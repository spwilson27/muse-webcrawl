Big beat

Big beat
Big beat brought breakbeats to mainstream audiences in the 1990s
Stylistic originsBreakbeat, acid house, hip hop, rock, techno
Cultural originsEarly 1990s, London and Brighton, England
Peak years1996–2000

Big beat is an electronic music genre that usually uses heavy breakbeats and synthesizer-generated loops and patterns — common in acid house/techno — and is typically accompanied by samples of rock and pop songs from the 1960s and 1970s, as well as film dialogue. Genres closely related to big beat include breakbeat and chemical break.

History

The style developed in British club culture in the early 1990s and was popularised by acts such as The Chemical Brothers, Fatboy Slim, The Prodigy, The Crystal Method, and Propellerheads. The London club The Sunday Social and the Heavenly Social nights, where the Chemical Brothers DJed, were incubators, as was the Big Beat Boutique in Brighton, associated with Fatboy Slim (Norman Cook), whose 1998 album You've Come a Long Way, Baby included the hits "The Rockafeller Skank" and "Praise You".

Characteristics

Big beat tracks are built around chopped and looped drum breaks, distorted basslines, siren-like synth riffs, and conspicuous samples — guitar stabs, vocal shouts, and psychedelic textures — arranged for maximum dancefloor impact. Tempos generally range from 110 to 135 BPM. The genre fused the energy of rave with the structures of pop songs, making it highly successful in advertising, film soundtracks, and mainstream charts in the late 1990s.

Decline and legacy

By the early 2000s big beat had faded as a chart force, criticised by some as formulaic, but its breakbeat-driven, sample-heavy approach influenced later electronic and pop production, and its leading acts remained major live draws.
