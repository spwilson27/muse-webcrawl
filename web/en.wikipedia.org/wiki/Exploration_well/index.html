Exploration well

An exploration well being drilled to test a prospective geological structure.

An exploration well is a deep hole drilled by oil and gas exploration companies to search for proven subsurface accumulations of oil and gas, either onshore or offshore. Exploration wells are drilled in areas where no production has yet been established, in order to determine whether hydrocarbons are present in a prospective geological structure, and if so, in what quantities.

Purpose

Before an exploration well (often called a "wildcat well" when drilled far from known fields) is drilled, geologists and geophysicists study seismic survey data and regional geology to identify a prospect — a structure, such as an anticline or fault trap, that could hold oil or gas. The exploration well tests that interpretation directly. If it succeeds, follow-up appraisal wells are drilled to delineate the size and extent of the discovery before a decision is made to develop a field.

Outcomes

Exploration wells are inherently risky: historically, only a minority of wildcat wells find commercially recoverable hydrocarbons. A well that finds no hydrocarbons, or quantities too small to be economic, is termed a dry hole and is plugged and abandoned. A successful well is usually cased and suspended pending appraisal, and core samples, wireline logs, and drill-stem test data gathered during drilling are used to estimate reserves.

Costs and risks

Exploration wells can be extremely expensive, particularly in deepwater offshore settings where a single well may cost over US$100 million. Risks include blowouts — uncontrolled releases of formation fluids — of which the 2010 Deepwater Horizon disaster is the most prominent example, arising during operations on the Macondo exploration well in the Gulf of Mexico.
