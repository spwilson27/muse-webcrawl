Counterpart theory

Counterpart theory is a theory in philosophical logic and metaphysics, most famously developed by David Lewis in his paper "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic" (1968) and his book On the Plurality of Worlds (1986). It provides a semantics for modal logic in terms of counterparts — similar, but not identical, individuals in other possible worlds — rather than trans-world identity.

Overview

According to Lewis, each individual exists in exactly one possible world. When we say that Humphrey could have won an election he actually lost, we do not say that Humphrey himself exists in another world where he won; rather, Humphrey has a counterpart in another world — someone who resembles Humphrey closely in relevant respects — who won. Truths about what is possible for an individual are thus made true by facts about his or her counterparts in other worlds.

The counterpart relation

The counterpart relation is a relation of comparative similarity, not identity. It is not necessarily transitive or symmetric, and which counterpart relations are operative can vary with context: a person may have several counterparts in a single world, and different respects of similarity (physical, psychological, historical) can privilege different counterparts.

Criticism

Saul Kripke objected that the Humphrey who might have won is Humphrey himself, not a mere similar person: the "Humphrey objection" holds that what matters is Humphrey's fate, not someone else's. Critics also question the ontology of Lewis's modal realism and the flexibility of the counterpart relation. Lewis replied that the intuition conflates qualitative similarity with identity and that counterpart theory avoids puzzles about trans-world identity, such as how one object could have contradictory properties in different worlds.

Related uses

The term "counterpart" also appears in personal-identity debates, temporal parts theories and the semantics of de re modality more broadly. Counterpart-theoretic semantics have been applied in formal work on quantified modal logic and in philosophical treatments of persistence through time.
