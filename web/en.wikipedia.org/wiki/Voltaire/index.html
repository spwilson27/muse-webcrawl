Voltaire (1694–1778), pen name of François-Marie Arouet, was a French Enlightenment writer, philosopher and historian, the age's most famous champion of civil liberties. Imprisoned in the Bastille and exiled to England, he returned with Letters on the English praising British tolerance and science; his novella Candide (1759) satirised Leibnizian optimism ('all is for the best') after the Lisbon earthquake, and his campaigns for victims of religious persecution — the Calas and Sirven cases — made 'Écrasez l'infâme' ('crush the infamous') the war-cry against fanaticism.

Thought

A deist who built a church while savaging churches' intolerance, Voltaire defended freedom of expression with unmatched wit ('I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it' is his biographer's paraphrase, not his sentence). His histories, plays, dictionary and vast correspondence made him Europe's literary dictator; his return to Paris in 1778 was a national apotheosis.
