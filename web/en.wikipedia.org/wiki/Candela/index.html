The candela (symbol: cd) is the unit of luminous intensity in the International System of Units (SI). It measures luminous power per unit solid angle emitted by a light source in a particular direction. A common wax candle emits light with a luminous intensity of roughly one candela.

Definition

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the candela is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the luminous efficacy of monochromatic radiation of frequency 540 × 1012 Hz, Kcd, to be 683 when expressed in the unit lm·W−1. In other words, a light source that emits monochromatic radiation of that frequency (green light near the peak sensitivity of the human eye) with a radiant intensity of 1/683 watt per steradian has a luminous intensity of one candela.

History

The candela replaced earlier units based on the flame of a standard candle. In 1948 the ninth General Conference on Weights and Measures adopted the "new candle" based on the luminance of a blackbody at the freezing point of platinum; it was renamed the candela in 1967, and the definition was tied to monochromatic radiation in 1979 before the 2019 redefinition in terms of fixed constants.

Relation to other units

Luminous flux is measured in lumens: one lumen equals one candela times one steradian. Illuminance, in lux, is luminous flux per unit area. The candela is one of the seven SI base units.
