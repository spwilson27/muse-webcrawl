Center of a group

In abstract algebra, the center of a group G, denoted Z(G), is the set of elements that commute with every element of G: Z(G) = {z ∈ G | zg = gz for all g ∈ G}. The center is always a normal subgroup of G, and it is itself abelian.

Properties

A group is abelian if and only if its center is the whole group. For a non-abelian p-group the center is always nontrivial, a key fact in the class equation argument. The quotient G/Z(G) is isomorphic to the group of inner automorphisms of G; consequently, if G/Z(G) is cyclic, then G is abelian, so for non-abelian G the quotient can never be a nontrivial cyclic group.

Examples

The center of the quaternion group Q8 is {1, −1}; the center of GL(n, F) is the group of nonzero scalar matrices; the dihedral group of order 2n has a trivial center when n is odd and a center of order 2 when n is even.
