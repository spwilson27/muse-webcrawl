The Buxaceae are a small family of flowering plants in the order Buxales, comprising roughly six genera and about 120 species of evergreen shrubs and small trees (and a few herbs), distributed widely in temperate and tropical regions of the Americas, Europe, Africa, and Asia. The family is best known for the genus Buxus — the boxwoods or boxes — widely planted as hedging and topiary, and for Pachysandra, a popular ground-cover.

Description

Members of the Buxaceae are mostly evergreen shrubs or small trees with simple, opposite (or rarely alternate), leathery leaves without stipules. The flowers are small, unisexual, and inconspicuous, with the plants monoecious or dioecious; male flowers typically bear four tepals and four or more stamens, while female flowers have six tepals and a three-chambered ovary. The fruit is a capsule or drupe with glossy black seeds.

Taxonomy

In the APG IV system the Buxaceae form, alone, the order Buxales, positioned among the early-diverging eudicots. Molecular studies have folded the former families Didymelaceae and Haptanthaceae into an expanded Buxaceae in some treatments. The genera generally recognised include Buxus (about 70 species), Pachysandra, Sarcococca (sweet box), Styloceras, Didymeles, and the single-species Haptanthus of Honduras, rediscovered in 2001 after being feared extinct.

Economic importance

Boxwood timber is exceptionally fine-grained, dense, and pale, historically prized for engraving blocks, woodwind instruments, chessmen, rulers, and inlay. In horticulture, Buxus sempervirens and its many cultivars are among the most widely used evergreen hedging plants in Europe and North America; in recent decades plantings have been severely damaged by the box tree moth (Cydalima perspectalis) and by box blight fungi. Sarcococca species are valued for their intensely fragrant winter flowers, and Pachysandra terminalis is a ubiquitous shade ground-cover.
