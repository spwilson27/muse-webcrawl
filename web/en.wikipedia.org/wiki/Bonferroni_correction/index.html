Bonferroni correction

In statistics, the Bonferroni correction is a method of counteracting the multiple comparisons problem: when several statistical tests are performed simultaneously, the probability that at least one of them falsely rejects a true null hypothesis (a Type I error) grows with the number of tests. The correction, named after the Italian mathematician Carlo Emilio Bonferroni and his inequalities, controls this family-wise error rate by testing each individual hypothesis at a significance level of α/m, where m is the number of hypotheses.

Rationale

If m independent tests are each conducted at significance level α = 0.05, the probability that at least one yields a false positive is 1 − (1 − α)m, which for m = 20 is about 64%. By Boole's inequality (the basis of the Bonferroni correction), the probability of at least one false rejection is at most the sum of the individual error probabilities, so testing each hypothesis at α/m guarantees a family-wise error rate no greater than α, without any assumptions about dependence among the tests.

Criticism and alternatives

The Bonferroni correction is simple and universally applicable but is widely regarded as conservative: by sharply lowering the per-test threshold it increases Type II errors and reduces statistical power, particularly when tests are correlated or the number of hypotheses is very large. The Holm–Bonferroni method is a uniformly more powerful step-down refinement that maintains the same guarantee. In fields performing very large numbers of tests, such as genomics, procedures controlling the false discovery rate (such as the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure) are often preferred.
