Plug-in electric vehicles in China

Plug-in electric vehicles in China constitute the largest market for new energy vehicles (NEVs)—battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs)—in the world. China has accounted for roughly half or more of global plug-in car sales each year since the mid-2010s, and its stock of plug-in vehicles on the road is by far the world's largest, in the tens of millions.

Government policy

The Chinese government designated electric vehicles a strategic industry under programmes such as the "Ten Cities, Thousand Vehicles" scheme (2009) and successive five-year plans, motivated by energy security (reducing oil imports), urban air pollution, and industrial policy aimed at leapfrogging foreign dominance in internal-combustion technology. Support has included purchase subsidies, exemption from purchase taxes, preferential licence plates in congested cities (where combustion-car plates are auctioned or rationed by lottery), and charging infrastructure investment. Direct subsidies were phased down and ended at the close of 2022, replaced by a "dual-credit" policy requiring manufacturers to earn credits for producing NEVs and fuel-efficient cars.

Market development

Sales grew from a few tens of thousands annually in the early 2010s to about 6.9 million NEVs in 2022, roughly a quarter of all new cars sold, and continued rising to around 9–11 million per year in 2023–2024, with monthly NEV penetration exceeding 50% at times in 2024. Domestic makers—including BYD, which became the world's largest producer of plug-in cars, as well as SAIC (with the Wuling Hongguang Mini EV, a best-seller), Geely, NIO, XPeng, Li Auto, and AITO—compete with foreign brands, notably Tesla, whose Shanghai Gigafactory opened in 2019. Intense competition has produced repeated price wars. China also dominates the battery supply chain through producers such as CATL and BYD, and is the largest exporter of electric cars.

Charging infrastructure

China operates the world's largest charging network, with millions of public and private charging points installed, coordinated through industry alliances and state grid companies, and a domestic standard connector (GB/T). Battery-swapping stations, pioneered commercially by NIO and others, provide an alternative model in some cities.
