Malay Archipelago

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Malay Archipelago (Malay/Indonesian: Kepulauan Melayu), also called Maritime Southeast Asia, is the archipelago between mainland Indochina and Australia, and between the Indian Ocean and the Pacific Ocean. It has also been called the "Malay world", "Nusantara", the "East Indies", and in the 19th century the "Indian Archipelago" or the "Indo-Australian Archipelago".

It is located between Mainland Southeast Asia and Australia, between the Indian and Pacific oceans. It is the largest archipelago by area and the third largest by number of islands in the world. It includes Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia (specifically East Malaysia), the Philippines, Singapore, and Timor-Leste (East Timor).

Geography

The archipelago covers approximately 25,000 islands and island groups. Major islands include Borneo, Sumatra, Java, Sulawesi, and New Guinea (the western part, belonging to Indonesia); the Philippine archipelago includes Luzon and Mindanao. The region is one of the most volcanically active in the world and lies within the Pacific Ring of Fire.

Etymology

The term "Malay Archipelago" is derived from the concept of the "Malay race", a discarded racial concept of a brown-skinned race inhabiting the region. The term was popularized by the English naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace, whose 1869 book The Malay Archipelago documented his eight years of natural history exploration in the region and led to the recognition of the faunal boundary now called the Wallace Line.

Demographics

The people of the archipelago are predominantly Austronesian, speaking related languages of the Malayo-Polynesian branch. The principal religions are Islam, Christianity, Buddhism, and Hinduism. The region has a population of over 380 million.
