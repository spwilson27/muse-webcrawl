White-tailed eagle

The white-tailed eagle (Haliaeetus albicilla), also called the white-tailed sea eagle or erne, is a very large bird of prey in the family Accipitridae, closely related to the bald eagle of North America, with which it forms a species pair. It is one of the largest flying birds in Europe and northern Asia.

Description

Adults measure 66–94 cm in length with a wingspan of 1.8–2.45 m. The plumage is brown overall, with a paler, yellowish head and neck and the short, wedge-shaped white tail that gives the species its name. The bill and feet are bright yellow and massive; juveniles are darker, with a dark tail and bill, attaining adult plumage at about five years. Females are noticeably larger than males.

Distribution and habitat

The species breeds across temperate and subarctic Eurasia, from Iceland and Scandinavia through Russia to the Pacific, favouring coasts, large lakes, and river valleys with old trees or cliffs for nesting. Northern populations are migratory, moving south in winter.

Feeding and breeding

White-tailed eagles feed mainly on fish and water birds, caught in shallow stoops or snatched from the surface, and readily scavenge carrion and pirate food from other birds. Pairs build huge stick eyries, reused and enlarged for decades, laying one to three eggs in early spring.

Conservation

Persecution and pesticide poisoning (notably DDT) caused severe declines in the 19th and 20th centuries, and the species was exterminated in Britain by 1918. Successful reintroductions — to Scotland from 1975 and to the Isle of Wight from 2019 — together with legal protection have restored it across much of its range, and it is now listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
