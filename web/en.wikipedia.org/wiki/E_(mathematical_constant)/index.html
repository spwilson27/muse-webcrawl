The number e is a mathematical constant, approximately equal to 2.71828, that is the base of the natural logarithm and the exponential function. It is sometimes called Euler's number after Leonhard Euler (who introduced the letter e for it), or Napier's constant after John Napier. Along with π, it is one of the most important constants in mathematics.

The number e is defined as the limit of (1 + 1/n)n as n approaches infinity, and equally as the sum of the infinite series 1 + 1 + 1/2! + 1/3! + 1/4! + … It is the unique number whose natural logarithm equals 1, and the exponential function ex is the unique function (up to a constant factor) equal to its own derivative.

History

The constant arose implicitly in seventeenth-century studies of compound interest: Jacob Bernoulli asked what happens as interest is compounded ever more frequently, leading to the limit definition. Napier's 1618 tables of logarithms contained values equivalent to logarithms to base e. Euler, in work from 1727 onwards, established the notation and proved that e is irrational; Charles Hermite proved in 1873 that it is transcendental, meaning it is not a root of any non-zero polynomial with integer coefficients.

Applications

The exponential function models processes of growth and decay: compound interest, population growth, radioactive decay, cooling and probability distributions such as the Poisson and normal distributions. In complex analysis, Euler's formula eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ links e to trigonometry, and Euler's identity eiπ + 1 = 0 is celebrated for uniting five fundamental constants. The number also appears in combinatorics (derangements), number theory and information theory, where natural logarithms use units called nats.
