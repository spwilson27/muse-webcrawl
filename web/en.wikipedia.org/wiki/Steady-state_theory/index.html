Steady-state theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Portrait photograph of Fred Hoyle, the steady-state theory's greatest champion]
Fred Hoyle in later life.

The steady-state theory was a cosmological model developed in 1948 by Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle as an alternative to the Big Bang: the universe expands, but new matter is continuously created (about one hydrogen atom per cubic kilometre per year) so that its large-scale appearance never changes — the 'perfect cosmological principle' in which the universe looks the same at all places and all times.

Steady-state made sharp predictions and was thus highly falsifiable — and falsified: radio-source counts showed the universe was different in the past (Martin Ryle's Cambridge surveys, 1950s–60s), and the discovery of the Cosmic microwave background in 1965 — relic heat the model could not naturally produce — ended the contest for most cosmologists. Hoyle defended modified versions for decades and (ironically) coined the name 'Big Bang' in a 1949 radio broadcast. The episode is a cherished case study in science: a beautiful, wrong theory that forced its rival to sharpen, and whose creation field has curious echoes in inflation's steady-state-like eternal regime.

Legacy

Steady-state cosmology drove the development of observational cosmology (source counts, radio telescopes) and its defeat by the microwave background is the standard example of a theory killed by a single class of observation. Hoyle's other great contribution — stellar nucleosynthesis (B²FH, 1957) — outlived his cosmology and is a pillar of the Big Bang account itself.
