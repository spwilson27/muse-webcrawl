Aperture grille
Used inColour cathode-ray tube (CRT) displays
Famous implementationsSony Trinitron; Mitsubishi Diamondtron
AlternativeShadow mask

An aperture grille is a technology used in colour cathode-ray tubes (CRTs) to direct electron beams onto the correct phosphors. Instead of the perforated metal sheet (shadow mask) used in most colour tubes, an aperture grille consists of a series of fine vertical wires held under tension in front of the screen. The wires separate the red, green, and blue phosphor stripes, and because they block less of the beam than a shadow mask, aperture-grille tubes can produce brighter, more saturated images.

History and characteristics

The aperture grille was the heart of Sony's Trinitron system, introduced in 1968, which used a single electron gun with three cathodes and became renowned for picture quality; Mitsubishi later offered a similar design in its Diamondtron tubes. Because the grille wires run vertically, the face of the tube is cylindrical rather than spherical, and one or two thin horizontal damper wires are needed to stop the vertical wires vibrating — these can appear as faint horizontal lines across the screen, a well-known Trinitron trait. Aperture pitch, the spacing of the stripes, is the measure of resolution for such tubes.
