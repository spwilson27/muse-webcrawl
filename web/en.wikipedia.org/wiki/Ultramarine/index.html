Ultramarine

Ultramarine is a deep blue pigment originally made by grinding the semi-precious stone lapis lazuli into powder. Its name means "beyond the sea", reflecting the import of lapis from mines in Afghanistan across the Mediterranean to Europe.

History

Natural ultramarine was the finest and most expensive blue available to Renaissance painters, often reserved in contracts for the robes of the Virgin Mary; Cennino Cennini called it "illustrious, beautiful, and most perfect". Its price exceeded that of gold. In 1826 a synthetic process was developed following a French prize competition, and the chemist Jean-Baptiste Guimet began producing artificial ultramarine, chemically identical to the mineral pigment based on lazurite, at a fraction of the cost.

Chemistry and uses

The colour arises from sulfur radical anions trapped in the sodalite cage structure of the mineral. Synthetic ultramarine is used in paints, plastics, cosmetics and laundry bluing agents that counteract yellowing of white fabrics.
