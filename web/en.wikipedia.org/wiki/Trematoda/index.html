Trematoda is a class of parasitic flatworms within the phylum Platyhelminthes, commonly known as flukes. All trematodes are parasites of molluscs and/or vertebrates. They are characterised by a flattened, leaf-like or cylindrical body with one or two suckers (the name derives from the Greek trēmatōdēs, "having holes"), a syncytial outer surface (tegument), and no anus in most species.

Life cycles

The great majority of trematodes belong to the subclass Digenea and have complex life cycles involving at least two hosts: a mollusc (usually a snail) as first intermediate host, in which asexual multiplication occurs through larval stages (miracidium, sporocyst, redia, cercaria), and a vertebrate definitive host in which the adult fluke reproduces sexually. Many species use additional intermediate hosts such as fish or crustaceans, in which the cercariae encyst as metacercariae. The smaller subclass Aspidogastrea parasitises molluscs and sometimes turtles and fish, with simpler cycles.

Medical and veterinary importance

Trematodes cause significant human disease. The blood flukes Schistosoma spp. cause schistosomiasis, infecting over 200 million people. Food-borne flukes include the liver flukes Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis spp. and Fasciola hepatica, the lung fluke Paragonimus westermani, and intestinal flukes such as Fasciolopsis buski. Fasciola hepatica is also a major parasite of sheep and cattle. The drug praziquantel is effective against most human fluke infections.
