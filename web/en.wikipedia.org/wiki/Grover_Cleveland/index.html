Stephen Grover Cleveland (18 March 1837 – 24 June 1908) was an American politician who served as the 22nd and 24th president of the United States from 1885 to 1889 and from 1893 to 1897. He was the first US president to serve non-consecutive terms (a distinction he held alone until Donald Trump's second election in 2024), and the first Democrat elected after the Civil War. A leader of the pro-business Bourbon Democrats, he championed fiscal conservatism, low tariffs, the gold standard and opposition to subsidies and imperialism.

Rise

Trained as a lawyer in Buffalo, New York, Cleveland built a rapid political career on a reputation for incorruptibility: sheriff of Erie County, reform mayor of Buffalo ("the veto mayor"), and in 1882 Governor of New York. In the bitter election of 1884 — the "Mudslinging Election" — he narrowly defeated Republican James G. Blaine despite a paternity scandal, answered by the famous chant "Ma, Ma, where's my Pa? Gone to the White House, ha ha ha!"

Presidencies

In his first term Cleveland vetoed hundreds of bills (including a Texas seed bill, arguing there was "no warrant in the Constitution" for such relief), expanded civil-service reform, and in 1886 married Frances Folsom in the White House — the only president to marry there. He won the popular vote in 1888 but lost the Electoral College to Benjamin Harrison. Returning in 1892, his second term was dominated by the Panic of 1893, a severe depression: Cleveland secured repeal of the Sherman Silver Purchase Act to defend the gold standard, splitting his party; he used federal troops to break the Pullman Strike of 1894 and resisted the annexation of Hawaii after the overthrow of Queen Liliʻuokalani, though he declined to restore her by force.

Later life and reputation

Cleveland retired to Princeton, New Jersey, served as a trustee of Princeton University, and died in 1908, reportedly with the last words "I have tried so hard to do right." Historians generally rank him as an above-average president for his integrity and defence of limited government, while criticising his handling of the 1893 depression.
