The Salvadoran Civil War was a twelve-year military conflict in El Salvador between the military-led government and the Farabundo Martí National Liberation Front (FMLN), a coalition of left-wing guerrilla groups. It is usually dated from the October 1979 coup to the signing of the Chapultepec Peace Accords on 16 January 1992 in Mexico City.

Background

El Salvador's economy was long dominated by a small landowning oligarchy — the "fourteen families" — while the rural majority lived in deep poverty. Decades of military rule and repression, including the 1932 peasant uprising and massacre known as La Matanza, entrenched the conflict. A reformist junta seized power in October 1979 but failed to halt right-wing death-squad violence.

Course of the war

In 1980 five guerrilla organisations united as the FMLN, and the assassination of Archbishop Óscar Romero in March 1980 and the murder of four American churchwomen later that year drew international attention. The United States, fearing another Nicaragua, supplied the Salvadoran military with billions of dollars in aid and advisers. The war's atrocities included the El Mozote massacre of December 1981, in which the Atlacatl Battalion killed hundreds of civilians, and the 1989 murder of six Jesuit priests at the Central American University, which helped push both sides toward negotiation.

Peace and aftermath

UN-brokered negotiations produced the Chapultepec Peace Accords, which demobilised the FMLN as a political party, purged and shrank the armed forces, and created a civilian police force. A UN Truth Commission attributed the overwhelming majority of abuses to government forces and death squads. An estimated 75,000 people died, and the war displaced a fifth of the population.
