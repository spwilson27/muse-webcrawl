Z4 (computer)

The Z4 was an electromechanical computer designed by Konrad Zuse and built by his company, Zuse Apparatebau, in the mid-1940s. It was the successor to the Z3, the world's first working programmable, fully automatic digital computer (1941), and is regarded as one of the earliest commercial computers.

Development and survival

Construction of the Z4 began in Berlin; to escape the bombing and the advancing Red Army, the machine was moved south and hidden in a barn in Hinterstein in the Allgäu. After the war, Zuse completed and refurbished it, and in 1950 it was delivered to the ETH Zürich, where it became the first computer in operation at a European university and was for a time the only working computer in continental Europe.

Design

Like the Z3, the Z4 was built from telephone relays (about 2,600) and used binary floating-point arithmetic with a 32-bit word length. It had a mechanical memory of 64 words, read instructions from punched tape (using discarded film stock), and performed several operations per second. At ETH it was used for engineering calculations, including work on aircraft design and the Grand Dixence dam.

Legacy

After service at ETH Zürich (1950–1955) the Z4 was transferred to a French aerodynamics institute near Basel. It is now exhibited at the Deutsches Museum in Munich.
