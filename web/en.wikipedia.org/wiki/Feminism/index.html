Feminism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Feminism is a range of socio-political movements and ideologies that aim to define and establish the political, economic, personal and social equality of the sexes. Feminism holds that societies prioritise the male point of view and that women are treated unjustly within them; its efforts to change this include fighting gender stereotypes and improving educational, professional and interpersonal opportunities and outcomes for women.

History

Precursors include Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792). Organised feminism is conventionally narrated in "waves": a first wave (19th–early 20th century) centred on legal rights and suffrage — won in New Zealand (1893) first, and in the US (1920), Britain (1918/1928) and most democracies by mid-century; a second wave (1960s–80s) addressing sexuality, family, work and reproductive rights, symbolised by Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949) and Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963); a third wave (1990s) emphasising diversity and intersectionality (Kimberlé Crenshaw's term for overlapping systems of disadvantage); and a digitally networked fourth wave associated with campaigns against sexual harassment, including #MeToo (from 2017). Feminist thought spans liberal, radical, socialist/Marxist, ecofeminist, postcolonial and other schools, sometimes in sharp mutual debate.

Impact and contention

Feminism reshaped law (anti-discrimination, marital-rape criminalisation, parental rights), education, employment and politics (quotas and parity laws in many countries), while remaining contested — over gender identity, sex work, religion and culture, and by organised anti-feminist movements. Global indices on political empowerment and economic participation record both substantial gains and persistent gaps.
