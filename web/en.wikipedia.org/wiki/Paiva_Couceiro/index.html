Henrique Mitchell de Paiva Cabral Couceiro (30 December 1861, in Lisbon – 11 February 1944, in Lisbon) was a Portuguese soldier, colonial governor, monarchist politician and counter-revolutionary; he was notable for his role during the colonial occupation campaigns in Africa, for the monarchist incursions against the Portuguese First Republic in 1911–12 and 1919, and for the role he played at the head of the monarchist countermovement of the Monarchy of the North.

Early life and military career

Couceiro was born in Lisbon into a family with military traditions; his father was an officer of the Royal Corps of Engineers. He studied at the Military College and the Polytechnic School before graduating in artillery from the Army School in 1884. He first served with an artillery regiment in Torres Vedras and Lisbon.

Colonial service in Africa

In 1889 Couceiro volunteered for service in Angola, where he won a reputation as one of the most effective of Portugal's colonial campaigners during the final "pacification" campaigns of the Portuguese Empire. He took part in operations in the Bié highlands and the south of the colony, and his 1890–91 campaign was celebrated in Portugal. In 1907 he was appointed Governor-General of Angola, a post he held until 1909, during which he sought to strengthen Portuguese administration and economic development in the colony.

Earlier, from 1901 to 1904, he had served as governor of the district of Lourenço Marques in Mozambique. His colonial writings and reports made him one of the most prominent public voices of Portuguese imperial expansion in Africa.

Monarchist counter-revolution

A convinced monarchist and a Catholic conservative, Couceiro refused to accept the Portuguese Republic proclaimed on 5 October 1910. From exile in Galicia, Spain, he organised armed incursions into northern Portugal in October 1911 and July 1912, hoping to provoke a royalist rising; both failed. In January 1919 he proclaimed the restoration of the monarchy in Porto, the short-lived "Monarchy of the North", and presided over its governing junta until republican forces retook the city the following month.

Later life

Defeated, Couceiro was again exiled, and later amnestied. In his later years he wrote on colonial and military questions and remained a symbol of the monarchist and traditionalist cause in Portugal. He died in Lisbon in 1944.
