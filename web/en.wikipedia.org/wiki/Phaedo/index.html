Phaedo

The Phaedo (Greek: Φαίδων, Phaidōn), also known to ancient readers as On the Soul, is one of the best-known dialogues of the Athenian philosopher Plato. Written in the early fourth century BC, it depicts the death of Socrates and is the second of four dialogues (with the Euthyphro, Apology and Crito) narrating the philosopher's trial and execution in 399 BC.

Summary

The dialogue is framed as a conversation in Phlius between Phaedo of Elis, who was present at Socrates' death, and Echecrates, to whom Phaedo recounts the events of the final day. In the prison cell, Socrates, shortly before drinking the hemlock, argues with his companions Simmias and Cebes that the soul is immortal and that the philosopher should welcome death as the liberation of the soul from the body.

Arguments for immortality

Socrates offers a series of arguments. The cyclical argument holds that opposites come from opposites, so the living must come from the dead as the dead come from the living. The argument from recollection claims that learning is really remembering knowledge the soul possessed before birth — for example, our recognition of equality itself — proving the soul's pre-existence. The affinity argument likens the soul to the invisible, divine and unchanging Forms rather than to the composite, perishable body. The final argument rests on the Theory of Forms: the soul, by its nature, brings life and so cannot admit death, just as fire cannot admit cold.

Objections from Simmias (that the soul might be merely a harmony of the body) and Cebes (that the soul might outlast many bodies yet still wear out) are answered before Socrates describes the fate of souls after death in a closing myth. The dialogue ends with Socrates drinking the poison calmly; Phaedo calls him "of all men of his time, the best, the wisest, and the most just."

Influence

The Phaedo was among the most influential texts of ancient philosophy, shaping Platonic, Neoplatonic and early Christian thinking about the soul. Its account of Socrates' death became the archetypal image of the philosopher facing death without fear.
