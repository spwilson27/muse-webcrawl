Episteme

Episteme (Ancient Greek: ἐπιστήμη, epistēmē, 'knowledge; science') is a philosophical term that refers to a principled system of understanding, or scientific knowledge. It differs from techne, which refers to craft or know-how, and from doxa, which refers to common belief or opinion.

In ancient Greek philosophy

Plato distinguished episteme, knowledge of the unchanging Forms, from doxa, opinion about the changing sensible world. In the Theaetetus he explored the definition of knowledge as justified true belief. Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics, classified episteme as one of five intellectual virtues, concerned with necessary and eternal truths demonstrable through reasoning.

Foucault's usage

The French philosopher Michel Foucault used episteme in a specialised sense in The Order of Things (1966) to denote the historical a priori that grounds knowledge and its discourses in a particular epoch: the total set of relations that, at a given period, unite the discursive practices giving rise to epistemological figures, sciences, and formalised systems. For Foucault, successive epistemes — such as the Renaissance, classical, and modern epistemes — define what can count as knowledge in their respective periods.
