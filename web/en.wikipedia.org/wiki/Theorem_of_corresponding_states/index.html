Theorem of corresponding states

Law (theorem) of corresponding states
Proposed by: Johannes Diderik van der Waals, 1873/1880
Statement: all fluids, when compared at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure, have approximately the same compressibility factor and behave alike
Key quantities: reduced temperature Tr = T/Tc, reduced pressure Pr = P/Pc, reduced volume Vr = V/Vc

The theorem of corresponding states (also called the law of corresponding states) is a principle of thermodynamics, first articulated by Johannes Diderik van der Waals, according to which all fluids behave similarly when compared at the same reduced temperature and reduced pressure — that is, temperature and pressure scaled by each substance's critical temperature and critical pressure.

Formulation

Starting from the van der Waals equation of state, whose constants depend on the gas, one can rewrite the equation in terms of reduced variables:

Pr = P / Pc,   Vr = V / Vc,   Tr = T / Tc

The equation then takes a universal form containing no substance-specific parameters:

(Pr + 3/Vr2)(3Vr − 1) = 8Tr

It follows that, within the van der Waals model, any two fluids at the same reduced pressure and reduced temperature have the same reduced volume — they are in "corresponding states".

Compressibility factor

In modern practical form, the principle is usually stated through the compressibility factor Z = PV/(RT). Experiment shows that Z for most simple fluids is, to a useful approximation, a universal function of reduced temperature and reduced pressure: Z = Z(Tr, Pr). This underlies the generalised compressibility charts (such as the Nelson–Obert charts), widely used in chemical engineering to estimate gas densities and fugacities when detailed volumetric data are lacking.

Accuracy and refinements

The two-parameter law works best for simple, roughly spherical, non-polar molecules such as argon, nitrogen, and the light hydrocarbons. Polar and associating fluids deviate systematically. Kenneth Pitzer introduced a third parameter, the acentric factor ω, characterising the non-sphericity of the molecule; in the Pitzer correlations Z is expressed as a base value for simple fluids plus a correction proportional to ω, greatly improving accuracy for engineering calculations.

Significance

Beyond its practical use, the law was historically important evidence that the same molecular laws govern all substances, and it remains an elegant example of scaling: near the critical point, fluids of very different chemical identity exhibit nearly universal behaviour.
