Path loss

Path loss
FieldTelecommunications, radio engineering
SymbolL, PL
UnitDecibel (dB)
Key modelFriis transmission equation; log-distance path loss model

Path loss, or path attenuation, is the reduction in power density (attenuation) of an electromagnetic wave as it propagates through space. Path loss is a major component in the analysis and design of the link budget of a telecommunication system.

The term is commonly used in wireless communications and signal propagation. Path loss may be due to many effects, such as free-space loss, refraction, diffraction, reflection, aperture-medium coupling loss, and absorption. Path loss is also influenced by terrain contours, environment (urban or rural, vegetation and foliage), propagation medium (dry or moist air), the distance between the transmitter and the receiver, and the height and location of antennas.

Free-space path loss

In free space, with no obstacles to cause reflection or diffraction, the path loss between two isotropic antennas follows the inverse-square law. The free-space path loss in decibels is given by the Friis transmission formula: FSPL(dB) = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) + 20 log10(4π/c), where d is the distance between antennas, f is the frequency, and c is the speed of light. Doubling the distance adds about 6 dB of loss, and doubling the frequency also adds about 6 dB.

Path loss exponent

In practical environments, path loss is often modelled with the log-distance path loss model, in which the loss grows as the distance raised to a path loss exponent n. In free space n = 2; in urban areas with shadowing and multipath, n typically ranges from 2.7 to 4.5, and inside buildings it can be higher still. Empirical models such as the Okumura model, the Hata model, and the COST 231 model are widely used for cellular network planning.

Causes

Path loss in real environments arises from geometric spreading of the wavefront, atmospheric absorption (notably by oxygen and water vapour at millimetre-wave frequencies), rain fade, diffraction around obstacles, reflection and scattering from buildings and terrain, and penetration losses through walls and foliage.

Applications

Accurate path loss prediction is essential for coverage planning in cellular networks, Wi-Fi deployment, satellite link design, and radio broadcasting. See also link budget, fading, and Friis transmission equation.
