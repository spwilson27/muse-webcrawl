Cinnabar is the bright scarlet to brick-red form of mercury(II) sulfide (HgS). It is the most common ore of mercury and the historic source of the brilliant red pigment vermilion. The name derives (through Latin cinnabaris) from Greek kinnabari, a word of uncertain, possibly Persian, origin once applied to several red substances including dragon's blood resin.

Occurrence

Cinnabar forms in low-temperature hydrothermal veins near recent volcanic rocks and hot springs. Famous deposits include Almadén in Spain (worked since Roman times), Idrija in Slovenia, Monte Amiata in Italy, and deposits in China (where the mineral and its pigment held special cultural significance), Kyrgyzstan and the western United States.

Uses and hazards

Roasting cinnabar in air releases mercury vapour, which is condensed to the metal — the industrial basis of mercury production for millennia. As vermilion, ground cinnabar coloured paintings, lacquerware and seals from antiquity onward, though it can darken with age. Because cinnabar is a mercury compound, it is toxic if inhaled as dust or heated; its past medicinal use in Chinese and Ayurvedic traditions is accordingly controversial. Mercury mining at Almadén and Idrija is inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
