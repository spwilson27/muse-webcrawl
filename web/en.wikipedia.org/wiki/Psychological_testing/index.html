Psychological testing

Psychological testing, also called psychological assessment, is the field of study that deals with the design, administration and interpretation of standardised procedures for measuring samples of behaviour, abilities, attitudes and other psychological characteristics. Psychological tests are used by psychologists in clinical, educational, occupational and research settings to understand individuals, make diagnoses, guide treatment and predict future behaviour.

History

The modern era of mental testing is usually traced to the work of Francis Galton in the nineteenth century, who attempted to measure individual differences in sensory and motor abilities, and to James McKeen Cattell, who coined the term "mental test". In 1905 Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon published the first successful intelligence scale, designed to identify schoolchildren in Paris who needed special educational help. Lewis Terman's revision at Stanford University produced the Stanford–Binet Intelligence Scales, and during the First World War group tests such as Army Alpha and Army Beta were used to classify large numbers of recruits.

Personality assessment developed along a separate track, from early questionnaire measures such as the Woodworth Personal Data Sheet to inventories like the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI) and projective techniques such as the Rorschach inkblot test and the Thematic Apperception Test.

Types of tests

Tests are commonly classified by what they measure and how they are administered:

  • Intelligence and achievement tests measure cognitive abilities and acquired knowledge, such as the Wechsler scales and standardised school examinations.
  • Personality tests assess traits, using either objective inventories with fixed response formats or projective techniques with ambiguous stimuli.
  • Neuropsychological tests evaluate brain function, memory, attention and executive functioning, often after injury or illness.
  • Clinical and diagnostic instruments screen for symptoms of mental disorders and support differential diagnosis.
  • Vocational and aptitude tests are used in personnel selection and career counselling.

Psychometric properties

The quality of a test is judged by its reliability (consistency of scores) and validity (whether it measures what it claims to measure and supports the inferences drawn from it). Standardisation requires uniform administration procedures and norms derived from representative samples. Professional standards, such as the Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing, govern test development, fairness and use.

Criticism and ethics

Psychological testing has been criticised for cultural bias, misuse of results, invasion of privacy and over-reliance on scores in high-stakes decisions such as hiring and school placement. Ethical guidelines require informed consent, confidentiality of results, use by qualified professionals and attention to the limitations of test scores.
