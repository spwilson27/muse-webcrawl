Structural adjustment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural adjustment is a set of economic reforms that a country must adhere to in order to secure a loan from the International Monetary Fund or the World Bank. The programmes, applied extensively to developing countries during the debt crises of the 1980s and 1990s, typically required fiscal austerity, currency devaluation, trade liberalisation, privatisation of state enterprises, deregulation, and the removal of subsidies and price controls — the package later summarised (and criticised) as the Washington Consensus.

The rationale was that balance-of-payments crises reflected structural distortions that financing alone could not cure; the loan conditions were meant to restore growth and creditworthiness. The record is fiercely disputed: adjustment coincided with a “lost decade” in much of Africa and Latin America, rising poverty in adjusting countries (UNICEF's Adjustment with a Human Face, 1987, was an early internal critique), and later acknowledgements by the IMF and Bank that capital-account liberalisation and over-rapid privatisation were mishandled in places. Successor frameworks (Poverty Reduction Strategy Papers from 1999) promised country ownership and social protection, and twenty-first-century IMF lending has placed more weight on social spending floors, though conditionality remains a live controversy wherever programmes are negotiated.
