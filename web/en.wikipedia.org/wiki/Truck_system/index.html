Truck system

Truck system
DefinitionPayment of wages in goods or in tokens redeemable only at the employer's shop
Associated withCompany towns, mining and mill communities, 18th–19th centuries
Suppressed byTruck Acts (Britain, from 1831); similar laws in the US and elsewhere

A truck system is an arrangement under which employees are paid wholly or partly in goods, or in tokens or credit ("scrip") redeemable only at a shop owned by the employer, rather than in legal tender. Because the company store held a monopoly, it could charge inflated prices, leaving workers indebted and effectively bound to their employer—a pattern captured in the song line "I owe my soul to the company store".

History

Truck payment was widespread in early industrial Britain—in mining, ironworks, mills and remote worksites—and in North American mining camps, logging towns and plantations. Employers defended it where coin was scarce and shops distant, but abuses, including payment in overpriced or shoddy goods and the truck shop's role in debt peonage, made it a target of reform.

Legislation

In Britain, a series of Truck Acts beginning in 1831 progressively required wages in specified industries to be paid in coin and restricted deductions. In the United States, state laws and later federal labour standards similarly curtailed scrip payment, though company-town scrip persisted into the 20th century. The International Labour Organization's Protection of Wages Convention (1949) prohibits payment in kind except under strict conditions.
