Aleut (Unangam Tunuu) is the language of the Aleut (Unangax̂) people, spoken in the Aleutian and Pribilof Islands of Alaska and the Commander Islands of Russia. It is the sole member of the Aleut branch of the Eskimo–Aleut language family, having diverged from the Eskimo (Inuit–Yupik) languages several thousand years ago. Aleut is severely endangered, with most fluent speakers elderly.

Dialects

Aleut's dialects form a chain along the island arc: Eastern Aleut (spoken on the Alaska Peninsula and islands east to Unimak and in the Pribilofs), Atkan (on Atka and Bering Island), and the now nearly extinct Attuan (formerly on Attu, transplanted to Bering Island, where it developed into a mixed language with Russian, sometimes called Copper Island Aleut). Western and eastern varieties differ in phonology and vocabulary, with limited mutual intelligibility across the chain.

Phonology and grammar

Aleut has a small consonant inventory with a three-vowel system (a, i, u, each short and long). It is a polysynthetic, ergative–absolutive language: verbs carry much of the information of a sentence, and a distinctive feature is its anaphoric system of reference tracking, in which nouns take "anaphoric" forms when their referent has been mentioned in a preceding clause, organizing discourse in place of the subordinate clauses familiar from European languages. Word order is subject–object–verb.

Writing and documentation

Aleut was first written in the 1820s–30s by the Russian Orthodox priest Ivan Veniaminov (later St. Innocent), who devised a Cyrillic orthography, translated religious texts, and compiled a grammar and dictionary. The modern practical orthography for Alaskan Aleut, using Latin letters, was developed in the 1970s at the Alaska Native Language Center, notably by Knut Bergsland, whose Aleut Dictionary (1994) is a landmark of documentation.

Revitalization

Aleut is taught in some island schools and through community and university programs, and dictionaries, grammars and recordings support ongoing revitalization efforts, though intergenerational transmission remains fragile.
