Karl Paul Polanyi (1886–1964) was an Austro-Hungarian economic historian and political economist best known for his book The Great Transformation (1944), a sweeping critique of the self-regulating market and of 19th-century market liberalism.

Life

Born in Vienna and raised in Budapest, Polanyi was active in Hungarian radical politics, served in the First World War, and worked as a journalist in interwar Vienna, where he participated in the socialist calculation debate. He emigrated to England in 1933, then to the United States in 1940, teaching at Bennington College and later Columbia University. His brother Michael Polanyi became a noted chemist and philosopher.

Ideas

Polanyi argued that economies are normally "embedded" in social relations, and that the attempt to create a self-regulating market — treating land, labour, and money as commodities, his "fictitious commodities" — provoked a protective double movement of social self-defence, with outcomes ranging from the welfare state to fascism. In economic anthropology he distinguished the formal and substantive meanings of "economic" and analysed reciprocity, redistribution, and market exchange as forms of integration. His work strongly influenced economic sociology and has been widely revived since the 2008 financial crisis.
