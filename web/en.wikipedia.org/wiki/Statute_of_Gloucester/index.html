Statute of Gloucester

The Statute of Gloucester (6 Edw. 1) is an English statute enacted in 1278 under King Edward I, named for the city of Gloucester where the parliament that passed it was held. It is a significant piece of medieval legislation concerning land law and legal procedure.

Contents

The statute contained seventeen chapters. Among its provisions, it introduced the remedy of damages in certain real actions, regulated wardship and the lord's rights, and required that plaintiffs recovering less than a set amount in personal actions would not recover costs. It also addressed the abuse of distraint and set out rules on the recovery of dower.

Legacy

Parts of the Statute of Gloucester remained influential in English common law for centuries; its costs provision is considered an ancestor of later rules on litigation costs. The statute was largely repealed in the 19th and 20th centuries by statute law revision acts, though fragments and its doctrinal effects persisted.
