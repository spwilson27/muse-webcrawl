Marginal revolution

The Marginal Revolution is the name given to the near-simultaneous but independent development, in the 1870s, of the theory of marginal utility by three economists: William Stanley Jevons in England, Carl Menger in Austria, and Léon Walras in Switzerland. It marked the transition from classical economics to neoclassical economics and is generally regarded as one of the founding events of modern economic theory.

Background

Classical economists such as Adam Smith, David Ricardo, and John Stuart Mill explained the value of goods largely by the labour or costs embodied in producing them. This labour theory of value struggled to explain the paradox of value: why water, essential to life, is cheap, while diamonds, frivolous, are expensive.

Precursors of marginal analysis included Johann Heinrich von Thünen, Jules Dupuit, Hermann Heinrich Gossen (whose 1854 Entwicklung der Gesetze des menschlichen Verkehrs anticipated much of the theory but was ignored until rediscovered), and Antoine Augustin Cournot.

The three founders

William Stanley Jevons

Jevons published The Theory of Political Economy in 1871, arguing that value depends entirely on utility, and that labour affects value only indirectly through its effect on utility. He developed the notion of "final degree of utility" — the utility of an additional increment of a good — diminishing as consumption increases.

Carl Menger

Menger's Grundsätze der Volkswirtschaftslehre (Principles of Economics, 1871) derived value from the satisfaction of human needs, emphasised the subjective nature of value, and explained the prices of factors of production by imputation from the value of consumer goods. Menger's work founded the Austrian School.

Léon Walras

Walras, in Éléments d'économie politique pure (1874–77), built a mathematical system of general equilibrium in which prices in all markets are determined simultaneously, with "rareté" (his term for marginal utility) at the centre.

Key ideas

  • Marginal utility: the value of a good is determined by the utility of the last (marginal) unit consumed, resolving the paradox of value — water has low marginal utility because it is abundant, diamonds high because they are scarce.
  • Subjective value: value originates in individual needs and perceptions, not in intrinsic properties of goods.
  • Marginal analysis: economic decisions are made at the margin — comparing incremental costs and benefits.
  • Methodological individualism: aggregate outcomes are explained from the choices of individuals.

Second generation and legacy

A second generation — including Friedrich von Wieser (who coined "marginal utility", Grenznutzen), Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, Alfred Marshall, John Bates Clark, Francis Ysidro Edgeworth, and Vilfredo Pareto — consolidated the revolution. Marshall's synthesis in Principles of Economics (1890) combined marginal utility with classical cost analysis. Marginalism became the core of neoclassical economics and remains fundamental to microeconomic theory today.
