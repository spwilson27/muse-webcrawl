Public international law

Public international law (also called the law of nations) is the body of rules, norms, and standards that applies between sovereign states and other actors recognized as subjects of international law, such as international organizations. It governs matters including the use of force, diplomatic relations, the law of the sea, international trade, human rights, the conduct of armed conflict, and the protection of the environment. It is distinct from private international law (conflict of laws), which concerns which domestic legal system applies to private disputes with cross-border elements.

Sources

The classic statement of the sources of international law is Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice, which directs the Court to apply: (a) international conventions (treaties); (b) international custom, as evidence of a general practice accepted as law; (c) the general principles of law recognized by civilized nations; and, as subsidiary means, (d) judicial decisions and the teachings of the most highly qualified publicists. Customary international law requires both consistent state practice and opinio juris — the belief that the practice is legally obligatory. Certain norms, known as jus cogens, are peremptory: they permit no derogation and override conflicting treaty obligations; examples include the prohibitions of genocide, slavery, and aggression.

Treaty law is codified principally in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969), which governs conclusion, interpretation, and termination, and enshrines the maxim pacta sunt servanda — agreements must be kept in good faith.

History

Rules governing relations between political communities are ancient, from Mesopotamian treaties to Roman jus gentium. The modern system is conventionally dated to the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which consolidated the sovereign territorial state. Hugo Grotius, whose De jure belli ac pacis (1625) systematized the law of war and peace, is often called the father of international law; the nineteenth century added congress diplomacy, arbitration, and the first humanitarian conventions. After the catastrophe of two world wars, the UN Charter (1945) prohibited the threat or use of force except in self-defense or under Security Council authorization, and the system expanded to embrace decolonization, human rights, and global economic regulation.

Subjects and enforcement

States remain the principal subjects of international law, but international organizations possess derivative legal personality (affirmed by the ICJ in the Reparations opinion of 1949), and individuals bear rights under human rights treaties and duties under international criminal law, as applied by tribunals from Nuremberg to the International Criminal Court.

Enforcement is decentralized compared with domestic systems: there is no world police force. Compliance is secured through reciprocity, reputation, domestic courts, international tribunals whose jurisdiction usually depends on state consent, countermeasures, and — uniquely — collective enforcement authorized by the UN Security Council under Chapter VII of the Charter. Critics have long questioned whether international law is "really" law, in the tradition of John Austin's command theory; most practitioners answer that states do in fact treat its rules as binding and routinely comply with them.

Principal fields

  • Law of the sea — codified in the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (1982): territorial seas, exclusive economic zones, freedom of navigation.
  • International humanitarian law — the Geneva Conventions of 1949 and their Additional Protocols, governing armed conflict.
  • International human rights law — the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948) and the International Covenants of 1966.
  • International economic law — the WTO agreements and bilateral investment treaties.
  • Diplomatic and consular law — the Vienna Conventions of 1961 and 1963.
  • International environmental law — framework conventions on climate change, biodiversity, and ozone depletion.
