The Great Fire of London was a major conflagration that swept through central parts of London from Sunday, 2 September to Thursday, 6 September 1666. The fire gutted the medieval City of London inside the old Roman city wall, consuming some 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, St Paul's Cathedral and most of the buildings of the City authorities. It threatened, but did not reach, the aristocratic district of Westminster. An estimated 70,000–80,000 inhabitants — the great majority of the City's population — were made homeless.

Outbreak and spread

The fire began shortly after midnight on Sunday, 2 September, in Thomas Farriner's bakery (or oven) in Pudding Lane, near London Bridge. Farriner maintained that he had extinguished his ovens, and suspicion for centuries fell on foreigners and Catholics; a disturbed French watchmaker, Robert Hubert, confessed and was hanged, though he was probably innocent. A strong east wind, a summer drought that had dried out the timber city, and the Lord Mayor Sir Thomas Bloodworth's hesitation to demolish houses to create firebreaks let the blaze spread quickly through warehouses of oil, tallow, hemp and pitch along the Thames.

Firefighting and end

Contemporary techniques — leather buckets, water squirts, fire hooks and engines pumping river water — were overwhelmed. King Charles II and his brother James, Duke of York, took direct command, ordering demolitions and the use of gunpowder to blow up houses in the fire's path. The wind dropped on Tuesday night and by Thursday the fire was effectively contained; Samuel Pepys's diary gives a famous eyewitness account of the disaster, including burying his wine and Parmesan cheese to protect them.

Aftermath and rebuilding

Remarkably few deaths were recorded, though modern historians suggest the toll among the poor went uncounted. The City was rebuilt on largely the same street plan under the Rebuilding Acts, with houses now of brick and stone: Christopher Wren designed the new St Paul's Cathedral and some 50 parish churches, and the Monument was erected near Pudding Lane to commemorate the fire. Proposals by Wren and John Evelyn for radical new street plans were rejected on property-rights grounds. The fire also brought lasting changes in building regulation and insurance, including the founding of the first fire-insurance companies in its wake.
