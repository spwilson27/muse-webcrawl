Red River Rebellion
Date: 1869–1870
Location: Red River Colony, Rupert's Land (now Manitoba, Canada)
Leader: Louis Riel
Result: Manitoba Act 1870; creation of the province of Manitoba

The Red River Rebellion (also known as the Red River Resistance) was a sequence of events in 1869–1870 in the Red River Colony, in present-day Manitoba, Canada. It began when the Hudson's Bay Company transferred Rupert's Land to the Dominion of Canada without consulting the local population — largely Métis — whose provisional government, led by Louis Riel, resisted the transfer until their rights were negotiated.

Background

The Red River Settlement, founded in 1812 at the confluence of the Red and Assiniboine rivers, was home to about 12,000 people, most of them French-speaking Catholic Métis. When Canada acquired Rupert's Land in 1869, Canadian surveyors arrived before the transfer was even effective, and the appointed lieutenant-governor, William McDougall, was barred from entering the territory.

The provisional government

Riel's followers seized Upper Fort Garry in November 1869, and a convention of English- and French-speaking parishes formed a provisional government with Riel as president. It drew up a List of Rights and negotiated directly with Ottawa. Tensions peaked with the trial and execution, on 4 March 1870, of Thomas Scott, an Orangeman from Ontario who had taken up arms against the government — an act that inflamed opinion in Ontario and haunted Riel for the rest of his life.

Outcome

The Manitoba Act, passed in May 1870, created the small province of Manitoba with guarantees for French-language rights, denominational schools, and 1.4 million acres of land for Métis families. A military expedition under Colonel Garnet Wolseley arrived in August 1870 to assert Canadian authority; Riel fled to the United States. The events remain foundational to Métis national identity, and Riel is now widely regarded as a founder of Manitoba.
