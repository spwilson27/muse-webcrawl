Financial Interest and Syndication Rules

The Financial Interest and Syndication Rules, widely known as the fin-syn rules, were a set of regulations enforced by the United States Federal Communications Commission (FCC) from 1970 to 1993. The rules prohibited the three major television networks — ABC, CBS, and NBC — from owning financial interests in the television programmes they aired in prime time, and from engaging in the domestic syndication of programmes after their network runs.

Purpose and effects

The rules were intended to prevent the networks from monopolising television production and distribution, and to encourage independent production companies. In practice, fin-syn shifted bargaining power to outside studios and production companies, and helped large independent suppliers — including companies built by producers such as Norman Lear and Lorimar — flourish. Combined with the Prime Time Access Rule, fin-syn reshaped the economics of American television for two decades.

Repeal

With the rise of cable television and a fourth network, Fox (which obtained waivers and was eventually exempted), the FCC relaxed the rules in 1991 and repealed them entirely in 1993, with a phase-out period ending in 1995. Repeal opened the way to vertical integration: networks merged with or were acquired by major studios (for example, Disney's purchase of ABC and the reunification of CBS with Viacom/Paramount), and in-house production came to dominate prime time.
