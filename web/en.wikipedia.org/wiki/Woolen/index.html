Woolen

Woolen (American English) or woollen (British English) is a type of yarn and the cloth made from it, produced from carded wool in which the fibres are not combed or aligned before spinning. Woolen yarns are soft, fuzzy, and bulky, and the fabrics made from them are warm, loosely woven, and less durable than worsteds. The term is contrasted with worsted, in which longer fibres are combed parallel and spun into a smooth, dense yarn.

Manufacture

Woolen spinning begins with carding — passing scoured wool between rollers covered with fine wire teeth — which opens the fibres into a fluffy web but leaves them pointing in many directions. The carded web is condensed into roving and spun with comparatively low twist on a spinning mule or its modern successors. Because the fibres are short and disordered, woolen yarn traps air, giving excellent insulation but a weaker, hairier thread. Typical woolen cloths include tweeds, flannels, blankets, meltons, and many knitting yarns.

Woolen system versus worsted system

The woolen system uses shorter staple wool and skips the combing and gilling steps that align fibres in the worsted system. Woolen fabrics consequently have a soft, matte, slightly fuzzy surface and do not hold a sharp crease; worsteds such as gabardine and serge are smoother, stronger, and more lustrous. Spinners measure woolen yarn fineness by the "cut" or "run" systems in some regions, whereas worsted yarn uses its own count system.

History

Woolen cloth was the backbone of the medieval and early-modern English wool trade, centred on regions such as the Cotswolds, East Anglia, and the West Riding of Yorkshire, and later of mill towns in New England. The industry was a major driver of enclosure, water-powered fulling mills, and early factory production. Although synthetic fibres displaced wool in much mass-market clothing during the twentieth century, woolen cloths remain standard for high-quality blankets, tweeds, and winter knitwear.
