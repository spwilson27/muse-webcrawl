Turbidity current

A turbidity current is a rapidly moving, sediment-laden density current that flows downward under clear water because the suspended sediment makes it denser than the surrounding fluid. Turbidity currents occur mainly under water, on the slopes of lakes and oceans, and are the principal mechanism by which sand and mud are transported from shallow continental shelves into the deep sea.

Behaviour and deposits

Turbidity currents may be triggered by earthquakes, storm waves, slope failure, or floods of sediment-laden river water. They accelerate down submarine canyons — sometimes reaching speeds of tens of kilometres per hour — eroding the seabed and incorporating more sediment, then spread out across the abyssal plain as they slow. As a current wanes, progressively finer sediment settles out, producing a characteristic graded bed called a turbidite, often organised into the sequence of divisions described by the Bouma sequence. Repeated events build vast deep-sea fans such as the Bengal Fan, the largest on Earth.

Evidence and significance

The classic evidence for powerful turbidity currents came from the 1929 Grand Banks earthquake off Newfoundland, after which a series of transatlantic telegraph cables broke in sequence downslope — the timings allowing scientists to estimate the current's speed. Turbidity currents remain a hazard to submarine cables and pipelines, and their ancient deposits (turbidites) form important hydrocarbon reservoirs and archives of past earthquakes and climate.
