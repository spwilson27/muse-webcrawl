Collateral estoppel

Also known as: Issue preclusion
Field: Civil procedure / common law

Collateral estoppel, also known as issue preclusion, is a common-law doctrine that prevents a party from relitigating an issue of fact or law that was actually litigated and necessarily decided in a prior action between the same parties or their privies, and that resulted in a final judgment on the merits.

Elements

Courts generally require that (1) the issue in the second action is identical to the issue decided in the first; (2) the issue was actually litigated and necessarily determined; (3) there was a final judgment; and (4) the party against whom preclusion is asserted had a full and fair opportunity to litigate. Unlike res judicata (claim preclusion), collateral estoppel applies to a specific issue, not the whole claim.

Mutuality and nonmutual use

Traditionally, only parties to the first action could invoke it (mutuality). Modern U.S. law, following cases such as Blonder-Tongue v. University of Illinois Foundation and Parklane Hosiery v. Shore, permits defensive and, within limits, offensive nonmutual collateral estoppel by a new party.

Purpose

The doctrine promotes judicial economy, consistency, and finality, sparing litigants and courts from duplicative litigation.
