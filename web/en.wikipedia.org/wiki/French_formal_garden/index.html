French formal garden

The French formal garden, also called the jardin à la française or garden in the French manner, is a style of garden based on symmetry and the principle of imposing order on nature. It reached its zenith in the 17th century in the gardens of André Le Nôtre, above all the Gardens of Versailles created for Louis XIV.

Characteristics

  • A central axis (perspective) extending from the house to the horizon, organizing the whole composition
  • Parterres of clipped boxwood embroidery (broderie) and coloured gravel, viewed from the elevated windows of the château
  • Geometric lawns, reflecting pools, and canals mirroring the sky
  • Bosquets (formal groves), clipped topiary, and allées radiating through woodland
  • Terraces, staircases, fountains, and statuary expressing classical mythology and the glory of the owner

History

The style grew out of the French Renaissance gardens inspired by Italian models, such as those of Catherine de' Medici. In the mid-17th century, the gardener André Le Nôtre (1613–1700) perfected the style at Vaux-le-Vicomte (for Nicolas Fouquet, 1656–1661) and then at Versailles, where the gardens became the greatest symbol of the absolute monarchy. Le Nôtre's principles spread across Europe, influencing gardens at Het Loo, Schönbrunn, Peterhof, and Hampton Court.

Decline and legacy

In the 18th century the informal English landscape garden reacted against French formality, though the jardin à la française remained the model for ceremonial and civic spaces. Elements of the style were revived in the 19th and 20th centuries, and historic examples such as Versailles, Vaux-le-Vicomte, the Tuileries, and Villandry are maintained as national monuments.
