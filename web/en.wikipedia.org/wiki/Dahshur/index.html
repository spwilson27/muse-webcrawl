Dahshur (Arabic: دهشور) is an ancient Egyptian royal necropolis and village in the Markaz El Badrashin, Giza Governorate, located about 40 kilometres (25 miles) south of Cairo on the west bank of the Nile. It is best known for several pyramids of the Old and Middle Kingdoms, above all the Bent Pyramid and the Red Pyramid built for Pharaoh Sneferu (reigned c. 2613–2589 BC), which document the experimental transition from the step pyramid to the true smooth-sided pyramid. The site forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage Site "Memphis and its Necropolis".

The pyramids of Sneferu

Sneferu, founder of the Fourth Dynasty, built three pyramids — at Meidum, and two at Dahshur. The Bent Pyramid begins at a steep angle of about 54 degrees but changes roughly halfway up to a shallower 43 degrees, giving it its distinctive bent profile; it retains more of its smooth Tura limestone casing than any other pyramid. Egyptologists generally interpret the change as a response to structural stress or cracking in the internal chambers. The Red Pyramid, named for the reddish hue of its core stones, was then built at the shallower angle throughout, becoming the first successful true pyramid and, at about 104 metres high, the third-largest in Egypt after those of Khufu and Khafre at Giza.

Middle Kingdom pyramids

Twelfth-Dynasty kings returned to Dahshur. Senusret III built a pyramid complex there with satellite pyramids of royal women; Amenemhat II built the so-called White Pyramid; and Amenemhat III built the Black Pyramid, named for its dark mudbrick core, whose labyrinthine interior and still-intact polished granite pyramidion are notable. In the Thirteenth Dynasty, King Hor was buried near Amenemhat III's pyramid; his largely intact burial, including the famous wooden ka statue of the king, was excavated in 1894.

The Dahshur treasure

In 1894–95 the French archaeologist Jacques de Morgan excavated the gallery tombs of princesses beside the pyramid of Senusret III and found the "Dahshur treasure": exquisite Middle Kingdom jewellery — pectorals inlaid with carnelian, lapis lazuli, turquoise and amethyst bearing the names of Senusret III and Amenemhat III — now masterpieces of the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

Modern history

Because the area lay within a restricted military zone for much of the later twentieth century, Dahshur was closed to tourists until 1996 and remains far less visited than Giza or Saqqara; the interior of the Bent Pyramid was opened to visitors only in 2019. The adjacent village of Dahshur is also known for carpet-making schools.
