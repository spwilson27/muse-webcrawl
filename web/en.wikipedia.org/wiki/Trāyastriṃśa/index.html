Trāyastriṃśa

Trāyastriṃśa (Sanskrit: त्रायस्त्रिंश; Pali: Tāvatiṃsa) is the second of the six heavens of the desire realm (Kāmadhātu) in Buddhist cosmology, and a realm of devas also recognized in Hinduism and Jainism. Its name means "the Thirty-Three", referring to the thirty-three gods who inhabit it, and it is said to lie on the summit of Mount Meru (Sumeru), the axis of the world.

Description

Trāyastriṃśa is ruled by Śakra (Pali: Sakka), the Buddhist form of the Vedic god Indra, who presides over the thirty-three devas from his palace, the Vejayanta. The heaven is described as a great city with golden walls, gardens such as the Nandana grove, the wish-fulfilling tree (Pāricchattaka), and the Sudhammā hall where the gods assemble. A day and a night there last a hundred human years, and its inhabitants live for a thousand such years, though they remain within the cycle of rebirth (saṃsāra) and are subject to death and decline.

In Buddhist tradition

The heaven figures prominently in Buddhist narrative. According to tradition, the Buddha ascended to Tāvatiṃsa during a rains retreat to teach the Abhidhamma to the devas, including his mother Māyā, who had been reborn there; his descent at Sankassa is celebrated in the festival of Devarohana. The Buddha's disciple Maudgalyāyana (Moggallāna) was also said to have visited the heaven. The devas of Trāyastriṃśa are recurrent figures in the Jātaka tales and the Pali Canon, where Sakka frequently intervenes in human affairs and questions the Buddha.

The thirty-three gods

The number thirty-three descends from the Vedic pantheon — traditionally enumerated as the eight Vasus, eleven Rudras, twelve Ādityas, plus Indra and Prajāpati — and the heaven is a shared element of Indic religions, appearing in Hindu and Jain cosmology as well.
