The turnip or white turnip (Brassica rapa subsp. rapa) is a root vegetable commonly grown in temperate climates worldwide for its white, fleshy taproot. The small, tender varieties are grown for human consumption, while larger varieties are grown as feed for livestock. The leaves, known as turnip greens, are also eaten, particularly in the southern United States.

History

Wild forms of the turnip and its relatives, the mustards and radishes, are found over western and central Asia. The turnip was domesticated in the eastern Mediterranean and was well known to the Greeks and Romans; Pliny the Elder praised it as an important food for the poor. In the Middle Ages it was a staple crop in northern Europe before the potato displaced it in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. In the British Isles, carved turnips were traditional Halloween lanterns before pumpkins took over in North America.

Description and cultivation

The turnip's taproot is roughly globular, white below ground and often purple, red, or greenish where exposed to sunlight. It grows best in cool weather in loose, well-drained loam, and matures quickly — often in 60 days — making it a common catch crop and cover crop. The root is rich in vitamin C and is eaten raw in salads, roasted, mashed, or in soups and stews such as the Scottish dish clapshot (with potatoes) and as a traditional accompaniment to haggis.
