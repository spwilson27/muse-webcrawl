Cell signaling

Cell signaling (or signal transduction) is the process by which cells communicate with one another and respond to their environment. In multicellular organisms, signaling coordinates growth, development, tissue repair, immunity and homeostasis. A signaling cell produces a chemical or electrical signal — a molecule such as a hormone, neurotransmitter, cytokine or growth factor — which is detected by a receptor on (or in) a target cell, triggering a cascade of intracellular events and a cellular response.

Types of signaling

Signaling is commonly classified by the distance the signal travels. In endocrine signaling, hormones travel through the bloodstream to distant targets. Paracrine signaling affects nearby cells, as at synapses — a special case sometimes called synaptic signaling. Autocrine signaling occurs when a cell responds to its own signals, and juxtacrine signaling requires direct cell-to-cell contact, as in the Notch pathway.

Receptors and pathways

Receptors are usually proteins. Cell-surface receptors bind hydrophilic signals and include G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), receptor tyrosine kinases such as the insulin and epidermal growth factor receptors, and ligand-gated ion channels. Intracellular receptors bind hydrophobic signals that cross the membrane, such as steroid hormones, and typically act as transcription factors. Activation of a receptor often initiates a signal transduction cascade involving second messengers — cyclic AMP, calcium ions, inositol trisphosphate — and phosphorylation cascades such as the MAPK/ERK pathway, which amplify the signal and alter gene expression or cell behaviour.

Importance in medicine

Defects in signaling underlie many diseases: loss of growth control contributes to cancer, insulin signaling failure to diabetes, and immune signaling defects to autoimmune disease. A large fraction of modern drugs act on signaling proteins, especially GPCRs and kinases; targeted cancer therapies such as imatinib work by blocking specific signaling enzymes.
