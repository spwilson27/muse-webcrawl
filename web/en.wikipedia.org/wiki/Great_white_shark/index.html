Great white shark

The great white shark (Carcharodon carcharias), also known as the white shark or white pointer, is a species of large mackerel shark found in the coastal surface waters of all the major oceans. It is one of the largest predatory fish in the world, with mature females reaching about 6.1 metres (20 ft) in length and over 1,900 kg (4,200 lb) in weight.

Description

The great white has a robust, torpedo-shaped body with countershaded coloration — grey above and white below — that camouflages it from both prey below and predators above. Its jaws carry large, triangular, serrated teeth in multiple rows. Like other lamniform sharks, it maintains a body temperature above that of the surrounding water through a counter-current heat exchanger (the rete mirabile), enabling it to remain active in cold seas. It can breach clear of the water when attacking prey from below.

Behaviour and diet

Great whites are apex predators that feed on fish, rays, other sharks, sea turtles, and marine mammals such as seals and sea lions; they also scavenge whale carcasses. Hunting strategies vary regionally: off South Africa they launch high-speed vertical attacks on Cape fur seals, while off California they bite and retreat, letting large prey weaken. The species is highly migratory; tracked individuals have crossed entire ocean basins, including a celebrated migration between South Africa and Australia.

Conservation

The great white is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. It is protected in several countries and was one of the first shark species to receive legal protection, though it remains subject to bycatch, trophy fishing pressure, and culling near swimming beaches. Reliable global population estimates are lacking; regional studies suggest some populations are stable or recovering under protection.

Relationship with humans

Great whites account for more recorded unprovoked attacks on humans than any other shark, yet most encounters are investigatory bites rather than predation attempts. The species' fearsome reputation was cemented by Peter Benchley's 1974 novel Jaws and its film adaptation. Cage diving tourism now gives the species a significant economic value alive in places such as South Africa, Mexico, and Australia.
