Politics of Israel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The politics of Israel operate within a parliamentary democracy without a complete written constitution: the Knesset (a unicameral 120-member parliament elected by nationwide proportional representation with a 3.25 per cent threshold) legislates Basic Laws that the Supreme Court has, since the 1990s “constitutional revolution”, treated as a constitution. The president is a ceremonial head of state; executive power lies with the prime minister and cabinet, which must hold the Knesset's confidence.

Israel's extreme proportionality produces many parties — religious (ultra-Orthodox and religious-Zionist), Arab, secular right (Likud) and centre-left — and coalition governments in every parliament; no party has ever won a majority. Defining cleavages are the Israeli–Palestinian conflict and security policy, religion and state (the rabbinical monopoly on Jewish marriage, military exemption of yeshiva students — struck down by the Supreme Court in 2024 after decades of interim arrangements), and the status of Arab citizens. Five elections between 2019 and 2022 reflected deep polarisation around Benjamin Netanyahu's long tenure and his criminal trial; the 2023 judicial-overhaul legislation triggered mass protests before the Gaza war that began with Hamas's attack of 7 October 2023 reordered politics again. This article describes the institutional framework; fast-moving events are deliberately treated at a structural level.
