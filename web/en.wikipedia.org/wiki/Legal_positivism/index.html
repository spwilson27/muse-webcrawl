Legal positivism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Legal positivism is the thesis in jurisprudence that the existence and content of law depend on social facts — enactment, custom, judicial decision — and not on its merits: there is no necessary connection between law and morality. In its classic command theory (Bentham, John Austin), law is the command of a sovereign backed by sanctions; in its mature 20th-century forms, H. L. A. Hart's The Concept of Law (1961) defines law as the union of primary rules of obligation with secondary rules (of recognition, change and adjudication), and Hans Kelsen's "pure theory" traces validity to a presupposed basic norm (Grundnorm).

Debates

Positivism's rivals are natural law theories (law that is grossly unjust is no law — the formula invoked in post-Nazi German jurisprudence and the Nuremberg debates) and Ronald Dworkin's interpretivism (legal rights include moral principles embedded in institutional history, so judges do not merely apply rules). Within positivism, inclusive ("soft") versions allow a system's rule of recognition to incorporate moral criteria (as constitutions do), while exclusive positivists (Raz) resist. The theory structures debates on civil disobedience, judicial review's legitimacy, and international law's status as law at all (Austin denied it; Hart offered a different analysis).
