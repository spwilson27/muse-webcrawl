The Juan Fernández Islands (Spanish: Archipiélago Juan Fernández) are a sparsely inhabited archipelago in the South Pacific Ocean, politically part of Chile. They lie about 670 kilometres (420 mi) west of the Chilean mainland at roughly the latitude of Valparaíso. The group consists principally of Robinson Crusoe Island, Alejandro Selkirk Island and the small islet of Santa Clara.

History

The islands were discovered in 1574 by the Spanish navigator Juan Fernández. They became a provisioning stop for ships rounding Cape Horn and a refuge for privateers. In 1704 the Scottish sailor Alexander Selkirk was marooned alone on the largest island for more than four years; his story inspired Daniel Defoe's novel Robinson Crusoe (1719). In 1966 Chile renamed the main island Robinson Crusoe Island and the western island Alejandro Selkirk Island to promote tourism.

During the First World War the German cruiser SMS Dresden was scuttled in Cumberland Bay in 1915. In February 2010 a tsunami generated by the Maule earthquake devastated the village of San Juan Bautista, the archipelago's only significant settlement.

Nature

The islands are of volcanic origin, with rugged peaks rising to nearly 1,000 metres. Their isolation has produced a distinctive flora with a very high proportion of endemic species, and the surrounding waters support the Juan Fernández fur seal and important seabird colonies. The archipelago is protected as a Chilean national park (declared 1935) and a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. The local economy depends on fishing, especially for the Juan Fernández lobster, and on tourism.
