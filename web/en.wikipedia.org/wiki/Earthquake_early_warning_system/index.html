Earthquake early warning system

An earthquake early warning (EEW) system is a system of accelerometers, seismometers, communication, computers, and alarms that is devised for quickly notifying adjoining regions of a substantial earthquake while it is in progress. This is not the same as earthquake prediction, which is currently not feasible.

Physical basis

An earthquake produces two main types of body waves. The fast-moving primary waves (P-waves) travel at roughly 6 km/s in the crust and cause little damage; the slower, more destructive secondary waves (S-waves) and surface waves travel at about half that speed. A sensor network near the epicentre can detect the P-waves and, by outrunning the damaging waves with electronic signals, provide a warning of a few seconds to more than a minute for locations farther away, depending on distance from the epicentre.

Deployed systems

Japan operates the world's most developed public system, run by the Japan Meteorological Agency since 2007, which issues warnings via television, radio and mobile phones. Mexico's Seismic Alert System (SASMEX) has served Mexico City since the early 1990s, using sensors along the Guerrero coast. On the west coast of the United States and Canada, the ShakeAlert system delivers alerts through wireless emergency alerts and apps. Taiwan, South Korea, Turkey, Israel and several other countries operate or are developing systems.

Uses of warning time

Even a few seconds of warning allows people to drop, cover and hold on; trains to slow or stop; surgeons to pause; elevators to halt at floors; and industrial systems and gas valves to shut down automatically.

Limitations

There is a "blind zone" near the epicentre where strong shaking arrives before any warning can be issued. Magnitude estimates from the first seconds of P-wave data can saturate for the largest earthquakes, and systems occasionally issue false or missed alerts.
