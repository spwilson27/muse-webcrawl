Business strategy

Business strategy concerns the choices a firm makes about how to compete in a particular market or industry.

Business strategy is the art and science of formulating, implementing, and evaluating cross-functional decisions that enable an organization to achieve its objectives within a specific business unit or market. While corporate strategy concerns the overall scope and direction of an enterprise — which industries to enter, which to exit — business strategy focuses on how the firm competes within a given industry: the sources of its competitive advantage, its positioning relative to rivals, and the configuration of resources and activities that sustain that position.

Levels of strategy

Strategy is commonly described at three levels. Corporate strategy addresses the portfolio of businesses and the allocation of resources among them. Business strategy (or competitive strategy) addresses how each business unit competes. Functional strategy concerns the plans of departments such as marketing, operations, finance, and human resources, which must align with the business-level choices above them.

Generic strategies

Michael Porter, in Competitive Strategy (1980), proposed three generic strategies: cost leadership, achieving the lowest cost structure in the industry; differentiation, offering products or services perceived as unique and commanding a price premium; and focus, serving a narrow segment exceptionally well, either on cost or differentiation. Porter warned that firms failing to commit to one approach risk becoming "stuck in the middle" with below-average profitability.

Strategic analysis

Business strategy draws on a set of analytical tools. The SWOT analysis inventories internal strengths and weaknesses against external opportunities and threats. Porter's five forces analysis examines industry structure: rivalry among incumbents, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, and the bargaining power of buyers and suppliers. The resource-based view, associated with Jay Barney, argues that sustainable advantage flows from resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable, and organized (VRIO). Scenario planning, the BCG growth-share matrix, and value-chain analysis are also widely used.

Formulation and implementation

Strategy formulation involves setting objectives, analysing the competitive environment, and choosing a course of action. Implementation — often regarded as the harder task — requires aligning structure, culture, incentives, and information systems with the chosen strategy. Frameworks such as the balanced scorecard translate strategic objectives into measurable targets across financial, customer, internal-process, and learning perspectives.

Emergent strategy

Henry Mintzberg distinguished deliberate strategy, realised as intended, from emergent strategy, patterns that develop from streams of decisions and adaptations over time. Most real strategies combine the two: a deliberate direction pursued while learning from experience. Related concepts include strategic intent, core competence (Prahalad and Hamel), and blue ocean strategy, which urges firms to create uncontested market space rather than fight over existing demand.
