Posthumous name

A posthumous name is an honorary name given to a person after their death. The practice was especially developed in the East Asian cultural sphere — China, Korea, Japan, and Vietnam — where emperors, kings, nobles, and officials received posthumous names (Chinese: 諡號, shìhào) that evaluated and summarized their lives and reigns.

Chinese tradition

In imperial China, posthumous names originated in the Zhou dynasty. A name of one or two characters assessed the deceased's conduct: for example, Wen (文, "civil/cultured") was favourable, while Yang (煬, "cruel") was condemnatory. Over time, emperors' posthumous names grew longer and more uniformly flattering; by the Ming and Qing dynasties they could run to more than twenty characters. Emperors are consequently often known to history by their temple names or era names instead.

Other countries

Korean kings of Goryeo and Joseon received elaborate posthumous names recorded in the royal annals. Japanese emperors receive posthumous names derived from their reign eras — for example, Emperor Hirohito is known in Japan as Emperor Shōwa. Vietnamese rulers likewise received posthumous and temple names under Chinese influence.

Related concepts

Posthumous names differ from temple names (given for worship in ancestral temples) and era names (chosen for a reign). In the West, analogous practices include canonization names and epithets such as "the Great" or "the Martyr" attached to rulers after death.
