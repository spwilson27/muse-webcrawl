The Arrhenius equation is a formula for the temperature dependence of reaction rates. The equation was proposed by Svante Arrhenius in 1889, based on the work of Dutch chemist Jacobus Henricus van 't Hoff, who had noted in 1884 that many reactions speed up by a factor of about two to three for each 10 °C rise in temperature (the Q10 rule).

Equation

k = A · e−Ea / (RT)

Here k is the rate constant, T is the absolute temperature (in kelvin), A is the pre-exponential factor — interpreted as the frequency of correctly oriented collisions — Ea is the activation energy of the reaction, and R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J·mol−1·K−1). An alternative form uses Boltzmann's constant kB with the activation energy expressed per molecule.

Interpretation

The exponential factor represents the fraction of molecular collisions with energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, as given by the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. Because the term is exponential, modest rises in temperature can greatly increase the rate of reaction, which is why many chemical and biological processes are so temperature-sensitive.

Arrhenius plot

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides gives ln k = ln A − Ea/R · (1/T). A plot of ln k against 1/T therefore yields a straight line whose slope is −Ea/R, allowing the activation energy to be measured experimentally.

Modified equations

The simple equation fits many reactions over limited temperature ranges, but deviations occur, for example in enzyme catalysis at high temperature, reactions with quantum tunnelling, or diffusion-controlled reactions. Modified Arrhenius forms add a temperature-dependent pre-exponential factor (A ∝ Tn), and transition state theory (the Eyring equation) provides a more detailed theoretical treatment.
