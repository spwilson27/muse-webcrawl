Post-translational modification

Post-translational modification (PTM) is the chemical modification of a protein after its synthesis (translation) by ribosomes. Proteins are built from 20 standard amino acids, but covalent modifications — the addition of chemical groups, the cleavage of peptide bonds, or the attachment of whole other proteins — vastly expand their functional diversity. More than 200 types of PTM are known, and they regulate protein activity, localisation, interactions and lifespan.

Types of modification

Phosphorylation, the addition of a phosphate group to serine, threonine or tyrosine by kinases (and its removal by phosphatases), is the most common regulatory PTM and underlies much of cell signalling. Glycosylation attaches sugar chains (N-linked in the endoplasmic reticulum, O-linked in the Golgi) and is crucial for cell-surface and secreted proteins. Ubiquitination tags proteins with the small protein ubiquitin, best known for marking them for destruction by the proteasome. Other major modifications include methylation and acetylation of lysines (central to histone regulation of gene expression), lipidation such as prenylation and palmitoylation (which anchors proteins to membranes), hydroxylation, disulfide bond formation, and limited proteolysis — the cleavage that activates zymogens such as proinsulin and caspases.

Detection and significance

PTMs are studied by mass spectrometry–based proteomics, phospho-specific antibodies and enrichment methods. Because dysregulated PTMs feature in cancer, diabetes and neurodegeneration, the enzymes that write and erase them — kinases above all — are major drug targets, as exemplified by the kinase inhibitor imatinib.
