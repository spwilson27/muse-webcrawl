Silver halide

Silver halide crystals form the light-sensitive layer of photographic film.

A silver halide (or silver salt) is one of the chemical compounds that can form between the element silver and one of the halogens. In particular, bromine (AgBr), chlorine (AgCl), iodine (AgI), and hydrogen fluoride (AgF) may each combine with silver to produce silver bromide, silver chloride, silver iodide, and silver fluoride respectively.

As a group, they are often referred to as the silver halides, and are often given the pseudo-chemical notation AgX. Although most silver halides involve silver atoms with oxidation states of +1, a few compounds with silver in the +2 state are known, of which silver(II) fluoride is the only stable one.

Applications

Silver halides are light-sensitive materials commonly used in photographic film and paper. When a silver halide crystal is exposed to light, a photochemical reaction liberates enough free metallic silver in the exposed portions of the film to supply catalytic sites on which, during development, additional silver ions in the film are reduced, producing a visible image. The unexposed silver halide is removed by "fixing" with sodium thiosulfate.

Photochromic lenses also employ silver halides: silver chloride and silver bromide crystals embedded in the glass darken reversibly under ultraviolet light. Silver iodide is used in cloud seeding to encourage rain formation, owing to its crystal structure resembling that of ice.

Structure and solubility

Silver chloride and silver bromide adopt the rock-salt (face-centred cubic) structure; silver iodide commonly adopts a wurtzite or zinc-blende structure. The silver halides are notable for their very low solubility in water, which decreases from AgCl through AgBr to AgI — a trend exploited in qualitative inorganic analysis, where precipitation of AgCl confirms the presence of chloride ions. They are soluble in ammonia and thiosulfate solutions through formation of complex ions such as [Ag(S2O3)2]3−.

Photographic process

In a photographic emulsion, silver halide microcrystals — mostly bromoiodide in fast films — are suspended in gelatin. Exposure creates a latent image of a few atoms of metallic silver per grain; the developer amplifies this by a factor of up to billions, reducing the whole grain to black metallic silver. Grains that were not exposed remain silver halide and are dissolved away by the fixer, leaving a negative image from which positive prints can be made.
