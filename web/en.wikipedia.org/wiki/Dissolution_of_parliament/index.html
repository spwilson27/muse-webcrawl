Dissolution of parliament

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In parliamentary and some other systems, a dissolution of parliament is the formal dismissal of a legislature, ending all members' terms and triggering a general election. Dissolution may occur automatically at the end of a fixed term, or earlier — at the government's request, after a lost confidence vote, or where government formation fails — with the power typically vested in the head of state acting on ministerial advice, subject to conventions and sometimes judicially policed limits.

Design questions

The timing discretion is politically valuable (incumbents choose favourable moments — the "snap election") and constitutionally dangerous (dissolutions used to escape scrutiny, or refused contentiously — Canada's King–Byng affair of 1926). Reforms constrain it: fixed-term parliaments legislation (the UK's 2011 Act, repealed in 2022 to restore the prerogative-style discretion with an ouster clause courts then respected), constructive no-confidence in Germany (where dissolution is tightly conditioned and engineered dissolutions via deliberate confidence losses were litigated in 1983 and 2005), and Norway's absolute fixed term (the Storting cannot be dissolved at all). Prorogation (suspending a session without dissolving) is the adjacent power whose abuse the UK Supreme Court curbed in 2019.
