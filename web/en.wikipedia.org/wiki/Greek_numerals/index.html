Greek numerals

Greek numerals, also known as Ionic, Ionian, Alexandrian, or alphabetic numerals, are a system of writing numbers using the letters of the Greek alphabet. In modern Greece they are still used for ordinal numbers and for numbering items such as chapters and volumes, much like Roman numerals in the West, while Arabic numerals are used for cardinal numbers.

The alphabetic system

The standard system assigns values to 27 letters: the 24 letters of the classical alphabet plus three archaic letters retained only as numerals—digamma/stigma (6), koppa (90), and sampi (900). Units 1–9 are α′ to θ′, tens 10–90 are ι′ to ῂ′, and hundreds 100–900 are ρ′ to ϙ′. Numbers are written additively: for example, 241 is written σμα′. A mark called the keraia distinguishes numerals from words. Multiples of 1,000 reuse the unit letters with a subscript stroke (ₑ). Larger numbers used the myriad (10,000), developed by Archimedes in The Sand Reckoner into a system capable of expressing enormous quantities.

History

Earlier Greek systems included the acrophonic (Attic or Herodianic) numerals, in use from the 7th century BC, whose signs were the initial letters of number words. The alphabetic system, originating in Ionia, spread during the Hellenistic period and became the standard notation of Greek mathematics and astronomy—used by Ptolemy with a sexagesimal fractional notation—and passed into use in Coptic, Gothic, and early Cyrillic numeral traditions.

Modern use

In present-day Greece the system is used in law and formal contexts for enumerating articles and chapters, for monarchs' regnal numbers, and similar ordinal purposes; lower-case letters are customary. Greek numerals also survive in mathematical notation via isopsephy (gematria), the practice of summing letter values of words.
