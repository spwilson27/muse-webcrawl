Austromarxism


Image placeholder: Austromarxist thinkers

Austromarxism was a current of Marxist thought developed within the Social Democratic Workers' Party of Austria in the early 20th century. Its leading theorists included Otto Bauer, Max Adler, Karl Renner, Rudolf Hilferding and Friedrich Adler.

Ideas

Austromarxism combined orthodox Marxist economics with original contributions on the national question, law, culture and education. Drawing on the experience of the multinational Habsburg Empire, Bauer's The Question of Nationalities and Social Democracy (1907) proposed "national-cultural autonomy": national groups would be constituted as autonomous public corporations of persons, regardless of territory, administering their own cultural and educational affairs. Renner developed related legal theories of the state, and Hilferding's Finance Capital (1910) analysed the merging of bank and industrial capital.

Practice

Politically, Austromarxism sought a middle path between reformist social democracy and Bolshevism. After 1918 the Austrian Social Democrats implemented extensive municipal reforms in "Red Vienna" — housing, welfare, health and education — while Bauer's theory of "integral socialism" aimed to reunite the split international workers' movement. The movement was destroyed by the Austrofascist regime after the February fighting of 1934.

Legacy

Tom Bottomore and others later collected Austromarxist writings, and the tradition influenced Eurocommunism and later democratic-socialist thought, particularly on multinational federations and cultural pluralism.
