Negation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In logic, negation, also called the logical not or logical complement, is an operation that takes a proposition P to another proposition "not P", written ¬P, ~P or P̄, that is interpreted intuitively as being true when P is false, and false when P is true. Negation is thus a unary logical connective. It may be applied as an operation on notions, propositions, truth values, or semantic values more generally. In classical logic, negation is normally identified with the truth function that takes truth to falsity (and vice versa). In intuitionistic logic, and more generally constructive logic, negation of a statement is proved by deriving a contradiction from it.

Definition

Classical negation is an operation on one logical value, typically the value of a proposition, that produces a value of true when its operand is false, and a value of false when its operand is true. So, if statement P is true, then ¬P (pronounced "not P") would therefore be false; and conversely, if ¬P is true, then P would be false.

The negation of a statement P can be written in many ways: ¬P (used in modern logic), ~P, P′, P̄, Fpq (Polish notation), and "not P".

Properties

  • Double negation: In classical logic, ¬¬P is equivalent to P (involution). In intuitionistic logic, P implies ¬¬P but not conversely; double-negation elimination is precisely what separates classical from intuitionistic reasoning.
  • De Morgan's laws: ¬(P ∧ Q) ≡ (¬P ∨ ¬Q) and ¬(P ∨ Q) ≡ (¬P ∧ ¬Q).
  • Contraposition: (P → Q) ≡ (¬Q → ¬P) in classical logic.
  • Negation and quantifiers: ¬∀x P(x) ≡ ∃x ¬P(x), and ¬∃x P(x) ≡ ∀x ¬P(x).

Rules of inference

In natural deduction, negation introduction derives ¬P from a derivation of a contradiction (⊥) from the assumption P — the classical basis of proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum). Negation elimination, in classical systems, allows one to conclude anything (ex falso quodlibet) from P and ¬P together, or to discharge ¬¬P to P.

Linguistics

In natural language, negation is expressed by words such as not, no, never and affixes like un- and non-. Many languages, such as French (ne … pas), use bipartite negation, historically explained by Jespersen's cycle. Double negatives may cancel (as in standard English logic) or reinforce negation (negative concord, as in Spanish or African-American Vernacular English).

Computing

In programming, negation appears as the Boolean NOT operator (! in C-like languages), the bitwise complement (~), and the NOT logic gate in digital circuits, a basic building block of computation.
