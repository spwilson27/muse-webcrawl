Atta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atta is a genus of New World ants of the subfamily Myrmicinae, containing some of the best-known leafcutter ants. Together with the related genus Acromyrmex, Atta species are famous for cutting fragments of leaves and carrying them back to their nests, not to eat directly but to feed the symbiotic fungus gardens on which the colony depends.

Description

Atta colonies are among the largest and most complex societies of any insect. A mature colony of Atta cephalotes or Atta laevigata may contain several million workers, divided into a continuous range of size castes: tiny "minima" gardeners that tend the fungus and ride on leaf fragments, media workers that cut and carry leaves, larger majors ("soldiers") with powerful mandibles that defend the colony, and the single enormous queen. Nests are vast underground cities of fungus chambers, refuse dumps, and ventilation passages that can extend many meters deep and cover hundreds of square meters at the surface.

Fungus growing

The ants' food fungus (family Agaricaceae, typically Leucoagaricus gongylophorus) is grown on a substrate of macerated leaf pulp. The symbiosis is an obligate mutualism tens of millions of years old and is considered a form of agriculture. Atta ants also maintain symbiotic antibiotic-producing bacteria that protect the gardens from parasitic molds such as Escovopsis.

Ecology and economic impact

Atta species range from the southern United States (notably Atta texana) through Central and South America. They are major herbivores in tropical forests, harvesting a significant fraction of leaf production, but they are also serious agricultural pests of crops, citrus, and forestry plantations. In parts of South America the queens (tanajura or içá) are collected and eaten as a seasonal delicacy.

Species

The genus contains roughly a dozen to fifteen recognized species, including A. cephalotes, A. colombica, A. laevigata, A. mexicana, A. robusta, A. sexdens, and A. texana.
