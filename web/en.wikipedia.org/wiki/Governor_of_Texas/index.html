Governor of Texas

The governor of Texas is the head of government of the U.S. state of Texas and the chief executive of the state's executive branch. The governor is also the commander-in-chief of the Texas Military Forces. Established by the Texas Constitution of 1876, the office directs the executive branch, signs or vetoes bills passed by the Texas Legislature, convenes special sessions of the legislature, and appoints officials to a wide range of state boards and commissions. Although textbooks traditionally described the Texas governorship as a formally weak office — because executive power is fragmented among independently elected officials in the "plural executive" — modern governors exercise substantial influence through appointments, the veto (including the line-item veto over appropriations), the bully pulpit and emergency powers. The incumbent is Greg Abbott, a Republican, in office since January 20, 2015.

History

Texas had governors under Spanish and Mexican rule, and presidents during the Republic of Texas (1836–1845). After annexation, J. Pinckney Henderson became the first governor of the state in 1846. The Constitution of 1876, written in reaction against the centralized power of Governor Edmund J. Davis during Reconstruction, deliberately weakened the office: it shortened terms (to two years until a 1972 amendment made them four), limited appointment powers, and created the plural executive. Notable governors include Sam Houston, John Hogg (the first native-born governor), Allan Shivers, John Connally, Ann Richards, George W. Bush (who resigned in 2000 to become U.S. president), and Rick Perry, the longest-serving governor in state history (2000–2015).

Powers and duties

Under Article IV of the Texas Constitution, the governor:

  • Is chief executive and commander-in-chief of the Texas Military Forces (National Guard and State Guard)
  • Signs or vetoes legislation; may veto line items in appropriations bills; vetoes may be overridden by two-thirds of both legislative chambers, though the legislature usually adjourns before it can respond
  • Calls the legislature into special session and sets its agenda ("the call")
  • Appoints, with Senate confirmation, members of more than 200 state boards and commissions, university regents, and (to fill vacancies) judges and some executive officials
  • Grants pardons and commutations on the recommendation of the Board of Pardons and Paroles
  • Delivers the State of the State address and proposes a budget (though the Legislative Budget Board dominates the budget process)
  • Exercises emergency powers, including disaster declarations

The plural executive

Unlike the U.S. president, the Texas governor shares executive power with separately elected officials: the lieutenant governor (who presides over the Senate and is often called the most powerful official in state government), the attorney general, the comptroller of public accounts, the commissioner of the General Land Office, the agriculture commissioner, and the three-member Railroad Commission (which regulates oil and gas). This fragmentation limits the governor's formal control over administration but not, in practice, the office's political influence.

Election

The governor is elected statewide every four years in midterm election years and takes office on the Tuesday after the third Monday of the January following the election. Candidates must be at least 30 years old, U.S. citizens, and Texas residents for five years before election. There is no term limit; Rick Perry served more than 14 years across three elected terms and succession. The governor's residence is the Governor's Mansion in Austin, occupied since 1856.
