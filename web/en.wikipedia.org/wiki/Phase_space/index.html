Phase space

In dynamical systems theory, a phase space is a space in which all possible states of a system are represented, with each possible state corresponding to one unique point in the phase space. For a mechanical system, the phase space usually consists of all possible values of position and momentum variables. The concept was developed in the late 19th century by Ludwig Boltzmann, Henri Poincaré, and Josiah Willard Gibbs.

Introduction

A phase space has coordinates given by the generalized coordinates and their conjugate momenta. A system with n degrees of freedom has a 2n-dimensional phase space. The evolution of the system traces a trajectory (phase-space trajectory) through the space, governed by Hamilton's equations. A plot of momenta against positions for a family of trajectories is called a phase portrait, which reveals qualitative features such as fixed points, periodic orbits, and chaotic attractors.

Examples

For a simple pendulum, phase space is two-dimensional: the angle θ and the angular momentum. Small oscillations trace ellipses around the stable fixed point at the origin, while sufficiently energetic motion produces trajectories that wrap around the cylinder in continuously rotating motion, separated by a separatrix. For a gas of N particles, phase space has 6N dimensions — an abstract space central to statistical mechanics.

Related concepts

Liouville's theorem states that the volume of a region of phase space is preserved under Hamiltonian time evolution, reflecting the incompressibility of the phase-space flow. In statistical mechanics, the density of systems in an ensemble is described by a probability distribution over phase space. In quantum mechanics, the Wigner function provides a quasi-probability representation of quantum states in phase space. The smaller configuration space, consisting of positions only, contrasts with the full phase space when momenta matter.
