Theriomorphism

Theriomorphism
EtymologyGreek thērion (θὴρ, "beast") + morphē ("form")
MeaningAttribution of animal form or character to a god, spirit or being
ContrastAnthropomorphism, zoomorphism

Theriomorphism is the attribution of animal characteristics or form to non-animal beings, especially gods and supernatural beings. A theriomorphic deity is one conceived of, wholly or partly, in the form of an animal. The term derives from the Greek words for "beast" and "form," and its study belongs to anthropology, religious studies and art history.

In ancient religion

Theriomorphism is especially prominent in the religion of ancient Egypt, whose pantheon included the falcon-headed Horus, the jackal-headed Anubis, the ibis-headed Thoth, the lioness Sekhmet and the crocodile Sobek. Hybrid forms — human bodies with animal heads — combined animal powers with human agency. In Greek religion, theriomorphic elements survive in beings such as the Minotaur, centaurs and satyrs, and in myths of gods transforming into animals, as Zeus became a bull or a swan. Hindu deities such as Ganesha, with his elephant head, and the avatars of Vishnu as fish, tortoise and boar likewise show theriomorphic form.

Interpretation

Nineteenth-century scholars such as E. B. Tylor interpreted theriomorphism as a survival of primitive animism and totemism; later scholarship has treated animal forms as sophisticated symbolic expressions of divine attributes — speed, strength, fertility, guardianship of the dead — rather than simple "animal worship." Theriomorphic imagery persists in heraldry, folklore, modern fantasy literature and popular culture, where animal-formed gods and shapeshifters remain familiar figures.
