Udal law

Udal law is a Norse-derived system of land tenure that survives in the Northern Isles of Scotland — Orkney and Shetland — alongside Scots law. Under udal tenure, land is held allodially, in absolute ownership, rather than as a feudal grant from a superior. The word derives from the Old Norse óðal, denoting inherited family land.

History

The islands were settled by Norse peoples from the early Viking Age and were ruled by Norway and later Denmark–Norway until they were pledged to Scotland in 1468–69 as security for the dowry of Margaret of Denmark, who married James III. Although Scotland annexed the islands, the local law — based on Norse customary law — was never wholly abolished, and udal rights continued to be recognized by Scottish courts, notably in disputes over the foreshore and seabed.

Key features

Unlike feudal tenure, udal land historically owed no feudal superior, though holders paid a tax called skat to the Crown. Inheritance traditionally divided land among all children (with variations by island group), contributing over centuries to the fragmentation of holdings into small parcels. Udal title can still be registered and can affect modern questions such as ownership of the foreshore, salmon fishing rights, and the seabed — issues with practical importance for harbours, fish farms, and offshore energy development around Orkney and Shetland.

Modern status

The Land Registration (Scotland) legislation and the abolition of feudal tenure in Scotland (effective 2004) have reduced the practical gulf between udal and other ownership, but udal law has never been formally extinguished and continues to be studied as a living remnant of Norse law in Britain, comparable to the udal traditions of the Faroe Islands and parts of Norway.
