Supply-side economics

Supply-side economics is a school of macroeconomic thought arguing that economic growth is most effectively created by lowering barriers for people to produce ("supply") goods and services — principally by reducing marginal tax rates and deregulation — which increases investment, work effort, and entrepreneurship. It rose to prominence in the late 1970s and shaped the economic policies of the Reagan administration in the United States ("Reaganomics") and influenced policy in the United Kingdom under Margaret Thatcher.

Core ideas

Supply-siders emphasise incentives at the margin: high marginal tax rates are said to discourage work, saving, and investment, so cutting them should expand the tax base and output. Associated ideas include the Laffer curve, popularised by economist Arthur Laffer, which posits that tax revenue is zero at both 0% and 100% rates and is maximised at some intermediate rate, implying that sufficiently high rates can be counterproductive. Advocates also favour free trade, reduced regulation, and sound money.

History

The term was coined in the 1970s by figures including Jude Wanniski, Robert Mundell, and Arthur Laffer, building on older classical ideas reaching back to Say's law. The Kemp–Roth tax cut proposal became the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, which cut marginal income tax rates substantially; further cuts followed in the Tax Reform Act of 1986.

Assessment and criticism

Economists debate the magnitude of supply-side effects. Critics argue that tax cuts primarily benefit high earners, that claims of cuts paying for themselves are not borne out by revenue data, and that demand-side factors drive short-run growth; the pejorative label "trickle-down economics" is often applied by opponents. Supporters respond that the policies contributed to the long expansions of the 1980s and 1990s, lower inflation (achieved jointly with tight monetary policy), and greater investment. Modern mainstream economics incorporates some supply-side mechanisms — incentives, human capital, and productivity — within broader models.
