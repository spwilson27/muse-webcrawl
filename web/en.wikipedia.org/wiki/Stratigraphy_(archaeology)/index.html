Stratigraphy (archaeology)

Stratigraphy is a key concept to modern archaeological theory and practice. Technically, it borrows from the geological concept of stratigraphy — the study of rock layers — and applies it to the layered deposits found at archaeological sites. Modern excavations rely on stratigraphic analysis to establish the sequence of events that formed a site.

Principles

The central principle is the law of superposition: in an undisturbed sequence, the layer deposited first lies at the bottom and later layers lie above it, so lower layers are older than higher ones. Archaeologists also use the principles of original horizontality and lateral continuity, and record interfaces — surfaces where one deposit cuts or seals another. Features that cut through layers, such as pits and ditches, are later than the deposits they cut.

Harris matrix

The relationships between contexts at a site are often represented using the Harris matrix, a diagram devised by Edward Harris in 1973 that arranges stratigraphic units in their chronological relationships. Stratigraphy provides relative dating; combined with absolute methods such as radiocarbon dating it allows the construction of site chronologies. See also excavation.
