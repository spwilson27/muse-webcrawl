Menehune

The Menehune are a mythological race of dwarf people in Hawaiian tradition who are said to live in the deep forests and hidden valleys of the Hawaiian Islands, far from human settlements. The Menehune are described as superb craftsmen who built temples (heiau), fishponds, roads, canoes, and houses. Some of these structures that Hawaiian folklore attributed to the Menehune still exist. They are said to have lived in Hawai'i before settlers arrived from Polynesia many centuries ago.

Their favorite food is the mai'a (plantain), but they also like fish. Legend has it that the Menehune appear only during the night. If they fail to complete their work by the time the sun comes up, they leave it unfinished. No one but their children and people connected to them are able to see the Menehune.

History and origins

Hawaiian scholars have speculated that the Menehune may be a legendary rendering of the first settlers of the Hawaiian Islands, the Marquesans, or that they were later arrivals from Tahiti displaced inland by a second wave of settlement. The term "manahune" in Tahitian refers to commoners or people of low social status, and some researchers believe the Menehune legends encode the memory of such a subjugated earlier population.

In the 1820 census of Kaua'i, 65 people listed themselves as Menehune, suggesting the term may have survived as a designation for a real community or lineage into the historical era.

Structures attributed to the Menehune

Several famous structures are credited to the Menehune in Hawaiian tradition, including the Menehune Ditch (Kiki a Ola), an irrigation ditch on Kaua'i that diverts water from the Waimea River; the Menehune Fishpond (Alekoko Fishpond) near Lihue; and various heiau and roadways. The remarkable precision of the stonework at these sites, unusual compared to other Hawaiian masonry, helped sustain the belief that they were built by a distinct, highly skilled people working in a single night.

In popular culture

The Menehune remain a familiar part of Hawaiian folklore and tourism imagery, appearing in children's stories, place names, and as mascots. Stories of the Menehune are often compared to European tales of brownies, leprechauns, and other small folk who labor unseen at night.
