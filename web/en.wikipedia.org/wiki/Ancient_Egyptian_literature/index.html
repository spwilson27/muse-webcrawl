Ancient Egyptian literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ancient Egyptian literature was written in the Egyptian language from the pharaonic period until the end of Roman domination of Egypt. It is the oldest known literature of Egypt and, along with Sumerian literature, among the earliest literatures in the world. Written in hieroglyphic inscriptions and in the cursive hieratic and later demotic scripts on papyrus and ostraca, it spans religious texts, wisdom instructions, tales, poetry, hymns, biographies, and letters.

Old and Middle Kingdom classics

The Middle Kingdom (c. 2000–1650 BC) is regarded as the classical age of Egyptian literature, its language (Middle Egyptian) becoming the standard for later formal writing. Its masterpieces include:

  • The Tale of Sinuhe — the story of a courtier who flees Egypt after a king's death, prospers in exile in Syria-Palestine, and longs to return home for burial; often considered the greatest work of Egyptian literature.
  • The Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor — a sailor consoles his commander with the tale of his encounter with a giant serpent on a magical island.
  • The Eloquent Peasant — a wronged peasant petitions a magistrate in nine increasingly ornate speeches on justice.
  • The Instructions — wisdom texts purporting to be advice from kings and viziers, such as the Instruction of Ptahhotep and the Instruction addressed to King Merikare.
  • The Prophecy of Neferti and the Admonitions of Ipuwer — "pessimistic" laments over social chaos.

New Kingdom and later literature

New Kingdom literature, increasingly written in Late Egyptian, includes love songs, the Tale of Two Brothers, the Doomed Prince, the Taking of Joppa, and the satirical and didactic works of scribal schools; the Satire of the Trades exhorts boys to become scribes. Hymns such as Akhenaten's Great Hymn to the Aten and the hymns to the Nile show the religious dimension of the art. In the first millennium BC, Demotic literature produced story cycles such as the tales of Setne Khaemwaset and the Petubastis cycle, the allegorical Myth of the Sun's Eye, and the Instruction of Onchsheshonqy. Egyptian literature influenced neighbouring cultures, and motifs such as the instruction genre have been compared to biblical wisdom books.

Scripts and survival

Monumental texts were carved in hieroglyphs, but everyday literature circulated in hieratic on papyrus and limestone flakes (ostraca) used in scribal training at places like Deir el-Medina. Much survives only in schoolboy copies, with their teacher's corrections in red ink. Knowledge of the literature was lost after the scripts fell out of use in late antiquity, and was recovered only after the decipherment of hieroglyphs by Jean-François Champollion in the 1820s.
