Lead paint

Lead paint or lead-based paint is paint that contains lead compounds, historically added as pigments (such as white lead and lead chromate) and as drying agents. Lead paint is durable, moisture-resistant, and dries quickly, which made it ubiquitous in homes, on metal structures, and in industrial coatings — but it is a serious health hazard, especially to children.

Health effects

Deteriorating lead paint produces contaminated dust and chips that young children ingest through normal hand-to-mouth behaviour. Lead is a potent neurotoxin: childhood exposure is associated with reduced IQ, behavioural problems, and developmental delay, and there is no known safe level of exposure. Adults face risks including kidney damage, reproductive harm, and hypertension. Many artists of past centuries are also thought to have suffered lead poisoning from their pigments.

Regulation

Several European countries restricted interior lead paint in the early twentieth century, and the League of Nations promoted bans in the 1920s. The United States limited lead in residential paint in 1971 and effectively banned its consumer sale in 1978 through the Consumer Product Safety Commission. Lead paint nevertheless remains common in older housing stock, and renovation work is regulated (for example by the EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule) to contain dust. Globally, the UN-backed Global Alliance to Eliminate Lead Paint promotes legal limits in countries where lead paint is still sold.

Testing and abatement

  • Inspection — X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysers detect lead in paint layers in situ; chemical swab kits give quick indications.
  • Abatement — removal, enclosure, or encapsulation of lead paint by trained contractors.
  • Interim controls — repair of peeling paint and specialised cleaning to reduce dust hazards.
