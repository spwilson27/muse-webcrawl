Maurice Merleau-Ponty

Maurice Merleau-Ponty (14 March 1908 – 3 May 1961) was a French phenomenological philosopher, strongly influenced by Edmund Husserl and Martin Heidegger. Best known for his work on perception, the body and embodiment, he argued against both empiricism and intellectualism, holding that the perceiving body — the "lived body" — is our primary means of knowing the world.

Life

Born in Rochefort-sur-Mer, Merleau-Ponty studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, where he met Jean-Paul Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir. He taught at the University of Lyon and the Sorbonne before being appointed, in 1952, to the chair of philosophy at the Collège de France — the youngest person then appointed to that institution. With Sartre he co-founded the journal Les Temps modernes, though the two later fell out over politics, notably in the dispute surrounding Merleau-Ponty's Adventures of the Dialectic (1955). He died suddenly of a heart attack in 1961, aged 53.

Philosophy

His first major work, The Structure of Behavior (1942), critiqued behaviourism and reductionist physiology. His masterpiece, Phenomenology of Perception (1945), argued that perception is not a passive reception of sense data nor a judgement of the intellect, but an embodied, pre-reflective openness to the world: the body is not an object among objects but the permanent condition of experience. Later concepts include the "chiasm" and the "flesh" in the unfinished The Visible and the Invisible (published posthumously, 1964), edited from his working notes.

Influence

Merleau-Ponty's thought has influenced cognitive science (especially embodied and enactive approaches to mind), psychology, art theory, film studies and architecture. His account of motor intentionality and the body schema remains a standard reference point in discussions of embodied cognition.
