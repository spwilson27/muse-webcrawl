Pernicious anemia is a type of vitamin B12 deficiency anemia caused by an autoimmune gastritis that destroys the parietal cells of the stomach. Because parietal cells produce intrinsic factor — a protein required for the absorption of vitamin B12 in the ileum — their loss leads to B12 malabsorption, impaired DNA synthesis in the bone marrow, and the production of abnormally large, fragile red blood cells (megaloblastic anemia). The name reflects its historically fatal course before effective treatment was found.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms develop insidiously and include fatigue, weakness, pallor, shortness of breath, a sore red tongue (glossitis), and loss of appetite. Neurological features — numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, balance problems, memory loss and mood change — may occur and can precede the anemia; untreated, they may become irreversible. Patients also have an increased risk of gastric cancer and gastric carcinoid tumours.

Cause and diagnosis

The condition is an autoimmune disease: antibodies attack parietal cells and/or intrinsic factor itself. It is associated with other autoimmune disorders such as type 1 diabetes and autoimmune thyroid disease, and is somewhat more common in people of northern European descent. Diagnosis rests on finding a low serum B12 level with macrocytic anemia, supported by antibodies to intrinsic factor or parietal cells, and elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine.

Treatment and history

Treatment is lifelong vitamin B12 replacement, traditionally by intramuscular injection, though high-dose oral B12 can be absorbed by passive diffusion and is increasingly used. In the 1920s George Whipple, George Minot and William Murphy showed that feeding liver cured the disease — work that won the 1934 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — and William Castle subsequently identified intrinsic factor as the missing gastric component. The vitamin itself was isolated in 1948.
