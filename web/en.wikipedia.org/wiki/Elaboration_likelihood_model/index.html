The elaboration likelihood model (ELM) of persuasion is a dual-process theory developed by Richard Petty and John Cacioppo describing how attitudes are formed and changed. It proposes that persuasive messages are processed along one of two routes — the central route or the peripheral route — depending on the recipient's motivation and ability to think carefully about the message, a process the theory calls elaboration.

The two routes

Under the central route, recipients scrutinise the strength and quality of arguments. Attitudes formed this way tend to be persistent over time, resistant to counter-persuasion, and predictive of behaviour. Under the peripheral route, attitudes are shaped by cues unrelated to argument quality — the attractiveness or credibility of the source, mood, the sheer number of arguments, or simple heuristics such as "experts can be trusted". Attitude change via the peripheral route is generally temporary and less predictive of behaviour.

Elaboration likelihood

How much a person elaborates depends on motivation (personal relevance of the topic, need for cognition, personal responsibility) and ability (knowledge, time, and freedom from distraction). High elaboration likelihood leads to central-route processing; low likelihood leads to peripheral processing. Variables can serve different roles: a factor such as source attractiveness may act as a peripheral cue under low elaboration but as an argument or a biasing influence under high elaboration.

Applications and evidence

The ELM is widely applied in advertising, health communication, and political campaigning. Classic experiments showed, for example, that when an issue was personally relevant, strong arguments persuaded more than weak ones regardless of the source's prestige, whereas when relevance was low, source expertise drove persuasion regardless of argument quality. The model has been influential but also debated, particularly regarding how cognitive and affective processes interact and whether the two routes are discrete or a continuum.
