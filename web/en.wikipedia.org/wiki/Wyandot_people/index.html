Wyandot people

The Wyandot people (also Wyandotte, Wendat and Huron) are an Indigenous people of North America, speakers of the Iroquoian Wyandot language. The historic Wendat Confederacy (the Huron Confederacy) comprised several nations living between Lake Simcoe and Georgian Bay in present-day Ontario, Canada, with an agricultural society based on longhouse villages growing corn, beans and squash.

History

French explorers and missionaries, including Samuel de Champlain from 1615, made contact with the Wendat, who became major trading partners in the fur trade. Epidemics of European diseases in the 1630s severely reduced their population. In 1649 the confederacy was dispersed by attacks from the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) during the Beaver Wars.

Surviving groups migrated in several directions: some settled near Quebec, ancestors of the Huron-Wendat Nation at Wendake; others regrouped in the west around Michilimackinac and later Detroit and Ohio, where they became known as the Wyandot. Wyandot leaders such as Tarhe played significant roles in the conflicts of the Old Northwest. Under pressure of Indian removal, most Ohio Wyandot ceded their lands and moved to Kansas in 1843; many later moved again to Indian Territory (Oklahoma).

Modern nations

Today recognised Wyandot communities include the Wyandotte Nation in Oklahoma, the Wyandot of Anderdon Nation in Michigan, the Wyandot Nation of Kansas, and the Huron-Wendat Nation in Quebec. Efforts at language revitalisation have revived the teaching of the Wyandot/Wendat language, which had ceased to be spoken as a first language by the mid-20th century.
