E-government (short for electronic government) is the use of digital information and communication technologies — particularly the internet — by government agencies to deliver public services, exchange information, conduct transactions and integrate administrative systems. Its goals typically include greater efficiency, transparency, accessibility and citizen participation.

Delivery models

E-government interactions are usually classified into four models: government-to-citizen (G2C), such as online tax filing, benefits applications and licence renewals; government-to-business (G2B), including procurement portals and regulatory filings; government-to-government (G2G), the sharing of data between agencies and levels of government; and government-to-employee (G2E), internal human-resources and payroll systems.

Development

Early e-government in the 1990s consisted largely of static information websites. Services then became transactional, allowing citizens to complete payments and applications online. Later stages emphasise joined-up, proactive and personalised services — sometimes called "digital government" — in which a single account or digital identity gives access to many services, and data is shared between agencies under a "once-only" principle. Leading examples include Estonia's X-Road data-exchange layer and e-Estonia services, Denmark's borger.dk portal, Singapore's Singpass, and the United Kingdom's GOV.UK platform.

Measurement

The United Nations publishes the E-Government Development Index (EGDI), combining measures of online services, telecommunications infrastructure and human capital. Denmark, Finland, South Korea, Estonia and other digitally advanced states regularly rank near the top.

Benefits and challenges

Advocates cite reduced administrative costs, faster service delivery, less petty corruption and better access for rural populations. Critics point to the digital divide — citizens without internet access, devices or digital skills may be excluded — as well as privacy and surveillance risks, cybersecurity threats, and the danger that failed large-scale IT projects waste public money. Accessibility standards and assisted-digital support are commonly used to mitigate exclusion.

Related forms

Mobile delivery of services is called m-government; the use of electronic tools to strengthen democratic participation, such as online consultations and e-voting, overlaps with e-democracy. Open government data initiatives publish machine-readable public datasets for reuse by citizens and businesses.
