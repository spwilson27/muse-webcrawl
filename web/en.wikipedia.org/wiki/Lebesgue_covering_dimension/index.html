Lebesgue covering dimension

Lebesgue covering dimension
FieldTopology; dimension theory
Introduced byHenri Lebesgue (1911); formalised by Eduard Čech
Notationdim X
Valuesdim ℝn = n; dim ∅ = −1

The Lebesgue covering dimension, or topological dimension, is one of several ways of defining the dimension of a topological space. The covering dimension of a space X is the smallest integer n such that for every open cover of X there is a refinement in which no point of X lies in more than n + 1 sets of the refinement. If no such n exists, the space is said to have infinite covering dimension; the empty set is assigned dimension −1 by convention.

Informally, a disk in the plane can be covered by small open sets arranged so that no point is covered more than three times (and three is best possible in general position) — matching its intuitively expected dimension of two. The famous theorem of Lebesgue states that Euclidean space ℝn has covering dimension exactly n, a fact closely tied to the invariance of domain: n is thereby shown to be a topological invariant of ℝn.

History

Lebesgue introduced the idea in 1911 while studying problems in approximation and mappings, arguing that the dimension of the n-cube is n because any sufficiently fine cover of it must have a point in common to n + 1 sets. Brouwer proved the invariance result in 1913, and the definition in its modern form is due largely to Čech and to the dimension theory developed by Menger and Urysohn in the 1920s.

Properties

For normal spaces the covering dimension agrees with the large inductive dimension. Covering dimension is monotone under passage to closed subspaces in normal spaces, additive over finite unions of closed sets, and behaves well under products (dim(X × Y) ≤ dim X + dim Y). One of its equivalent characterisations: dim X ≤ n if and only if every continuous map from a closed subset of X into the n-sphere extends over X.
