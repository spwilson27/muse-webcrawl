Epidermis (botany)

The epidermis in plants is a single layer of cells that covers the leaves, flowers, roots and stems of plants. It forms a boundary between the plant and the external environment, and is the outermost cell layer of the primary plant body. In some older works the epidermis of roots is referred to as the epiblem or rhizodermis.

Function

The epidermis serves several functions: it protects against water loss, regulates gas exchange, secretes metabolic compounds, and (in roots) absorbs water and mineral nutrients. It is usually transparent, since epidermal cells typically contain few chloroplasts, allowing light to reach the photosynthetic tissues beneath.

Structure

Epidermal cells are tightly interlocked and often coated on their outer surface with a waxy cuticle that reduces water loss. Scattered among the ordinary epidermal cells are specialized structures:

  • Stomata — pores flanked by pairs of guard cells that contain chloroplasts and regulate gas exchange and transpiration.
  • Trichomes — hair-like outgrowths that can deter herbivores, trap moisture, or secrete substances.
  • Root hairs — extensions of root epidermal cells that greatly increase absorptive surface area.

Development

The epidermis is derived from the outermost layer of the apical meristem (the protoderm in tunica-corpus theory). In many plants, cell division in this layer occurs almost exclusively anticlinally, so the layer expands in surface area without thickening.
