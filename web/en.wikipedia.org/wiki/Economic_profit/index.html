Economic profit

Economic profit (or loss) is the difference between the revenue received from the sale of an output and the costs of all inputs used, including both explicit costs and opportunity costs. It differs from accounting profit, which is revenue minus explicit costs only — the costs a firm actually pays out, such as wages, rent and materials.

Definitions

Economic profit = total revenue − (explicit costs + implicit costs). Implicit costs include the forgone return on the owner's capital and the salary the owner could earn elsewhere. When economic profit is zero, the firm is said to earn a normal profit: it covers all its costs, including opportunity costs, and has no incentive to enter or leave the industry.

Role in market theory

In theories of perfect competition, positive economic profit in an industry attracts new entrants, increasing supply and lowering price until economic profit is driven to zero in the long run. Conversely, losses drive firms out. Persistent economic profit is therefore associated with barriers to entry or market power, as under monopoly. Economic profit is not the same as monopoly rent or windfall gain, though it can include both; economists also distinguish it from the entrepreneur's reward for innovation in dynamic theories such as Schumpeter's.

Related concepts

Measures such as economic value added (EVA) apply the same idea in corporate finance, subtracting a charge for the cost of capital from operating profit.
