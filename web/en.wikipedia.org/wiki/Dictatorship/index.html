Dictatorship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dictatorship is an autocratic form of government characterised by a leader, or a group of leaders, who hold governmental powers with few to no limitations. Politics in a dictatorship are controlled by the dictator and their inner circle, through influence, factionalism and suppression of civil liberties and the media.

Origins

The term derives from the Roman dictatura: a constitutional office in the Roman Republic, granted for six months in emergencies with near-absolute power — famously held by Cincinnatus and, fatefully, by Sulla and Julius Caesar in the Republic's last century. The modern sense, denoting permanent unaccountable rule, crystallised in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Types

Scholars classify dictatorships as military (juntas and strongmen), one-party (institutionalised party-states), personalist (rule concentrated in an individual and family networks), and monarchical (hereditary absolute rule), with many hybrids. Dictatorships differ in how leaders are selected and removed, how elites are managed, and how much repression they employ; most hold elections of some kind, usually uncompetitive, and some deliver periods of economic growth, though the average record on rights and long-run development is poor and succession is a chronic vulnerability.
