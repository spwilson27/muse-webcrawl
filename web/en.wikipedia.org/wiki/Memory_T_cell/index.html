Memory T cell

Memory T cells are a subset of T cells that have previously encountered and responded to their cognate antigen, persisting long-term after an infection has resolved or after vaccination. They form a key part of immunological memory, enabling the adaptive immune system to mount a response that is faster, stronger and more effective upon re-encounter with a pathogen than the primary response of naive T cells.

Subsets

The principal subsets include central memory T cells (TCM), which circulate through lymphoid tissues and express CCR7 and CD62L; effector memory T cells (TEM), which circulate through peripheral tissues and can rapidly produce effector cytokines; and tissue-resident memory T cells (TRM), which remain permanently in non-lymphoid tissues such as skin, lung and gut mucosa, providing a first line of defence at barrier sites. Memory cells typically express the CD45RO isoform (in humans) as opposed to CD45RA on naive cells.

Function

Memory T cells survive for years or decades, maintained by homeostatic proliferation driven by cytokines such as IL-7 and IL-15. Their rapid recall underlies the protection conferred by vaccines and by prior infection.
