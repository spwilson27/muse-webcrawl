LocationNear Heraklion, Crete, Greece

Knossos is the largest Bronze Age archaeological site on Crete and the ceremonial and political centre of the Minoan civilisation. Its palace — a sprawling complex of more than a thousand interlocking rooms — is associated in Greek myth with King Minos, the labyrinth, and the Minotaur.

History

The site was inhabited from the Neolithic period (c. 7000 BCE). The first palace was built around 1900 BCE and rebuilt after an earthquake around 1700 BCE on a grander scale, with multi-storey halls, grand staircases, sophisticated plumbing and drainage, storerooms of giant pithoi, and vivid frescoes of bull-leaping, processions, and marine life. Around 1450 BCE Knossos came under Mycenaean Greek influence — its later tablets are in Linear B Greek — and the palace was finally destroyed around 1375 BCE.

Excavation

After preliminary work by Minos Kalokairinos in 1878, Arthur Evans bought the site and excavated from 1900, controversially reconstructing parts of the palace in painted concrete. His restorations remain debated but made Knossos one of Greece's most visited sites. The finds are largely displayed in the Heraklion Archaeological Museum.
