Riotinto, officially Minas de Riotinto, is a town and municipality in the province of Huelva, in the autonomous community of Andalusia, Spain. It is named after the Rio Tinto ("red river"), whose waters run a deep red-orange colour from the iron and copper minerals dissolved in them, and it is the centre of one of the world's oldest mining districts.

History

The ores of the Iberian Pyrite Belt around Riotinto were worked from the Copper Age and by the Tartessians, Phoenicians and Romans, who mined silver, copper and gold on a large scale. After long decline, the mines were revived in the 18th and 19th centuries, and in 1873 the Spanish government sold them to a British-led consortium that founded the Rio Tinto Company — the origin of today's Rio Tinto Group.

Under British ownership the district was transformed: open-pit mines such as Corta Atalaya, among the largest in Europe, railways to the port of Huelva, a pier for loading ore ships, and an English-style neighbourhood, Barrio Reina Victoria, for company staff. Labour conditions provoked repeated strikes, including the suppressed miners' protest of 1888, remembered as the "year of the shootings" (el año de los tiros), and a major general strike in 1920.

In 1954 the mines passed to Spanish ownership. Mining declined in the late 20th century, though copper mining has continued intermittently under later operators.

Mining heritage

The area is now promoted through the Riotinto Mining Park, which includes the Mining Museum, the preserved Edwardian house Casa 21 in the English quarter, a tourist train running along the old railway beside the coloured river, and views over the Cerro Colorado and Corta Atalaya pits.

Football

Riotinto is regarded as the birthplace of Spanish football: British mine workers played the game there from the 1870s, and a football and recreation club organised by the company in 1878 predates Recreativo de Huelva, Spain's oldest surviving club. The local team is Riotinto Balompié.

The river

The Rio Tinto rises near the town and flows about 100 km to the Atlantic at Huelva. Its extreme acidity (pH around 2) and high metal content sustain extremophile bacteria; the environment has been studied by NASA scientists as an analogue for conditions on Mars.
