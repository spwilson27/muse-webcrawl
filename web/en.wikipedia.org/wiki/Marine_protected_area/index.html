A marine protected area (MPA) is a protected area of a sea, ocean, estuary, or large lake. MPAs restrict human activity for a conservation purpose, typically to protect natural or cultural resources. Such restrictions may include limits on fishing, development, and access; fully protected zones that prohibit all extractive activity are often called marine reserves or no-take zones. MPAs are widely used as a tool in marine conservation and fisheries management.

History and development

The first marine protections were local and ancient — traditional closures in the Pacific, for example — but modern MPAs date from the twentieth century; the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park (1975) became the emblematic large-scale model. The IUCN defines an MPA as a clearly defined geographical space, recognised, dedicated and managed, through legal or other effective means, to achieve the long-term conservation of nature with associated ecosystem services and cultural values. Global coverage expanded rapidly in the 2000s and 2010s with the designation of very large MPAs around remote island territories, including Papahānaumokuākea (United States), the Chagos Archipelago, and the Ross Sea region MPA (2016), the world's largest at about 1.55 million km² under CCAMLR.

Benefits and evidence

Well-enforced no-take reserves consistently show higher biomass, density, and average size of exploited species inside their boundaries than comparable fished areas, with benefits building over decades. "Spillover" of adult fish and export of larvae may enhance surrounding fisheries, though the strength and scale of these effects are debated. MPAs also protect ecosystems against other stressors only indirectly — they do not by themselves address climate change, ocean acidification, or pollution from outside their borders.

Targets and criticism

Under the Convention on Biological Diversity's Aichi Targets, parties aimed to conserve 10% of coastal and marine areas by 2020; the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022) raised the goal to 30% by 2030 ("30 by 30"), echoed by the UN High Seas (BBNJ) Treaty of 2023, which created mechanisms for MPAs in international waters. Critics note that much declared coverage is weakly protected or unenforced ("paper parks"), that big remote MPAs may be designated where fishing pressure is already low, and that conservation can displace fishing communities — issues of design, enforcement, and equity that determine whether an MPA achieves its stated goals.

Notable examples

  • Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, Australia
  • Ross Sea region Marine Protected Area, Antarctica
  • Galápagos Marine Reserve, Ecuador
  • Papahānaumokuākea, United States
