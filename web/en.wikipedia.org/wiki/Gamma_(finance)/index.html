Gamma (finance)

In mathematical finance, gamma (Γ) is one of the "Greeks" — measures of the sensitivity of a derivative's price to changes in its underlying parameters. Gamma is the rate of change of an option's delta with respect to changes in the price of the underlying asset; equivalently, it is the second derivative of the option's value with respect to the underlying price. Delta hedgers use gamma to gauge how frequently a hedge must be rebalanced.

Definition

For a derivative value V and underlying price S, gamma is Γ = ∂²V / ∂S². In the Black–Scholes model, European call and put options on the same strike and expiry have identical gamma, given by Γ = φ(d1) / (Sσ√T), where φ is the standard normal density, σ is volatility and T is time to expiry.

Properties

Gamma is greatest for at-the-money options and for options near expiration, and approaches zero for options deep in or out of the money with long maturities. Long option positions have positive gamma: the holder's delta moves in their favour as the underlying moves, producing convexity — gains accelerate and losses decelerate. Short options carry negative gamma, so a delta-hedged short-gamma book must buy high and sell low when rebalancing, the cost of which is offset by theta (time-decay) income. Gamma, delta and theta are linked through the Black–Scholes partial differential equation.

Use in trading

Market makers monitor aggregate gamma exposure of their books and of the options market as a whole; large dealer short-gamma positions can amplify price moves ("gamma squeezes" and hedging flows), a dynamic widely discussed during episodes such as the January 2021 short squeezes. Gamma scalping — systematically rebalancing a delta hedge around a long option — is a volatility trading strategy that profits when realised volatility exceeds implied volatility.
