Earth system science

Earth system science (ESS) is the interdisciplinary study of the Earth as an integrated system, examining the interactions among the atmosphere, hydrosphere, cryosphere, geosphere and biosphere, and the human influence on them. Rather than studying each sphere in isolation, ESS focuses on the flows of energy and matter between them, the feedbacks they generate, and the behaviour of the whole system over timescales from days to billions of years.

The Earth system: coupled atmosphere, oceans, land, ice and biosphere.

Origins

The field coalesced in the 1980s, notably through NASA's Earth System Sciences Committee and its 1986 "Bretherton diagram", which mapped the physical, chemical and biological processes linking the spheres. It drew on earlier systems thinking, James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis, and the recognition—driven by satellite observation and concern over climate change—that phenomena such as the carbon cycle could only be understood as a whole.

Key concepts

Earth system science studies biogeochemical cycles (carbon, nitrogen, water), feedback loops that amplify or damp change, tipping points and planetary boundaries, and the coupled models—Earth system models—that extend climate models to include interactive carbon cycles, ice sheets and ecosystems. The concept of the Anthropocene, an epoch in which human activity is a dominant influence on the planet, arose directly from this systems perspective.

Applications

ESS underpins climate prediction, assessments by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, global environmental monitoring by satellite, and sustainability science.
