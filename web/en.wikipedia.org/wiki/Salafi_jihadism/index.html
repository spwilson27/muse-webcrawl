Salafi jihadism

Salafi jihadism, also called jihadist Salafism, is a transnational religious-political ideology combining a literalist, puritanical reading of Salafi Islam with the doctrine of armed jihad against governments and powers deemed apostate or hostile to Islam. The term was popularised by the scholar Gilles Kepel to describe the movement that produced al-Qaeda and, later, the Islamic State (ISIS). Most Salafis — quietist and political alike — reject the jihadi current, and many Salafi scholars have condemned it.

Beliefs

Salafi jihadists emphasise tawhid (absolute monotheism) extended to politics: any law not derived from sharia is idolatry, and rulers who govern otherwise are apostates open to takfir (excommunication) and violent removal. Central concepts include al-wala' wa-l-bara' (loyalty to Islam and disavowal of unbelief), the restoration of a caliphate, and a global framing of jihad that, in Osama bin Laden's 1998 fatwa and Ayman al-Zawahiri's writings, made the United States and its allies — the "far enemy" — a primary target alongside local regimes, the "near enemy" prioritised by earlier Egyptian groups.

Origins and development

The ideology's intellectual roots lie in the works of Sayyid Qutb, whose concept of modern jahiliyya (ignorance) legitimised revolt against Muslim rulers; in the Afghan jihad against the Soviet Union (1979–89), organised by Abdullah Azzam and the Maktab al-Khidamat, which internationalised the fighter networks; and in the Algerian and Egyptian insurgencies of the 1990s. Al-Qaeda emerged from the Afghan milieu in 1988 and carried out the September 11 attacks in 2001. A more extreme offshoot, originating with Abu Musab al-Zarqawi's organisation in Iraq, proclaimed the Islamic State caliphate in 2014 and seized territory in Iraq and Syria before its defeat by 2019. Affiliates and rivals have since operated from the Sahel to Afghanistan and Southeast Asia.

Criticism

Mainstream Sunni scholarship, including leading Salafi authorities, rejects the jihadi interpretation of classical texts, its practice of takfir, and its targeting of civilians. The 2004 Amman Message and the 2014 open letter to ISIS leader Abu Bakr al-Baghdadi set out theological refutations signed by hundreds of scholars.
