Proprietary software

Proprietary software, also known as non-free software or closed-source software, is computer software for which the software's publisher or another person reserves some licensing rights to use, modify, share, or copy it. It relies on the concept of copyright and, sometimes, patent law, and thus is often — though not always — distributed without its source code, in contrast to free and open-source software.

Licensing

Users of proprietary software typically receive an end-user license agreement (EULA) granting use under defined conditions while the vendor retains ownership of the software itself. Distribution is usually in compiled form, and modification, reverse engineering, and redistribution are restricted. Business models include perpetual licenses, subscriptions, and software as a service, as well as free-of-charge variants such as freeware, shareware, and freemium products—which remain proprietary despite their price.

History and debate

In the early decades of computing, software was commonly shared and shipped with source code; the commercialization and restriction of software from the late 1960s onward prompted the rise of the free software movement and copyleft licensing. Debates continue over user control, security auditing, vendor lock-in, and interoperability. See also free software and software license.
