Qualified majority voting

[Image: Ministers seated in the Council of the European Union chamber in Brussels during a vote]

Qualified majority voting (QMV) is a mechanism used by the Council of the European Union and the European Council to adopt decisions without the unanimity of all member states. Under the system in force since 1 November 2014, a qualified majority is a "double majority": at least 55 per cent of the member states (15 of 27) voting in favour, representing at least 65 per cent of the total population of the Union. A blocking minority must include at least four member states; otherwise the qualified majority is deemed attained.

Development

Weighted voting existed in the Council from the Treaty of Rome onwards, with votes weighted roughly by country size. The system nearly collapsed in practice after France's "empty chair" boycott of 1965 produced the Luxembourg compromise, under which member states claimed a right of veto where very important national interests were at stake. The Single European Act (1986) genuinely revived QMV for internal-market legislation, and the Treaties of Maastricht, Amsterdam and Nice extended its scope. The Treaty of Nice introduced a complex triple majority (weighted votes, majority of states, and 62 per cent of population on request), which the Treaty of Lisbon replaced with the simpler double-majority system after a transitional period ending in 2017.

Operation

When the Council acts on a proposal from the Commission or the High Representative, the double majority described above applies. When it does not act on such a proposal, the threshold of member states rises to 72 per cent, still with 65 per cent of population. Abstentions by members present in person or represented do not prevent adoption of acts requiring unanimity, but under QMV an abstention counts as a vote against, since the required majorities must actively be reached. In practice, the Council still seeks consensus: most acts adopted under QMV are passed without a formal contested vote, with presidencies testing whether a qualified majority exists before putting texts forward. QMV is the standard voting rule for the ordinary legislative procedure jointly with the European Parliament, but unanimity remains required in sensitive areas such as taxation, most foreign and security policy, social security coordination in part, and treaty change.

The Ioannina compromise

The Ioannina compromise of 1994, originally devised at the time of the 1995 enlargement, allows a group of states close to a blocking minority to request continued discussion for a reasonable time. A version of the mechanism was carried over into the Lisbon arrangements, applying from 2014 to states representing specified shares of a blocking minority.
