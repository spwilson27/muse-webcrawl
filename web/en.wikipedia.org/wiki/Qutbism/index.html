Qutbism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Qutbism is the Islamist ideology derived from the thought and writings of the Egyptian thinker Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966), a leading member of the Muslim Brotherhood who was executed by the Egyptian government of Gamal Abdel Nasser. Qutb's ideas, set out most influentially in his book Milestones (1964) and his Quranic commentary In the Shade of the Quran, became foundational for many later jihadist movements.

Core ideas

Central to Qutbism is the claim that the modern world — including self-described Muslim societies and their governments — has lapsed into jahiliyyah, the pre-Islamic state of ignorance and idolatry, because sovereignty (hakimiyya) belongs to God alone and human-made laws usurp it. Qutb argued that a dedicated vanguard of true believers must separate itself from jahili society, undertake jihad — which he understood as an active, revolutionary struggle — and establish an Islamic state governed by sharia. His concepts of takfir (declaring professed Muslims to be unbelievers) were later taken further by more radical followers than Qutb himself had.

Influence

Qutb's writings influenced a wide spectrum of movements, from Brotherhood offshoots to militant organisations such as Egyptian Islamic Jihad, al-Qaeda and, in mutated form, the Islamic State. Figures including Ayman al-Zawahiri cited Qutb as a formative influence. At the same time, mainstream Brotherhood leaders and many scholars have criticised or distanced themselves from the revolutionary reading of Qutb, and researchers debate how directly Qutb's own writings prescribe violence against civilians.

Criticism

Critics within Islam argue that Qutb's concepts of jahiliyyah and takfir break with classical jurisprudence, while political analysts note that Qutbism reflects the conditions of Nasserist Egypt — mass imprisonment and torture of Brotherhood members — as much as theology.
