Rebecca Nurse (née Towne; February 21, 1621 – July 19, 1692) was a devoutly religious elderly woman executed for witchcraft by the government of the Province of Massachusetts Bay in New England during the Salem witch trials in 1692. She was the wife of Francis Nurse, a respected farmer and community leader in Salem Village (now Danvers), and the sister of two other accused women, Mary Eastey and Sarah Cloyce.

Background

Born Rebecca Towne in Great Yarmouth, England, she emigrated with her family to Massachusetts Bay Colony as a child. She married Francis Nurse around 1640, and the couple eventually settled on a large farm in Salem Village. Rebecca was a church member in good standing and widely regarded as a model of piety, which made the accusation against her especially shocking to contemporaries.

Accusation and trial

In March 1692, during a long-running dispute over land boundaries and village politics, Rebecca Nurse was accused of witchcraft by members of the Putnam family and the afflicted girls. More than three dozen community members signed a petition attesting to her good character — an unusual act of support. At her trial in late June, the jury initially returned a verdict of not guilty, but after an outburst from the accusers and a pointed remark from Chief Judge William Stoughton questioning one of her statements, the jury reconsidered and convicted her.

Governor William Phips issued a reprieve, but pressure from influential figures led to its withdrawal. She was excommunicated by the Salem church and hanged on Gallows Hill on July 19, 1692, along with Sarah Good, Elizabeth Howe, Susannah Martin and Sarah Wildes.

Legacy

Her execution is often cited as a turning point that deepened public unease about the trials. In 1711 her family received compensation from the colonial government, and in 1712 the Salem church reversed her excommunication. Her homestead in Danvers is preserved as a museum. Rebecca Nurse is a central character in Arthur Miller's play The Crucible (1953), where she symbolises quiet integrity in the face of hysteria.
