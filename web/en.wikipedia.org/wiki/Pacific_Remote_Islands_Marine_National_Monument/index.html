The Pacific Remote Islands Marine National Monument is a group of unorganized, mostly unincorporated United States Pacific island territories in the central Pacific Ocean, managed as a marine national monument and one of the largest marine conservation areas in the world. It preserves the pristine coral reef, seabird, marine mammal and deep-sea ecosystems around some of the most isolated atolls and reefs on the planet.

History

President George W. Bush established the monument on 6 January 2009 (as the Pacific Remote Islands Marine National Monument) under the Antiquities Act of 1906, protecting the waters out to 50 nautical miles around Howland and Baker Islands, Jarvis Island, Johnston Atoll, Kingman Reef, Palmyra Atoll and Wake Island. On 25 September 2014, President Barack Obama used a presidential proclamation to expand the monument's boundaries around several of the possessions to the full 200-nautical-mile limit of the U.S. exclusive economic zone, increasing the protected area roughly sixfold to nearly 490,000 square miles and banning commercial fishing throughout it. In April 2025, a presidential proclamation moved to reopen much of the monument area to commercial fishing, an action promptly challenged in court.

Ecology

The monument protects some of the healthiest coral reef ecosystems remaining in the Pacific, with abundant apex predators — sharks, jacks and groupers — together with sea turtles, dolphins, whales and vast nesting colonies of seabirds. Palmyra Atoll and Kingman Reef are noted for exceptionally high coral cover and predator biomass, studied intensively by scientists; the surrounding deep waters include seamounts and ancient coral communities. Land areas are administered largely as national wildlife refuges (several of them closed to the public), while NOAA and the Fish and Wildlife Service co-manage the marine monument, balancing conservation with scientific research and cultural heritage, including World War II sites at Wake and Johnston.
