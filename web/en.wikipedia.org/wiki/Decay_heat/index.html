Decay heat

Decay heat is the heat released as a result of radioactive decay. This heat is produced as an effect of radiation on materials: the energy of the alpha, beta or gamma radiation is converted into the thermal movement of atoms.

Decay heat occurs naturally from the decay of long-lived radioisotopes and is a major contributor to the internal heating of the Earth. In a nuclear reactor, decay heat continues to be generated after the reactor is shut down and the fission chain reaction stops, which is why a reactor needs continued cooling for a considerable time after shutdown.

Occurrence in reactors

In a nuclear reactor, decay heat is principally produced by the beta and gamma decay of fission products that accumulate during operation. Immediately after shutdown, decay heat is roughly 6–7% of the reactor's full thermal power. It falls rapidly at first, following roughly a power-law decay:

Time after shutdownDecay heat (approx. % of full power)
1 second~6.5%
1 hour~1.5%
1 day~0.4%
1 week~0.2%
1 year~0.02%

If this heat is not removed, the fuel can overheat and melt, as occurred in the Three Mile Island accident (1979), the Chernobyl disaster (1986, driven initially by other causes), and the Fukushima Daiichi accident (2011), where loss of cooling after a tsunami led to meltdowns days after the reactors had shut down.

Spent fuel

Spent nuclear fuel continues to generate decay heat for years and must be cooled in spent fuel pools before it can be transferred to dry cask storage, in which passive air cooling is sufficient. Decay heat is the principal reason spent fuel is considered to require long-term management in geological repositories.

Applications

Decay heat is deliberately used in radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs), which power spacecraft such as Voyager 1 and the Curiosity rover using the heat from plutonium-238 decay. Similar sources heat remote lighthouses and navigation beacons.
