Maha Shivaratri

Maha Shivaratri
Observed byHindus
TypeHindu festival
SignificanceMarriage of Shiva and Parvati; the night Shiva performed the Tandava

Maha Shivaratri (Sanskrit: महाशिवरात्रि, romanized: Mahāśivarātri, lit. 'The Great Night of Shiva') is a Hindu festival celebrated annually in honour of the deity Shiva. According to the Hindu calendar, the festival is observed on the fourteenth day of the first half (night start with darkness, i.e. Krishna Paksha) of the month of Magha, corresponding to February or March in the Gregorian calendar. Unlike most Hindu festivals, which are celebrated during the day, Maha Shivaratri is observed at night, with devotees keeping an all-night vigil.

Significance and legends

Several legends are attached to the festival. It is widely believed to mark the wedding of Shiva and Parvati. Another tradition holds that on this night Shiva performed the Tandava, the cosmic dance of creation, preservation, and dissolution. Yet another story tells of a hunter who unwittingly worshipped Shiva by dropping bilva leaves onto a lingam through the night while waiting in a tree, and was thereby granted liberation — a tale used to explain the customs of fasting, vigil, and bilva offerings.

Observances

Devotees fast through the day, visit Shiva temples, and perform abhisheka, bathing the lingam with water, milk, honey, and other offerings, accompanied by the chanting of "Om Namah Shivaya". The night is divided into four quarters (prahars), each with its own worship. Major celebrations take place at the twelve Jyotirlinga shrines, such as Varanasi's Kashi Vishwanath Temple and the Mahakaleshwar Temple in Ujjain. In Kashmir, the festival is celebrated by Kashmiri Pandits as Herath over a longer period. It is also a major public holiday in Nepal, where it is observed at the Pashupatinath Temple in Kathmandu.
