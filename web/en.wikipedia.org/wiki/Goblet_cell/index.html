Goblet cell


Goblet cells interspersed among absorptive cells of the intestinal villi.

Goblet cells are simple columnar epithelial cells specialised for secreting gel-forming mucins, the principal components of mucus. They are found scattered among the lining cells of the respiratory, gastrointestinal and reproductive tracts and on the conjunctiva of the eye, and are named for their goblet-like shape: a narrow base containing the nucleus and a swollen apical portion distended with mucus granules.

Structure and secretion

The apical region of the cell is packed with membrane-bound mucin granules, while the basal stem contains the nucleus, rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus. The main secretory product in the intestine is MUC2, and in the airways MUC5AC and MUC5B. On release — by both continuous exocytosis and rapid compound exocytosis in response to acetylcholine, allergens, irritants or inflammatory mediators — the stored mucins expand enormously on hydration to form the protective mucus gel layer. In the colon this mucus forms two layers, an inner sterile layer attached to the epithelium and a looser outer layer colonised by the microbiota.

Function and clinical significance

Goblet-cell mucus lubricates and protects epithelia from mechanical injury, digestive enzymes, pathogens and inhaled particles, and underlies mucociliary clearance in the airways. Dysfunction is central to several diseases: in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, goblet-cell hyperplasia and metaplasia produce excess mucus; in ulcerative colitis and cystic fibrosis the mucus layer is abnormal; and goblet cell adenocarcinoma is a rare appendiceal tumour. In the small intestine, goblet cells also participate in oral tolerance by passing luminal antigens to underlying immune cells via goblet-cell-associated antigen passages.
