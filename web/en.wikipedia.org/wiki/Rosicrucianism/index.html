Rosicrucianism

Rosicrucianism is a spiritual and cultural movement that arose in early 17th-century Europe with the publication of several manifestos announcing a secret brotherhood of alchemists and sages — the Brotherhood of the Rosy Cross — said to possess esoteric wisdom transmitted from ancient times. The movement influenced Freemasonry, occultism and later esoteric organisations, many of which claim Rosicrucian heritage.

The manifestos

Three anonymous works appeared in Germany: the Fama Fraternitatis (1614), the Confessio Fraternitatis (1615) and the Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosenkreutz (1616), the last associated with the theologian Johann Valentin Andreae. They told of Christian Rosenkreutz, a legendary founder said to have lived 106 years (1378–1484), travelled in the East, and founded an order devoted to healing and reform of the arts and sciences. The manifestos sparked a sensation across Europe; hundreds of replies were published, though no verifiable brotherhood ever came forward.

Beliefs and themes

Rosicrucian texts blend Hermeticism, alchemy, Kabbalah, astrology and Christian mysticism, calling for a universal reformation of knowledge and religion. Whether the original brotherhood existed, the ideas fed into the intellectual currents that produced the scientific revolution, and figures such as René Descartes and Elias Ashmole were rumoured or claimed to be Rosicrucians.

Later organisations

From the 18th century, Rosicrucian degrees appeared in Freemasonry (notably the Gold- und Rosenkreutz order). Modern claimants include the Ancient Mystical Order Rosae Crucis (AMORC, founded 1915), the Rosicrucian Fellowship of Max Heindel, and the Lectorium Rosicrucianum; the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn also drew heavily on Rosicrucian symbolism.
