Haecceity

Haecceity (from the Latin haecceitas, meaning "thisness") is a term from medieval Scholastic philosophy, first systematically developed by John Duns Scotus, denoting the discrete qualities, properties or characteristics of a thing that make it a particular thing, as distinct from the general qualities it shares with other things of its kind.

Concept

Scotus held that a common nature, such as "humanity," is neither strictly universal nor strictly particular in itself, but becomes an individual through contraction by an individuating principle he called haecceitas. A thing's haecceity is what makes Socrates this individual rather than merely an instance of the human species; it is sometimes described as a non-qualitative property of identity, or an "individual difference" added to the common nature.

Related concepts

Haecceity is often contrasted with quiddity (from Latin quidditas, "whatness"), which refers to the essential nature of a thing — what kind of thing it is — as opposed to what makes it this particular token of that kind. In contemporary analytic philosophy, the term is sometimes used in discussions of identity and possible worlds to refer to the property of being identical with a particular individual, as in "the haecceity of Socrates" being the property of being Socrates.

The 20th-century philosopher Gilles Deleuze took up the term, employing "haecceities" for individuations such as a season, an hour or a wind, distinct from the individuation of persons or things.
