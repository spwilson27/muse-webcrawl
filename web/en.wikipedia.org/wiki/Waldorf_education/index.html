Waldorf education

Waldorf education, also known as Steiner education, is based on the educational philosophy of Rudolf Steiner, the founder of anthroposophy. Its pedagogy emphasises the role of imagination in learning and strives to integrate the intellectual, practical, and artistic development of pupils. The first Waldorf school was founded in 1919 in Stuttgart, Germany, for the children of workers at the Waldorf-Astoria cigarette factory, from which the movement takes its name.

Developmental model

Steiner divided childhood into three developmental stages of roughly seven years each. In the first seven years, learning is experiential and imitative, centred on play. The second stage, ages seven to fourteen, emphasises imagination and learning through feeling and the arts, taught largely by a single class teacher who ideally stays with the same class for all eight years. The third stage, from fourteen to twenty-one, focuses on independent critical thinking and abstract reasoning.

Curriculum

Teaching is organised in "main lesson" blocks of several weeks devoted to one subject, taught in the first two hours of the day. The arts are integrated throughout: painting, music, handwork, woodwork, and eurythmy, a movement art devised by Steiner. Reading instruction is typically delayed until around age seven, and textbooks are little used in the early grades; pupils create their own main-lesson books. Standardised testing and grading are minimised in the early years.

Schools worldwide

There are more than 1,200 Waldorf schools and some 2,000 kindergartens in over 75 countries, making the movement one of the largest independent school movements internationally. In some countries, such as Germany and the Netherlands, Waldorf schools receive substantial public funding.

Criticism

Critics have questioned the anthroposophical foundations of the pedagogy, including Steiner's ideas about child development and reincarnation, and the movement's historical positions on race. Some studies find Waldorf pupils perform comparably to peers in later schooling, while others note initial lags in reading and science knowledge that narrow over time.
