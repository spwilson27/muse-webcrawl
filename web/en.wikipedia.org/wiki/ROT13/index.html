ROT13

ROT13 (rotate by 13 places, sometimes hyphenated ROT-13) is a simple letter substitution cipher that replaces a letter with the 13th letter after it in the Latin alphabet. ROT13 is a special case of the Caesar cipher which was developed in ancient Rome. Because there are 26 letters in the modern Latin alphabet, 13 is exactly half the alphabet, so applying ROT13 twice restores the original text; encryption and decryption are the same operation, and the cipher is its own inverse.

Usage

ROT13 provides no real cryptographic security; rather, it is used to obscure text at a glance — classically on Usenet to hide puzzle answers, plot spoilers, and potentially offensive material, so that readers must consciously choose to decode it. Many newsreaders and email clients included a one-keystroke ROT13 decode function. For example, "hello" becomes "uryyb" and vice versa. In the Unicode era it sometimes doubles as a playful obfuscation in forums and challenge puzzles. Variants such as ROT47 apply the same idea to a wider range of ASCII characters.

Example

The following sentence illustrates the transformation: "Why did the chicken cross the road?" becomes "Jul qvq gur puvpxra pebff gur ebnq?" Letters A–M map to N–Z and vice versa; digits and punctuation are left unchanged.
