Ariel (The Tempest)

Ariel is a spirit who appears in William Shakespeare's play The Tempest (1611). Ariel is bound to serve the magician Prospero, who rescued him from the tree in which the witch Sycorax had imprisoned him. Ariel carries out Prospero's commands, creating the storm that opens the play and manipulating the shipwrecked courtiers, in hope of finally earning his freedom.

Role in the play

Before Prospero's arrival, Ariel served the witch Sycorax, who refused to release him for disobedience and trapped him inside a cloven pine, where he remained in torment for twelve years until Prospero freed him. In gratitude, and under promise of eventual liberty, Ariel performs Prospero's magic: he raises the tempest, charms Ferdinand and the courtiers with music, appears as a harpy to rebuke the "three men of sin", and gathers the conspirators for judgement. When Prospero at last renounces magic, he sets Ariel free: "Be free, and fare thou well!"

All hail, great master! grave sir, hail! I come
To answer thy best pleasure; be't to fly,
To swim, to dive into the fire, to ride
On the curl'd clouds; to thy strong bidding task
Ariel and all his quality.

Sources and name

The name Ariel means "lion of God" in Hebrew and was given to spirits and angels in various traditions; Shakespeare may also have drawn on contemporary demonological literature, where Ariel appears as a spirit of the air. Unlike the earthy Caliban, Ariel represents air, music, and swiftness.

Performance history

Ariel was traditionally played by a woman or a young performer to emphasise the spirit's ethereal quality, though modern productions cast actors of all genders. The role demands singing and, in many productions, aerial or dance movement. Ariel's songs, including "Come unto these yellow sands" and "Full fathom five", are among the most famous lyrics in Shakespeare.
