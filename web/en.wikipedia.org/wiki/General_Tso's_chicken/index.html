General Tso's chicken is a deep-fried chicken dish coated in a sweet, slightly spicy sauce that is popular in North American Chinese restaurants. It is named after Zuo Zongtang (1812–1885), a Qing dynasty statesman and military leader from Hunan, though there is no recorded connection between Zuo and the dish, and it is not eaten in Hunan.

Origins

The dish is usually credited to Peng Chang-kuei, a Hunanese chef and former banquet chef for the Nationalist government, who created a dish named for Zuo in Taiwan in the 1950s; his version was more sour and less sweet. In the 1970s, chef Tsung Ting Wang of New York's Shun Lee Palace adapted and popularised the sweetened, crisp-fried version that became a staple of American Chinese take-out. When Peng opened a restaurant in New York in 1973 and later in Hunan in the 1990s, his original version fared less well with local diners.

Preparation

Chunks of dark-meat chicken are battered, deep-fried, and stir-fried in a sauce of soy sauce, rice vinegar, sugar or honey, Shaoxing wine, garlic, ginger and dried red chilies, often served with broccoli. The 2014 documentary The Search for General Tso traces its history.
