Inflatable boat

An inflatable boat is a lightweight boat constructed with sides and bow made of flexible tubes containing pressurised gas. For smaller craft the floor and hull are often flexible, while larger types — known as rigid inflatable boats (RIBs) — combine inflatable tubes with a rigid hull and deck. Inflatable boats range from small dinghies and rafts to craft used by navies and rescue services.

History

Early inflatable craft were developed in the 19th century for lifesaving and military purposes; Charles Goodyear's vulcanised rubber and later materials such as PVC, Hypalon, and neoprene-coated fabrics made practical inflatable boats possible. During the Second World War inflatable boats were used for reconnaissance and raiding. The French company Zodiac became a household name in the post-war era, and in some countries "zodiac" is a generic term for inflatable boats.

Types and uses

Soft inflatables serve as yacht tenders, fishing boats, white-water rafts, and recreational craft; they can be deflated and rolled up for transport. Rigid inflatable boats, with deep-V fibreglass or aluminium hulls, are widely used as lifeboats, military patrol craft, dive boats, and by coast guards and special forces, owing to their stability, buoyancy, and seaworthiness in rough water.

Construction

Multiple independent air chambers provide safety: if one chamber is punctured, the others keep the boat afloat. Floors may be slatted, high-pressure inflatable ("air deck"), or rigid. Propulsion is usually by oars, paddles, or outboard motors mounted on a transom.
