KISS principle

KISS, an acronym for "keep it simple, stupid!", is a design principle first noted by the U.S. Navy in 1960. The KISS principle states that most systems work best if they are kept simple rather than made complicated; therefore, simplicity should be a key goal in design, and unnecessary complexity should be avoided. The phrase has been associated with aircraft engineer Kelly Johnson of Lockheed's Skunk Works.

Origin

The acronym was reportedly coined by Kelly Johnson, lead engineer at the Lockheed Skunk Works, creators of aircraft such as the U-2 and SR-71 Blackbird. Johnson told his designers that anything they built had to be repairable by an average mechanic in the field under combat conditions with only simple tools; if their designs were not simple, cheap and easy to fix, they would cost lives. The term "KISS principle" and its expansion were widely used in the U.S. Department of Defense by the 1970s.

Variations

Because "stupid" can be read as an insult, gentler backronyms are common, including "keep it simple, silly", "keep it short and simple", and "keep it simple and straightforward". Related expressions include Albert Einstein's attributed maxim that everything should be made "as simple as possible, but not simpler", Leonardo da Vinci's "simplicity is the ultimate sophistication", and the engineering rule of thumb known as Occam's razor.

Usage

The principle is invoked in software engineering (as a counterweight to feature creep and over-engineering), military logistics, business communication, education and product design. In software it is related to the philosophies "worse is better" and the Unix philosophy of small, single-purpose tools, and contrasts with approaches that favour elaborate frameworks for anticipated rather than actual needs.
