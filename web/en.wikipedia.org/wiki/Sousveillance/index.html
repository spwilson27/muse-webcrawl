Sousveillance

Sousveillance
Coined bySteve Mann
EtymologyFrench sous ("below") + veiller ("to watch")
Contrasts withSurveillance ("watching from above")
Related conceptsEquiveillance, lifelogging, copwatch

Sousveillance is the practice of recording an activity from the perspective of a participant in that activity, typically using portable or wearable personal recording devices — "watching from below". The term was coined by Canadian engineer and inventor Steve Mann, from the French prefix sous- ("below") and veiller ("to watch"), as the inverse of surveillance, in which authorities watch from above.

Definitions and forms

Mann distinguishes several forms. In its original sense, sousveillance means individuals recording their own encounters, often to hold powerful institutions accountable — sometimes described as "inverse surveillance". A broader sense covers the pervasive personal recording of everyday life (lifelogging), including wearable cameras, dashcams, and body cameras worn by citizens. Mann proposed the concept of equiveillance: an equilibrium between surveillance and sousveillance in which neither party monopolises the record of an interaction.

History and notable cases

Mann began experimenting with wearable computing and cameras in the 1970s and 1980s at MIT and later the University of Toronto, documenting his own treatment by security guards and businesses; a 2012 incident at a Paris McDonald's, in which employees allegedly assaulted him over his EyeTap device, became a widely reported example. Citizen sousveillance has repeatedly shaped public events: the 1991 Rodney King beating video, bystander footage of police conduct such as the killing of George Floyd in 2020, and the work of Copwatch groups that patrol to record police activity.

Legal and ethical issues

Sousveillance raises contested legal questions about consent, wiretapping and audio-recording statutes, and police attempts to seize or delete citizens' recordings; courts in several jurisdictions have affirmed a right to record officials performing public duties. Ethically, critics note that ubiquitous personal recording can itself chill behaviour and erode bystanders' privacy, blurring the line Mann drew between watching the powerful and watching everyone.

Technology

Enabling technologies include camera phones, action cameras, dashcams, smart glasses, and police-style body-worn cameras adopted by private citizens and journalists. Mann's later work on "veillance" and the "veillance society" frames the politics of these devices within surveillance studies, a field further developed by scholars such as David Lyon.
