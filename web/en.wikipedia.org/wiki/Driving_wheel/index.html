Driving wheel

A driving wheel is a wheel on a locomotive that is driven by the locomotive's pistons (or turbine or electric traction motor) via connecting rods or gearing, and which transmits tractive effort to the rail. On a steam locomotive the driving wheels are usually much larger than the leading and trailing wheels, and are coupled together by coupling (side) rods so that they turn together.

Steam locomotives

On a conventional steam locomotive, the driving wheels are powered by the pistons through main rods connected to crank pins on the wheels. The diameter of the drivers strongly influences performance: large drivers (of 2 metres or more) favour high speed, as on express passenger locomotives, while small drivers give greater tractive effort at low speed for freight work. The number of driving axles forms part of the Whyte notation used to describe locomotive wheel arrangements — for example, a 4-6-2 "Pacific" has three pairs of driving wheels.

Diesel and electric locomotives

On diesel-electric and electric locomotives, each axle in a powered bogie (truck) may be driven by its own traction motor, so all wheels in the bogie are driving wheels of roughly equal, modest diameter. Older diesel-mechanical and rod-drive electric locomotives used jackshafts and coupling rods similar to steam practice.

Other uses

The term is also used for the powered wheel of other machinery, such as the wheel of a traction engine or steamroller that propels the vehicle, and for the large wheel of a stationary engine that drives a belt or line shaft.
