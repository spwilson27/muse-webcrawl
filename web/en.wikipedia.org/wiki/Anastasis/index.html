Anastasis (Greek: ἀνάστασις, "rising again") is the Greek term for resurrection, used in the New Testament and in Christian theology for the resurrection of Jesus Christ. In Byzantine and Eastern Orthodox art, the Anastasis is the standard iconographic image of Easter and of the Harrowing of Hell: Christ, in radiant white or gold robes, descends into Hades and raises Adam and Eve — and with them all righteous humanity — by the hand from their tombs, trampling the broken doors of hell beneath his feet.

In theology and liturgy

The word appears frequently in the Greek New Testament for the general resurrection of the dead awaited at the last day (for example, "the resurrection of the just", Luke 14:14). The Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem is known in Greek as the Church of the Anastasis (Ναὸς τῆς Ἀναστάσεως), since it enshrines the site of Christ's resurrection.

In art

Early Christian art usually symbolised the resurrection indirectly, but from roughly the ninth century the Anastasis scene became the canonical Easter image in the Byzantine world. Celebrated examples include the mosaics of Hosios Loukas and Daphni in Greece and the great fresco in the parekklesion of the Chora Church (Kariye Mosque) in Istanbul, c. 1316–1321, showing Christ pulling Adam and Eve from their sarcophagi amid a crowd of Old Testament figures.
