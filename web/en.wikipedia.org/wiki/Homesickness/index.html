Homesickness

Homesickness is the distress or impairment caused by actual or anticipated separation from home. It is characterized by acute longing and preoccupying thoughts of home and attachment figures, and commonly occurs in children and adults who leave home for school, university, military service, work, or migration. In its usual form it is a normal, temporary reaction, but intense homesickness can resemble depressive and anxiety symptoms, with somatic complaints such as disturbed sleep and appetite.

History

The condition was first described medically by the Swiss physician Johannes Hofer in 1688 under the name nostalgia (from the Greek nostos, "homecoming", and algos, "pain"), coined for the melancholy observed in Swiss mercenaries serving far from home. For centuries nostalgia was treated as a potentially fatal medical disorder, particularly among soldiers; only in the 19th and 20th centuries did the meaning of "nostalgia" shift to a sentimental longing for the past generally, while "homesickness" became the standard term for separation distress.

Risk factors and coping

Psychological research identifies the strongest risk factors as a person's prior homesickness experience, attachment style, low perceived control over the separation, cultural distance, and negative expectations or attitudes toward the new environment. Studies of boarding-school pupils, first-year university students, and expatriates suggest most cases resolve as familiarity and new social ties develop. Recommended coping strategies include maintaining (but not excessive) contact with home, building routines and friendships in the new setting, keeping familiar objects, and reframing the separation as time-limited; for children, preparation and "practice" separations reduce risk. Severe or persistent homesickness that impairs functioning may warrant professional support.
