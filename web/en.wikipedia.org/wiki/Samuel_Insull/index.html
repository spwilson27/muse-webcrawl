Samuel Insull (11 November 1859 – 16 July 1938) was a British-born American business magnate who built an empire of electric utility companies in the United States. A former secretary to Thomas Edison, he was a key figure in creating America's integrated electrical infrastructure; the collapse of his holding-company empire during the Great Depression became a symbol of 1920s financial excess and led to major regulatory reforms.

Early life and Edison

Born in London, Insull began work as a clerk and shorthand writer. In 1881 he emigrated to the United States to become personal secretary to Thomas Edison. He proved a formidable organiser, managing Edison's business affairs and helping to build the Edison General Electric Company works at Schenectady, New York. He rose to be a vice-president of the newly formed General Electric in 1892 but left after being passed over for its presidency.

Chicago utilities empire

Insull moved to Chicago in 1892 to head the small Chicago Edison Company. He pioneered the economics of mass electricity supply: consolidating competing generating companies, adopting large steam turbines, promoting off-peak demand (including electric traction), and cutting prices to expand the market. His Commonwealth Edison supplied Chicago, and through layered holding companies — notably Middle West Utilities — he assembled an empire of electric, gas and traction properties across many states, valued in the billions of dollars at its late-1920s peak. Insull also had interests in Chicago's elevated railways and financed the Civic Opera House.

Collapse and trial

The Insull holding-company structure, built on leverage, collapsed in 1932 amid the Depression, wiping out the savings of many small investors. Insull fled to Europe; he was extradited from Turkey in 1934 and tried in the United States on charges including mail fraud and embezzlement. He was acquitted on all counts. He died of a heart attack in a Paris Métro station in 1938, reportedly with only a few francs in his pocket.

Legacy

The Insull collapse spurred landmark reforms: the Public Utility Holding Company Act of 1935, which broke up and strictly regulated utility holding companies, and strengthened federal securities regulation. His career remains a classic case study in both the building of modern utility networks and the dangers of pyramided corporate finance.
