Separation (air traffic control)

In air traffic control, separation is the name for the concept of keeping an aircraft outside a minimum distance from another aircraft to reduce the risk of those aircraft colliding, as well as to prevent accidents due to secondary factors such as wake turbulence. Air traffic controllers apply separation standards using radar surveillance, procedural (non-radar) rules, or both; where separation cannot be assured, flight in visual meteorological conditions may transfer responsibility for collision avoidance partly to pilots under the "see and avoid" principle.

Vertical separation

Between the surface and an altitude of 29,000 feet (FL290), no aircraft should come closer vertically than 500 feet to another unless one is climbing or descending. Above FL290 the standard historically was 2,000 feet; the introduction of Reduced Vertical Separation Minima (RVSM), phased in between 1997 and 2005, reduced this to 1,000 feet between FL290 and FL410 for approved aircraft, greatly increasing airspace capacity.

Horizontal separation

Lateral separation is the angular or distance-based spacing between aircraft on different routes or tracks, while longitudinal separation applies to aircraft on the same track, measured in time or distance (e.g., a specified number of minutes or nautical miles). Radar separation minima are commonly 5 nautical miles in en-route airspace and 3 nautical miles in terminal areas, though values vary by jurisdiction and equipment.

Wake turbulence

Separation minima are increased behind heavy aircraft to allow wingtip vortices to dissipate; aircraft are classified by maximum take-off weight into categories such as heavy, medium, and light, with "super" added for the Airbus A380.

Loss of separation

An occurrence in which separation minima were infringed is termed a loss of separation (or "operational error"/"operational deviation") and is investigated to prevent recurrence. Conflict detection tools such as short-term conflict alert (STCA) warn controllers of predicted losses.
