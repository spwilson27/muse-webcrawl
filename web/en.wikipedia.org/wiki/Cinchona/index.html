Cinchona is a genus of flowering plants in the family Rubiaceae, native to the cloud forests of the tropical Andes of western South America. Its bark is the natural source of quinine and related alkaloids, for centuries the only effective treatment for malaria.

Description

Cinchonas are evergreen shrubs or trees reaching up to about 15–20 metres, with opposite leaves and clusters of fragrant pink, white or red tubular flowers. Some two dozen to forty species are recognized, many of them narrowly distributed in Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia and Colombia.

History

Indigenous Andean peoples knew the fever-reducing properties of the bark, and Jesuit missionaries carried "Peruvian bark" or "Jesuit's bark" to Europe in the 17th century. A famous (and probably embellished) legend attributes its introduction to the Countess of Chinchón, wife of the Viceroy of Peru; Linnaeus named the genus Cinchona in her honour, misspelling the name. In 1820 the French chemists Pierre Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Bienaimé Caventou isolated the active alkaloid quinine.

To break the South American monopoly, British and Dutch expeditions smuggled seeds and plants in the 19th century — notably those collected by Clements Markham and Charles Ledger. Dutch plantations in Java, using high-quinine Cinchona ledgeriana grafted onto hardy rootstock, came to dominate world supply, making the Java monopoly a strategic crisis when it fell to Japan in 1942 and spurring Allied development of synthetic antimalarials.

Uses

Quinine extracted from cinchona bark remains in limited medical use, and the alkaloid gives tonic water its characteristic bitterness. Bark extracts also flavour bitter aperitifs such as some amari. The genus appears on the coat of arms of Peru, reflecting its historical importance.
