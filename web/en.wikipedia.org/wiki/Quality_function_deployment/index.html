Quality function deployment

The House of Quality, the central matrix of QFD.

Quality function deployment (QFD) is a method developed in Japan beginning in the late 1960s to transform the "voice of the customer" — user demands and needs — into engineering characteristics and quality requirements for a product or service. It was formalised by Shigeru Mizuno and Yoji Akao, and first implemented at Mitsubishi's Kobe shipyard in 1972 before being adopted by Toyota and later by manufacturers worldwide.

Method

QFD proceeds through a series of linked matrices, of which the best known is the House of Quality. Customer requirements ("whats") are listed down the left side and weighted by importance; technical descriptors ("hows") run across the top. The body of the matrix rates the strength of the relationship between each customer requirement and each technical characteristic, while the triangular "roof" captures correlations among the technical descriptors themselves. Benchmarking rows compare the product with competitors from the customer's viewpoint and from engineering tests. In the classic four-phase model, the outputs of each matrix become the inputs of the next: product planning feeds part deployment, which feeds process planning, which feeds production planning.

Benefits and criticism

Advocates credit QFD with shorter development time, fewer late design changes, and products better matched to customer needs, because teams must agree explicitly on priorities and trade-offs early. Critics note that the matrices become unwieldy for complex products, that customer-requirement weights are subjective, and that the method requires substantial cross-functional effort.
