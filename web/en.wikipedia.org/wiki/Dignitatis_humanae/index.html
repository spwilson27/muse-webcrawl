Dignitatis humanae (Latin for "Of the Dignity of the Human Person") is the Second Vatican Council's Declaration on Religious Freedom. It was promulgated by Pope Paul VI on 7 December 1965, having been passed by a vote of 2,308 to 70 among the assembled bishops. The declaration is considered one of the council's most significant and controversial texts.

Content

The declaration holds that the human person has a right to religious freedom, understood as immunity from coercion in civil society in matters of religion: no one is to be forced to act against their beliefs, nor restrained from acting in accordance with them, whether in private or in public, individually or in association with others, within due limits. It grounds this right in the dignity of the human person, known through reason and revelation.

At the same time, the declaration states that it "leaves untouched the traditional Catholic doctrine on the moral duty of men and societies toward the true religion and toward the one Church of Christ," a formulation that has been the subject of extensive theological debate.

Background and drafting

The text was prepared by the Secretariat for Promoting Christian Unity under Cardinal Augustin Bea, with major contributions from the American Jesuit theologian John Courtney Murray, who had earlier been silenced on the topic of church–state relations. Its passage required several redraftings and survived attempts to have it withdrawn from the council agenda.

Reception

Dignitatis humanae was welcomed by ecumenical partners and by advocates of human rights, and it influenced Catholic engagement with liberal democracy. Some traditionalist Catholics, most notably Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre and the Society of Saint Pius X, have rejected the declaration as incompatible with earlier papal teaching, a disagreement that contributed to the 1988 Écône consecrations.
