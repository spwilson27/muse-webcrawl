Going Up the Country


Canned Heat, whose recording made the song famous.

"Going Up the Country" (also rendered "Going Up the Country") is a blues song adapted and recorded by the American blues-rock band Canned Heat. Released in 1968 on the album Living the Blues, it became the band's biggest hit and an unofficial anthem of the Woodstock generation.

Origins and recording

The song was adapted by Canned Heat's Alan "Blind Owl" Wilson from "Bull Doze Blues", a 1928 recording by the Texas bluesman Henry Thomas, whose quill-pipe accompaniment inspired the track's distinctive flute-like riff, played on the recording by Jim Horn. Wilson rewrote Thomas's lyrics into a celebration of leaving the city for rural freedom — "I'm going up the country, baby don't you wanna go" — and sang lead in his characteristic high tenor. The single reached number 11 on the US Billboard Hot 100 and charted internationally in 1969, becoming Canned Heat's signature tune.

Woodstock and legacy

Canned Heat performed the song during their set at the Woodstock festival in August 1969, and the recording was used over the opening travel scenes of the 1970 Woodstock documentary film, forever associating it with the festival and the back-to-the-land movement of the counterculture. The song has since appeared in numerous films, advertisements and cover versions, and is recognised as one of the defining recordings of the late-1960s blues revival led by bands such as Canned Heat, who modelled themselves as revivalists and collectors of pre-war American blues.
