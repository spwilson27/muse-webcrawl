Pan-Arabism

[Image: The pan-Arab colours — black, white, green and red — as used on flags of Arab states]

Pan-Arabism (Arabic: الوحدة العربية, al-waḥda al-ʿarabiyya) is a nationalist ideology that advocates the political unification of the Arab world — the Arabic-speaking countries of North Africa and Western Asia — into a single state or close federation. It holds that Arabs constitute one nation bound by common language, culture, and history, and that their division into separate states is an artificial legacy of Ottoman rule and European colonialism.

Origins and development

Pan-Arab ideas grew out of the Arab cultural revival (Nahda) of the late 19th century and took political form during and after the First World War, notably in the Arab Revolt of 1916–18. Thinkers such as Sati' al-Husri supplied a linguistic theory of Arab nationhood, and Michel Aflaq's Ba'ath Party, founded in Syria in the 1940s, combined Arab unity with socialism. The Arab League, founded in 1945, institutionalised cooperation while falling short of union.

Heyday

Pan-Arabism reached its height under Egypt's Gamal Abdel Nasser, whose charisma and defiance of Britain and France at Suez (1956) made him the movement's leader. In 1958 Egypt and Syria merged as the United Arab Republic, but Syria seceded in 1961, illustrating the difficulty of unity in practice. The Arab defeat in the Six-Day War of 1967 gravely damaged Nasserism and the ideology's prestige, and the 1979 Egyptian–Israeli peace treaty, which led to Egypt's suspension from the Arab League, marked its further decline.

Legacy

From the 1970s, state-centred nationalism and political Islam gained ground at pan-Arabism's expense, though Arab identity remains a potent cultural and political force, and institutions such as the Organization of Islamic Cooperation and the Arab League continue to invoke Arab solidarity. Historians debate the relative weight of ideology, leadership rivalries, and state interests in the movement's failures.
