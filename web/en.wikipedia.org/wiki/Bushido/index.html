Bushido (Japanese: 武士道, "the way of the warrior") is a code of conduct associated with the Japanese samurai, emphasising martial virtue, loyalty, honour and indifference to death. Though its roots lie in the medieval warrior houses, the code was articulated most fully in the peaceful Edo period and was popularised worldwide by Nitobe Inazō's book Bushido: The Soul of Japan (1899).

The virtues

Nitobe summarised the code in seven virtues: rectitude (gi), courage (yū), benevolence (jin), politeness and respect (rei), honesty (makoto), honour (meiyo) and loyalty (chūgi), to which self-control is often added. A samurai who failed in honour might redeem himself by the ritual suicide of seppuku, and the loyal retainer who avenged his lord — as in the famous story of the forty-seven rōnin — became the code's exemplary hero.

Historical development

Warrior values developed from the Kamakura period onward under the influence of Confucian ethics, Zen Buddhism and Shinto. In the Edo period authors such as Yamaga Sokō and Yamamoto Tsunetomo, whose Hagakure (c. 1716) declared that "the way of the samurai is found in death", theorised the ethic for a warrior class now serving as administrators. Historians caution that "bushido" as a single timeless code is partly a Meiji-era construction, shaped to serve the modern Japanese state.

Modern legacy

After 1868 the code was invoked to define Japanese national morality and military spirit, an appropriation carried to extremes in the Second World War. In contemporary Japan and abroad, bushido survives chiefly in the martial arts (budō), in business ethics, and as a popular cultural image of the samurai.
