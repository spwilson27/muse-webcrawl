Smoked haddock

Smoked haddock is haddock that has been preserved by smoking. It is a traditional product of the fishing ports of north-east Scotland and northern England, and an ingredient in dishes such as Cullen skink and kedgeree. The best-known traditional varieties are Finnan haddie and the Arbroath smokie.

Varieties

Finnan haddie

Finnan haddie (or Finnan haddock) is cold-smoked haddock, representative of a technique originating in the fishing village of Finnan (Findon) near Aberdeen in north-east Scotland, where fish were smoked over peat fires — by legend originally after a fire in a fish-curing shed in the 18th century. The fish are split, lightly brined and smoked until pale gold; Finnan haddie is traditionally poached in milk and served with butter.

Arbroath smokie

The Arbroath smokie is hot-smoked: pairs of whole haddock, tied by the tail, are salted and smoked over hardwood in a half-barrel known as a smokie pit, giving a ready-to-eat fish with a coppery skin. It originates with the fishing community of Auchmithie and is associated with Arbroath in Angus; it holds EU/UK protected geographical indication (PGI) status.

Production

Traditional smoking uses smouldering oak, beech or peat in a smokehouse for hours; cold-smoking keeps the temperature low so the fish remains raw and must be cooked before eating, while hot-smoking cooks the fish through. Much modern "smoked haddock" is produced industrially and may be dyed yellow to imitate the golden colour of the traditional product; undyed fish is paler and is marketed as such.

Culinary use

Smoked haddock is the centrepiece of Cullen skink, a thick Scottish soup of smoked haddock, potatoes and onions from the town of Cullen in Moray. It is also used in kedgeree (with rice, eggs and curry spices), in fish pies, omelette Arnold Bennett (created at London's Savoy Hotel for the writer Arnold Bennett), and simply poached with a poached egg on top.
