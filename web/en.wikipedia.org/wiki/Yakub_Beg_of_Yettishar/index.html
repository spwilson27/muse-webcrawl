Muhammad Yakub Beg (1820 – May 1877) was a Kokandi adventurer and military leader who became the ruler of Yettishar, a state he carved out of southwestern Xinjiang (Kashgaria) during the great Muslim rebellions against Qing China. Known to contemporaries as the "Atalik Ghazi" and dubbed by some Western writers the "Ruler of Kashgaria," he built a short-lived but internationally noticed Central Asian emirate at the height of the Anglo-Russian "Great Game."

Rise to power

Yakub Beg, of Tajik or Uzbek origin from the Khanate of Kokand, rose as a military commander and was sent in 1865 to Kashgar to assist a Khoja claimant amid the Dungan Revolt. He quickly displaced his nominal masters, conquered the oasis cities of the Tarim Basin — Kashgar, Yarkand, Khotan, Aksu, and others — and by about 1867 ruled the "seven cities" of Yettishar, later extending influence toward Ürümqi and Turpan. He imposed strict Islamic law, taxed heavily, but restored order and commerce, and he sought recognition abroad: he signed a commercial treaty with Russia (1872), received British missions (including the Forsyth mission of 1873–74) and concluded a treaty with Britain in 1874, and obtained nominal investiture from the Ottoman sultan, whose flag and titles he adopted.

Fall

The Qing, having suppressed the revolts in Shaanxi and Gansu, sent General Zuo Zongtang to reconquer Xinjiang. Zuo's methodical advance in 1876–77 overthrew Yakub Beg's northern positions; Yakub Beg died in May 1877 at Korla — by illness, suicide, or assassination, accounts differ — and his state dissolved as his sons quarrelled. Qing forces retook Kashgar by December 1877, ending Yettishar.
