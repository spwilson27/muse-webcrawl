Deer in the United Kingdom

Deer have been an important part of the fauna of the United Kingdom for thousands of years. Six species are now established in the wild: two are considered native — the red deer and the roe deer — while the fallow deer, sika deer, muntjac and Chinese water deer were all introduced by humans. With no natural predators remaining in Britain, deer populations are managed by culling, and their growing numbers have significant effects on woodland ecology.

Species

The red deer (Cervus elaphus) is Britain's largest native land mammal and is particularly associated with the open moorlands of the Scottish Highlands, though herds also survive in the Lake District, Exmoor and the New Forest. The roe deer (Capreolus capreolus) is a smaller, more solitary species found in woodland throughout much of Britain.

The fallow deer was brought to Britain, probably by the Normans, for hunting in deer parks. The sika deer, Reeves's muntjac and Chinese water deer all escaped or were released from private collections, notably Woburn Abbey, during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and have since spread from those centres.

Distribution and numbers

Total deer numbers in Britain are not known precisely but are widely believed to be at their highest level for a millennium, with estimates in excess of two million animals. Muntjac in particular have expanded their range steadily northwards from the Home Counties. Deer are absent from some islands but are otherwise found across most of England, Scotland and Wales; they are a comparatively recent arrival over much of Northern Ireland.

Impact and management

Heavy browsing by deer prevents natural regeneration of woodland, damages crops and gardens, and contributes to road traffic collisions — tens of thousands of deer-vehicle collisions are estimated to occur each year. In England, the Forestry Commission and private landowners carry out extensive culling, and venison from culled animals enters the food chain. The Deer Act 1991 regulates close seasons and methods of taking deer in England and Wales, while in Scotland deer management is overseen under the Deer (Scotland) Act 1996, administered by NatureScot.
