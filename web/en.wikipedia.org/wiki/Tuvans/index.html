The Tuvans (Tuvan: Тывалар) are a Turkic ethnic group native to Tuva, in southern Siberia, with communities also in Mongolia and China. They speak Tuvan, a Siberian Turkic language, and are renowned for khöömei, the overtone ("throat") singing tradition in which a vocalist produces two or more pitches simultaneously.

History

The Tuvan homeland, the basin of the upper Yenisei, was ruled in succession by Xiongnu, Göktürk, Uyghur, Kyrgyz, Mongol, and Qing powers; the Tuvans (as Uriankhai) were administered by Qing China until 1911. The Tuvan People's Republic existed as a nominally independent state from 1921 until its annexation by the Soviet Union in 1944. Tuva is today a republic within the Russian Federation, and Tuvans form the large majority of its population of about 330,000.

Culture

Traditional Tuvan life centred on nomadic herding of sheep, goats, cattle, yaks, horses, and reindeer (among the Dukha in the north-east), with felt yurts as dwellings. Tuvan culture blends Tibetan Buddhism — introduced from Mongolia — with indigenous shamanism. Throat-singing styles such as khöömei, sygyt, and kargyraa are inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The republic's capital is Kyzyl, near the geographical centre of Asia.
