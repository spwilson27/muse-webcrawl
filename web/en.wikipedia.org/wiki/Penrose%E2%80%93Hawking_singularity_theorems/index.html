Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems

Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems
AuthorsRoger Penrose (1965); Stephen Hawking; Hawking & Penrose (1970)
FieldGeneral relativity, mathematical physics
ResultUnder generic conditions, gravitational collapse and the expanding universe are geodesically incomplete — they contain singularities

The Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems are a set of results in general relativity showing that singularities — points where spacetime geodesics end and the classical theory breaks down — are not artifacts of idealized symmetric models but a generic prediction of Einstein's theory. They imply that a collapsing star forms a singularity inside a black hole, and that the universe began in a singular state, the Big Bang.

Background and results

Roger Penrose proved the first theorem in 1965, introducing the concept of a trapped surface: once matter collapses so far that even outgoing light rays are dragged inward, gravitational focusing makes a singularity inevitable. Stephen Hawking adapted the methods to cosmology, and the Hawking–Penrose theorem of 1970 showed singularities follow from assumptions such as an energy condition, the absence of closed timelike curves, and the existence of a trapped surface or a point where converging light rays reconverge.

Significance

The theorems rest on causal geometry rather than on solving Einstein's equations for specific matter, so their conclusions are robust. Their endpoint — geodesic incompleteness — signals where general relativity must give way to a quantum theory of gravity. Penrose's contribution was cited in his 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.
