Electron donor

An electron donor is a chemical entity that transfers electrons to another compound. It is a reducing agent, and by donating electrons it becomes oxidized itself in the process. The counterpart species that accepts the electrons is called an electron acceptor.

In chemistry

In redox chemistry, electron donors include metals such as sodium and zinc, hydride reagents, and organic compounds able to give up electrons. In Lewis acid–base terms, a Lewis base is an electron-pair donor. Electron donors are also fundamental to organic electronics and photochemistry, where donor molecules are paired with acceptors in charge-transfer complexes.

In biology

In metabolism, electron donors fuel cellular respiration and other energy-yielding processes. Common biological electron donors include organic molecules such as glucose, fatty acids, and glycerol, as well as reduced compounds such as NADH and FADH2, which deliver electrons to electron transport chains. Some microorganisms use inorganic donors — hydrogen, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur, ferrous iron, or ammonia — in chemolithotrophy. In photosynthesis, water serves as the electron donor for most oxygenic phototrophs.

In semiconductor physics

In semiconductors, a donor is a dopant atom that, when added to a semiconductor, releases a free electron, producing an n-type semiconductor. Phosphorus and arsenic are common donors in silicon.
