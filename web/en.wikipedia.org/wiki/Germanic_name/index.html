Germanic name

Germanic names, inherited from Proto-Germanic, are traditionally dithematic: they are formed by combining two elements (themes), such as *berhtaz "bright" and *hrabanaz "raven." This system, shared across the Germanic peoples, survived for centuries in names like Adolf ("noble wolf"), Bernard ("brave bear"), and Gertrude ("spear strength").

Structure

Dithematic names combine a first and a second element drawn from a stock of themes denoting animals, weapons, kinship, gods, and abstract qualities — for example sigi- "victory," -mund "protection," -hild "battle," wulf- "wolf." Parents often combined one element from each parent's name, or repeated an element among siblings (alliteration), a practice visible in heroic poetry such as Beowulf. Some early names were monothematic bynames or short forms, e.g., Karl ("man, free man") or Ernst ("seriousness").

Development

In the early medieval period, dithematic naming was near-universal among the Germanic aristocracy of England, Francia, and Scandinavia. From roughly the 11th century onward, Christian names of Latin, Greek, and Hebrew origin increasingly displaced native names, first in southern Europe and later in the north, though Germanic names persisted strongly in Scandinavia and Germany. Many survive today, sometimes in altered forms: Old English Æðelræd gives Ethelred; Old Norse Guðrún gives Gudrun; continental names such as Heinrich, Friedrich, Wilhelm, and Ludwig became widespread in English as Henry, Frederick, William, and Louis (via French).
