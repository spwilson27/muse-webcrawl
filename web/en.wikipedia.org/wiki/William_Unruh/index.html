William George Unruh (born August 28, 1945) is a Canadian theoretical physicist at the University of British Columbia whose research concerns gravity, quantum mechanics, and their intersection. He is best known for the Unruh effect, the prediction that an accelerating observer will perceive empty space as a warm bath of thermal radiation, and for proposing "dumb holes" — acoustic analogues of black holes — as a way to test Hawking radiation in the laboratory.

Education and career

Unruh studied at the University of Manitoba, where he received his BSc in 1967, and at Princeton University, where he received his PhD in 1971 under the supervision of John Archibald Wheeler. After postdoctoral work he joined the University of British Columbia, where he has spent most of his career, and he long directed the Cosmology and Gravity Program of the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research.

The Unruh effect

In 1976 Unruh showed that the notion of a particle depends on the observer's motion through spacetime: a uniformly accelerating detector in a vacuum will register a temperature proportional to its acceleration, T = ħa / (2πckB). The effect is intimately related to Hawking radiation and has become a cornerstone of quantum field theory in curved spacetime.

Analogue gravity

In 1981 Unruh proposed that sound waves in a transsonic fluid flow create an acoustic horizon analogous to a black hole's event horizon, opening the field now called analogue gravity. Experiments on such "sonic black holes" in fluids and Bose–Einstein condensates have since reported evidence consistent with analogue Hawking emission.
