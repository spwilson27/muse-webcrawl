Howard Phillips Lovecraft (August 20, 1890 – March 15, 1937) was an American writer of weird, science, fantasy, and horror fiction. He is best known for his creation of the Cthulhu Mythos, a shared fictional universe populated by cosmic entities indifferent to humanity. Although he achieved only modest success during his lifetime, publishing mainly in pulp magazines such as Weird Tales, Lovecraft is now regarded as one of the most significant and influential 20th-century authors of horror and weird fiction.

Early life

Lovecraft was born in Providence, Rhode Island, into a prosperous family whose fortunes collapsed during his childhood. His father suffered a psychotic episode and died in a psychiatric hospital in 1898; Lovecraft was raised by his mother, aunts, and grandfather, the industrialist Whipple Van Buren Phillips, whose library introduced him to classical literature and The Arabian Nights. A sickly and precocious child, he had an early fascination with astronomy and wrote amateur scientific journals. His mother's death in 1921, also in a mental hospital, deeply affected him.

Career

Lovecraft became involved in amateur journalism, serving as president of the United Amateur Press Association, and through it met his future wife Sonia Greene. After a brief and unhappy period in New York City (1924–1926), he returned to Providence, where he wrote most of his best-known fiction, including "The Call of Cthulhu" (1928), "The Dunwich Horror" (1929), The Whisperer in Darkness (1931), At the Mountains of Madness (1936) and "The Shadow over Innsmouth" (1931, published 1936). He supported himself partly by ghostwriting and revising the work of other authors, and he corresponded voluminously with a circle of writers including Robert E. Howard, Clark Ashton Smith, Robert Bloch and August Derleth.

Themes and influences

Lovecraft's fiction is characterised by "cosmicism": the philosophy that humanity is insignificant in a vast, indifferent universe, and that forbidden knowledge tends to destroy those who seek it. Recurring elements include ancient extraterrestrial gods (the Great Old Ones), fictional grimoires such as the Necronomicon, decaying New England towns, and unreliable narrators driven mad by what they learn. His principal influences included Edgar Allan Poe, Lord Dunsany, Arthur Machen and Algernon Blackwood. Lovecraft's xenophobic and racist views, expressed in his fiction and letters, have been widely discussed and criticised in reassessments of his work.

Death and legacy

Lovecraft died of cancer of the small intestine in Providence on March 15, 1937, aged 46, in relative poverty and obscurity. After his death, August Derleth and Donald Wandrei founded Arkham House to preserve his work in book form, and the Cthulhu Mythos was expanded by many successors. Since the 1970s his reputation has grown steadily; his work has influenced writers such as Stephen King, Neil Gaiman and China Miéville, filmmakers including John Carpenter and Guillermo del Toro, and games from role-playing games to video games. The annual World Fantasy Award was modelled on his likeness until 2015, when it was redesigned following debate over his views.
