The Whitechapel murders were a series of murders committed in or near the impoverished Whitechapel district of the East End of London between 1888 and 1891. The Metropolitan Police file on the case contains eleven unsolved murders of women. Five of the victims — Mary Ann Nichols, Annie Chapman, Elizabeth Stride, Catherine Eddowes, and Mary Jane Kelly — are known as the "canonical five" and are generally attributed to a single unidentified killer who became known as Jack the Ripper.

The canonical five

The five murders occurred between 31 August and 9 November 1888, in the small area spanning Whitechapel and Spitalfields. The victims were impoverished women, several of whom worked as casual prostitutes. Their throats were cut and their bodies mutilated, leading police and press to conclude that the killer possessed anatomical knowledge. Catherine Eddowes was murdered in the City of London, and on the night of her death Elizabeth Stride was also killed — the so-called "double event". Mary Jane Kelly, the last of the five, was murdered and extensively mutilated indoors in Miller's Court on 9 November 1888.

Investigation and publicity

The investigation involved both the Metropolitan Police and the City of London Police, and was headed at first by Inspector Frederick Abberline. The murders received unprecedented press coverage, and hundreds of letters claiming to be from the killer were received; the name "Jack the Ripper" itself came from one such letter, the "Dear Boss" letter, widely considered a journalist's fabrication. Letters such as the "Saucy Jacky" postcard and the "From Hell" letter — the latter accompanied by half a human kidney — remain famous. Despite a huge police effort, house-to-house enquiries, and the involvement of the Whitechapel Vigilance Committee, no one was ever charged.

Other victims in the file

The Whitechapel murders file also includes Emma Elizabeth Smith and Martha Tabram (1888, before the canonical five), and Rose Mylett, Alice McKenzie, the unidentified "Pinchin Street torso", and Frances Coles (1889–1891). Whether these were the work of the same killer has been debated ever since.

Legacy

The murders exposed the poverty of the Victorian East End and contributed to reforms in housing and policing. The case remains one of the most famous unsolved murder investigations in the world, generating a vast literature of suspect theories known as "Ripperology".
