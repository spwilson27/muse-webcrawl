Hereditary fructose intolerance

SpecialtyMedical genetics, endocrinology
SymptomsVomiting, hypoglycemia, jaundice, failure to thrive after eating fructose
CausesMutations in the ALDOB gene (aldolase B deficiency), autosomal recessive
TreatmentStrict avoidance of fructose, sucrose and sorbitol
Frequency~1 in 20,000–30,000 newborns

Hereditary fructose intolerance (HFI) is an inborn error of fructose metabolism caused by a deficiency of the enzyme aldolase B (fructose-1-phosphate aldolase), encoded by the ALDOB gene on chromosome 9. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. Unlike benign fructose malabsorption, HFI is a potentially life-threatening condition: ingested fructose becomes trapped as fructose-1-phosphate in the liver, kidney and intestine, poisoning those organs and causing dangerous hypoglycemia.

Pathophysiology

Dietary fructose (from fruit, table sugar — sucrose — honey, and many processed foods) is normally split by aldolase B into dihydroxyacetone phosphate and glyceraldehyde. In HFI this step fails, so fructose-1-phosphate accumulates, sequestering inorganic phosphate and depleting cellular ATP. The consequences include blocked glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis (hence severe hypoglycemia despite full glycogen stores), hepatocellular damage, and proximal renal tubular dysfunction.

Presentation

Affected infants are well while fed only breast milk or lactose-based formula; symptoms begin at weaning, when fruit, sweetened foods or sucrose-containing formulas are introduced: vomiting, poor feeding, lethargy, sweating, jaundice and failure to thrive. Continued exposure can lead to liver failure, seizures, coma and death, whereas undiagnosed children who survive often develop a protective, intense aversion to sweet foods — a classic clinical clue — along with a strikingly low rate of dental caries.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is confirmed by genetic testing of ALDOB (common variants include p.Ala150Pro) or, historically, by enzyme assay of liver biopsy or a hazardous fructose tolerance test, now avoided. Treatment is dietary and lifelong: strict exclusion of fructose, sucrose and sorbitol (which is converted to fructose). With dietary compliance the prognosis is excellent and liver damage largely reversible; inadvertent exposure in adult life usually causes milder, self-limiting symptoms.
