A&M Records, Inc. v. Napster, Inc., 239 F.3d 1004 (9th Cir. 2001), was a landmark United States copyright case in which the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit held that Napster, the peer-to-peer file-sharing service, could be liable for contributory and vicarious infringement of the plaintiff record companies' copyrights. The decision effectively ended Napster's original free file-sharing service.

Background

Napster, launched in 1999 by Shawn Fanning and Sean Parker, let users share MP3 music files through a central index of available songs. The RIAA member labels, led by A&M Records, sued in December 1999. The district court (Judge Marilyn Hall Patel) granted a preliminary injunction in July 2000 ordering Napster to remove infringing material.

Decision

The Ninth Circuit affirmed in large part: Napster's users directly infringed by reproducing and distributing recordings; Napster knowingly encouraged and assisted that infringement (contributory liability) and profited from it while having the right and ability to supervise users (vicarious liability). It rejected Napster's fair-use and "space-shifting" defences and held the Audio Home Recording Act inapplicable, while modifying the injunction to require the labels to identify their infringing works and Napster to police its index accordingly. Unable to filter completely, Napster shut down its service in July 2001 and later entered bankruptcy.

Significance

The case established how secondary-liability doctrines apply to peer-to-peer networks and shaped later litigation (notably MGM v. Grokster, 2005) and the industry's eventual shift to licensed streaming.
