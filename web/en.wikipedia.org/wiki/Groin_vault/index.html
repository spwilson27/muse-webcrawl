A groin vault or cross vault is produced by the intersection at right angles of two barrel vaults. The word groin refers to the edge between the intersecting vaults; compare groin. Sometimes the arches of groin vaults are pointed instead of round.

In comparison with a barrel vault, a groin vault provides good economies of material and labour. The thrust is concentrated along the groins or arrises (the four diagonal edges formed by the intersection of the two barrel vaults), so the vault need only be abutted at its four corners.

History

Groin vaults were regularly employed by the Romans, notably in large imperial structures such as the Baths of Diocletian and the Basilica of Maxentius in Rome, where massive groin-vaulted bays spanned halls tens of metres wide. The technique was revived in medieval Romanesque architecture and developed into the rib vault characteristic of Gothic architecture.

Geometry and construction

The geometry of a groin vault is simplest when two barrel vaults of equal diameter intersect over a square bay. The resulting curved surface consists of four webs (or cells), separated by diagonal groins. Because the loads are channelled to the corners, the walls between the supporting piers can be opened up with large windows or arches, an advantage exploited in Roman basilicas and later in Gothic churches.

Groin vaults can also be constructed over rectangular bays, though the intersecting barrels then have different radii and the geometry becomes more complex; medieval masons sometimes used ploughshare vaulting to resolve these junctions.
