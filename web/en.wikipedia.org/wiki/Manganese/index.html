Manganese is a chemical element with the symbol Mn and atomic number 25. It is a hard, brittle, silvery transition metal, often found in minerals in combination with iron. Manganese is a transition metal with a multifaceted array of industrial alloy uses, particularly in stainless steels. It improves strength, workability, and resistance to wear. Manganese oxide is used as an oxidising agent, as a rubber additive, and in glass making, fertilisers, and ceramics. Manganese sulfate can be used as a fungicide.

History

Manganese compounds were used in prehistoric cave paints and later in glassmaking. The element was first isolated in 1774 by Johan Gottlieb Gahn, who reduced manganese dioxide with carbon; Carl Wilhelm Scheele and others had earlier recognised that the ore pyrolusite contained a new element. The name derives from the Latin magnes, referring to the magnetic properties of pyrolusite.

Occurrence and production

Manganese makes up about 0.1% of the Earth's crust and is the twelfth most abundant element. The most important ore is pyrolusite (MnO2). Major producers include South Africa, Australia, China, Gabon, and Brazil. Vast quantities of manganese nodules occur on the ocean floor. Most manganese is consumed in steel production as ferromanganese and silicomanganese alloys.

Biological role and safety

Manganese is an essential trace nutrient involved in bone formation and in enzymes such as arginase and manganese superoxide dismutase. Chronic overexposure, particularly inhalation of dust in mining and welding, can cause manganism, a neurological disorder with symptoms similar to Parkinson's disease.
