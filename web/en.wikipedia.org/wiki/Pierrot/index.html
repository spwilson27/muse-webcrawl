Pierrot

Pierrot is a stock character of pantomime and commedia dell'arte, whose origins lie in the late seventeenth-century Italian troupe of players performing in Paris. His character in contemporary popular culture is that of a sad clown, pining for love of Columbine, who usually breaks his heart and leaves him for Harlequin. Performing unmasked, with a whitened face, he wears a loose white blouse with large buttons and wide white pantaloons. Sometimes he appears with a frilled collarette and a hat, usually with a close-fitting black skullcap.

History

Pierrot's origins as Pedrolino, one of the zanni (comic servants) of the commedia dell'arte, date to the sixteenth century. In Italy, Pedrolino was a servant figure, often the butt of jokes but also resourceful. Transformed in France, Pierrot became the naive, moonstruck, melancholy lover, dressed in white and associated with the moon. The French pantomime Jean-Gaspard Deburau, in the early nineteenth century at the Théâtre des Funambules in Paris, gave the character his modern silent, wistful form.

In the arts

Pierrot has inspired painters such as Watteau (notably the painting commonly called Gilles), poets, and composers. Arnold Schoenberg's Pierrot lunaire (1912), a setting of Albert Giraud's poems in Otto Erich Hartleben's translation, is a landmark of atonal music. The character also appears in works by Paul Verlaine, Jules Laforgue, and T. S. Eliot, and remains a staple of mime and carnival imagery.
