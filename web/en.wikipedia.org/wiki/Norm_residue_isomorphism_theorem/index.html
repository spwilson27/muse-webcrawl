Norm residue isomorphism theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the norm residue isomorphism theorem (or Bloch–Kato conjecture) is a long-sought result relating Milnor K-theory and Galois cohomology. It states that the norm residue homomorphism, which relates the n-th Milnor K-group of a field to its n-th Galois cohomology group with coefficients in roots of unity, is an isomorphism.

Statement

Let F be a field containing a primitive ℓ-th root of unity, with ℓ prime to the characteristic of F. The norm residue (or Galois symbol) map

KMn(F) / ℓ → Hn(F, μ⊗nℓ)

is an isomorphism for all n ≥ 0. For n = 1 this is Kummer theory; for n = 2 it is the Merkurjev–Suslin theorem.

History

The conjecture grew out of work by Spencer Bloch and Kazuya Kato, with roots in conjectures of Alexander Beilinson and John Milnor. The cases of small degree were established over several decades: n = 2 by Alexander Merkurjev and Andrei Suslin (1982), and n = 3, ℓ = 2 by Merkurjev–Suslin and Mark Rost.

The general theorem was proved by Vladimir Voevodsky, using motivic cohomology and results including Rost's norm variety machinery and the work of Charles Weibel, who helped assemble elements of the proof. Voevodsky received the Fields Medal in 2002 in part for his work on the related Milnor conjecture (the case ℓ = 2). The full proof was published in 2011 by Weibel in the Annals of Mathematics, summarizing Voevodsky's work: "The proof of the Bloch–Kato conjecture".

Consequences

The theorem implies the Quillen–Lichtenbaum conjecture and underlies computations of the cohomology of fields and étale cohomology of schemes, with applications in algebraic K-theory and the theory of motives.
