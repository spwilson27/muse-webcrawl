Enterprise resource planning

Enterprise resource planning (ERP) is the integrated management of an organisation's core business processes, mediated by software that shares a common database and data model. ERP systems tie together functions such as finance, human resources, manufacturing, supply chain, procurement, sales and customer service, so that a transaction in one area — a shipped order, for example — automatically updates inventory, accounts and forecasts elsewhere.

History

ERP evolved from inventory control systems of the 1960s and material requirements planning (MRP) in the 1970s, which expanded into manufacturing resource planning (MRP II) in the 1980s. The term "enterprise resource planning" was coined by the Gartner Group in 1990 as the scope widened to the whole enterprise. Vendors such as SAP, Oracle, PeopleSoft, Baan and J.D. Edwards built large suites during the 1990s, when fear of the year-2000 problem drove a wave of implementations; since the 2000s, cloud-based (SaaS) ERP and two-tier architectures have become common.

Characteristics

A typical ERP system offers: an integrated, real-time database; modular applications that can be deployed selectively; embedded "best practice" business processes; and organisation-wide reporting and analytics. Implementation is notoriously expensive and disruptive, often requiring business process re-engineering; failed projects — such as Hershey's 1999 rollout, which disrupted Halloween deliveries — are standard case studies. Total cost includes licences or subscriptions, hardware or cloud infrastructure, integration, training and ongoing maintenance.

Extensions

Modern suites are often extended with customer relationship management (CRM), supply chain management (SCM), business intelligence and e-commerce modules, and increasingly with embedded artificial intelligence for forecasting, invoice matching and anomaly detection. Major contemporary vendors include SAP (S/4HANA), Oracle (Fusion/NetSuite), Microsoft (Dynamics 365) and Workday.
