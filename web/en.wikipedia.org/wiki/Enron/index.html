Enron

Enron Corporation
Founded: 1985 (merger of Houston Natural Gas and InterNorth)
Defunct: December 2001 (bankruptcy)
Headquarters: Houston, Texas
Key people: Kenneth Lay, Jeffrey Skilling, Andrew Fastow
Peak revenue: ~$101 billion (2000)

Enron Corporation was an American energy, commodities and services company based in Houston, Texas. Formed in 1985 from a merger of natural gas pipeline companies, it grew into one of the world's largest energy traders, named "America's Most Innovative Company" by Fortune for six consecutive years. Its collapse in late 2001 — the largest corporate bankruptcy in American history at the time — revealed systematic accounting fraud and triggered the Enron scandal, the destruction of its auditor Arthur Andersen, and sweeping regulatory reform.

Rise

Under chairman Kenneth Lay and, later, CEO Jeffrey Skilling, Enron moved from owning gas pipelines to trading energy as a commodity, creating markets in electricity, broadband capacity, weather derivatives and more. It aggressively used mark-to-market accounting to book projected future profits immediately, and its share price climbed to a peak of about $90 in August 2000, giving it a market value near $70 billion.

Fraud and collapse

CFO Andrew Fastow and others created complex off-balance-sheet special-purpose entities (such as LJM, Chewco and the "Raptors") that hid debt and losses while enriching insiders. When these structures unravelled in autumn 2001, Enron restated years of earnings, its credit was cut to junk, a rescue merger with Dynegy failed, and on 2 December 2001 it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy with some $63 billion in assets. Thousands of employees lost jobs and retirement savings held in Enron stock.

Aftermath

Skilling was convicted of fraud and conspiracy in 2006 and sentenced to 24 years (later reduced); Lay was convicted but died before sentencing; Fastow pleaded guilty and cooperated. Arthur Andersen's obstruction conviction destroyed the firm. The scandal prompted the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, tightening corporate governance and auditor independence, and "Enron" became a byword for corporate fraud.
