StarCraft in esports

Professional competition in Blizzard Entertainment's real-time strategy games StarCraft (1998), its expansion Brood War (1998), and the sequel StarCraft II (2010) is widely credited with launching modern esports. South Korea became the centre of the phenomenon: professional Brood War leagues ran on dedicated television channels for over a decade, top players became celebrities, and the scene established many conventions of competitive gaming — salaried team houses, televised finals in arenas, and a star system of individual players.

Rise in South Korea

The growth of PC bangs (LAN gaming centres) after the 1997 Asian financial crisis created a huge player base. Cable channels OnGameNet and MBCGame launched televised leagues — the Starleague and the MSL — around 1999–2002, and corporate teams sponsored by firms such as SK Telecom, Samsung and KT fielded professional squads under the oversight of the Korea e-Sports Association (KeSPA). Finals filled stadiums and, in 2005, a reported audience of over 100,000 in Busan. Star players included Lim Yo-hwan ("BoxeR"), the "Terran Emperor"; Hong Jin-ho ("YellOw"); and Lee Young-ho ("Flash"), often considered the greatest Brood War player. Non-Korean ("foreigner") players occasionally challenged in early years, but Korean dominance became near total.

Match-fixing scandal and transition

In 2010 a match-fixing and illegal betting scandal led to bans and criminal charges for several prominent players, damaging the scene's reputation. From 2010 the release of StarCraft II split the community; Blizzard's licensing disputes with KeSPA shaped a complicated transition, and the Korean Brood War leagues ended by 2012. StarCraft II developed its own global circuit — including the GSL in Seoul and Blizzard's World Championship Series — with notable champions such as Park "Dark" Ryung-woo and Joona "Serral" Sotala of Finland, the first non-Korean world champion of the modern era.

Revival

After the release of StarCraft: Remastered in 2017, Brood War competition revived in Korea, notably through the AfreecaTV StarCraft League (ASL), demonstrating the game's enduring competitive depth more than two decades after release.
