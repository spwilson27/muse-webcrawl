Al-Mustaʿlī (Arabic: المستعلي, "the one raised up") is the regnal title commonly used for Abū al-Qāsim Aḥmad, the ninth Fatimid caliph and, in the reckoning of Musta'li Isma'ilis, the nineteenth Imam. He reigned from 1094 until his death in 1101. Because reference works and modern scholarship usually give his full regnal name, he is most often discussed under the entry Al-Musta'li Billah; this page summarises his reign and the succession crisis that followed it.

Succession crisis

On the death of his father al-Mustansir in 1094, the powerful vizier al-Afdal Shahanshah installed the youngest son, Ahmad al-Musta'li, as caliph, passing over the elder son Nizar, who was widely believed to have been designated heir. Nizar fled to Alexandria and rebelled, but was defeated and died in captivity. The disputed succession split the Isma'ili movement permanently: Nizar's supporters in Persia and Syria — led by Hasan-i Sabbah — rejected al-Musta'li and formed the Nizari branch, while the da'wa in Egypt, Yemen and western India recognised al-Musta'li, founding the Musta'li branch.

Reign

Real power lay with the vizier al-Afdal, a military strongman whose Armenian-origin family dominated the late Fatimid state. Al-Musta'li's reign coincided with the arrival of the First Crusade in the Levant: Jerusalem, taken from the Seljuks by al-Afdal's forces in 1098, was captured by the Crusaders in 1099, and a Fatimid army sent to recover it was defeated at Ascalon. Fatimid–Crusader diplomacy, marked by mutual misunderstanding, failed to produce an anti-Seljuk alliance.

Al-Musta'li died in 1101 and was succeeded by his five-year-old son al-Amir, with al-Afdal remaining in control. The Musta'li line of imams is held by Musta'li Bohras to have continued in concealment after 1130, with leadership exercised by the Da'i al-Mutlaq.
