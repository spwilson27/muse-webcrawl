Lupus

Lupus (systemic lupus erythematosus)
SpecialtyRheumatology

Lupus, technically systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), is an autoimmune disease in which the body's immune system attacks its own tissues, causing widespread inflammation and damage in affected organs. It can involve the joints, skin, brain, lungs, kidneys and blood vessels. Women are affected about nine times more often than men, typically beginning between the ages of 15 and 45.

The butterfly-shaped malar rash across the cheeks and nose

Signs and symptoms

Common symptoms include painful and swollen joints, fever, chest pain, hair loss, mouth ulcers, swollen lymph nodes, fatigue and a red rash — most famously the butterfly-shaped malar rash over the cheeks and nose, present in about a third to a half of cases. Symptoms vary widely between patients and fluctuate in flares and remissions. Kidney involvement (lupus nephritis) is a major cause of serious illness.

Causes

The cause is not fully understood. Genetics, female sex hormones, ultraviolet light, certain infections (such as Epstein–Barr virus) and some medications are implicated. Diagnosis is based on a combination of symptoms and laboratory tests, particularly antinuclear antibodies (ANA), found in most cases.

Treatment

There is no cure, but treatment can control symptoms and prevent organ damage. Hydroxychloroquine is a mainstay for most patients; corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants are used for flares and organ involvement. Biologic agents such as belimumab are approved for certain patients.

History

The name lupus (Latin for wolf) dates to medieval descriptions of skin lesions thought to resemble a wolf's bite. The systemic nature of the disease was recognised in the late 19th century by Moriz Kaposi, and the LE cell test of 1948 advanced diagnosis.
