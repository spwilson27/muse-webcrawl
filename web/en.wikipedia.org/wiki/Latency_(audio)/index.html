Latency (audio)

Latency in audio is the delay between an audio signal entering a system and emerging from it. In digital audio systems, latency is introduced by analogue-to-digital and digital-to-analogue conversion, by buffering, and by signal processing. It is usually measured in milliseconds.

Causes

Computer audio systems process sound in blocks (buffers). A larger buffer reduces the risk of dropouts but increases latency; a smaller buffer lowers latency at a higher CPU cost. Additional delay comes from converter chips, plug-in processing in a digital audio workstation, and any lookahead functions such as limiters.

Perceptual effects

Latency matters most when a performer hears themselves through the system, as in software monitoring while recording. Delays above roughly 10 milliseconds can disturb singers and instrumentalists, and even smaller delays mixed with the direct sound cause comb filtering. Live sound engineers and broadcasters also manage latency so that audio stays in synchronisation with video. Analogue systems have negligible latency, which is one reason hardware monitoring remains common in studios.
