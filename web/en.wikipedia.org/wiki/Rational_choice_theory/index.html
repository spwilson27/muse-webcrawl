Rational choice theory

Rational choice theory
FieldEconomics, political science, sociology
Core assumptionAgents act to maximise their utility given beliefs and constraints
Key figuresAnthony Downs, Mancur Olson, Gary Becker
RelatedGame theory, public choice

Rational choice theory is a framework for understanding and modelling social and economic behaviour. It assumes that individuals make choices that maximise their own utility or preferences, subject to the information and constraints available to them, and builds explanations of collective outcomes from these individual decisions.

Assumptions

The theory typically assumes agents have complete and transitive preferences, act instrumentally to achieve their goals, and (in strong forms) hold consistent beliefs and discount the future consistently. Individuals need not be selfish in a narrow sense — altruistic preferences can be included — but the theory requires that behaviour be systematically related to goals.

Applications

Rational choice underpins neoclassical economics, public choice theory (Downs's theory of voting, Olson's logic of collective action), Gary Becker's economics of crime and the family, the rational-choice analysis of religion, and much of modern game theory.

Criticism

Critics from behavioural economics document systematic deviations — framing effects, loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting, satisficing under bounded rationality — while sociologists object that preferences are socially formed and not all action is instrumental. Defenders reply that the theory is a modelling idealisation whose value lies in the predictions it generates.
