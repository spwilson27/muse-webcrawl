Marginalia

Marginalia (from Latin margo, margin) are marks made in the margins of a book or other document, including notes, glosses, comments, corrections, and doodles written by readers, editors, or copyists.

History

Medieval manuscripts frequently carried glosses—interlinear and marginal commentary that was integral to scholarly reading, especially of Scripture and classical texts like the works of Aristotle. With printing, marginal notes became a space for private engagement: famous examples include the annotations of Samuel Taylor Coleridge, who coined the modern term and whose extensive marginalia in friends' books were prized publications in their own right, and the mathematical note associated with Fermat's Last Theorem, written in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica around 1637, claiming a proof the margin was too narrow to contain.

Scholarly interest

Historians of reading study marginalia as evidence of how books were read and understood; the annotations of notable readers can add considerable value to a copy. Conversely, librarians and booksellers have often treated marginal notes in circulating copies as damage.
