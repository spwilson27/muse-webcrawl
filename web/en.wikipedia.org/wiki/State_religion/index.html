State religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A state religion (also an established religion or official religion) is a religion or creed approved and supported by a state, typically with legal privileges: public funding, a role in state ceremonies, established churches' bishops in legislatures (the Church of England's Lords Spiritual), religious education in public schools, or constitutional designation. States with official religions include England (Anglican), Denmark (Lutheran), Greece (Orthodox), most Muslim-majority states of the Middle East and North Africa (Islam), Thailand and Bhutan (Buddhism in varying forms) and Costa Rica (Roman Catholicism).

Establishment exists on a spectrum: from theocracy (Iran, Vatican City) through established churches with broad toleration of other faiths, to merely ceremonial recognition. The opposite models are strict separation (the United States' Establishment Clause; France's Laïcité since 1905) and official atheism (historically, communist states). Disestablishments have proceeded steadily (Sweden separated church and state in 2000; Norway loosened its establishment in 2012–2017), while new establishments or tightenings occasionally occur. Political debate weighs establishment's symbolic and integrative claims against equal citizenship for minorities and non-believers.
