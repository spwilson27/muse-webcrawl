Nail (anatomy)

A nail is a claw-like plate of keratin found at the tip of the fingers and toes in most primates. Nails correspond to claws in other animals. Fingernails and toenails are made of a tough protective protein called alpha-keratin, a polymer also found in the hooves, claws, and horns of vertebrates.

Structure

The nail consists of the nail plate (the visible hard part), the nail bed beneath it, the matrix at its base where new cells are produced, the pale crescent-shaped lunula, the cuticle sealing the plate to the skin, and the surrounding nail folds. The plate is translucent; its pink appearance comes from the rich blood supply of the nail bed showing through.

Growth

Nails grow continuously throughout life, produced by the matrix as new keratinocytes push older cells forward. Fingernails grow at roughly 3.5 mm per month — about twice the rate of toenails — and growth is faster in summer, in the dominant hand, and in youth. A fingernail replaced after loss takes about four to six months to regrow fully; a toenail may take twelve to eighteen months.

Function

Nails protect the sensitive tips of the digits, enhance fine touch by providing counter-pressure to the fingertip pulp, and serve as tools for scratching, prying, and manipulating small objects. The evolution of flat nails rather than claws in primates is linked to grasping and precision grip.

Health and disease

Nail appearance can signal systemic disease. Clubbing is associated with cardiopulmonary disorders; spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia) with iron deficiency; yellow, thickened nails with fungal infection (onychomycosis). Common disorders include ingrown toenails, paronychia (inflammation of the nail folds), psoriasis of the nails, and brittle nail syndrome. Habitual nail biting is called onychophagia.
