The Car Allowance Rebate System (CARS), popularly known as "cash for clunkers", was a United States federal programme in 2009 that provided economic incentives for consumers to purchase new, more fuel-efficient vehicles when trading in less efficient ones. Authorised by the Consumer Assistance to Recycle and Save Act, it ran from 24 July to 24 August 2009.

How it worked

Buyers who traded in a drivable vehicle with a combined fuel-economy rating of 18 mpg or less received a credit of $3,500 or $4,500, depending on the improvement in mileage of the new vehicle. Traded-in vehicles had to be scrapped — their engines were permanently disabled with a sodium silicate solution — to remove them from the road and prevent resale. Initially funded with $1 billion, the programme was so popular that Congress added $2 billion within weeks; about 677,000 vehicles were traded in.

Effects and debate

The programme temporarily boosted US auto sales during the Great Recession and retired hundreds of thousands of inefficient vehicles. Economists have debated its cost-effectiveness: studies estimated that many subsidised purchases would have occurred anyway or were pulled forward from later months, and assessments of its environmental benefit per dollar vary widely.
