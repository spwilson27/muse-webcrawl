Megathrust earthquake

Megathrust earthquakes occur at subduction zones at destructive convergent plate boundaries, where one tectonic plate is forced underneath another. They are interplate earthquakes, slipping along the thrust fault (the megathrust) that forms the plate interface. Megathrust earthquakes are the most powerful earthquakes on Earth, capable of moment magnitudes (Mw) exceeding 9.0, and frequently generate devastating tsunamis.

Terminology and characteristics

The term "megathrust" refers to an extremely large thrust fault. The shallow portion of the plate interface may lock for centuries, accumulating strain that is released suddenly in an earthquake. Because the fault area can extend for over 1,000 kilometres (as in the 2004 Sumatra earthquake), megathrust events have long rupture durations and produce strong shaking over wide areas.

Examples

  • 1960 Valdivia earthquake, Chile — Mw 9.5, the largest instrumentally recorded earthquake
  • 1964 Alaska earthquake — Mw 9.2
  • 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami — Mw 9.1–9.3
  • 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, Japan — Mw 9.0–9.1
  • 1952 Kamchatka earthquake — Mw 9.0
