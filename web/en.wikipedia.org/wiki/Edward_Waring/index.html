Edward Waring

Edward Waring (1736 – 15 August 1798) was an English mathematician who entered Cambridge University as a sizar and became Senior Wrangler in 1757. He was elected a Fellow of Magdalene College and in 1760 became Lucasian Professor of Mathematics, holding the chair until his death.

He made extensive studies in the theory of equations, number theory, and other branches of mathematics. He is best known for Waring's problem, which he stated without proof in his book Meditationes Algebraicae (1770). The problem asserts that every natural number is the sum of at most g(k) k-th powers of natural numbers, for each exponent k.

Life and career

Waring was born in Old Heath, near Shrewsbury, Shropshire, the son of a wealthy farmer. He was educated at Shrewsbury School and was admitted as a sizar to Magdalene College, Cambridge, on 24 May 1753. His performance in the Senate House examination was remarkable: he graduated as Senior Wrangler in 1757.

He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1763 and received its Copley Medal in 1784. In his later years he suffered from deep melancholy, brought on by a severe chill, and he died at Plealey, Pontesbury, on 15 August 1798.

Works

His principal work, Meditationes Algebraicae, was published in 1770 and expanded in later editions. It contains investigations into the roots of equations, symmetric functions, and the transformation of equations. Many results were stated without proof, including the famous problem that bears his name; a complete solution of Waring's problem was not achieved until the twentieth century, with important contributions by David Hilbert, who proved in 1909 that g(k) always exists.
