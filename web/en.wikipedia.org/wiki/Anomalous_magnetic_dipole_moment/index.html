Anomalous magnetic dipole moment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Feynman diagrams of the leading contributions to the electron's anomalous magnetic moment]
Radiative corrections shift the electron's g-factor from exactly 2.

The anomalous magnetic dipole moment of a particle is the difference between its magnetic moment's g-factor and the value predicted by the Dirac equation (g = 2 for the electron). Denoted a = (g − 2)/2, the anomaly arises from quantum fluctuations — the particle's interactions with virtual particles — and provides one of the most sensitive tests of the Standard Model.

For the electron, Julian Schwinger's 1948 result a = α/2π ≈ 0.00116 was the first triumph of renormalised Quantum electrodynamics; the full QED calculation now extends to five loops (over 12,000 Feynman diagrams) and agrees with experiment to better than a part in a trillion — the most precise theory–experiment match in science. For the muon, whose anomaly is ~40,000 times more sensitive to heavy virtual particles, Brookhaven (2001) and Fermilab (2021–2025) measurements found a ≈ 0.00116592, with a long-standing tension against data-driven Standard Model predictions that lattice-QCD calculations have since substantially reduced — an evolving story at the frontier of precision physics.

Why it matters

Because virtual particles of every kind circulate in the loops, the anomaly is a summing point for all of physics: QED, electroweak and hadronic contributions all enter, and undiscovered particles would too. The muon g−2 programme is thus a discovery instrument complementary to colliders — any confirmed deviation from the Standard Model prediction would signal new physics.
