Cathedra

Cathedra
EtymologyLatin cathedra, from Greek kathedra (καθέδρα), "seat"
MeaningThe throne or chair of a bishop
Related termA church containing a cathedra is a cathedral

A cathedra is the raised throne of a bishop in the early Christian basilica. When used with this meaning, it may also be called the bishop's throne. With time, the related term cathedral became synonymous with the "seat" or principal church of a bishopric. The word derives from the Latin cathedra, itself from the Greek kathedra, meaning a seat or bench.

History and use

In the ancient world the cathedra was a chair with arms, used by teachers and persons of authority; in early churches the bishop presided from a seat placed in the apse, flanked by benches for the presbyters. The cathedra thus became the visible symbol of the bishop's teaching and governing authority within his diocese. Its presence defines a cathedral church, regardless of the building's size, and a bishop is formally installed in his diocese by being seated in his cathedra.

Ex cathedra

In Roman Catholic theology, the phrase ex cathedra ("from the chair") refers to the Pope speaking in his official capacity as pastor and teacher of all Christians. Under the doctrine of papal infallibility, defined by the First Vatican Council in 1870, definitions pronounced ex cathedra on faith and morals are held to be infallible.

Cathedra of Saint Peter

The Cathedra Petri in St Peter's Basilica in Rome is a celebrated relic — an ornate throne, enclosed in a monumental bronze casing by Gian Lorenzo Bernini, traditionally associated with Saint Peter himself and venerated as a symbol of the papal office.
