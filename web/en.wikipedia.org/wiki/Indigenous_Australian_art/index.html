Indigenous Australian art

Indigenous Australian art encompasses the art forms created by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, from some of the world's oldest continuous artistic traditions — rock art and bark painting tens of thousands of years old — to a thriving contemporary art movement. Traditional art is deeply connected to the Dreaming (Tjukurpa), the ancestral narratives that map the land, law, and identity of each community.

Traditional forms

Rock engravings and paintings survive across Australia, notably in Kakadu, the Kimberley (including the Gwion Gwion figures), and the Sydney Basin. Bark painting flourished in Arnhem Land, using cross-hatched rarrk designs. Other traditional and ceremonial forms include carved and painted burial poles, woven pandanus and fibre art, decorated shields and tools, and ephemeral ground sculptures and body painting made for ceremony.

The Papunya movement

In 1971–72, at the government settlement of Papunya in the Northern Territory, schoolteacher Geoffrey Bardon encouraged senior men including Kaapa Tjampitjinpa and Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri to paint ancestral designs in acrylic on board and canvas. The resulting "dot painting" style — which partly obscured sacred imagery — launched the Western Desert art movement, now a major presence in Australian and international art markets. Emily Kame Kngwarreye of Utopia became one of Australia's most celebrated artists in the 1990s.

Contemporary art

Urban Indigenous artists such as Tracey Moffatt, Gordon Bennett, Brook Andrew, and Richard Bell address colonisation, identity, and politics in photography, installation, and painting. The movement is supported by community art centres across remote Australia, and Indigenous art is a significant cultural export, though it has also faced problems of exploitation and forgery that led to resale royalty and authenticity measures.
