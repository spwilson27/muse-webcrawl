Intelligence explosion

An intelligence explosion is a hypothetical scenario in which an artificial intelligence system improves itself recursively — each generation designing a still more capable successor — producing a rapid, runaway increase in intelligence that far surpasses human cognitive abilities. The idea is central to discussions of the technological singularity and existential risk from artificial intelligence.

Origins

The concept is usually traced to I. J. Good, who wrote in 1965 that an "ultraintelligent machine" able to design even better machines would leave human intelligence far behind, constituting an intelligence explosion. Vernor Vinge and Ray Kurzweil later popularized related ideas of an approaching singularity.

Arguments and criticism

Proponents such as Nick Bostrom argue that recursive self-improvement, fast computer speeds, and large copyable populations of AIs could produce very rapid capability gains. Critics respond that intelligence improvements face diminishing returns, bottlenecks in experimentation and the physical world, and that intelligence is not a single quantity that scales freely.
