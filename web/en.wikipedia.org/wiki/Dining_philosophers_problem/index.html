Dining philosophers problem

The dining philosophers problem is an example problem often used in concurrent algorithm design to illustrate synchronisation issues and techniques for resolving them. It was originally formulated in 1965 by Edsger Dijkstra as a student exam exercise — in terms of computers competing for access to tape drives — and was soon re-cast in its philosophical form by Tony Hoare.

The problem

Five silent philosophers sit at a round table with a bowl of spaghetti. A fork (in some versions, chopsticks) is placed between each pair of adjacent philosophers. Each philosopher alternates between thinking and eating; to eat, a philosopher must pick up the two forks nearest to them — one on the left and one on the right. After eating, they put both forks down and resume thinking. The problem is to design a protocol such that no philosopher starves.

Issues and solutions

The naive solution — each philosopher picks up the left fork, then waits for the right — can deadlock: if all five pick up their left forks simultaneously, each waits forever for a fork that will never be released. There may also be resource starvation, livelock, and lack of fairness. Classic solutions include imposing an ordering on the resources (numbering the forks and always acquiring the lower-numbered one first, which breaks circular wait); introducing a waiter (arbitrator) who grants permission to pick up forks; limiting the number of philosophers seated at once; and Dijkstra's semaphore-based approach. The Chandy–Misra solution (1984) gives a fully distributed, deadlock- and starvation-free scheme for the generalised drinking philosophers problem.
