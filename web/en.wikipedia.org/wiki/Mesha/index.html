Mesha (Moabite: 𐤌𐤔𐤏; Hebrew: מֵישַׁע, Mêšaʿ) was a king of Moab in the 9th century BCE, known principally from the Mesha Stele, a monumental inscription in the Moabite language that he erected at Dibon, his capital, and from the account of his wars in the Hebrew Bible (2 Kings 3).

The Mesha Stele, now in the Louvre

The Mesha Stele

The stele, discovered in 1868 at Dhiban in modern Jordan by the missionary F. A. Klein, is a black basalt slab bearing a 34-line inscription in the name of Mesha. In it, Mesha credits his god Chemosh for delivering Moab from subjugation to Israel: he recounts that Omri, king of Israel, and his son had oppressed Moab for many years because Chemosh was angry with his land, but that in Mesha's day Chemosh relented, enabling Mesha to defeat Israel, retake Moabite towns such as Ataroth and Nebo, and rebuild cities, roads, and waterworks. The inscription is one of the longest Iron Age inscriptions found in the Levant and the most extensive inscription in the Moabite language.

Biblical account

According to 2 Kings, Mesha was a sheep-breeder who paid an enormous annual tribute to Israel of 100,000 lambs and the wool of 100,000 rams. After the death of Ahab, he rebelled against King Jehoram. Jehoram, allied with Jehoshaphat of Judah and the king of Edom, invaded Moab from the south; the campaign nearly ended in disaster for Mesha, who, besieged in Kir-hareseth, offered his eldest son as a burnt offering on the city wall, after which the Israelite coalition withdrew. Scholars debate how to reconcile the biblical narrative with the stele's triumphal account, but together they illuminate the politics of the 9th-century southern Levant.
