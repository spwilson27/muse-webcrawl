Sackcloth

Sackcloth is a coarse woven fabric, traditionally made of goat's hair, flax or hemp, used for making sacks and, in ancient and biblical practice, worn as a garment signifying mourning, repentance or humility. The English phrase "sackcloth and ashes" comes from the combined customs of wearing the rough cloth and sitting in or sprinkling oneself with ashes.

Biblical and historical use

In the Hebrew Bible, sackcloth is worn by Jacob mourning for Joseph, by David, by the people of Nineveh in the Book of Jonah — who clothe even their animals — and by prophets calling Israel to repentance. The practice continued in Judaism and early Christianity, and hair shirts (cilices) served a similar penitential purpose in medieval monasticism. The coarse cloth irritated the skin, a deliberate discomfort accompanying prayer and fasting.

The fabric

Sackcloth is a plain-weave material with a loose, open texture. Modern equivalents include burlap and hessian, woven from jute, still used for sacks, upholstery backing, gardening and erosion control. Historically the term also described cloth woven from the black hair of goats in Cilicia, prized for tents and sails as well as mourning dress.
