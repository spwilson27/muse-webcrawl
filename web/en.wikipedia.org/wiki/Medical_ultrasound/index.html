Medical ultrasound

[Image: An obstetric ultrasound image]
A sonogram produced during pregnancy

Medical ultrasound (also called diagnostic sonography or ultrasonography) is a medical imaging technique that uses high-frequency sound waves (ultrasound) to produce real-time images of structures inside the body. A transducer emits pulses of sound (typically 2–18 megahertz) and detects returning echoes, whose timing and intensity a computer converts into an image. Because it uses no ionising radiation and is portable and relatively inexpensive, ultrasound is usually the first imaging test for many conditions.

Applications

Ultrasound is best known for obstetric imaging — monitoring fetal development and detecting anomalies — but it is used throughout medicine: echocardiography for the heart, abdominal imaging of the liver, gallbladder (gallstones), kidneys and pancreas, vascular studies using Doppler techniques to measure blood flow, musculoskeletal and thyroid imaging, guidance for biopsies and injections, and point-of-care examinations in emergency and critical care. Doppler modes (colour, spectral and power Doppler) display flow and allow measurement of velocities and valve function. Therapeutic uses include lithotripsy and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU).

History and safety

Medical ultrasound grew out of Second World War sonar. In the 1950s Ian Donald and colleagues in Glasgow pioneered obstetric applications, and through the 1960s–70s grey-scale and real-time scanning spread worldwide. Decades of use have shown no confirmed harmful effects at diagnostic intensities, though professional bodies recommend prudent use (the ALARA principle) because of theoretical thermal and mechanical bioeffects.
