John Foster Dulles (February 25, 1888 – May 24, 1959) was an American diplomat, lawyer, and politician who served as United States Secretary of State under President Dwight D. Eisenhower from 1953 until shortly before his death in 1959. A Republican and a prominent figure of the early Cold War, he was a central architect of American foreign policy during the 1950s, advocating a hard line against communism.

Early life and career

Dulles was born in Washington, D.C., into a family with a strong tradition of public service: his grandfather John W. Foster and his uncle Robert Lansing had both served as Secretary of State. He studied at Princeton and at the Sorbonne in Paris, then took a law degree from George Washington University. He joined the New York law firm Sullivan & Cromwell, where he became a leading international lawyer, and served as a delegate to the Paris Peace Conference in 1919.

Secretary of State

As Secretary of State, Dulles promoted the policy of "massive retaliation" and the practice of brinkmanship, declaring a willingness to go "to the verge" of war to deter communist expansion. He was a driving force behind the creation of alliance systems such as SEATO and the Baghdad Pact, and he played key roles in the 1953 Iranian coup and the 1954 Guatemalan coup. His tenure also encompassed the Korean armistice, the First Indochina War and the Suez Crisis of 1956.

Later life and legacy

Dulles resigned in April 1959 while suffering from cancer and died the following month. Washington Dulles International Airport, opened in 1962, is named in his honour. His brother Allen Dulles served as Director of Central Intelligence during much of the same period.
