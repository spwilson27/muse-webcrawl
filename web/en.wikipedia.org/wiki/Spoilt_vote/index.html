Spoilt vote

In an election, a spoilt vote (also called a spoiled ballot, invalid vote, null vote or informal vote) is a ballot that is not counted because it has been incorrectly marked, damaged, or left blank in a way that the voter's intention cannot be determined — or, in some cases, deliberately defaced as a protest.

Causes

Ballots may be spoilt accidentally through confusing ballot design, marking more candidates than allowed (overvoting), or errors in postal voting. Others are spoilt deliberately: voters write slogans, draw pictures, or select every candidate to register dissatisfaction with the choices offered. In countries with compulsory voting, such as Australia — where informal votes typically run at several percent — deliberate spoiling is one way dissenting citizens comply with the duty to attend while withholding a valid vote.

Counting rules

Rules vary: some jurisdictions count a ballot if the voter's intent is clear even if formalities are imperfect; others apply strict marking rules. In the 2000 US presidential election in Florida, disputes over partially detached punch-card chads turned spoilt-ballot standards into a central legal battleground.

As protest

Organised spoilt-vote or "none of the above" campaigns occur where voters wish to signal rejection of all candidates. India formally added a NOTA (None of the Above) option in 2013, though selecting it does not invalidate the election result.
