Mendicant orders

The mendicant orders are religious orders in the Roman Catholic Church whose members originally renounced all property, both individually and corporately, depending for their support on the charity of the faithful — that is, on begging (Latin mendicare, to beg). Arising in the early 13th century, they differed from older monastic orders by living and preaching in cities rather than in secluded monasteries.

The great orders

  • Franciscans (Order of Friars Minor), founded by St Francis of Assisi in 1209.
  • Dominicans (Order of Preachers), founded by St Dominic in 1216, emphasising study and preaching against heresy.
  • Carmelites, originating with hermits on Mount Carmel, reorganised as a mendicant order in the 13th century.
  • Augustinian friars (Order of St Augustine), formed in 1256 from hermit communities following the Rule of St Augustine.

Impact

The friars transformed medieval religious life: they staffed the new universities (producing scholars such as Thomas Aquinas, a Dominican, and Bonaventure, a Franciscan), served as missionaries across Europe, Asia and later the Americas, and ministered to urban populations neglected by the parish system. Their ideal of apostolic poverty provoked controversy — the Franciscans split over the absolute poverty question in the usus pauper dispute — and the papacy gradually permitted the orders to hold property through agents.
