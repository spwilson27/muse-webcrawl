Hedge fund

[Image: a trader monitoring several screens of charts in a hedge fund office at night]

A hedge fund is a pooled Investment vehicle, usually organised as a limited Partnership, that pursues absolute returns for wealthy or institutional investors using strategies restricted in ordinary mutual funds: leverage, short selling, derivatives, arbitrage and concentrated bets. The name comes from Alfred Winslow Jones's 1949 fund, which 'hedged' long stock purchases with shorts.

Strategies, fees and controversy

Common strategies include long/short equity, global macro (trading currencies, rates and commodities on macroeconomic views — the style of George Soros's Quantum Fund, which famously profited from sterling's 1992 ERM exit), event-driven and relative-value arbitrage. The classic fee structure is '2 and 20' — 2% of assets plus 20% of profits — now compressed under investor pressure. Hedge funds are lightly regulated compared with retail funds because their investors are deemed sophisticated; their failures can still be systemic — the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management required a Fed-brokered rescue. The industry manages several trillion dollars, concentrated in the United States, and its performance versus low-cost benchmarks is perennially debated.
