Beatrice Webb

Martha Beatrice Webb, Baroness Passfield (née Potter; 22 January 1858 – 30 April 1943) was an English sociologist, economist, socialist and social reformer. Often working jointly with her husband Sidney Webb, she co-founded the London School of Economics, the Fabian Society's leading partnership, and the magazine New Statesman. Her Minority Report of the 1909 Royal Commission on the Poor Laws laid intellectual foundations for the British welfare state. She is credited with coining the term "collective bargaining".

Life and work

Born into a wealthy railway magnate's family, Beatrice Potter educated herself, worked incognito as a rent collector and investigator in London's East End, and assisted her cousin Charles Booth's survey of London poverty. Her first book, The Co-operative Movement in Great Britain (1891), established her reputation. In 1892 she married Sidney Webb; their "partnership" produced monumental studies including The History of Trade Unionism (1894) and Industrial Democracy (1897), and a nine-volume history of English local government.

Appointed to the Royal Commission on the Poor Laws in 1905, and frustrated by its majority's timidity, she and Sidney issued their own Minority Report (1909), proposing the break-up of the Poor Law, public health and employment services, and a national minimum standard of life — a programme substantially realised by the Attlee government after 1945. The Webbs' visit to the USSR produced the controversial Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? (1935). Beatrice's extensive diaries, published in edited form, are a major source for British political and social history.
