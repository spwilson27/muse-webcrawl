Proximate cause

Field: Tort law (common law)
Leading case: Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co. (1928)
Tests: Foreseeability; directness

In law, a proximate cause is an event sufficiently related to an injury that the courts deem it to be that injury's cause. Together with cause-in-fact (the "but-for" test), proximate cause is one of the two elements of causation a plaintiff must prove in a negligence claim; it functions as a limit on liability for consequences that are too remote or unforeseeable.

Tests

Under the foreseeability (Cardozo) approach, a defendant is liable only for harms to plaintiffs within the zone of foreseeable danger — the holding of Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad. Under the directness approach associated with In re Polemis, a defendant is liable for all direct consequences of a negligent act even if their extent was unforeseeable, provided some harm was foreseeable. Intervening and superseding causes can break the chain of proximate causation.

Scope

Proximate cause doctrine also appears, in varying forms, in criminal law, insurance ("efficient proximate cause"), and statutory interpretation of civil liability statutes.
