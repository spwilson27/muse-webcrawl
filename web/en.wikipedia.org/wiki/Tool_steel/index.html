Tool steel

Tool steel
Material typeCarbon and alloy steel
Key propertiesHardness, wear resistance, toughness, hot hardness
ApplicationsCutting tools, dies, moulds, punches

Tool steel is any of a family of carbon and alloy steels particularly well suited to being made into tools — cutting and drilling implements, dies, moulds, and punches. Tool steels are distinguished by their hardness, resistance to abrasion and deformation, and their ability to hold a cutting edge at elevated temperatures. They are typically melted and processed with extra care to control inclusions and carbide distribution, and are supplied in the annealed condition for machining before being hardened by heat treatment.

Classification

The AISI–SAE classification groups tool steels by quenching medium and application: W-series (water-hardening plain carbon grades), O-series (oil-hardening cold-work), A-series (air-hardening cold-work), D-series (high-carbon, high-chromium cold-work), S-series (shock-resisting), H-series (hot-work, H1–H19 chromium, H20–H39 tungsten, H40–H59 molybdenum), T- and M-series (tungsten and molybdenum high-speed steels used for cutting tools), and P-series (plastic mould steels). High-speed steels such as M2 can cut at red heat without losing hardness, enabling high machining speeds.

Properties and manufacture

Alloying elements such as tungsten, molybdenum, chromium, vanadium, and cobalt form hard carbides that resist wear; carbon content (commonly 0.5–1.5%) governs attainable hardness after quenching and tempering. Because performance depends so heavily on heat treatment, tool steels are specified together with hardening and tempering cycles. Premium grades are made by powder metallurgy, which gives a finer, more uniform carbide structure and improved toughness for demanding applications such as fine-blanking dies and high-performance cutting tools.
