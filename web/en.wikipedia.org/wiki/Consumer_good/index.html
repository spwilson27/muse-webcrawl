Consumer goods

Consumer goods are products bought by individuals and households for personal consumption rather than for manufacturing or resale. They are the final goods of an economy, in contrast to intermediate goods used in production and capital goods (such as machinery) used to produce other goods. The consumer goods sector is a large part of most economies and is often used as an indicator of economic health.

Classification by durability

  • Fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG), or non-durables: inexpensive products consumed quickly and purchased frequently — food, beverages, toiletries and cleaning products. They are typically sold in large volumes with low margins.
  • Durable goods: products with a lifespan of more than three years, such as automobiles, furniture, appliances and consumer electronics. Purchases are often postponed in downturns, making durables a sensitive economic indicator (tracked, for example, in the U.S. durable goods orders report).
  • Services are sometimes treated alongside consumer goods as intangible consumer products, such as haircuts, repairs and entertainment.

Classification by shopping behaviour

Marketing theory (after Melvin Copeland, 1923) divides consumer goods into convenience goods bought routinely with minimal effort; shopping goods, such as clothing and furniture, compared on price and quality; specialty goods with unique characteristics for which buyers expend special effort; and unsought goods, such as insurance, that consumers do not normally think of buying.

Economics

Consumer spending (personal consumption expenditures) typically accounts for roughly two-thirds of GDP in advanced economies, so the production and retail of consumer goods drives business cycles. The industry spans branded manufacturers (e.g., food, beverage and household-product multinationals), private-label production, and retail distribution through supermarkets, department stores and e-commerce.
