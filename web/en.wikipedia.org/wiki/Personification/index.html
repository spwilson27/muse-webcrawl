Personification is the representation of a thing or abstraction as a person, in literature and art. In literature it is a figure of speech in which human qualities — speech, emotion, intention, action — are attributed to non-human things: "the wind whispered", "opportunity knocked". In visual art, abstract concepts such as Justice, Death, the seasons, nations and virtues are depicted as human figures, a device often shading into allegory.

In rhetoric and literature

The classical rhetorical term is prosopopoeia, in which an absent, dead or imaginary person — or an abstraction — is made to speak. Personification is central to fables, where animals act and talk as humans; to medieval allegory such as Piers Plowman and Everyman, whose characters are personified abstractions like Good Deeds and Knowledge; and to poetry across traditions, from Homer's personified Strife and Sleep to modern verse. It differs from the broader anthropomorphism, which attributes human traits to animals or gods as an actual belief or narrative premise rather than a figure of speech.

In art and culture

Classical and Renaissance art systematised personifications: Cesare Ripa's Iconologia (1593) catalogued how each virtue, vice, art and place should be pictured. Nations acquired human embodiments — Britannia, Germania, Marianne for France, Columbia and later Uncle Sam for the United States. Death as a skeletal reaper, Father Time with his scythe and hourglass, and the blindfolded Justice remain among the most recognisable personifications in Western culture.
