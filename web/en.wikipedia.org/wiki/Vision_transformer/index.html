Vision transformer

A vision transformer (ViT) is a type of artificial neural network based on the transformer architecture, applied to image recognition tasks. Introduced by a team at Google Brain in the 2020 paper "An Image is Worth 16×16 Words", the vision transformer showed that a pure transformer applied directly to sequences of image patches could achieve state-of-the-art results on image classification benchmarks, rivalling and eventually surpassing convolutional neural networks (CNNs).

Architecture

An input image is divided into a grid of fixed-size patches (typically 16×16 pixels). Each patch is flattened and linearly projected to an embedding vector — analogous to the tokens of a language model — and combined with a learned positional embedding. A learnable classification token, similar to BERT's [CLS] token, is prepended to the sequence. The sequence is then processed by a standard transformer encoder of multi-head self-attention and feed-forward layers, and the classification token's final state feeds a classifier head.

Training and scaling

Because ViTs lack the inductive biases of CNNs — locality and translation equivariance — they underperform when trained on modest datasets alone, but excel when pre-trained on very large datasets such as ImageNet-21k or Google's internal JFT-300M, then fine-tuned on the target task. This scaling behaviour helped drive the broader shift toward large pre-trained "foundation models" in computer vision.

Variants and influence

Numerous variants followed, including DeiT, which used distillation to train effectively on ImageNet-1k alone; the Swin Transformer, which introduced hierarchical shifted windows for dense prediction tasks such as object detection and segmentation; and hybrid CNN–transformer designs. Vision transformers also underpin multimodal systems: CLIP uses a ViT image encoder alongside a text transformer, and ViT encoders are standard in vision-language models.
