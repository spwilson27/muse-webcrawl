Anaerobic respiration

Process typeCellular respiration
Final electron acceptorInorganic molecule other than O2
Common acceptorsNitrate, sulfate, CO2, Fe(III)

Anaerobic respiration is a form of cellular respiration in which an electron transport chain is used to generate energy, but the final electron acceptor is an inorganic substance other than molecular oxygen. It is carried out by many bacteria and archaea living in environments without free oxygen, such as deep sediments, wetlands and the digestive tracts of animals.

Mechanism

As in aerobic respiration, electrons from an energy source such as glucose or hydrogen pass along membrane-bound carriers, pumping protons across the membrane and driving ATP synthesis by chemiosmosis. Because alternative acceptors such as nitrate, sulfate, ferric iron or carbon dioxide have lower reduction potentials than oxygen, the ATP yield is smaller than in aerobic respiration, though much greater than in fermentation.

Types

Denitrifying bacteria reduce nitrate to nitrogen gas, playing a key role in the nitrogen cycle. Sulfate-reducing bacteria produce hydrogen sulfide, while methanogenic archaea reduce carbon dioxide to methane. Some organisms, such as Escherichia coli, are facultative anaerobes that switch between oxygen and nitrate depending on availability.

Distinction from fermentation

Anaerobic respiration differs from fermentation in using an electron transport chain and an external electron acceptor; fermentation instead relies on substrate-level phosphorylation and internal organic acceptors such as pyruvate.
