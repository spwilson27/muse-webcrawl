A single reed is a type of reed used to produce sound in certain woodwind instruments, in which a single thin strip of material — traditionally giant cane (Arundo donax), increasingly also plastic or composite — vibrates against a mouthpiece to set the air column in motion. The principal orchestral and band instruments using a single reed are the clarinets and the saxophones.

A single reed clamped to a clarinet mouthpiece by a ligature.

How it works

The reed is held flat against the mouthpiece's table by a metal or fabric ligature, leaving a narrow gap at the tip. When the player blows, air pressure and the Bernoulli effect cause the flexible reed tip to beat against the mouthpiece facing hundreds of times per second, chopping the airstream into pulses at the frequency determined by the instrument's air column. In Hornbostel–Sachs classification, single-reed instruments are "clarinets" in the broad sense (422.2), distinguished from the double-reed "oboes" (422.1) such as the oboe and bassoon, where two reeds vibrate against each other.

Reeds as consumables

Single reeds are graded by stiffness (commonly numbered 1 to 5) and are notoriously variable and short-lived: players rotate several reeds, break in new ones gradually, and discard them as the cane softens or chips. Synthetic reeds, produced since the 1960s and refined with modern materials, trade some tonal character for durability and consistency, and are favoured by many marching, jazz and pit musicians.
