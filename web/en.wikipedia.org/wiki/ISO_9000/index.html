The ISO 9000 family is built around a process approach and the plan–do–check–act cycle.

The ISO 9000 family is a set of international standards for quality management systems (QMS) maintained by the International Organization for Standardization. First published in 1987, the standards define the criteria for a quality management system and are used by organisations of all sizes and sectors worldwide to demonstrate the ability to consistently provide products and services that meet customer and regulatory requirements. Over one million organisations in more than 170 countries have been certified to the family's certifiable standard, ISO 9001.

The family

  • ISO 9000 — fundamentals and vocabulary of quality management systems.
  • ISO 9001 — requirements for a QMS; the only standard in the family against which organisations are certified.
  • ISO 9004 — guidance for sustained success and managing quality in a broader, performance-oriented sense.
  • ISO 19011 — guidelines for auditing management systems.

Principles

The standards rest on seven quality management principles: customer focus, leadership, engagement of people, the process approach, improvement, evidence-based decision making, and relationship management. ISO 9001:2015 organises its requirements using the "high-level structure" (Annex SL) shared with other management system standards such as ISO 14001 (environment) and ISO 45001 (occupational health and safety), and places new emphasis on risk-based thinking and the context of the organisation. The underlying improvement model is the plan–do–check–act (PDCA) cycle associated with W. Edwards Deming.

Certification

ISO itself does not certify organisations. Certification is carried out by independent, usually nationally accredited, certification bodies, which audit the organisation's QMS periodically. ISO 9001 certification is often a condition of doing business in supply chains, particularly in manufacturing, aerospace (via the derivative AS9100), automotive (IATF 16949), and medical devices (ISO 13485).

Criticism

The standards have been criticised for encouraging bureaucratic documentation, for certifying the existence of processes rather than the quality of outcomes, and for variability in audit rigour. Empirical studies of certification's effect on business performance show mixed results, with benefits depending heavily on whether adoption is internally motivated or merely for marketing and tendering purposes.
