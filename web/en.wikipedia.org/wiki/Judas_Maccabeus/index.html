Judas Maccabeus (also spelled Judah Maccabee; Hebrew: יהודה המכבי, Yehudah HaMakabi; died 160 BCE) was a Jewish priest (kohen) and the leader of the Maccabean Revolt against the Seleucid Empire (167–160 BCE). The third son of the priest Mattathias of Modi'in, he took command of the revolt after his father's death and won a series of victories against far larger Seleucid armies, culminating in the recovery and purification of the Temple in Jerusalem in 164 BCE — an event commemorated by the festival of Hanukkah.

Background

The Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, seeking to Hellenise his realm, outlawed Jewish religious practice, desecrated the Temple and erected an altar to Zeus in it — the "abomination of desolation" of the Book of Daniel. In 167 BCE the priest Mattathias killed a royal officer at Modi'in and fled to the hills with his five sons, beginning the revolt. On his deathbed in 166 BCE Mattathias named Judas, famed for his courage, as military commander; his surname Maccabeus is traditionally explained as "the Hammer".

Military campaigns

Using guerrilla tactics and night marches, Judas defeated Seleucid forces under Apollonius, Seron (at Beth Horon), and the generals Gorgias and Lysias (at Emmaus and Beth Zur), repeatedly overcoming superior numbers. In December 164 BCE he liberated Jerusalem and rededicated the Temple, instituting the eight-day festival of Hanukkah. He went on to campaign in Gilead, Galilee and Idumea to relieve persecuted Jewish communities, while also seeking Roman friendship; 1 Maccabees records an early treaty of alliance with the Roman Republic.

Death

After Antiochus IV's death the Seleucid regent Lysias granted religious freedom, but the struggle for full independence continued. In April 160 BCE, at the Battle of Elasa near Ramallah, Judas — deserted by much of his force — faced the general Bacchides with perhaps 800–1,000 men against 20,000. He refused to flee and was killed in the fighting. His brothers Jonathan and Simon buried him at Modi'in and carried on the struggle, eventually founding the Hasmonean dynasty.

Legacy

Judas is remembered as a national hero of Jewish history and a symbol of resistance to religious persecution. The books of 1 and 2 Maccabees preserve his story; Handel's oratorio Judas Maccabaeus (1746) made his name famous in Christian Europe, and the Maccabiah Games take their name from his.
