Beowulf

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The first page of Beowulf in the Cotton Vitellius manuscript: scorched edges from the 1731 Ashburnham House fire around the opening words]

Beowulf is an Old English epic poem of 3,182 alliterative lines, the most famous work of Anglo-Saxon literature. It survives in a single manuscript (the Nowell Codex, c. 1000) and was composed at an unknown date between the eighth and early eleventh centuries by an unknown Christian poet, retelling pagan Scandinavian legend: the hero Beowulf's fights with the monster Grendel, Grendel's mother, and, fifty years later as an old king, a dragon.

Set in Denmark and Geatland (southern Sweden), the poem opens in the hall Heorot, plagued by Grendel; Beowulf tears off the monster's arm bare-handed, descends to the mere to kill its mother with a giant's sword, and returns home laden with honour. The third movement — aged Beowulf, the dragon's wrath at a stolen cup, the cowardice of his thanes and the loyalty of Wiglaf, the hero's death and ship-burial of treasure — gives the poem its elegiac weight.

The manuscript and recovery

The unique manuscript, Cotton Vitellius A XV, was damaged in the fire at Ashburnham House in 1731 and crumbled at the edges thereafter; its first full edition was Thorkelin's of 1815. For a century scholars mined it as a historical quarry; J. R. R. Tolkien's 1936 lecture 'Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics' remade it as a poem about heroism and mortality, and Tolkien's own fiction is steeped in it. Seamus Heaney's translation (1999) became a best-seller and won the Whitbread Award.

Language and form

The poem's alliterative long line, its kennings ('whale-road' for the sea, in the conventional gloss) and its digressive, ring-structured narrative define Old English poetics. Modern English readers meet it in translation, where versions by Heaney, Tolkien and others argue, line by line, over how strange or how homely its voice should sound.
