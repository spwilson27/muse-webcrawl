Leo Max Frank (April 17, 1884 – August 17, 1915) was an American factory superintendent who was convicted in 1913 of the murder of Mary Phagan, a 13-year-old employee of the Atlanta pencil factory he managed. His trial, conviction, and eventual lynching — after Georgia's governor commuted his death sentence — became one of the most notorious episodes in American legal history, fuelling both the founding of the Anti-Defamation League and the revival of the Ku Klux Klan. Frank, who was Jewish, was posthumously pardoned in 1986, and the consensus of historians is that he was wrongfully convicted.

The murder and trial

Mary Phagan was found murdered in the basement of the National Pencil Company factory in Atlanta on 27 April 1913, the day after Confederate Memorial Day. Frank, the factory's superintendent and a Cornell-educated engineer raised in Brooklyn, was arrested and tried that summer. The prosecution's case rested heavily on the testimony of Jim Conley, the factory's janitor, who claimed Frank had confessed to him and enlisted his help; physical evidence was thin and contradictory. The trial took place in an atmosphere saturated with sensational press coverage and antisemitic agitation, led by the populist politician Tom Watson's newspaper The Jeffersonian. Frank was convicted and sentenced to hang. Appeals, including to the U.S. Supreme Court, failed, though Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes dissented, protesting the mob-dominated character of the proceedings.

Commutation and lynching

In June 1915, on his last day in office, Governor John Slaton reviewed the evidence and commuted Frank's sentence to life imprisonment, declaring that he could not let the state execute a man whose guilt he doubted. The decision ended Slaton's political career and provoked riots. Frank was transferred to the state prison farm at Milledgeville, where another inmate slashed his throat (he survived). On the night of 16 August 1915, a party of armed men who called themselves the Knights of Mary Phagan — including prominent citizens of Marietta, Phagan's hometown — abducted Frank from the prison, drove him to Marietta, and hanged him the next morning before a crowd. No one was ever prosecuted.

Aftermath and legacy

The lynching shocked the nation. It contributed directly to the founding in 1913–15 era of the Anti-Defamation League of B'nai B'rith, of which Frank had been an Atlanta chapter president, and, in reaction two months later, William J. Simmons refounded the Ku Klux Klan atop Stone Mountain, partly in Frank's shadow. In 1982, Alonzo Mann, Frank's former office boy, swore an affidavit that he had seen Conley carrying Phagan's body — testimony he had withheld as a frightened teenager. In 1986 the Georgia State Board of Pardons and Paroles pardoned Frank, noting the state's failure to protect him and to prosecute his killers, while not formally exonerating him. The case has inspired books (notably Steve Oney's And the Dead Shall Rise, 2003), the musical Parade (1998), and continuing debate; in 2019 Fulton County's district attorney established a conviction integrity unit review of the case.
