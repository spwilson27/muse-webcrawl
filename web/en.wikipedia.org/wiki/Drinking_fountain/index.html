Drinking fountain


A modern drinking fountain with an angled jet.

A drinking fountain, also called a water fountain or bubbler, is a public fixture that dispenses drinking water, typically as a small upward-arching jet that allows a person to drink directly from the stream without a cup. They are commonly installed in schools, parks, railway stations, workplaces and other public places.

History

Public provision of free drinking water grew with the nineteenth-century sanitary and temperance movements. In London the Metropolitan Free Drinking Fountains Association, founded in 1859, erected ornate stone fountains to give the poor an alternative to beer and gin. In the United States, temperance advocates likewise promoted fountains in public squares. Early designs bubbled water continuously upward; later designs introduced valves, angled jets and guards to improve hygiene by preventing the drinker's mouth from contacting the nozzle and contaminated water from falling back onto it.

Design and hygiene

Modern fountains are usually stainless steel or porcelain units with a bubbler head, push-button or sensor valve, and often a refrigeration unit. Building codes in many jurisdictions specify stream height and angle, guard geometry and accessibility, including lower basins for wheelchair users. Many newer installations add a bottle-filling station to encourage reuse and reduce plastic waste.

Decline and revival

During the later twentieth century many public fountains fell into disrepair as bottled water became cheap and ubiquitous. Concerns about hygiene, vandalism and maintenance costs accelerated closures. In the twenty-first century, plastic-waste campaigns and public-health advocacy led cities such as London and San Francisco to restore networks of public drinking fountains and refill stations.
