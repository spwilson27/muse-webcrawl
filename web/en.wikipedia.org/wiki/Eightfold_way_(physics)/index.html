Eightfold way (physics)

In particle physics, the eightfold way is a classification scheme for hadrons (mesons and baryons) proposed independently in 1961 by Murray Gell-Mann and Yuval Ne'eman. It organises the known hadrons into multiplets of the flavour symmetry group SU(3), plotting them by electric charge and strangeness into hexagonal and triangular patterns reminiscent of a periodic table. Gell-Mann named the scheme after the Noble Eightfold Path of Buddhism.

The multiplets

Under the scheme, the spin-1/2 baryons (proton, neutron, Lambda, Sigmas and Xis) form an octet, the mesons form an octet plus a singlet, and the spin-3/2 baryons form a decuplet of ten particles. At the time the decuplet had one missing member, a triply strange particle with charge −1; the scheme predicted its existence, mass and properties.

The Omega-minus

The predicted particle, the Ω⁻ (Omega-minus), was discovered at Brookhaven National Laboratory in 1964 with almost exactly the predicted properties, a triumph that convinced physicists of the scheme's validity. The success also led Gell-Mann to propose that the SU(3) structure arises because hadrons are built from three fundamental constituents — the quarks (up, down and strange) — introduced in the quark model of 1964.

Legacy

The eightfold way was a decisive step toward the modern Standard Model: flavour SU(3) is now understood as an approximate symmetry arising from the similar masses of the three lightest quarks, and quantum chromodynamics provides the underlying theory. Gell-Mann received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1969 for this and related work.
