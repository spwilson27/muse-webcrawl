Andrei Dmitrievich Sakharov (21 May 1921 – 14 December 1989) was a Soviet physicist and a Nobel laureate in Physics for his activism in civil liberties and reforms. He was the designer of the RDS-37, the Soviet Union's first two-stage hydrogen bomb, and co-inventor (with Igor Tamm) of the tokamak. In cosmology he proposed the Sakharov conditions (1967) — the requirements any theory must meet to explain the universe's excess of matter over Antimatter — and early models of induced gravity.

From the late 1950s Sakharov campaigned against nuclear testing (helping achieve the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty) and for human rights, becoming the Soviet Union's most famous dissident: his 1968 essay Progress, Coexistence, and Intellectual Freedom circulated worldwide; the 1975 Nobel Peace Prize was accepted by his wife Yelena Bonner because he was barred from travelling; in 1980 he was exiled to Gorky, and freed by Gorbachev in 1986 in the emblem of glasnost. He died in 1989, a people's deputy arguing for a democratic constitution.

Physics

Sakharov's physics legacy spans weapon design (the 'layer cake' and radiation-implosion third idea), magnetic confinement fusion, explosive magnetic generators, the baryon-asymmetry conditions that still frame cosmology (CP violation among them), and speculative gravity — an output matched by few, achieved before he turned to dissent full-time.
