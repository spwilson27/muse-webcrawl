CRISPR gene editing

CRISPR gene editing (pronounced "crisper") is a genetic engineering technique in molecular biology by which the genomes of living organisms may be modified. It is based on a simplified version of the bacterial CRISPR-Cas9 antiviral defense system. By delivering the Cas9 nuclease complexed with a synthetic guide RNA (gRNA) into a cell, the cell's genome can be cut at a desired location, allowing existing genes to be removed or new ones added in vivo.

History

CRISPR sequences were first noticed in bacterial genomes in 1987, and their role in adaptive immunity against viruses was established in the 2000s. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier, and colleagues showed that Cas9 could be directed by a single guide RNA to cut any chosen DNA sequence; in 2013, Feng Zhang's and George Church's groups demonstrated editing in human and mouse cells. Doudna and Charpentier received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020 for the development of the method.

Mechanism

The guide RNA contains a scaffold sequence that binds Cas9 and a ~20-nucleotide spacer that matches the target DNA. Cas9 cuts both DNA strands near a short "PAM" sequence, producing a double-strand break. The cell repairs the break either by error-prone non-homologous end joining, which often inactivates the gene, or by homology-directed repair using a supplied template, enabling precise sequence changes. Related tools include base editors and prime editors, which modify DNA without double-strand breaks.

Applications

CRISPR is used widely in basic research to knock out or modify genes in cell lines and model organisms, in agriculture to create disease-resistant and higher-yield crops, and in medicine. In 2023, the first CRISPR-based therapy, Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel), was approved for sickle cell disease and beta thalassemia. Controversies include off-target effects, mosaicism, and the ethics of germline editing, highlighted by the 2018 announcement of genome-edited babies in China.
