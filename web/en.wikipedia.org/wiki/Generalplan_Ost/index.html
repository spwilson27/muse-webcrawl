The Generalplan Ost (GPO; German: [ɡenəˈʁaːlˌplaːn ˈɔst]; English: Master Plan for the East) was a secret plan developed by Nazi Germany for the large-scale ethnic cleansing, extermination and Germanization of the Slavic and other populations of Eastern Europe during and after World War II. It formed the demographic blueprint of the Lebensraum policy in the territories conquered from Poland and the Soviet Union.

Development

Planning began after the invasion of Poland in 1939 under the Reich Commission for the Strengthening of Germanhood, headed by Heinrich Himmler. Several versions were prepared between 1940 and 1942 by planners including agricultural scientist Konrad Meyer and officials of the Reich Security Main Office. The plan envisaged, over some 25–30 years, the removal of tens of millions of people classified as racially undesirable: scholarly reconstructions based on a critique by Erhard Wetzel estimate intended removals of roughly 31 million people from Poland and the western Soviet Union, with further figures for other Slavic populations, to be replaced by millions of German settlers.

Methods envisaged

The plan combined mass expulsions beyond the Urals, enslavement, deliberate famine (related to the Hunger Plan), forced sterilization, kidnapping of "racially valuable" children, and extermination. Parts of it were already implemented during the war through mass murder, deportations, and settlement actions in regions such as the Zamość area, contributing to the deaths of millions of civilians in occupied Eastern Europe.

Evidence and legacy

Most copies of the plan were destroyed at the end of the war; knowledge of it rests on surviving memoranda, Wetzel's commentary on an RSHA draft, and testimony at the Nuremberg trials (notably the RuSHA trial). Historians regard the Generalplan Ost as central evidence of the genocidal character of Nazi occupation policy in the East.
