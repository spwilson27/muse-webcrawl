Temperature107–108 K
CompositionIonised plasma, mainly hydrogen and helium with heavy elements
Observed viaX-ray bremsstrahlung; Sunyaev–Zeldovich effect

In astronomy, the intracluster medium (ICM) is the superheated plasma present at the centre of a galaxy cluster. The gas consists primarily of ionised hydrogen and helium and accounts for the bulk of the baryonic matter in clusters, outweighing the stars in all their galaxies combined. It is heated to tens of millions of kelvin by gravitational collapse and shocks as the cluster forms.

Properties and observation

At such temperatures the ICM emits strongly in X-rays, principally by bremsstrahlung (free–free emission), and it was the discovery of extended X-ray emission from clusters that revealed the medium. X-ray telescopes such as Chandra and XMM-Newton map its density and temperature, while the Sunyaev–Zeldovich effect — distortion of the cosmic microwave background by hot electrons — offers a complementary probe used for cluster surveys. The ICM is enriched with heavy elements (its iron abundance is roughly one-third of the Sun's) expelled from galaxies by supernovae and galactic winds.

Cooling and feedback

Gas at the dense centres of many clusters radiates energy fast enough that it should cool and flow inward within a billion years, yet observed star formation and cold gas fall far short of the naive prediction — the "cooling flow problem". The accepted resolution is feedback from the central active galactic nucleus: jets from the supermassive black hole inflate bubbles that reheat the ICM and regulate its cooling.
