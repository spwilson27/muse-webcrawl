Scott Joplin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Scott Joplin in 1903: the composer of the 'Maple Leaf Rag' in a formal studio portrait, moustached, in a dark suit]

Scott Joplin (c. 1868 – 1 April 1917) was an American composer and pianist, the 'King of Ragtime'. His piano rags — above all the 'Maple Leaf Rag' (1899) and 'The Entertainer' (1902) — fused African-American syncopation with European march and classical forms into the first distinctively American composed music, and the seedbed of jazz.

Joplin insisted his ragtime be played exactly as written, 'never fast'. He aspired beyond the dance hall: his ballet Rag Time Dance (1902) and the opera Treemonisha (1911) — about an educated Black woman leading her community out of superstition — were produced in his lifetime only in fragments; Treemonisha's full staging came in 1972, and Joplin received a posthumous Pulitzer Prize in 1976.

Sedalia and St Louis

Born near Linden, Texas, to a musical family, Joplin was taught by the German-American teacher Julius Weiss, who grounded him in European harmony. In Sedalia, Missouri, he played in the clubs where ragtime was crystallising and published the Maple Leaf Rag with the white publisher John Stark — its sheet music sold steadily for two decades and made Joplin's reputation and income.

Revival

Joplin died in New York in 1917 of syphilis-related illness, his ambitions unfulfilled and his music fading with ragtime itself. The 1970s revival — Joshua Rifkin's recordings, the film The Sting (1973), which carried Marvin Hamlisch's adaptation of 'The Entertainer' to No. 3 in the charts — restored him as a founding father of American music.
