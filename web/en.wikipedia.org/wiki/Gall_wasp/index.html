Gall wasp

Gall wasps, also called gallflies, are a family of small wasps, the Cynipidae, in the order Hymenoptera. They are famous for their ability to induce the formation of plant galls — distinctive abnormal growths on plants inside which the wasp larvae develop. About 1,300 species are known, the great majority of them on oak trees.

Biology

Gall wasps are tiny insects, typically 1–8 mm long, with laterally compressed bodies. A female lays eggs into plant tissue; the plant responds, under chemical direction from the insect, by producing a gall — a structured growth of nutritive tissue that feeds and shelters the larva while it develops through its stages, pupates, and finally chews its way out as an adult.

Alternation of generations

Many oak gall wasps have one of the most complex life cycles of any insect: an alternation between a sexual generation and an all-female, parthenogenetic (agamic) generation, which produce entirely different galls on different parts of the tree, or even on different species of oak. The classic example is the oak apple gall wasp Biorhiza pallida: its sexual generation develops in small galls on oak roots, while the agamic generation produces the large, spongy "oak apples" on twigs.

Common galls

  • Oak apples — round, spongy galls on twigs.
  • Marble galls — hard, spherical galls induced by Andricus kollari.
  • Knopper galls — deformed acorns produced by Andricus quercuscalicis.
  • Robin's pincushion — the mossy rose bedeguar gall of Diplolepis rosae.
  • Spangle galls — small disc-shaped galls on the undersides of oak leaves.

Relations with other organisms

Galls support rich communities: inquiline wasps live inside galls made by others, and numerous parasitoid wasps attack the gall-makers in turn. Historically, oak galls rich in tannin were used to make iron gall ink, the standard writing ink of Europe for centuries.
