An IATA airport code, also known as an IATA location identifier, IATA station code or simply a location identifier, is a three-letter geocode designating airports and metropolitan areas around the world, defined by the International Air Transport Association (IATA). The codes are used throughout the passenger airline industry on tickets, reservations, timetables, departure boards and baggage tags, and are published in IATA's Airline Coding Directory.

Assignment

Codes are usually derived from the name of the city or airport: many take the first letters of the city (e.g. SIN for Singapore), the airport's name (JFK for New York's John F. Kennedy International, CDG for Paris Charles de Gaulle), or a former name (ORD for Chicago O'Hare, from Orchard Field; PEK from Peking). When several airports serve one city, IATA also assigns metropolitan codes such as NYC, LON and TYO. Because the supply of three-letter combinations is finite (~17,576), codes of defunct airports are eventually recycled, and assignments are coordinated to avoid confusion between nearby locations.

History

The system grew out of two-letter codes used by US airlines and weather stations in the 1930s, expanded to three letters as air travel expanded after the Second World War; IATA standardised the system with its founding era resolutions. In the United States, codes often derive from National Weather Service identifiers with a dropped prefix (e.g. LAX from the weather station code "LA"), which explains the otherwise puzzling X in LAX, PDX and PHX.

Other codes

IATA codes also cover some railway stations, ferry ports and bus terminals that participate in airline reservation systems. They should not be confused with four-letter ICAO airport codes (e.g. EGLL for Heathrow) used in flight operations, or with FAA identifiers used domestically in the United States.
