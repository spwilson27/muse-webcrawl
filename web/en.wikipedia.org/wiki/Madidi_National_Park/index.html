Madidi National Park

Madidi National Park (Spanish: Parque Nacional Madidi) is a protected area in the upper Amazon river basin in north-western Bolivia, in the departments of La Paz and Beni. Established in 1995 and covering roughly 18,958 km², it spans an extraordinary altitudinal range — from glaciers and puna grasslands in the Andes at nearly 6,000 m down to lowland rainforest at about 180 m — making it one of the most biologically diverse protected areas on Earth.

Geography and ecosystems

Madidi protects a continuous gradient of ecosystems: high Andean peaks and cloud forests (yungas), dry inter-Andean valleys, foothill forests, and vast lowland Amazonian rainforest and flooded savannas. Major rivers include the Madidi, Tuichi, Beni and Heath. Together with neighbouring protected areas in Bolivia and Peru (including Manú National Park), it forms one of the largest contiguous conservation landscapes in South America.

Biodiversity

The park is celebrated for its species richness: estimates include around 5,000–6,000 plant species, more than 1,000 bird species (about 11% of the world's total), and large populations of jaguars, spectacled bears, giant otters, manatees, pink river dolphins and at least eleven species of primates. New species continue to be described from the park; the Madidi titi monkey (Plecturocebus aureipalatii) was identified in 2004 and its naming rights were auctioned to fund conservation.

People and tourism

Indigenous peoples including the Tacana, Quechua, Aymara and Ese Ejja live in and around the park. Community-run ecolodges such as Chalalán on the Tuichi River, reached from the gateway town of Rurrenabaque, are frequently cited models of indigenous ecotourism. Threats to the park include illegal logging, gold mining, road proposals and a long-debated hydroelectric dam project on the Beni River.
