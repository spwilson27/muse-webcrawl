The Milgram experiment(s) on obedience to authority figures were a series of social psychology experiments conducted by Yale University psychologist Stanley Milgram beginning in 1961. Participants were instructed by an experimenter to administer what they believed were increasingly painful electric shocks to a "learner" (a confederate) for wrong answers. The studies found that a surprisingly high proportion of ordinary people were willing to obey authority even when doing so conflicted with their personal conscience, results Milgram published in his 1974 book Obedience to Authority.

Procedure

Volunteers were assigned the role of "teacher" (the draw was rigged) and seated before a shock generator with switches from 15 to 450 volts, labelled up to "Danger: Severe Shock." The learner, an actor, received no real shocks but played recorded protests, screams, and eventually silence. If the teacher hesitated, the experimenter issued a sequence of standardised prods, ending with "You have no other choice; you must go on."

Results

In the best-known baseline condition, 26 of 40 participants (65%) continued to the maximum 450 volts. Variations — moving the learner into the same room, adding dissenting peers, or removing the experimenter's presence — changed obedience rates dramatically, showing the power of situational factors. No participant stopped before the 300-volt level in the baseline voice-feedback condition.

Criticism and legacy

The experiments provoked lasting debate about research ethics, including psychological harm and deception, and helped drive modern informed-consent and debriefing standards. Later analyses have questioned aspects of Milgram's reporting and interpretation, but partial replications in several countries have found broadly similar obedience levels. The work remains a landmark in understanding obedience, alongside the Stanford prison experiment and the Eichmann trial that partly inspired it.
