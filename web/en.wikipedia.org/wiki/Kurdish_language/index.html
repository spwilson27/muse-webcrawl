Kurdish (کوردی, Kurdî) is a continuum of Northwestern Iranian languages spoken by the Kurds across the region of Kurdistan, spanning south-eastern Turkey, northern Iraq, western Iran and northern Syria, with a large diaspora in Europe. It is not a single unified language but a group of closely related varieties, the principal ones being Kurmanji (Northern Kurdish, the most widely spoken, in Turkey, Syria and parts of Iraq and Iran), Sorani (Central Kurdish, dominant in Iraqi Kurdistan and western Iran) and Southern Kurdish. Together with Zaza and Gorani (often grouped as Zaza-Gorani), they form the Kurdish language complex; whether Zaza and Gorani count as Kurdish dialects is debated.

Writing systems

Kurdish is written in different scripts by region: Kurmanji is written mainly in the Latin-based Hawar alphabet developed by Celadet Alî Bedirxan in the 1930s; Sorani is written in a modified Perso-Arabic script with letters devised to represent Kurdish vowels precisely, making it a near-complete alphabet rather than an abjad. Kurds in the former Soviet Union used a Cyrillic-based alphabet. Arabic-script Kurdish also historically used the Ottoman Turkish presses in Constantinople.

Status and history

Sorani is an official language of Iraq and of the Kurdistan Region, whose institutions publish and broadcast in it; Kurdish-language education and media were long suppressed in Turkey, where use of Kurdish in public life was banned after 1980 and liberalised only from the 1990s onward. Classical Kurdish literature includes the poet Ahmad Khani's epic Mem û Zîn (1692) and the work of Nalî; a rich oral tradition of dengbêj singer-poets preserves epic and historical material. Kurdish grammar is characterised by ergative alignment in past tenses (in Kurmanji and Sorani), a system of oblique and direct cases in Kurmanji, ezafe constructions linking nouns, and split-S or split-ergativity patterns typical of Iranian languages.
