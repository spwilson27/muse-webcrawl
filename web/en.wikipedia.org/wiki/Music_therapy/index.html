Music therapy

Music therapy is a therapeutic approach that uses music-based interventions — listening, singing, playing instruments, improvisation and songwriting — within a therapeutic relationship to address physical, emotional, cognitive and social needs. It is delivered by credentialed music therapists and is distinguished from simply playing music for patients or music education.

History

The modern profession emerged after the First and Second World Wars, when musicians performed for hospitalised veterans and clinicians observed significant positive effects on physical and emotional trauma. The first music therapy degree programme was established at Michigan State University in 1944, and national professional associations followed, including the American Music Therapy Association (formed 1998 by merger) and the British Association for Music Therapy. Influential models include Nordoff–Robbins creative music therapy, Guided Imagery and Music (Bonny method), and neurologic music therapy.

Applications and evidence

Music therapy is used in mental health, dementia care, stroke and brain-injury rehabilitation, palliative care, neonatal care and work with autistic children. Randomised trials and Cochrane reviews have found evidence of benefit for, among other outcomes, reducing agitation and improving mood in dementia, aiding gait rehabilitation after stroke (rhythmic auditory stimulation), and lessening anxiety and pain perception in medical settings. Effects vary by condition and intervention, and researchers caution that studies are often small; nonetheless music therapy is now an established allied health profession in many countries.
