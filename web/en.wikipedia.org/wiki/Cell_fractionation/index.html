Cell fractionation

Cell fractionation is the process used to separate cellular components while preserving individual functions of each component. This is a method that biochemists have used to study the structure, composition, and function of organelles. The most common procedure involves homogenising the cells to break the plasma membrane and then separating the components by differential centrifugation at successively higher speeds.

Process

Homogenisation

Tissue or cultured cells are first suspended in a cold, isotonic buffer (commonly sucrose solution) and disrupted by a Potter–Elvehjem homogeniser, sonication, or repeated freeze–thaw cycles. The goal is to break open the cells while leaving organelles intact. The resulting mixture is called a homogenate.

Differential centrifugation

The homogenate is spun in a series of steps at increasing centrifugal force. Nuclei and unbroken cells pellet at low speed (about 1,000 × g); mitochondria, chloroplasts, and lysosomes pellet at medium speed (about 10,000–20,000 × g); and microsomes (fragments of endoplasmic reticulum), small vesicles, and ribosomes require ultracentrifugation at around 100,000 × g. The remaining supernatant is the cytosol.

Density gradient centrifugation

For finer separation, fractions can be layered over a sucrose or Percoll density gradient and centrifuged until components settle at their buoyant density (isopycnic separation), resolving organelles of similar size but different density, such as lysosomes and peroxisomes.

History

The technique was pioneered in the 1930s–1950s by Albert Claude, Christian de Duve, and George Palade, whose work with fractionation and electron microscopy laid the foundations of modern cell biology and was recognised with the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1974.
