Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, commonly known as GLP-1 receptor agonists or incretin mimetics, are a class of medications that activate the GLP-1 receptor, mimicking the action of the endogenous incretin hormone glucagon-like peptide-1. They are used mainly in the treatment of type 2 diabetes and obesity.

Mechanism and effects

GLP-1 receptor activation increases glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppresses glucagon release, slows gastric emptying, and promotes satiety through central appetite pathways. The resulting effects include improved glycaemic control and substantial body-weight reduction — with agents such as semaglutide producing average weight losses around 15% in clinical trials. Cardiovascular outcome trials have shown reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events for several drugs in the class.

Agents and side effects

Approved agents include exenatide (the first, approved in 2005), liraglutide, dulaglutide, semaglutide (injected and oral), and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide. The most common side effects are gastrointestinal — nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and constipation — especially during dose escalation; rare concerns include gastroparesis and a possible risk of medullary thyroid carcinoma reflected in boxed warnings.
