An altarpiece is a work of art — usually a painting, relief, or sculptural ensemble — set on, behind, or above an altar in a Christian church. Altarpieces were the central focus of devotion and the most prestigious commissions of the later Middle Ages and Renaissance, serving simultaneously as liturgical furnishing, teaching image for congregants, and memorial of the patron who funded them.

Forms and terminology

Altarpieces range from single panels to elaborate foldable structures:

  • Diptych — two panels; often small, for private devotion.
  • Triptych — a central panel with two hinged wings, which could be closed on ordinary days (showing subdued, often grisaille, imagery) and opened on feast days to reveal the full splendour inside.
  • Polyptych — many panels, sometimes in two registers with a predella (a row of small scenes along the base).
  • Retable — a framework or shelf rising behind the altar bearing images; the great Spanish retablos mayores fill entire east walls with gilded sculpture.
  • Pala — the Italian large single-field altarpiece that replaced polyptychs in the High Renaissance.

History

Images behind altars appear sporadically in earlier centuries, but the altarpiece became standard from the 13th century, when liturgical change — the priest celebrating facing the altar against the east wall — left the space above and behind the altar free for imagery, and when carved retables in northern Europe and painted panels in Italy developed rapidly. Giotto, Duccio (whose Maestà for Siena Cathedral had narrative scenes on both faces), and their contemporaries made the altarpiece the premier vehicle of painting.

The 15th century produced masterpieces such as the Ghent Altarpiece (completed 1432) by Hubert and Jan van Eyck, Rogier van der Weyden's Descent from the Cross, and the sculpted winged altarpieces of Veit Stoss and Tilman Riemenschneider in Germany. Matthias Grünewald's Isenheim Altarpiece (c. 1512–1516), with its multiple opening positions, marks a culmination. After the Reformation, altarpieces were destroyed or removed in much of Protestant Europe (iconoclasm), while the Counter-Reformation Catholic Church renewed them on a grand scale, favouring large single canvases and sculptural altar ensembles in the Baroque manner.

Function

Altarpieces anchored the Mass visually: their subjects — most often the Virgin and Child, the Crucifixion, or the patron saints of the church or donor — matched the altar's dedication and the liturgy celebrated before them. Winged altarpieces changed appearance with the church calendar, making the object itself a participant in the rhythms of worship. Today many survive dismembered in museums, their panels separated among collections, while others remain in situ.
