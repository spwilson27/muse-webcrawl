Link rot

Link rot (also called link death, link breaking, or reference rot) is the phenomenon of hyperlinks tending over time to cease to point to their originally targeted file, web page, or server due to that resource being relocated to a new address or becoming permanently unavailable. A link that no longer points to its target is called a broken link, dead link, or dangling link.

The familiar 404 error encountered when following a rotted link.

Causes

Link rot arises because websites are reorganised, domains lapse or change hands, content-management systems restructure URLs, and servers are decommissioned. A related problem, sometimes distinguished as content drift or reference rot, occurs when the address still works but the content at the destination has changed so that it no longer supports the statement for which it was cited.

Prevalence

Studies of scholarly and legal citation have repeatedly found high rates of decay. Analyses of links in academic journals have found that a substantial fraction become inaccessible within a few years of publication, and a widely cited study of United States Supreme Court opinions found that roughly half of the URLs cited no longer led to the original material. Studies of general web pages similarly suggest that links have a half-life measured in years rather than decades.

Prevention and repair

Mitigations include the use of persistent identifier schemes such as DOIs, permalinks and PURLs; archiving cited pages with services such as the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine or archive.today; and citation templates that automatically include archived copies. Webmasters can reduce breakage by keeping URLs stable and providing redirects from old addresses.
