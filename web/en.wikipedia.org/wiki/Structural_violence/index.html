Structural violence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Structural violence is a term referring to a form of violence wherein some social structure or social institution may harm people by preventing them from meeting their basic needs or rights. The term was coined by the Norwegian sociologist and peace researcher Johan Galtung, who introduced it in his 1969 article "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research".

Definition

Galtung defined violence as the cause of the difference between the potential and the actual realisation of human well-being. Where that difference is produced not by a direct actor inflicting physical harm but by the way a society is organised — through unequal distributions of income, healthcare, education, or political power — the violence is structural: it is built into the structure of society and shows up as unequal life chances. Because there is no single identifiable perpetrator, structural violence is also called indirect violence, in contrast to direct, behavioural violence.

Applications

The concept has been widely used in medical anthropology, notably by Paul Farmer, who applied it to explain how poverty, racism, and political-economic arrangements shaped the distribution of diseases such as tuberculosis and HIV/AIDS in Haiti and elsewhere. In this usage, structural violence describes how large-scale social forces — including economic policies and institutionalised discrimination — become embodied as illness and premature death among the poor.

The term has also been applied to global inequalities in life expectancy and infant mortality, gender-based disparities in access to resources, and the health effects of environmental degradation concentrated in marginalised communities.

Criticism

Critics argue that the concept stretches the meaning of "violence" so broadly that it risks losing analytical precision, conflating injustice, inequality, and misfortune with violence proper. Others respond that the term's rhetorical force is precisely its point: it draws attention to harms that are normalised and rendered invisible precisely because they lack a dramatic event or a single villain.
