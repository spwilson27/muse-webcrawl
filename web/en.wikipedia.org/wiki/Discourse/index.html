Discourse is a general term for written or spoken communication, particularly extended, structured uses of language — a conversation, a speech, a body of texts — and, in social theory, the system of statements, concepts and practices through which a subject is defined and governed. The word comes from the Latin discursus, "running to and fro".

In linguistics

Linguists use discourse for language above the level of the sentence: how utterances connect into coherent stretches of speech or writing. Discourse analysis studies such features as cohesion, turn-taking in conversation, genre and register, and how speakers signal information structure. A discourse marker, such as "well", "so" or "anyway", organises the flow of talk without adding propositional content.

In social theory

Michel Foucault gave the term its influential theoretical sense: a discourse is a regulated set of statements that systematically forms the objects of which it speaks — for example, medical discourse constitutes "the patient", and legal discourse constitutes "the criminal". Discourses are bound up with power and institutions (see disciplinary institutions): they define what can be said, who may speak, and what counts as true in a given period. Jürgen Habermas used the term differently, for the rational-critical communication of the public sphere and his discourse ethics.

Discourse analysis

Methods range from conversation analysis and pragmatics to critical discourse analysis (associated with Norman Fairclough and Teun van Dijk), which examines how texts reproduce ideology and social inequality, and Foucauldian analysis of archives and institutions. Applications include media studies, political communication, organisational studies and education.
