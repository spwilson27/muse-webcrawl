Single-photon avalanche diode

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) is a solid-state photodetector based on a p–n junction reverse-biased at a voltage above its breakdown voltage — so-called Geiger mode operation. In this regime, a single photo-generated charge carrier can trigger a self-sustaining avalanche multiplication that produces a large, standardised electrical pulse, allowing individual photons to be detected and counted.

Operating principle

An ordinary avalanche photodiode is biased below breakdown and gives a gain proportional to the light level. A SPAD is instead biased a few volts above the breakdown voltage. The junction then sits in a metastable state: when a carrier — created by an absorbed photon or, undesirably, by thermal generation — enters the high-field region, impact ionisation triggers a macroscopic avalanche current within tens of picoseconds. The avalanche is stopped by a quenching circuit that lowers the bias below breakdown, after which the device is recharged; the interval during which the diode is insensitive is called the dead time. The output is a digital pulse whose leading edge marks the photon's arrival with timing jitter that can be as low as tens of picoseconds.

Performance parameters

  • Photon detection efficiency (PDE) — the probability that an incident photon produces a counted pulse; silicon SPADs can exceed 50–70 percent in the visible, while InGaAs devices serve the near-infrared telecom bands with lower efficiency.
  • Dark count rate — spurious pulses from thermally generated carriers; reduced by cooling and by small active areas.
  • Afterpulsing — carriers trapped during an avalanche and released later cause correlated false counts.
  • Timing jitter — uncertainty in the measured arrival time of the photon.
  • Crosstalk — in arrays, photons emitted during one avalanche can trigger neighbouring pixels.

SPAD arrays and applications

Because SPADs can be fabricated in standard CMOS processes, large arrays (image sensors) of SPAD pixels, each with its own timing electronics, have been developed. Applications include time-of-flight (ToF) ranging and LiDAR — including the direct-ToF sensors in consumer devices — fluorescence-lifetime imaging microscopy (FLIM), time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC), quantum key distribution and quantum optics, positron emission tomography (in the form of silicon photomultipliers, which are arrays of microcell SPADs), Raman spectroscopy and astronomy.
