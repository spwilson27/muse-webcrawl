Charbagh

A charbagh (Persian: چارباغ, chahār bāgh, "four gardens") is a Persian and Islamic quadrilateral garden layout based on the four gardens of Paradise described in the Quran. The quadrants are divided by walkways or flowing water and represent the four elements (earth, air, fire, water), the four Zoroastrian sacred elements, or the four rivers of paradise.

The charbagh of the Taj Mahal, divided by raised walkways and water channels.

Origins

The concept originated in Achaemenid Persia: the palace gardens of Cyrus the Great at Pasargadae (c. 6th century BCE) were laid out geometrically with stone water channels, the earliest surviving evidence of the fourfold plan. The form spread across the Islamic world with Persian culture, from the Alhambra and the Generalife in Spain to Timurid Samarkand and Bukhara.

In South Asia

The Mughal emperors made the charbagh a defining feature of their gardens in India and Pakistan. Babur, nostalgic for the gardens of Kabul and Samarkand, built charbaghs at Agra and elsewhere. The form reached its monumental peak in tomb gardens — Humayun's Tomb in Delhi and the Taj Mahal at Agra, where the fourfold garden fronts the mausoleum beside the Yamuna — and in pleasure gardens such as the Shalimar Gardens of Lahore and Kashmir.

Symbolism and design

The intersecting water channels stand for the Quranic rivers of water, milk, wine and honey flowing through paradise (jannah). Raised walkways, central pavilions or mausolea, and terraced levels adapted the scheme to sloping sites, as in Kashmir's hillside gardens. The geometric order was understood as an earthly image of divine order imposed on the wilderness.
