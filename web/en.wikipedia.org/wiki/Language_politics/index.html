Language politics

Language politics is the field of political decisions, laws and public debates concerning which languages are used, recognised, promoted or suppressed within a society or state. It is closely related to language planning and sociolinguistics, and it shapes questions of national identity, education, administration and minority rights.

Key issues

Language politics typically turns on a small set of recurring decisions:

  • Official status — which languages governments use for law, courts and administration.
  • Medium of instruction — which language or languages are used in schools and universities.
  • Script and spelling reform — state-directed changes such as Turkey's 1928 adoption of the Latin alphabet, or Kazakhstan's planned transition from Cyrillic.
  • Standardisation — the selection of one dialect as the national standard, as with standard Mandarin in China or Bahasa Indonesia in Indonesia.
  • Minority-language rights — guarantees for regional or indigenous languages, such as those in the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages.

Examples

In India, language politics produced the States Reorganisation Act of 1956, which redrew state boundaries along linguistic lines, and recurring disputes over the status of Hindi versus English and the state languages. In Canada, the Official Languages Act of 1969 made English and French co-official federally, while Quebec's Charter of the French Language (Bill 101) promotes French within the province. In Belgium, the division between Dutch-speaking Flanders and French-speaking Wallonia structures the country's federal system. In Sri Lanka, the Sinhala Only Act of 1956 is widely cited as a factor in the island's later civil conflict.

Language politics also includes bottom-up movements: language revival for Irish, Welsh, Basque and Catalan; campaigns for the recognition of sign languages; and debates over gendered language, spelling reform and the global spread of English.
