Okazaki fragments

Okazaki fragments are short sequences of DNA nucleotides that are synthesized discontinuously on the lagging strand during DNA replication and later linked together to form a continuous strand. They are named after the Japanese molecular biologists Reiji Okazaki and Tsuneko Okazaki, who with colleagues first demonstrated their existence in 1968.

Mechanism

Because DNA polymerase can synthesize DNA only in the 5' to 3' direction, the strand whose template runs the "wrong" way relative to fork movement — the lagging strand — must be built in short segments, each initiated by an RNA primer laid down by primase. In bacteria the fragments are roughly 1,000–2,000 nucleotides long; in eukaryotes about 100–200 nucleotides, approximately the length of DNA in a nucleosome. The RNA primers are removed by DNA polymerase I (in bacteria) or by flap-processing enzymes (in eukaryotes), the gaps are filled with DNA, and the nicks are sealed by DNA ligase.

Significance

The discovery resolved a puzzle posed by the antiparallel structure of DNA: how one of the two strands could be replicated while the fork moves in a single direction. The discontinuous model was confirmed by experiments showing that newly synthesized DNA briefly exists as small pieces before being joined into chromosomal-length molecules.
