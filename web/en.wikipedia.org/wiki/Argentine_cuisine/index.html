Argentine cuisine is the culinary tradition of Argentina, shaped by indigenous peoples, Spanish colonial settlement, and large waves of Italian and Spanish immigration in the 19th and 20th centuries. It is best known for its beef-centered cooking, above all the asado, a social barbecue ritual central to Argentine culture.

Main dishes

The asado features cuts such as vacío, entraña, chorizo, morcilla, and provoleta grilled over wood or charcoal, often accompanied by chimichurri sauce. Empanadas — baked or fried pastries with regional fillings — are ubiquitous, with famous variants from Salta, Tucumán, and Mendoza. Other staples include the milanesa (breaded cutlet, often served a la napolitana), locro (a hearty corn and bean stew eaten on national holidays), humita, and choripán.

Italian influence

Italian immigration brought pasta and pizza to the center of the Argentine table: tallarines, ñoquis (gnocchi, traditionally eaten on the 29th of the month), sorrentinos, and a thick, cheesy Buenos Aires-style pizza served with fainá (chickpea flatbread).

Desserts and drinks

Dulce de leche is the national sweet, found in alfajores, pancakes, and ice cream. Argentina is a major wine producer, famed for Malbec from Mendoza. The infused drink mate, shared from a gourd through a metal straw, is a daily social ritual.
