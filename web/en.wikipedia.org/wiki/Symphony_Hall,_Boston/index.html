Symphony Hall is a concert hall located at 301 Massachusetts Avenue in Boston, Massachusetts, widely regarded as one of the finest concert halls in the world for its acoustics. Designed by the architectural firm McKim, Mead and White, it was built in 1900 for the Boston Symphony Orchestra, which continues to perform there, as does the Boston Pops Orchestra.

Acoustics

Symphony Hall was the first concert hall designed using quantitative acoustical science. Harvard physics professor Wallace Clement Sabine, the founder of architectural acoustics, calculated the hall's reverberation time and shaped its "shoebox" design — long, narrow and high, modelled on the Neues Gewandhaus in Leipzig — to achieve a reverberation time of about 1.9 seconds with the hall occupied. It is regularly ranked alongside Vienna's Musikverein and Amsterdam's Concertgebouw among the world's best halls.

Building

The hall is in the Italian Renaissance Revival style, of brick and limestone. Sixteen replicas of classical statues line the upper walls; the stage floor stages were reportedly built on rubber pads to isolate vibration. Its 4,800-pipe Aeolian-Skinner organ, installed in 1949, is considered one of the finest concert organs in the United States. The hall was designated a U.S. National Historic Landmark in 1999.

History

The Boston Symphony, founded in 1881 by Henry Lee Higginson, had outgrown the old Boston Music Hall by the 1890s. Ground was broken in 1899 and the new hall opened on October 15, 1900. The Boston Pops, founded in 1885, also performs there in the spring season, when the orchestra seats are replaced by café tables.
