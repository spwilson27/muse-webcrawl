Varroa destructor

KingdomAnimalia
PhylumArthropoda
ClassArachnida
OrderMesostigmata
FamilyVarroidae
SpeciesV. destructor

Varroa destructor, commonly known as the Varroa mite, is an external parasitic mite that attacks and feeds on honey bees. It is one of the most damaging pests of the western honey bee (Apis mellifera) and is a major factor in global honey bee colony losses. The mite originally parasitized the eastern honey bee (Apis cerana) in Asia and switched hosts to A. mellifera in the 20th century, spreading worldwide through the movement of infested colonies.

Biology and life cycle

The reddish-brown female mite is about 1.1 mm long and 1.6 mm wide, with a flattened oval body adapted to clinging to bees. A foundress mite enters a brood cell shortly before it is capped and lays eggs that develop alongside the growing bee; the mites feed on the bee's fat body tissue. Mature female mites emerge with the adult bee and disperse to other bees and colonies. Because drone brood remains capped longer, mites preferentially reproduce in drone cells.

Impact

Heavy infestations weaken bees, shorten their lifespan, and deform wings. Critically, Varroa mites transmit and amplify several bee viruses, notably deformed wing virus, turning otherwise benign infections into colony-killing epidemics. Untreated colonies of A. mellifera typically collapse within two to three years of infestation.

Control

Beekeepers manage Varroa with acaricides such as amitraz and synthetic pyrethroids (though resistance is widespread), organic acids such as formic and oxalic acid, thymol, mechanical methods such as drone brood removal and screened bottom boards, and breeding of mite-resistant bee stock, including selection for Varroa-sensitive hygiene behaviour.
