Horse-drawn vehicle

A horse-drawn vehicle is a conveyance pulled by one or more horses. Such vehicles were the principal means of wheeled land transport for people and goods for several thousand years, from the war chariots of antiquity until they were largely displaced by motor vehicles in the early 20th century.

Types

Vehicles for passengers include carriages, coaches, cabriolets, hansom cabs, landaus, victorias, broughams and sleighs. Working vehicles include wagons, carts, drays, stagecoaches used for freight, and agricultural implements adapted for draught.

Harness and construction

A vehicle is connected to the horse by a harness of leather straps, with shafts or a pole transmitting the pull, and a collar or breastband distributing the load. Early improvements included the pivoting front axle for steering and, later, steel springs and rubber tyres for comfort.

Decline and survival

Mass motorisation after 1900 ended the era of horse transport in most cities, but horse-drawn vehicles survive in tourism, ceremony, sport (combined driving), and in communities such as the Amish that restrict motor vehicle use.
