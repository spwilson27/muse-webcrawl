The A5 is a major primary route in England and Wales running from London to the ferry port of Holyhead on Anglesey. This article covers the English section; the road continues through Wales as the A5 road (Wales). For much of its length it closely follows the Roman Watling Street, and its rebuilding in the early 19th century by Thomas Telford as the London–Holyhead mail coach road was one of the great civil engineering projects of its age.

Route (England)

The road begins at Marble Arch in central London as Edgware Road, running north-west through Kilburn, Cricklewood and Edgware. Leaving London it passes through Elstree and Radlett to St Albans, then Dunstable — where it crosses the Chiltern Hills near the Dunstable Downs — and on to Milton Keynes, which it skirts as a dual carriageway. North of Milton Keynes the A5 continues through Towcester, Weedon Bec and Rugby, then passes Hinckley, Atherstone (where it follows the line of Watling Street especially closely), Tamworth and Lichfield. West of Cannock it passes through Gailey and onwards to Telford and Shrewsbury, where the English section ends at the Welsh border near Nescliffe. The modern M6 motorway and the M1 motorway have taken much of its long-distance traffic.

History

Watling Street was one of Roman Britain's principal roads, running from Richborough and London to Wroxeter and on into Wales. In 1815 Parliament commissioned Thomas Telford to rebuild the London–Holyhead road to speed the mail between London and Dublin, then a vital link in the United Kingdom. Telford's road straightened gradients, rebuilt bridges and provided an even macadam surface; his tollhouses and milestones survive along the route. The road was given the number A5 in the first British road numbering scheme of 1922–23.

During the 20th century numerous bypasses were built, and the opening of the M1 (1959) and M6 (from 1958) progressively relieved the A5 of through traffic between London, the Midlands and the North West. Nevertheless, substantial stretches remain primary routes, and sections in the Midlands form part of the strategic road network managed by National Highways.
