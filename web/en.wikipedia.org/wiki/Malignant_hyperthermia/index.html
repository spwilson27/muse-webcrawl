Malignant hyperthermia

Malignant hyperthermia (MH) is a severe, potentially fatal reaction to certain drugs used for general anaesthesia — principally the volatile inhalational agents (such as halothane, sevoflurane, and desflurane) and the depolarising muscle relaxant succinylcholine — in genetically susceptible individuals. It occurs in roughly 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 250,000 anaesthetics.

Mechanism and signs

In susceptible people, exposure triggers uncontrolled release of calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum of skeletal muscle, most often due to variants in the ryanodine receptor gene RYR1 (and less often CACNA1S). The result is sustained muscle contraction and a hypermetabolic state: rapidly rising end-tidal carbon dioxide, tachycardia, muscle rigidity (classically masseter spasm), hyperthermia, acidosis, hyperkalaemia, and rhabdomyolysis. Rising temperature is often a late sign despite the condition's name.

Treatment

Management is an emergency: stop the triggering agent, call for help, hyperventilate with 100% oxygen, and give the specific antidote dantrolene (2.5 mg/kg IV, repeated as needed), which blocks calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Active cooling, correction of hyperkalaemia and acidosis, and intensive-care monitoring follow. Before dantrolene's introduction in the late 1970s, mortality exceeded 70%; with prompt treatment it has fallen below 5%.

Susceptibility and testing

MH susceptibility is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern and is linked with myopathies such as central core disease and King–Denborough syndrome. Diagnosis is by genetic testing or the in-vitro contracture test on a fresh muscle biopsy. A similar syndrome occurs in stress-susceptible pigs (porcine stress syndrome), historically important in identifying the genetic basis.
