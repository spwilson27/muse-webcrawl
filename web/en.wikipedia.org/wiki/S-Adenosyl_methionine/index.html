S-Adenosyl methionine

S-Adenosyl methionine (SAM, SAMe)
Chemical typeSulfonium compound; co-substrate
Derived fromMethionine + ATP
Main rolesMethyl donor in transmethylation; aminopropyl donor; radical chemistry
Discovered1952, by Giulio Cantoni

S-Adenosyl methionine (SAM, also written SAMe and AdoMet) is a common co-substrate involved in methyl group transfers, transsulfurations and aminopropylations. It is synthesised in the cell from the amino acid methionine and ATP by the enzyme methionine adenosyltransferase, and is found in virtually all living cells.

Biochemistry

SAM's positively charged sulfonium centre makes its methyl group highly reactive. More than 40 metabolic reactions transfer that methyl group to substrates such as nucleic acids, proteins, lipids and small molecules, catalysed by methyltransferase enzymes. Methylation of DNA and histones by SAM-dependent enzymes is a central mechanism of epigenetic regulation. After donating its methyl group, SAM becomes S-adenosyl homocysteine, which is recycled through the methionine cycle.

SAM is also the precursor for polyamine synthesis (via decarboxylated SAM, which donates aminopropyl groups) and the key substrate of radical SAM enzymes, a large superfamily that uses SAM to generate 5′-deoxyadenosyl radicals for difficult chemical transformations.

Medicine and supplements

SAMe is sold as a dietary supplement in the United States and as a prescription drug in some countries. It has been studied for depression, osteoarthritis and liver disease; evidence of benefit is mixed and generally modest. Because the molecule is unstable, commercial products use stabilised salts such as the butanedisulfonate.
