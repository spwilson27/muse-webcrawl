Amazigh

Amazigh (plural Imazighen) redirects here. The Amazigh peoples, commonly known historically as Berbers, are a diverse grouping of distinct ethnic groups indigenous to North Africa who predate the historic Arab migrations to the Maghreb. Their lands, called Tamazgha, stretch from the Siwa Oasis in Egypt westward to the Canary Islands and from the Mediterranean Sea to the Niger River.

Language

The Amazigh languages (collectively Tamazight) form a branch of the Afroasiatic language family, including Tarifit, Kabyle, Central Atlas Tamazight, Tachelhit and Tuareg varieties. They are written in three scripts: the ancient Tifinagh alphabet, the Latin alphabet, and the Arabic script. Tamazight gained official status in Morocco's 2011 constitution and in Algeria in 2016.

History

Amazigh kingdoms such as Numidia and Mauretania flourished in antiquity; figures such as Masinissa, Jugurtha and Juba II are part of the historical record, as is resistance to Rome by Tacfarinas and to the Arab conquest by leaders including Kusaila and the queen Dihya (al-Kahina). In the Middle Ages, Amazigh dynasties — the Almoravids, Almohads and Marinids — ruled much of the Maghreb and al-Andalus. Amazigh identity movements, from the 1980 "Berber Spring" in Algeria onward, have campaigned for language rights and cultural recognition, symbolised by the Amazigh flag adopted in 1997.
