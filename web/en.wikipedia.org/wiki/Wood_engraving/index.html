Wood engraving

Wood engraving is a printmaking technique in which an artist works an image into a block of wood. It is a relief printing technique, but unlike the woodcut, the image is engraved on the end grain of a hard wood — usually boxwood — using a burin (graver), the tool of metal engravers. The surface is cut away except for the raised printing areas; fine white lines and tonal effects are produced by varying the cuts.

History

The technique was developed and perfected by the English artist Thomas Bewick in the late 18th century. Because an engraved block is type-high, illustrations could be printed together with movable type in the same press run, making wood engraving the dominant method of book and newspaper illustration throughout the 19th century. Large workshops in London, Paris, and New York employed engravers to reproduce artists' drawings on blocks, sometimes assembled from several bolted pieces so multiple engravers could work at once.

Technique

The engraver cuts into the polished end grain with a variety of burins: gravers for fine lines, spitstickers for curved lines, and tint tools for parallel-line textures. Areas that print black are left uncut; areas that print white are cut deepest. The technique allows extremely fine detail and rich tonal gradations, but the block size is limited by the diameter of the boxwood tree.

Decline and revival

Photomechanical reproduction (halftone and line blocks) displaced wood engraving for commercial illustration by the 1890s. The technique survived as a fine art medium in the 20th century, notably in the work of artists such as Eric Gill, Robert Gibbings, and Clare Leighton, and through societies such as the Society of Wood Engravers (founded 1920).
