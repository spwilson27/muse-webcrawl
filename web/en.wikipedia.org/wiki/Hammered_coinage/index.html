Hammered coinage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hammered coinage is the most common form of coin produced from the invention of coinage in the 7th century BCE until the development of modern coin-minting machinery in the 16th and 17th centuries. Coins were struck by hand: a metal blank (flan or planchet) was placed on a fixed lower die (anvil die) set in a block, the upper die (trussel) was placed on top, and a heavy hammer blow drove the design into both faces of the blank.

Characteristics

Because each strike was manual, hammered coins vary greatly in centring, weight and completeness of the design; weak, crooked or double strikes are common. The irregular edges left the coinage vulnerable to clipping — shaving metal from the rim — which eventually prompted governments to adopt milled (machine-made) coinage with lettered or reeded edges. In England the hammered series ended with the Great Recoinage of 1696, following the introduction of milled coinage under Charles II from 1662.

Process

Blanks were cut or cast from sheets or rods of precious or base metal, adjusted to weight (sometimes by filing, leaving adjustment marks), heated in some traditions, then positioned by hand with tongs or fingers. A skilled moneyer could strike many hundreds of coins a day, but dies wore and cracked quickly and had to be cut by hand with punches.

Collecting

Hammered coins, from Greek and Roman issues to medieval pennies and early modern crowns, form a major field of numismatics; their hand-made character means individual dies can be identified and die-studies used to reconstruct mint output.
