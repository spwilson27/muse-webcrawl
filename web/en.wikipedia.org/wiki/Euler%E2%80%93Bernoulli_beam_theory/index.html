Euler–Bernoulli beam theory

Euler–Bernoulli beam theory (also known as engineer's beam theory or classical beam theory) is a simplification of the linear theory of elasticity which provides a means of calculating the load-carrying and deflection characteristics of beams. Developed from work by Leonhard Euler and Daniel Bernoulli in the 18th century, it remains a cornerstone of structural and mechanical engineering.

Assumptions and equation

The theory assumes that plane cross-sections of the beam remain plane and perpendicular to the beam's neutral axis after deformation, and that the beam is slender. The governing relation links the bending moment M to the curvature of the deflection curve: EI d²w/dx² = M(x), where E is Young's modulus, I is the second moment of area and w is the transverse deflection. Differentiating leads to the fourth-order Euler–Bernoulli beam equation relating applied load to deflection.

Applications and limits

The theory accurately predicts deflections and stresses for long, slender beams under bending, and underlies the design of bridges, buildings and machine elements. For short, deep beams, shear deformation becomes significant and the more general Timoshenko–Ehrenfest beam theory is used instead.
