Queen Anne style architecture in the United States

Queen Anne style architecture was one of a number of popular Victorian architectural styles that emerged in the United States during the period from roughly 1880 to 1910. In America the style is distinct from the British Queen Anne Revival, drawing instead on a free mix of medieval, Renaissance, and vernacular sources promoted by architects such as Richard Norman Shaw in England and popularized in the United States through pattern books, magazines, and the building press.

The style is characterized by asymmetrical façades, dominant front-facing gables, steep and irregular rooflines, towers and turrets, wraparound porches, and a rich variety of surface textures achieved with patterned shingles, contrasting siding, and applied ornament—sometimes called the "gingerbread" trim associated with the Eastlake movement.

Characteristics

  • Asymmetrical façade with a dominant, steeply pitched, irregular roof
  • Front-facing gable, often decorated with patterned shingles
  • Round, square, or polygonal towers and turrets
  • Partial or full-width wraparound porches
  • Varied wall textures: clapboard, shingles, brick, and stone combined
  • Bay windows, oriels, spindlework, and turned porch posts
  • Leaded and stained glass, especially in upper sash and transom windows

History

The American Queen Anne style arrived from Britain, where Shaw and others had developed a "Free Renaissance" manner for country and suburban houses. It was showcased at the Philadelphia Centennial Exposition of 1876, where British government buildings in the style attracted wide attention. Publications such as The American Architect and Building News spread the vocabulary rapidly. The style benefited from the balloon frame, mass-produced millwork, and railway distribution of cheap ornament, which allowed even modest builders to assemble picturesque houses from catalogue parts.

Leading American practitioners included firms in Boston, Chicago, and San Francisco; the Carson Mansion in Eureka, California (1884–1886), built by newsprint magnate William Carson, is often cited as one of the most lavish surviving examples. George F. Barber of Knoxville, Tennessee, sold Queen Anne designs by mail order across the country.

Decline and legacy

By about 1900–1910 the style fell from fashion, displaced by the Colonial Revival, Craftsman, and other early twentieth-century modes. Large numbers of Queen Anne houses survive in American suburbs and small towns, and many are listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The exuberant, colorful painting schemes of surviving examples inspired the "Painted Ladies" of San Francisco.
