Tooth fairy

The tooth fairy is a fantasy figure of early childhood in many Western and Western-influenced cultures. Tradition holds that when a child loses a deciduous (baby) tooth, they place it under their pillow or in a glass beside the bed before sleep, and the tooth fairy visits during the night to exchange the tooth for a small payment — traditionally a coin.

Origins

The modern American tooth fairy crystallised in the early 20th century, appearing in print in a 1908 Chicago newspaper column and popularised by Esther Watkins Arnold's 1927 play The Tooth Fairy. It draws on older customs: in medieval Europe children's teeth were buried, burned or fed to animals to prevent witchcraft or ensure strong adult teeth; Norse tradition knew a tandfé ("tooth fee"); and in France and Spain a small mouse — La Petite Souris, or Ratoncito Pérez in Spain — performs the exchange.

Function and belief

Folklorists and child psychologists note the ritual eases children's anxiety about the sometimes alarming experience of losing a tooth and marks a rite of passage. Surveys of parents show the "going rate" per tooth is tracked as a light-hearted economic indicator in some countries. Like Santa Claus and the Easter Bunny, the tooth fairy is one of the few imaginary figures commonly presented to children as real.
