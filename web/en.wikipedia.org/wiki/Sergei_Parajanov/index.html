Sergei Parajanov (Armenian: Սերգեյ Փարաջանով; Georgian: სერგო ფარაჯანოვი; Russian: Серге́й Ио́сифович Параджа́нов; born Sarkis Hovsepi Parajaniants; 9 January 1924 – 20 July 1990) was a Soviet film director and artist of Armenian descent who made significant contributions to Soviet cinematography through Ukrainian, Armenian, and Georgian cinema. He invented his own cinematic style, owing little to the dominant socialist realism of the Soviet Union.

Parajanov is regarded by many as one of the most original and influential filmmakers of the 20th century. Directors and critics including Federico Fellini, Andrei Tarkovsky, and Michelangelo Antonioni praised his work, while Jean-Luc Godard remarked that "in the temple of cinema there are images, light and reality. Sergei Parajanov was the master of that temple."

Early life

Parajanov was born Sarkis Parajaniants to Armenian parents Iosif Parajaniants and Siranush Bejanyan in Tiflis (now Tbilisi), Georgia, then part of the Soviet Union. The city was home to a large Armenian community, and the young Parajanov was exposed to a rich mixture of Armenian, Georgian, Russian, and other cultures that would later infuse his films. He studied at the Gerasimov Institute of Cinematography (VGIK) in Moscow under Ukrainian director Alexander Dovzhenko, graduating in 1952.

Career

After graduating, Parajanov worked at the Dovzhenko Film Studios in Kiev, directing several documentaries and feature films in the approved socialist realist style, works he later disowned. His breakthrough came with Shadows of Forgotten Ancestors (1965), based on the novel by Mykhailo Kotsiubynsky, which depicted the culture of the Hutsul people of the Carpathian Mountains with exuberant camera movement and ethnographic detail. The film won international acclaim and made him a major figure in world cinema.

His next major film, The Color of Pomegranates (1969), made at Armenfilm, was a stylized biography of the 18th-century Armenian poet Sayat-Nova. Rather than a conventional narrative, the film presents a series of tableaux evoking the poet's inner life. Soviet authorities, frustrated by its experimental form, shelved the film and had it re-edited by Sergei Yutkevich without Parajanov's consent.

Imprisonment and later work

Parajanov's outspokenness, unconventional lifestyle, and support for Ukrainian national culture drew the hostility of Soviet authorities. In 1973 he was arrested on charges widely regarded as trumped up and sentenced to five years in a labor camp in Ukraine and Siberia. Numerous artists and intellectuals worldwide campaigned for his release, and he was freed in 1977, a year before his sentence ended. Barred from filmmaking for years, he returned in the 1980s with The Legend of the Suram Fortress (1985) and Ashik Kerib (1988), both made in Georgia.

Death and legacy

Parajanov died of lung cancer in Yerevan in 1990. The Sergei Parajanov Museum opened in Yerevan in 1991, housing his collages, drawings, and dolls. His films continue to be celebrated at retrospectives worldwide, and his house-museum remains one of Armenia's most popular cultural sites.
