Konzo

Konzo
SpecialtyNeurology
SymptomsSudden-onset spastic paraparesis (leg weakness and stiffness)
CauseChronic cyanide exposure from insufficiently processed bitter cassava, with protein-deficient diet
RegionsSub-Saharan Africa (DR Congo, Mozambique, Tanzania, others)

Konzo is an irreversible neurological disease causing sudden-onset spastic paralysis of the legs. It occurs in epidemic form among poor rural communities in sub-Saharan Africa that rely heavily on bitter cassava as a staple food. The name comes from the Yaka language of the Congo, meaning "tied legs".

Cause

Konzo is caused by chronic dietary exposure to cyanide released from cyanogenic glucosides (mainly linamarin) in bitter cassava that has not been adequately processed—especially during droughts, when processing is shortened and diets become almost exclusively cassava-based, lacking the sulphur-containing amino acids needed to detoxify cyanide to thiocyanate. Outbreaks disproportionately affect women of childbearing age and children, and typically follow food shortages.

Clinical features and prevention

The disease begins abruptly, often overnight, with symmetrical spastic weakness of the legs; in severe cases the arms, speech, and vision may be affected. There is no cure, though rehabilitation can help. Prevention centres on safer cassava processing—such as the "wetting method" of flour treatment, which greatly reduces cyanide content—along with dietary diversification and promoting low-cyanide cassava varieties. Konzo was first described in the Congo in the 1930s and major epidemics were documented in Mozambique (1981) and Tanzania (1985).
