The Royal Institute of British Architects (RIBA) is a professional body for architects primarily in the United Kingdom, but also internationally, founded to advance architecture and promote knowledge of the arts and sciences associated with it. Members use the post-nominal letters RIBA, and chartered status is a widely recognised mark of professional standing.

History

The institute was founded in 1834 as the Institute of British Architects in London, receiving its Royal Charter in 1837 under William IV; Queen Victoria granted the "Royal" prefix in 1866. In 1934 it moved to its present headquarters at 66 Portland Place, designed by George Grey Wornum. Statutory regulation of the title "architect" in the UK is handled by the Architects Registration Board (since 1997), while RIBA validates architecture courses and sets professional standards.

Activities and awards

RIBA administers prestigious awards including the Royal Gold Medal (given annually on the monarch's behalf since 1848), the Stirling Prize for the best new British building (since 1996), and the RIBA International Prize. Its library and collections — including the RIBA Drawings Collection, much of it held jointly with the V&A at V&A+RIBA Architecture Gallery — form one of the world's great architectural archives. The RIBA Plan of Work is a standard framework for building design and construction processes in the UK.
