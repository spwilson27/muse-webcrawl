Airship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A large rigid airship over a landscape in the 1930s style]
A rigid airship in flight.

An airship (dirigible) is a lighter-than-air aircraft that can navigate under its own power, using a large gas envelope filled with a lifting gas — historically hydrogen, today almost always helium. Airships range from small advertising blimps to the vast rigid Zeppelins of the early 20th century, whose transatlantic passenger services ended after the Hindenburg disaster of 1937.

Types

Rigid airships have an internal framework (Zeppelins, R100); semi-rigids use a partial keel; non-rigid blimps keep their shape by gas pressure alone (Goodyear blimps). Lifting gas provides buoyancy, engines drive propellers for thrust, and fins with rudders and elevators steer.

Golden age

Germany\'s Zeppelins bombed in the First World War and then carried passengers in luxury; the Graf Zeppelin circumnavigated the globe in 1929. The British R101 crash (1930) and the American Akron and Macon losses (1933, 1935) thinned the fleets before Hindenburg ended the era.

Modern use

Blimps persist for advertising, camera platforms and research; companies are developing hybrid airships combining buoyant lift with aerodynamic lift for cargo to remote areas, and the modern Zeppelin NT flies sightseeing tours in Germany.
