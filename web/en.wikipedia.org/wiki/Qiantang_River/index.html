The Qiantang River, earlier known in English as the Tsientang River, is the largest river in Zhejiang Province, China. Rising near Mount Liugang in Anhui, it flows about 459 km northeast through Hangzhou — where its upper course, called the Fuchun River, and the connected Xin'an River and Qiandao Lake are celebrated scenic areas — before emptying into Hangzhou Bay on the East China Sea. The river and bay are famous for the world's largest tidal bore, a wave up to 9 metres (30 ft) high that races upstream at up to 40 km/h.

The tidal bore

The funnel shape of Hangzhou Bay, combined with the riverbed's rapid narrowing and shallowing, amplifies the incoming tide into a steep-fronted bore (涌潮). The bore is at its most spectacular around the Mid-Autumn Festival (the 18th day of the 8th lunar month), when crowds have gathered to watch it since at least the Song dynasty; poets such as Su Dongpo celebrated the spectacle, and watching the tide became one of the classic sights of Hangzhou. The bore is dangerous: spectators are periodically swept away, and engineered seawalls along the estuary — built and rebuilt since the era of the Wuyue king Qian Liu, legendary "King of the Sea" — protect the low-lying delta from tidal flooding.

Geography and use

The Qiantang drains about 55,600 km². Its main tributaries include the Heng River, the Puyang River and the Cao'e River. Six bridges cross the river at Hangzhou, including the Qiantang River Bridge (1937), the first modern bridge designed by a Chinese engineer, Mao Yisheng, which was blown up during the Second Sino-Japanese War and later rebuilt. The river provides drinking water to Hangzhou, transport, and a name for the Qiantang tidal-bore surfing competitions held annually, in which surfers ride the bore for kilometres.
