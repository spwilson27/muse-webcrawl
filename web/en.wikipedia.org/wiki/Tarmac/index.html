Tarmac

Tarmac, short for tarmacadam, is a road surfacing material made by combining crushed stone (macadam) with tar. Patented by the English inventor Edgar Purnell Hooley in 1902, it improved on John Loudon McAdam's earlier macadam roads, whose loose stone surfaces were dusty, easily displaced and unsuited to motor traffic. The name is a portmanteau of tar and McAdam.

History

Hooley, a Nottinghamshire county surveyor, noticed in 1901 that a barrel of tar spilled at an ironworks had solidified the loose slag on a nearby road. He patented a process for mixing graded crushed stone with heated tar and founded the company that became Tarmac Limited. Tarmacadam was laid widely on roads in Britain and abroad in the early 20th century.

Usage of the term

Modern road surfaces are usually asphalt concrete — stone bound with bitumen rather than tar — but "tarmac" remains a generic term in British English for paved surfaces, and is used worldwide for airport aprons and runways ("the tarmac"), even where the actual material is asphalt or concrete. The word's use as a generic term has been the subject of trademark disputes, as Tarmac remains a registered name of the successor construction company.

Properties

Tar-bound surfaces are flexible and quick to lay but soften in heat and are more susceptible than asphalt to damage from spilled fuel and solvents, one reason tar was largely replaced by bitumen after the Second World War.
