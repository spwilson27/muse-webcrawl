Headframe

A headframe (gallows frame, winding tower, hoist frame, or headstock) is the structural frame above an underground mine shaft. The headframe supports a wheel or sheave on its top, over which the rope or cable that raises and lowers the cage or skip passes. The sheave is driven by a hoist or winder housed in a nearby engine house. Modern headframes are made of steel or concrete; in earlier times they were constructed of wood, and wooden headframes may still be seen at older or historical mines.

Structure

The headframe is a tower-like structure standing directly over the mine shaft. Its principal components include:

  • The sheave (or winding wheel), mounted at the top of the frame, which guides the hoisting rope from the vertical shaft to the hoist drum in the engine house.
  • The legs or supports, which carry the load of the sheave and the hoisting rope.
  • The backstays, inclined supports that resist the horizontal pull of the rope between the sheave and the winding engine.
  • The loading station or tipple at the base, where cages are loaded and unloaded.

Headframes are commonly of the four-post (or tower) type, the two-post type, or the A-frame type. In modern deep mines, concrete winding towers with the winding engine mounted at the top (tower-mounted winders) have largely replaced separate engine houses, allowing the ropes to run vertically from the drum to the shaft.

Function

The headframe's function is to support the sheave at a height sufficient for cages or skips to be raised clear of the shaft collar and for men and materials to be loaded and unloaded. In coal mining, the structure at the surface associated with the headframe, where coal is screened and loaded into wagons or trucks, is often called a tipple or colliery headgear.

Symbols of mining regions

Headframes have become prominent symbols of mining regions and communities. In many mining towns the headframe is the tallest structure and a landmark. Examples include the headframes of Butte, Montana, in the United States; the preserved winding towers of the Ruhr coalfield in Germany, where they form part of the Route der Industriekultur; and the headframes of the Witwatersrand goldfields around Johannesburg, South Africa. In German mining tradition, the headframe (Fördergerüst) is an emblem of the industry and frequently appears in regional heraldry and logos.

Notable headframes

The Big Muskie and silver mines aside, notable examples include:

  • The San José Mine headframe in Chile, used in the rescue of 33 miners in 2010.
  • The Zollern Colliery winding tower in Dortmund, Germany, an ornate brick structure often called a "castle of work".
  • The Clanwilliam and other deep-level gold mine headframes on the Witwatersrand, among the tallest in the world above shafts reaching nearly 4 kilometres deep.
