The ocellated turkey (Meleagris ocellata) is a species of turkey found in the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico, northern Belize and northern Guatemala. It is one of only two living turkey species, the other being the wild turkey (Meleagris gallopavo) of North America. It was formerly placed in its own genus, Agriocharis, but is now grouped with the wild turkey in Meleagris.

Description

The ocellated turkey is smaller than the wild turkey, with hens weighing about 3 kg and males about 5 kg. Its plumage is strikingly iridescent, with body feathers shimmering in bronze, green, blue and gold. The tail feathers have distinctive eye-spots (ocelli) — grey-blue tips ringed with black, gold and copper — which give the species its name. The head is bare and bright blue with orange-red nodules; males have a blue crown with wart-like growths and a pinkish snood. Both sexes have sharp spurs, and males lack the "beard" of bristles characteristic of the wild turkey.

Behaviour

Ocellated turkeys feed on the ground on seeds, leaves, fruits and insects, moving through tropical forest, savanna and agricultural clearings. During the breeding season (roughly February to April) males display and produce a distinctive song: beginning with low drumming-like notes and ending in a high-pitched gobbling phrase. Females lay 8–15 eggs in a shallow ground scrape.

Status

The species is listed as Near Threatened by the IUCN, owing to hunting for food and habitat loss. It is an important game bird for local communities and a sought-after species for international birdwatchers and turkey hunters, particularly around protected areas such as Tikal in Guatemala.
