Withholding tax

A withholding tax is an income tax that a payer of income is required to deduct (or "withhold") from a payment and remit directly to the government, rather than the recipient paying the tax later. The recipient is credited with the amount withheld against their total tax liability; if too much was withheld, a refund is due, and if too little, a further payment is required. Withholding is the principal collection mechanism for wage income in most developed tax systems.

Types

Employment income

Employers withhold tax from salaries and wages under systems such as PAYE (Pay As You Earn) in the United Kingdom and Ireland, and federal/state payroll withholding in the United States. In the US, employees indicate allowances and filing status on Form W-4, and employers report wages and tax withheld annually on Form W-2. The current US system of federal income tax withholding dates from the Current Tax Payment Act of 1943, introduced to fund the Second World War effort.

Payments to non-residents

Many countries impose withholding taxes on dividends, interest and royalties paid to foreign recipients. The United States generally imposes a statutory 30% rate on such "FDAP" income paid to non-residents, frequently reduced by income tax treaties; the EU Interest and Royalties Directive eliminates withholding between associated EU companies. The US FATCA regime (2010) added a 30% withholding threat to enforce reporting by foreign financial institutions.

Other payments

Withholding may also apply to gambling winnings, pension distributions, backup withholding where a payee fails to supply a taxpayer identification number, and to sales of real property by foreign persons (e.g. FIRPTA withholding in the US).

Rationale and criticism

Withholding raises compliance dramatically — wage income subject to withholding and third-party reporting is reported at rates near 99% in the US, compared with far lower rates for self-employment income — because the tax is collected before the taxpayer ever receives the money, and it smooths government cash flow through the year. Critics argue that it reduces the salience of taxation ("fiscal illusion"), and that systematic over-withholding amounts to an interest-free loan from taxpayers to the government.
