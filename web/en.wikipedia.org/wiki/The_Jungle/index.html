The Jungle

Author: Upton Sinclair
Published: 1906
Genre: Novel, muckraking fiction
Publisher: Doubleday, Page & Co.

The Jungle is a 1906 novel by the American journalist and novelist Upton Sinclair (1878–1968). Serialized in the socialist newspaper Appeal to Reason in 1905 and published in book form the following year, it depicts the harsh conditions and exploited lives of immigrants in Chicago's meatpacking district, Packingtown, at the turn of the twentieth century.

Plot

The novel follows Jurgis Rudkus, a Lithuanian immigrant who comes to Chicago with his family full of hope. Working in the stockyards, he encounters injury, corruption, wage theft, and the grinding machinery of industrial capitalism. One by one the family is destroyed: his wife Ona dies in childbirth, his son drowns in a muddy street, and Jurgis drifts through homelessness and petty crime before finding a measure of redemption in socialist politics.

Impact

Sinclair intended the book as an indictment of capitalism — he famously remarked, "I aimed at the public's heart, and by accident I hit it in the stomach." Public outrage focused instead on the novel's nauseating descriptions of unsanitary meat production, including passages about spoiled and contaminated meat. The scandal helped push President Theodore Roosevelt to sign the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act in 1906, landmarks of Progressive Era reform and foundations of modern American food regulation.

Legacy

The Jungle remains a canonical work of muckraking literature, widely taught in American schools, and a landmark in the history of both investigative journalism and the American labor movement.
