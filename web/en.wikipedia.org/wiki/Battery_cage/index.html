Battery cage

Battery cages in a laying facility

Battery cages are a housing system for egg-laying hens in intensive poultry farming. The name derives from the arrangement of identical cages in rows and tiers, like cells in a battery. A typical conventional cage houses several hens with a floor area per bird smaller than a sheet of A4 paper, a sloped wire floor for egg collection, and automated feeding and watering lines.

History

Cage systems for layers developed in the United States and Europe in the mid-20th century and became the dominant form of egg production because they separated birds from their droppings, reduced disease and feed waste, and allowed large flocks to be managed with little labour.

Welfare concerns

Animal welfare scientists and campaigners criticize conventional cages for preventing natural behaviours such as nesting, perching, dust-bathing, and wing-flapping, and for the associated problems of feather pecking, bone weakness, and frustration. The influential 1965 Brambell Report in Britain and later scientific reviews underpinned regulatory reform.

Regulation

The European Union banned conventional battery cages from 2012 under Council Directive 1999/74/EC, permitting only "enriched" or furnished cages with more space, a nest box, perches, and litter, or non-cage systems. Several U.S. states, including California, have enacted cage-free requirements, and many food companies have committed to cage-free egg sourcing.
