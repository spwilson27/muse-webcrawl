Luminous efficacy is a measure of how well a light source produces visible light. It is the ratio of luminous flux (in lumens) to power (in watts), and is expressed in lumens per watt (lm/W). Depending on context, the power may be the radiant flux of the source's output ("luminous efficacy of radiation") or the total electric power consumed by the source ("luminous efficacy of a source" or wall-plug efficacy).

The photopic luminosity function, defining the eye's sensitivity to each wavelength

Maximum efficacy

The maximum possible luminous efficacy of radiation is 683 lm/W, achieved by monochromatic light at 555 nm, the wavelength to which the human eye is most sensitive under bright (photopic) conditions. Real white-light sources achieve far less: incandescent lamps around 10–17 lm/W, fluorescent lamps 50–100 lm/W, and modern LED lamps commonly 100–200 lm/W or more, against a theoretical ceiling for high-quality white light of roughly 240–300 lm/W.

Luminous efficacy of radiation

The luminous efficacy of radiation measures only the fraction of electromagnetic power that is converted to light weighted by the luminosity function, ignoring losses in producing that radiation. It is computed by integrating the spectral power distribution against the CIE photopic luminosity function V(λ), scaled by the constant 683 lm/W.
