In electrical engineering, the maximum power transfer theorem states that, to obtain maximum external power from a power source with internal resistance, the resistance of the load must equal the resistance of the source as viewed from its output terminals. Moritz von Jacobi published the maximum power (transfer) law around 1840; it is also referred to as "Jacobi's law".

The theorem results in maximum power transfer from one part of a circuit to another, but not maximum efficiency in power transfer. If the resistance of the load is made larger than the resistance of the source, then efficiency is higher, since a higher percentage of the source power is transferred to the load, but the magnitude of the load power is lower since the total circuit resistance increases.

Explanation

A source of voltage V with series (internal) resistance RS drives a load resistance RL. The current is I = V / (RS + RL), and the power dissipated in the load is:

PL = I²RL = V²RL / (RS + RL)²

Differentiating with respect to RL and setting the derivative to zero shows that this power is maximised when RL = RS. At that point half the source voltage appears across the load and half across the source resistance, so the efficiency is exactly 50%.

Impedance matching

For alternating current circuits the theorem generalises: maximum average power is transferred when the load impedance is the complex conjugate of the source impedance. This principle, called impedance matching, is central to radio-frequency engineering, where transmission lines must be terminated in their characteristic impedance to avoid reflections. See also Thévenin's theorem.

Calculus-based proof

For a purely resistive circuit, P = V²RL/(RS + RL)². The derivative dP/dRL vanishes when (RS + RL)² − 2RL(RS + RL) = 0, which simplifies to RS = RL.
