Separatory funnel

Two immiscible liquids separated in a funnel.

A separatory funnel, also known as a separation funnel, extraction funnel, or colloquially a sep funnel, is a piece of laboratory glassware used in liquid–liquid extraction to separate the components of a mixture into two immiscible solvent phases of different densities — typically an aqueous phase and an organic phase. It is one of the most common tools in synthetic chemistry for working up reaction mixtures and purifying products.

Design

The funnel consists of a conical or pear-shaped glass body with a stopper at the top and a narrow stem at the bottom closed by a stopcock, made of ground glass or PTFE (Teflon). The conical (Squibb) form, with a gently sloping cone, allows the boundary between the layers to be identified precisely; the pear-shaped form provides more volume relative to its height. Sizes range from a few millilitres to several litres. Larger funnels are mounted in a support stand or ring clamp above a receiving vessel such as an Erlenmeyer flask or beaker.

Use

The two liquid phases and the mixture to be separated are added through the top, usually filling the funnel no more than two-thirds full. The stoppered funnel is inverted and shaken to mix the phases thoroughly, greatly increasing the surface area across which a dissolved substance can transfer from one phase to the other according to its partition coefficient. Shaking can generate pressure from volatile solvents or gas-forming reactions, so the funnel is vented frequently by opening the stopcock while inverted, pointing the stem away from people.

After shaking, the funnel is returned to its stand and the phases are allowed to separate completely. The stopper is removed, and the denser lower layer is drained through the stopcock into a receiving vessel, closing the stopcock just as the interface reaches it. The upper layer is poured out from the top to avoid contamination by droplets in the stem. A "wash" with brine (saturated sodium chloride solution) is commonly used to break emulsions and to draw water out of the organic layer before drying.

Theory

Extraction exploits differences in solubility: a compound distributes itself between two immiscible solvents in a fixed ratio, the distribution or partition coefficient. Because the fraction extracted depends on this ratio, several extractions with small volumes of solvent are more efficient than a single extraction with the same total volume. Acid–base extractions vary the pH to convert compounds between neutral and ionic forms, moving them selectively between organic and aqueous phases to separate acids, bases, and neutral substances.
