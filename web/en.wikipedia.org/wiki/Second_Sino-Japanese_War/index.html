The Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) was a military conflict fought between the Republic of China and the Empire of Japan. It made up the Chinese theatre of the wider Pacific War of the Second World War. The beginning of the war is conventionally dated to the Marco Polo Bridge Incident on 7 July 1937, when a dispute between Japanese and Chinese troops in Beijing escalated into a full-scale invasion.

After the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941, the war merged into the greater conflict of World War II as a major front in the Pacific Theatre. In China the conflict is known as the War of Resistance against Japan.

Background

Japan had steadily expanded its foothold in China since the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95). It annexed Korea in 1910, seized Manchuria after the Mukden Incident in 1931 and installed the puppet state of Manchukuo, and pushed into Jehol and northern China through the mid-1930s. Chinese resentment, boycotts of Japanese goods, and clashes between troops grew throughout the decade.

Course of the war

Japanese forces captured Beiping (Beijing), Shanghai (after a fierce three-month battle in 1937), and the capital Nanjing, where occupying troops committed the Nanjing Massacre against civilians and prisoners. The Nationalist government under Chiang Kai-shek retreated up the Yangtze to Chongqing. Major campaigns included the Chinese victory at Taierzhuang (1938), the battles of Wuhan and Changsha, and the deliberate breaching of Yellow River dikes in 1938, which flooded vast areas and caused enormous civilian deaths.

By 1939–41 the front stabilised: Japan controlled the coast and major eastern cities but could not subdue China's interior; guerrilla warfare by Communist forces from their base areas in the north expanded during the Hundred Regiments Offensive (1940) and subsequent Japanese "Three Alls" reprisal campaigns. In 1944 Japan launched Operation Ichi-Go, its largest offensive, seizing airfields in south China but failing to knock China out of the war.

End and casualties

Following the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet invasion of Manchuria in August 1945, Japan surrendered; the formal ceremony for Japanese forces in China took place in Nanjing on 9 September 1945. Chinese casualties were immense: estimates commonly cite 14 to 20 million Chinese dead, the great majority civilians, with millions more displaced. Japanese military deaths in China are estimated at over one million.

Legacy

The war devastated China, weakened the Nationalist government through inflation and exhaustion, and helped pave the way for the Communist victory in the resumed Chinese Civil War. Memory of the conflict remains central to modern Chinese nationalism and continues to affect Sino-Japanese relations.
