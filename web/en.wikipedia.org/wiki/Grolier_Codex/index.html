Grolier Codex

The Grolier Codex, now officially known as the Códice Maya de México, is a pre-Columbian Maya book and the oldest surviving manuscript from the Americas. It consists of eleven surviving pages (of an original twenty or so) of bark paper coated with gesso, folded accordion-style and painted on one side with a Venus almanac — astronomical tables tracking the planet Venus, accompanied by images of deities.

Discovery and authenticity debate

The codex surfaced in the 1960s, said to have been found by looters in a cave in Chiapas, Mexico, and was exhibited at the Grolier Club in New York in 1971, from which it took its former name. For decades many scholars suspected it was a forgery because of its unknown provenance and stylistic peculiarities. However, scientific analysis — including radiocarbon dating of the paper to the thirteenth century (around 1119–1259 CE on calibrated ranges reported by researchers) and study of its pigments, such as Maya blue, and iconography — progressively supported its authenticity. In 2016 a team of researchers pronounced it genuine, and in 2018 Mexico's National Institute of Anthropology and History officially recognised it as an authentic pre-Hispanic codex, renaming it the Códice Maya de México. It is held by the Museo Nacional de Antropología in Mexico City. Only three other Maya codices — the Dresden, Madrid and Paris codices — are universally accepted.
