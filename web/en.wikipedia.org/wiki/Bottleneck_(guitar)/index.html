Bottleneck (guitar)

Bottleneck guitar is a method of playing slide guitar in which the player wears the severed neck of a glass bottle — or a modern tube of glass, brass, steel or ceramic — on one finger of the fretting hand and glides it along the strings to produce continuous, vocal-like pitch bends. The technique is foundational to Delta blues and remains common in blues, country and rock.

[ Image: A guitarist's hand wearing a glass bottleneck slide over the ring finger ]

Origins

Slide playing in the American South developed in the early 20th century, influenced by Hawaiian guitar music and by homemade instruments such as the diddley bow. Blues players in the Mississippi Delta, lacking money for commercial accessories, cut the necks off bottles — medicine and whiskey bottles were prized — smoothed the edges, and slipped them over a finger. Early masters included Blind Willie Johnson and Son House; Robert Johnson, Charley Patton and later Muddy Waters made the sound emblematic of Delta and Chicago blues.

Technique

The slide is worn on the ring or little finger, leaving the other fingers free to fret normally. The player rests the slide lightly across the strings directly above a fret for correct intonation, keeps string pressure minimal, and mutes the strings behind the slide to suppress unwanted overtones. Open tunings — open G and open D especially — let whole chords sound under a straight slide. Heavier slides give more sustain; glass is prized for warmth, metal for brightness and volume.

Legacy

Bottleneck playing passed into rock through players such as Duane Allman, who famously used a Coricidin medicine bottle, Ry Cooder and Bonnie Raitt. The term "bottleneck" is now used interchangeably with "slide" for the accessory itself.
