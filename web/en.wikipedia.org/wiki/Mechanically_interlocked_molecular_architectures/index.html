Mechanically interlocked molecular architectures

In chemistry, mechanically interlocked molecular architectures (MIMAs) are molecules that are connected as a consequence of their topology. This connection of molecules is analogous to the way a ring is held on a string, or two interlocked rings: the components cannot be separated without breaking covalent bonds, even though there is no direct chemical bond between them. The link is called a mechanical bond.

Main classes

  • Catenanes: two or more macrocycles interlocked like links in a chain.
  • Rotaxanes: a macrocycle threaded onto a linear "dumbbell" molecule whose bulky stopper groups prevent dethreading.
  • Molecular knots: a single strand tied into a knot, such as the trefoil knot.
  • Molecular Borromean rings: three rings interlocked so that removing any one frees the other two.

Synthesis

Early syntheses (Wasserman, 1960, statistical threading of a catenane) gave tiny yields. Modern routes use templates — metal ions, hydrogen bonding, donor–acceptor stacking, or active-template synthesis — to organise components before covalent capture, a strategy developed by Jean-Pierre Sauvage (metal-templated catenanes, 1983) and J. Fraser Stoddart (donor–acceptor rotaxanes and catenanes).

Motion and applications

Because the components can move relative to one another without breaking bonds, MIMAs underpin molecular machines: molecular shuttles, switches, elevators, and artificial molecular pumps. Sauvage, Stoddart, and Ben Feringa shared the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the design and synthesis of molecular machines, much of it built on interlocked architectures. Proposed applications include drug delivery, smart materials, and molecular electronics.
