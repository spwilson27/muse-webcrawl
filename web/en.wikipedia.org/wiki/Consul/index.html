Consul

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A consul is an official appointed by the government of a state to live in a foreign city or region, carrying out a mix of commercial, administrative and protective functions for the sending state and its nationals: issuing visas, renewing passports, assisting citizens in distress (arrest, hospitalisation, death, evacuation), notarising documents, promoting trade and investment and reporting local conditions. Consuls are governed by the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations (1963) — whose Article 36 right of consular access for detained nationals has generated major litigation (the ICJ's LaGrand (2001) and Avena (2004) judgments against the United States) — and are headed by a consul-general in major posts, with consular (not diplomatic) immunities: functional, narrower than diplomats'.

Consulates and consulates-general in commercial capitals (New York, Shanghai, Dubai) often outsize the embassies in political capitals in staff and workload; honorary consuls — local notables with limited functions — extend the network cheaply. The office's roots are medieval (merchant-consuls in Mediterranean ports judging their own nationals' disputes, the ancestry of the capitulations system), and the consular service became a professional branch of foreign ministries in the nineteenth century.
