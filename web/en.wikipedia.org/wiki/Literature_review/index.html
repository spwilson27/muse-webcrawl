Literature review

A literature review is a scholarly survey and critical evaluation of the existing published research on a particular topic. It summarises, synthesises and analyses prior studies in order to establish what is known, identify debates and gaps, and situate new research within the field. Literature reviews appear as standalone review articles, and as a standard chapter or section of theses, dissertations and research papers.

Purposes

  • To demonstrate familiarity with the state of knowledge on a topic.
  • To identify inconsistencies, controversies and unanswered questions ("gaps").
  • To develop a theoretical framework and justify the research questions and methods of a new study.
  • To avoid duplicating work already done and to build cumulatively on earlier findings.

Types

  • Narrative (traditional) review — a qualitative summary of a body of literature, organised thematically or chronologically; flexible but open to selection bias.
  • Systematic review — follows an explicit, reproducible protocol: a defined search strategy, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and critical appraisal of each study. Especially common in medicine and the social sciences.
  • Meta-analysis — statistically combines the numerical results of multiple studies to produce a pooled estimate of an effect.
  • Scoping review — maps the extent and nature of research on a broad topic.
  • Integrative review — combines experimental and non-experimental studies to develop theory.

Process

Writing a literature review generally involves: defining the research question; searching bibliographic databases (such as PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus or Google Scholar); screening results against criteria; critically appraising the selected studies; and synthesising them — organising the literature by themes, methods or schools of thought rather than merely summarising source by source. Reference management software and reporting standards such as PRISMA (for systematic reviews) support the process.
