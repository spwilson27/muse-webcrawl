Military Service Act (Canada)

The Military Service Act was a 1917 statute enacted by the Parliament of Canada that introduced conscription — compulsory military service — for the First World War. Passed by the government of Prime Minister Robert Borden in response to dwindling voluntary enlistment, it made all male British subjects in Canada aged 20 to 45 liable for service, subject to exemptions.

Background

By 1916–17 casualties at the front, including at Vimy Ridge, outpaced recruitment, and Borden, having visited the front, concluded that conscription was necessary to maintain Canada's divisions in the field. The announcement in May 1917 provoked fierce opposition, particularly in Quebec, where French Canadians saw little obligation to fight in what they viewed as a British imperial war.

Consequences

To secure passage and re-election, Borden formed a Union Government coalition and passed the Wartime Elections Act and Military Voters Act, which together shaped the bitter "khaki election" of December 1917, won by the Unionists while Quebec voted overwhelmingly for Wilfrid Laurier's Liberals. Resistance included riots in Quebec City at Easter 1918 in which four civilians were killed. Of roughly 400,000 men found liable, many sought exemptions; about 48,000 conscripts were sent overseas and some 24,000 reached units in France before the Armistice.

Legacy

The Act and the Conscription Crisis of 1917 deepened the linguistic and regional divisions of Canadian politics for a generation. A second conscription crisis followed in the Second World War, handled this time by Mackenzie King's government under the National Resources Mobilization Act.
