Carotid stenting

Carotid stenting (carotid artery stenting, CAS) is an endovascular procedure in which a self-expanding metallic stent is placed across a narrowed segment of the carotid artery to widen the lumen and restore blood flow to the brain. It is used to treat carotid artery stenosis and reduce the risk of stroke.

Procedure

The procedure is usually performed through a catheter inserted in the common femoral artery (or, in transcarotid approaches, directly through the neck). An embolic protection device — a filter or flow-reversal system — is commonly deployed beyond the lesion to catch debris released during angioplasty and stent placement. The stent is then positioned across the plaque and expanded, and dual antiplatelet therapy is given afterwards.

Comparison with surgery

CAS is an alternative to carotid endarterectomy. Large trials such as CREST found broadly similar overall outcomes, with stenting carrying a somewhat higher risk of periprocedural stroke (especially in older patients) and surgery a higher risk of myocardial infarction and cranial nerve injury. Stenting is often favoured for patients at high surgical risk, with hostile neck anatomy or restenosis after endarterectomy.
