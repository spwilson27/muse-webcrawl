Parmenides of Elea (Greek: Παρμενίδης; c. 515 – c. 460 BC) was an ancient Greek philosopher from Elea, a Greek city in southern Italy. He was the founder of the Eleatic school of philosophy, which also included Zeno of Elea and Melissus of Samos. He is considered one of the most important of the pre-Socratic philosophers and a foundational figure in Western metaphysics.

Poem

Parmenides' only known work is a poem, conventionally titled On Nature (Περὶ φύσεως), of which about 160 lines survive, largely thanks to quotations by Simplicius and other later writers. The poem is written in hexameter verse and is divided into three parts: a proem, in which a goddess receives the narrator; "The Way of Truth" (aletheia), which presents his deductive metaphysics; and "The Way of Opinion" (doxa), which gives a cosmology of appearances.

Philosophy

In the Way of Truth, Parmenides argues that reality ("what is") is ungenerated and indestructible, whole, uniform, continuous, and unchanging. His foundational claim is that "being is, and non-being is not": because non-being cannot exist or even be thought of, change, motion, and plurality — which would require something to come from nothing or pass into nothing — are impossible. The world of the senses, which presents change and multiplicity, is therefore mere appearance.

This argument, the first extended piece of deductive reasoning in Western philosophy, deeply influenced Plato and the entire subsequent tradition of metaphysics and logic. His student Zeno devised his famous paradoxes to defend Parmenides' conclusions against critics.
