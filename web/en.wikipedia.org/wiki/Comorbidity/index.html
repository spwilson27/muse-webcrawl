Comorbidity

In medicine, comorbidity is the presence of one or more additional conditions often co-occurring (that is, concomitant or concurrent) with a primary condition. Comorbidity describes the effect of other conditions a patient might have besides the primary condition of interest, and can be physiological or psychological in nature. In the context of mental health, comorbidity often refers to disorders that overlap with each other, such as major depressive disorder and generalised anxiety disorder.

Definition and history

The term was coined by the epidemiologist Alvan R. Feinstein in 1970 to describe additional clinical entities that exist or occur during the clinical course of a patient with an index disease under study. Comorbidity is distinct from multimorbidity, which makes no reference to an index condition, although the two terms are often used interchangeably in practice.

Clinical significance

Comorbid conditions complicate diagnosis, treatment and prognosis. They may interact with the primary disease directly, share risk factors with it, or arise as a consequence of it or its treatment. Common examples include type 2 diabetes co-occurring with hypertension and obesity, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease co-occurring with cardiovascular disease. Patients with multiple conditions face polypharmacy — the use of many medications — with increased risks of adverse drug interactions and poorer adherence.

Measurement

Several indices quantify comorbidity for research and risk adjustment. The Charlson comorbidity index, developed in 1987, predicts ten-year mortality from a weighted sum of conditions such as heart disease, cancer and diabetes. The Elixhauser system, based on administrative data, is also widely used in health services research.
