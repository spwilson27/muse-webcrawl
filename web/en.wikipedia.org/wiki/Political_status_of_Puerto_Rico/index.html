Political status of Puerto Rico

The political status of Puerto Rico is that of an unincorporated territory of the United States, organized as a commonwealth under the official Spanish name Estado Libre Asociado de Puerto Rico ("Free Associated State of Puerto Rico"). Puerto Rico's head of state is the President of the United States, while its head of government is an elected governor. Puerto Ricans have been U.S. citizens since 1917, but residents of the island cannot vote in U.S. presidential elections and have only a non-voting Resident Commissioner in the U.S. House of Representatives.

History

Spain ceded Puerto Rico to the United States under the Treaty of Paris (1898) after the Spanish–American War. The Foraker Act of 1900 established a civilian government, and the Jones–Shafroth Act of 1917 granted statutory U.S. citizenship to Puerto Ricans. In a series of Supreme Court decisions known as the Insular Cases, Puerto Rico was classified as an "unincorporated territory" — belonging to, but not an integral part of, the United States.

In 1950, Public Law 600 authorized Puerto Ricans to draft their own constitution, which took effect in 1952 and established the Commonwealth. The United Nations removed Puerto Rico from its list of non-self-governing territories in 1953, though the UN Special Committee on Decolonization has repeatedly called for a self-determination process since.

Status options

  • Statehood — admission as the 51st U.S. state, supported by the New Progressive Party (PNP).
  • Commonwealth (status quo) — continuation of the current arrangement, historically associated with the Popular Democratic Party (PPD), which has also advocated an "enhanced" or sovereign commonwealth.
  • Independence — full sovereignty, supported by the Puerto Rican Independence Party (PIP).
  • Free association — sovereignty combined with a compact of association with the United States.

Referendums

Local plebiscites have been held in 1967, 1993, 1998, 2012, 2017, 2020, and 2024. In the 2020 referendum, a majority (about 52.5%) answered "yes" to a straight statehood yes/no question. In November 2024, a non-binding status referendum among statehood, independence, and free association gave statehood a plurality/majority of votes cast. None of these votes is binding on the U.S. Congress, which retains ultimate authority over the territory under the Territorial Clause of the U.S. Constitution. A congressional bill, the Puerto Rico Status Act, proposing a binding vote among statehood, independence, and free association passed the House of Representatives in 2022 but did not advance in the Senate.
