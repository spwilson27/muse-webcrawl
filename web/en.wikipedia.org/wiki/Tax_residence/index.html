Tax residence

ConceptStatus determining tax jurisdiction
Common testsDays present, domicile, permanent home
ConsequenceUsually taxation on worldwide income

Tax residence is a concept that determines the jurisdiction to which a taxpayer is subject for income tax purposes. A person or company that is tax resident in a jurisdiction is generally liable there to tax on worldwide income, while non-residents are typically taxed only on income sourced in that jurisdiction. Because rules differ between countries, an individual may be resident in more than one jurisdiction (dual residence) or, rarely, in none.

Individuals

Countries determine individual residence by varied tests: the number of days physically present (183 days is common); domicile or habitual abode; the location of a permanent home and close personal and economic ties; or citizenship, as in the United States, which taxes citizens and green-card holders as residents regardless of where they live. The United States uses a substantial presence test based on days of presence over three years. Some states within federations, such as New York and California, apply their own residence rules.

Companies

Corporate residence is usually based on the place of incorporation, the place of central management and control, or both. This determines which country may tax the company's profits and which treaty network applies, and it has been a central issue in corporate inversions and base erosion disputes.

Tax treaties and tie-breakers

Where two countries claim an individual as resident, double tax treaties following the OECD Model Tax Convention apply tie-breaker rules in sequence: permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality, and finally mutual agreement between the authorities. Tax residence certificates are issued to allow taxpayers to claim treaty benefits.
