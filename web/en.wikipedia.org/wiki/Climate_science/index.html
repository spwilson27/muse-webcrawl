Climate science

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Climate science, also called climatology in its descriptive branch, is the scientific study of the climate system — the long-term patterns of temperature, precipitation, wind, and other variables that characterise a region or the planet as a whole, and the physical, chemical, and biological processes that govern them.

Scope

Climate scientists study the atmosphere, oceans, cryosphere, land surface, and biosphere and their interactions. The field draws on meteorology, oceanography, glaciology, geology, and atmospheric chemistry, and relies on direct instrumental records (systematic since the mid-nineteenth century), satellite observations (since the 1970s), and proxy records such as ice cores, tree rings, and sediments that extend the record back hundreds of thousands of years.

History

Foundations were laid in the nineteenth century: Joseph Fourier described the greenhouse effect in the 1820s, John Tyndall measured the infrared absorption of gases in 1859, and Svante Arrhenius calculated in 1896 how changes in atmospheric carbon dioxide could alter global temperature. In 1938 Guy Stewart Callendar argued that warming was already under way; the Keeling Curve of atmospheric CO₂ measurements began in 1958. Since 1988 the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has periodically assessed the field's findings.

Methods

General circulation models (GCMs) simulate the climate system numerically and are tested against observations and past climates. Key research topics include attribution of observed warming, feedbacks such as ice–albedo and water vapour, carbon-cycle dynamics, and regional projections of impacts such as sea-level rise and changing precipitation.
