FunctionCell-surface receptor mediating endocytosis of cholesterol-rich LDL

The low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDL receptor or LDLR) is a cell-surface protein that binds cholesterol-carrying LDL particles in the blood and internalises them by receptor-mediated endocytosis, delivering cholesterol to the cell while the receptor recycles back to the surface.

Mechanism

LDL receptors cluster in clathrin-coated pits. After binding LDL via apolipoprotein B-100, the complex is endocytosed; the acidic endosome triggers release of the particle, which is degraded in lysosomes, freeing cholesterol. The intracellular cholesterol then suppresses both new receptor synthesis and the cell's own cholesterol synthesis (through SREBP regulation), a feedback system characterised by Michael Brown and Joseph Goldstein, who shared the 1985 Nobel Prize for the work. The protein PCSK9 binds the receptor and targets it for degradation, reducing receptor numbers.

Clinical significance

Mutations in the LDLR gene cause familial hypercholesterolaemia, characterised by very high LDL cholesterol, tendon xanthomas and premature cardiovascular disease. Statins raise LDL-receptor expression, and PCSK9 inhibitors preserve receptors — both lowering blood LDL.
