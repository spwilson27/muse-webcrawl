Hierarchy of angels

In the angelology of different religions, a hierarchy of angels is a ranking system of angels. The most influential Christian scheme was set out by the early-6th-century author Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite in his work De Coelesti Hierarchia (On the Celestial Hierarchy), which arranged the angels into three hierarchies (spheres) of three orders or "choirs" each — nine choirs in total.

The Dionysian hierarchy

First sphere (oldest angels)

  • Seraphim — the highest order, "the burning ones", attendants at the throne of God
  • Cherubim — guardians endowed with fullness of knowledge
  • Thrones — bearers of God's throne and symbols of divine justice

Second sphere

  • Dominions — regulate the duties of lower angels
  • Virtues — govern nature and bestow grace and miracles
  • Powers — warrior angels who resist evil spirits

Third sphere

  • Principalities — watch over nations and earthly rulers
  • Archangels — great messengers, such as Michael, Gabriel and Raphael
  • Angels — the lowest order, closest to humanity, including guardian angels

Other traditions

Medieval theologians such as Thomas Aquinas adopted and refined the Dionysian scheme, while some later authors rearranged the orders. Jewish angelology, as systematised by Maimonides in the Mishneh Torah, lists ten ranks of angels, and Islamic tradition recognises its own orders of angels (malāʾika) headed by the four archangels.
