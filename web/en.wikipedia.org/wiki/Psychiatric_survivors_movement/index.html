Psychiatric survivors movement

The psychiatric survivors movement is a social and political movement of people who identify as having survived psychiatric treatment — including involuntary commitment, forced medication, electroconvulsive therapy and seclusion — and who campaign for the rights of mental health service users, the reform or abolition of coercive psychiatry, and peer-run alternatives. Participants variously describe themselves as psychiatric survivors, ex-patients, consumers, service users, or "consumers/survivors/ex-patients" (c/s/x).

History

Organised ex-patient activism emerged in the early 1970s in North America and Europe, influenced by the civil rights movement and by critiques of psychiatry such as Thomas Szasz's The Myth of Mental Illness (1961) and Erving Goffman's Asylums (1961). Early groups included the Insane Liberation Front in Portland, the Mental Patients' Liberation Front in Boston, and the Mental Patients' Union in London (1973). Judi Chamberlin's On Our Own (1978) became a foundational text advocating patient-controlled alternatives. Publications such as the newspaper Madness Network News linked the movement internationally.

Goals and activities

Common demands include an end to forced treatment and involuntary commitment, protection of legal rights, informed consent, peer support and crisis services run by service users themselves, and recognition of psychiatric disability discrimination. The movement contributed to the development of the recovery model and of peer support as a profession, and its disability-rights framing influenced the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), whose interpretation by UN bodies calls for replacing substituted decision-making with supported decision-making. Annual "Mad Pride" events celebrate mad identity and culture.
