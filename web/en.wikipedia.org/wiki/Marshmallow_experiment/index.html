Stanford marshmallow experiment

The Stanford marshmallow experiment was a series of studies on delayed gratification conducted in the late 1960s and early 1970s by psychologist Walter Mischel at Stanford University. The experiments became among the most famous in psychology and were widely interpreted as showing that the ability to delay gratification in childhood predicts success later in life, though later replications have substantially qualified that conclusion.

Procedure

Children — preschoolers, typically four to six years old, drawn largely from Stanford's Bing Nursery School — were seated alone in a room with a treat (a marshmallow, cookie or pretzel) placed in front of them. The experimenter told the child that if they waited until the experimenter returned (about 15 minutes) without eating the treat, they would receive two treats; if they could not wait, they could ring a bell, summoning the experimenter, but would receive only the one treat. Researchers observed the children's strategies — covering their eyes, singing, inventing games — for diverting attention from the temptation.

Follow-up findings

Follow-up studies by Mischel and colleagues reported that children who waited longer tended, as adolescents, to score higher on the SAT, be rated by their parents as more competent and self-reliant, and cope better with frustration and stress; later follow-ups associated longer waiting with lower body-mass index and differences in brain responses to temptation. These correlations made the test a popular symbol of self-control and willpower.

Replications and reinterpretation

A 2018 conceptual replication by Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan, using a much larger and more representative sample, found the predictive effect of waiting time was roughly half the size originally reported and largely disappeared once family background and early cognitive ability were controlled. Other work has shown children's waiting depends rationally on their environment: children who had experienced an unreliable experimenter waited far less, suggesting the test partly measures trust that promised rewards will materialise. The "marshmallow test" is now often cited as a case study in the replication crisis and in the dangers of over-interpreting small, unrepresentative samples.
