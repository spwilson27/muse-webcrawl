Paleovirology

Paleovirology is the study of viruses that existed in the distant past and of the traces they have left in modern genomes. Because viruses do not form fossils in the ordinary sense, the field relies on endogenous viral elements (EVEs) — fragments or whole copies of viral genomes that became integrated into the germline DNA of hosts and were inherited by their descendants — and on viral sequences recovered from ancient tissues and permafrost. These molecular fossils, sometimes tens of millions of years old, allow researchers to reconstruct the long-term evolution and host associations of virus families.

Endogenous viral elements

Retroviruses integrate into host DNA as part of their normal life cycle, so when infection reaches the germline, the provirus can be passed down the generations; about 8% of the human genome derives from such ancient retroviral infections. Other virus families — including bornaviruses, filoviruses, parvoviruses and circoviruses — have also left non-retroviral EVEs. By comparing orthologous insertions among related species, researchers date the original infections, often pushing the known origins of virus families from thousands to tens of millions of years, and revealing a history of repeated host-switching and co-divergence with hosts.

Ancient viruses

Direct recovery is possible for geologically young material: complete genomes of the 1918 influenza virus were reconstructed from preserved victims, and giant viruses (such as Pithovirus and Mollivirus) revived from Siberian permafrost around 30,000 years old proved still infectious to amoebae — findings that attract both scientific interest and biosafety debate.

Significance

Paleovirology recalibrates molecular-clock estimates of viral evolution (viral lineages generally evolve far more slowly over long timescales than short-term measurements suggest), documents extinct viral diversity, and shows how hosts have repeatedly co-opted viral genes — the placenta-forming syncytins, for example, derive from retroviral envelope proteins captured independently in several mammal lineages.
