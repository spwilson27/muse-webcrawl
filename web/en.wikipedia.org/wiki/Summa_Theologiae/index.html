The Summa Theologiae (Latin for "Summary of Theology"), also written Summa Theologica, is the best-known work of the Italian Dominican friar and theologian Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274). Composed between 1265 and 1274, it remained unfinished at Aquinas's death. The Summa is a comprehensive manual of Catholic theology and one of the most influential works of Western scholasticism and philosophy.

Structure

The work is divided into three parts. The Prima Pars treats God, the Trinity, creation, angels and human nature. The Secunda Pars, the largest, deals with moral theology: human acts, virtues and vices, law and grace. The Tertia Pars covers Christ, the sacraments and the last things; Aquinas left it incomplete at the treatment of penance, and his followers completed it with a supplement drawn from his earlier writings.

Each topic is organised into questions, each subdivided into articles following a strict format: a question is posed ("Whether…"), objections are listed, a contrary authority is cited (sed contra), Aquinas gives his resolution (respondeo), and each objection is answered in turn. The Secunda Secundae alone contains 512 questions and 2,652 articles.

Content and arguments

The Summa contains Aquinas's famous Five Ways — five arguments for the existence of God based on motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection and final causes. It systematically integrates Aristotelian philosophy with Christian doctrine, treating faith and reason as harmonious paths to truth. Its treatment of natural law became foundational for Catholic moral theology and influential in ethics and legal philosophy.

Influence

Although initially controversial, the Summa became the standard theological textbook of the Dominican order and, after the Council of Trent, of Catholic seminaries generally. Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Aeterni Patris (1879) placed Thomism at the centre of Catholic intellectual life, a position reaffirmed in the 20th century. The standard Latin edition is the Leonine edition, commissioned in 1882.
