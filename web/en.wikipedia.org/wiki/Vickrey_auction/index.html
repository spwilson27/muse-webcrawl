Vickrey auction

A Vickrey auction or sealed-bid second-price auction is a type of sealed-bid auction in which the highest bidder wins but pays the price bid by the second-highest bidder. It is named after Canadian economist William Vickrey, who provided the first scholarly analysis of it in game-theoretic terms in his seminal 1961 paper "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders". Vickrey's work later contributed to his sharing of the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1996.

The Vickrey auction is closely related to the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves auction and to the English auction. In dominant-strategy terms, it is strategically equivalent to an English (open ascending) auction: each bidder's dominant strategy is to bid exactly their own true valuation of the item, a property known as truthfulness or incentive compatibility.

Properties

Truthful bidding as a dominant strategy

In a Vickrey auction with a single indivisible item, bidding one's true valuation is a weakly dominant strategy. If a bidder bids above their valuation, they risk winning and paying a price above what the item is worth to them; if they bid below it, they risk losing an item they could have won at a price at or below their valuation. Because the price paid is determined by others' bids rather than one's own, no deviation from truthful bidding can ever improve a bidder's outcome.

Efficiency

Since truthful bidding is dominant, the item is allocated to the bidder who values it most, making the auction allocatively efficient in the standard private-values model. Revenue, however, can be lower than in a first-price auction when valuations are low or few bidders participate; under the assumptions of the revenue equivalence theorem, expected revenue equals that of other standard auction formats.

Examples and applications

Second-price mechanisms appear in the sale of stamps and other collectibles, in some procurement settings, and — in generalized form — in online advertising auctions. The generalized second-price auction once used for search advertising is related but is not a true Vickrey auction and is not generally truthful. Vickrey's analysis also underpins the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism used in mechanism design.

Criticisms and limitations

Vickrey auctions are vulnerable to collusion among bidders and to shill bidding by a dishonest seller who can insert a fake second bid just below the winner's. They can also produce very low revenue when the second-highest valuation is far below the highest. These practical weaknesses help explain why pure Vickrey auctions are rarer in practice than their theoretical elegance might suggest.

History

Second-price sealed auctions were used informally by stamp collectors from the late nineteenth century. Vickrey's 1961 paper in the Journal of Finance gave the first rigorous game-theoretic treatment, analyzing first- and second-price auctions and proving their strategic equivalence under symmetry assumptions. His broader research programme on incentive-compatible mechanism design laid foundations for modern auction theory.
