Bank of Amsterdam

The Bank of Amsterdam (Dutch: Amsterdamsche Wisselbank, "Amsterdam Exchange Bank") was a public deposit and giro bank founded by the city of Amsterdam in 1609. It is widely regarded as a forerunner of the modern central bank, and its stable unit of account — the bank guilder, or banco money — became the standard settlement medium of European wholesale trade in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

Foundation and operation

The bank was created to remedy the chaos of the early modern coinage: hundreds of debased and clipped foreign coins circulated in Amsterdam, and merchants could not be sure what a coin was worth. The Wisselbank accepted coins and bullion by weight and fineness, credited depositors in bank guilders, and settled payments by transfer between accounts. Because large bills of exchange in Amsterdam had legally to be settled through the bank, its balances became indispensable, and bank money traded at a premium (agio) of about 4–5 percent over current coin.

For much of its history the bank was run conservatively as a full-reserve institution, and its reputation underpinned Amsterdam's position as Europe's commercial and financial capital.

Decline and closure

From the 1760s, and especially during the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–1784), the bank made large secret loans to the Dutch East India Company and the city that it could not recover. When the lending became public in the 1790s, confidence collapsed; the bank guilder fell to a discount, and the institution limped on under the Batavian Republic and the Kingdom of the Netherlands until its liquidation in 1820, its functions absorbed by De Nederlandsche Bank (founded 1814). Adam Smith had praised the bank in The Wealth of Nations as a model of sound public banking — a judgement that made its fall a canonical cautionary tale in monetary economics.
