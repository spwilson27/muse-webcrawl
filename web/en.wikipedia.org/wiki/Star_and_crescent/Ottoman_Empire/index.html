Ottoman Empire

The Ottoman Empire, historically known to its inhabitants as the Sublime Ottoman State, was a vast empire founded around 1299 by Osman I in Anatolia. At its height in the 16th and 17th centuries it spanned three continents — southeastern Europe, Western Asia, and North Africa — controlling much of the Mediterranean world. The empire endured for more than six centuries, dissolving in 1922 after the First World War.

Rise and expansion

From a small Turkish beylik on the Byzantine frontier, the Ottomans expanded across the Balkans and Anatolia. In 1453 Sultan Mehmed II conquered Constantinople, ending the Byzantine Empire and making the city — renamed Istanbul — his capital. Under Selim I the empire absorbed Syria, Egypt, and the Hejaz, gaining custody of Mecca and Medina. Under Suleiman the Magnificent (r. 1520–1566), often regarded as the empire's greatest ruler, Ottoman armies reached the gates of Vienna and Ottoman fleets dominated the Mediterranean and the Indian Ocean trade routes.

Government and society

The empire was ruled by the sultan of the House of Osman, advised by the grand vizier and the imperial council (Divan). The millet system granted recognized religious communities a degree of self-government. The devshirme levy recruited Christian boys for the elite Janissary infantry and the imperial administration. Ottoman law combined dynastic kanun with Islamic sharia, and the empire's economy rested on agriculture, long-distance trade, and a sophisticated system of tax farming and pious foundations (waqfs).

Decline and dissolution

Military defeats from the late 17th century — Vienna (1683), Karlowitz (1699) — marked a long period of territorial contraction, punctuated by reform eras such as the Tanzimat (1839–1876). After defeat alongside the Central Powers in the First World War, the empire was partitioned by the Allies. The Turkish War of Independence followed; the sultanate was abolished on 1 November 1922, and the Republic of Turkey was proclaimed in 1923.
