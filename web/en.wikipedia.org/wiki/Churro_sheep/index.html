Churro sheep

Navajo-Churro sheep
Country of origin: Spain / United States
Use: Wool, meat
Traits: Hardy, long coarse double-coated fleece, often four horns
Conservation status: Threatened (recovered from near-extinction)

Churro sheep, known in the United States as Navajo-Churro, descend from the Churra breed of the Iberian Peninsula, brought to the Americas by Spanish conquistadors and settlers in the 16th century — the first domesticated sheep introduced to North America. They became foundational livestock for Hispanic and later Navajo communities in the American Southwest.

Characteristics

Navajo-Churro are small, hardy sheep adapted to the arid, high-desert environment. They have a double coat: a coarse, hairy outer fleece prized for durable weaving and a fine, soft inner undercoat for insulation. Rams and sometimes ewes may have two sets of horns (four horns total). Colours range across nearly the whole natural sheep palette — whites, blacks, browns, greys, and patterned fleeces.

History

For the Navajo, sheep became central to culture and livelihood — "sheep is life" — providing wool for the famous Navajo rugs and blankets, meat, and milk products. United States government stock-reduction programs in the 1930s and subsequent replacement by fine-wool breeds drove the old phenotype nearly to extinction by the 1970s.

Conservation

The Navajo Sheep Project, founded in the 1970s by Lyle McNeal, collected surviving old-type sheep and rebuilt breeding flocks. The breed is now registered by the Navajo-Churro Sheep Association and listed by the Livestock Conservancy as threatened, with numbers steadily recovering. Its wool has become sought after again for authentic Navajo weaving.
