Saint David

Saint David (Welsh: Dewi Sant; c. 500 – c. 589) was a Welsh bishop of Mynyw (St Davids) during the sixth century. He is the patron saint of Wales and one of its most revered national figures. His feast day, St David's Day, is celebrated on 1 March, traditionally the date of his death.

Life

Details of David's life come largely from the Buchedd Dewi ("Life of David"), written by the monk Rhygyfarch in the late eleventh century, some five hundred years after David lived. According to tradition, he was the son of Sant, king of Ceredigion, and Non — later Saint Non — and was born near Capel Non during a thunderstorm. Educated at Henfynyw and Whitland under teachers including Paulinus of Wales, David founded monasteries across Wales and southwest England, most importantly the austere community at Glyn Rhosyn, the site of modern St Davids.

David's monks followed a strict rule: they ploughed without draught animals, drank only water, ate only bread with salt and herbs, and spent their evenings in prayer and study. This asceticism earned David the Welsh epithet Dewi Ddyfrwr, "David the water-drinker".

Miracles and cult

The best-known miracle associated with David is said to have occurred at the Synod of Brefi around 560: as he preached against the Pelagian heresy, the ground beneath him rose into a hill so that the whole crowd could see and hear him, and a dove settled on his shoulder. David was recognised as a saint by Pope Callixtus II in 1120, and two pilgrimages to St Davids were held equivalent to one to Rome. His famous last words, recorded by Rhygyfarch — "Be joyful, keep the faith, and do the little things" (Gwnewch y pethau bychain) — remain a popular Welsh maxim, and daffodils and leeks are worn in his honour on St David's Day.
