Amber

Amber
CompositionFossilized tree resin (amorphous organic polymer)
Hardness2–2.5 on the Mohs scale
ColourYellow-orange-brown; also red, green, blue (rare)
Major sourceBaltic region (succinite)
Age rangeCarboniferous to recent; mostly Cenozoic

Amber is fossilized tree resin that has been appreciated for its colour and natural beauty since Neolithic times. Much prized as a gemstone, amber is made into a variety of decorative objects and jewellery, and was historically used as a healing agent in folk medicine. Amber also preserves organisms trapped in the sticky resin — most famously insects — providing palaeontologists with exceptionally detailed fossils.

Most amber is found in the form of Baltic amber (succinite), a Eocene resin of coniferous forests, of which the world's largest deposits lie around the Baltic Sea, notably in Russia's Kaliningrad Oblast. Other important sources include Dominican and Mexican amber (from the extinct legume tree Hymenaea protera) and Burmese amber (burmite) from Myanmar, about 99 million years old, which has yielded Cretaceous feathers, small dinosaurs and early birds.

Formation

Amber begins as resin exuded by trees, which hardens on exposure and, if buried in sediments and subjected to heat and pressure over millions of years, polymerizes into amber; younger, incompletely fossilized material is called copal. Because it is organic and soft, amber can be carved and polished easily, and rubbed amber becomes electrostatically charged — the Greek word for amber, ēlektron, is the origin of the word "electricity".

History and trade

Amber from the Baltic travelled prehistoric trade routes — the so-called Amber Road — to the Mediterranean world, where it was treasured in Mycenaean Greece and Rome. The famous Amber Room of the Catherine Palace near St Petersburg, panelled with amber and gold leaf, was looted in the Second World War and has been recreated. In China, amber was burned as incense and used in traditional medicine.

Inclusions and science

Organisms trapped in resin are preserved in three dimensions with microscopic fidelity; amber has yielded ants, spiders, flowers, lizards, feathers and even, in Burmese amber, the tail of a small feathered dinosaur. Claims of DNA recovery from amber inclusions inspired Jurassic Park but have not withstood scientific scrutiny, as DNA degrades far faster than amber's age.

Imitations

Amber is widely imitated with plastics, glass and copal; tests such as flotation in saturated salt water (in which true amber floats), hardness and the smell of pine when warmed help distinguish genuine material.
