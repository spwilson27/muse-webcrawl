John Tuzo Wilson CC OBE FRS FRSC (October 24, 1908 – April 15, 1993) was a Canadian geophysicist and geologist who achieved worldwide acclaim for his contributions to the theory of plate tectonics. He introduced the concepts of transform faults and volcanic hotspots and described the cycle of ocean opening and closing now known as the Wilson cycle.

Early life and career

Wilson was born in Ottawa to a family of Scottish engineering background — his father, John Armistead Wilson, was a pioneer of Canadian civil aviation. Tuzo Wilson studied physics and mathematics at the University of Toronto, geology at Cambridge, and took one of the first geophysics doctorates in North America at Princeton under Harry Hess. During the Second World War he served with the Royal Canadian Engineers, reaching the rank of colonel. In 1946 he became professor of geophysics at Toronto, a post he held until 1974, and he later served as founding director general of the Ontario Science Centre (1964–1974).

Contributions to plate tectonics

Long a sceptic of continental drift, Wilson was converted in the early 1960s and then supplied several of the theory's key ideas. In 1963 he proposed that the Hawaiian island chain formed as the Pacific plate moved over a relatively fixed "hotspot" in the mantle, the islands growing older toward the northwest — a key argument for moving plates. In 1965 he defined a third class of plate boundary, the transform fault, predicting that ocean-floor fractures offset mid-ocean ridges and that motion along them is opposite to what ordinary faulting would suggest; seismology soon confirmed the prediction. His 1966 paper asking "Did the Atlantic close and then re-open?" introduced what Danish geologist Kevin Burke named the Wilson cycle of ocean basins.

Honours

Wilson received the Geological Society's Wollaston Medal and the Vetlesen Prize (both 1978), was a Companion of the Order of Canada, and has a medal of the Canadian Geophysical Union, several mountains and a seamount named after him. The J. Tuzo Wilson Professorship at Toronto commemorates him.
