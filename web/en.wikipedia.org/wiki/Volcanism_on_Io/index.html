Volcanism on Io

Volcanism on Io, a moon of Jupiter, is represented by the presence of volcanoes, volcanic pits and lava flows on the surface. Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System. Its volcanism was discovered in 1979 by the Voyager 1 spacecraft, whose images captured a plume rising from the volcano Pele.

Tidal heating

Unlike Earth, whose internal heat comes mainly from radioactive decay, Io's volcanism is powered by tidal flexing: Jupiter's immense gravity, modulated by orbital resonances with Europa and Ganymede, continually squeezes and stretches Io, dissipating enormous energy as heat in its interior. This keeps much of Io's mantle partially molten.

Style of volcanism

Ionian eruptions are dominated by sulfur and sulfur dioxide. Plumes of gas and dust can rise 500 km above the surface before falling back and painting vast areas in red, yellow, white and black frost. Silicate lava flows also occur; temperatures measured by the Galileo spacecraft suggest some lavas are as hot as those on the early Earth. Hundreds of volcanic features are catalogued, including paterae (irregular crater-like depressions), lava lakes such as Loki Patera, and the great plume volcanoes Prometheus and Pele.

Effects

Ionian eruptions continually resurface the moon, erasing impact craters entirely — Io has none. Material ejected from Io feeds Jupiter's magnetosphere: sulfur and oxygen ions form the Io plasma torus, and a faint atmosphere of sulfur dioxide is maintained by volcanic outgassing and sublimation.

Exploration

Io's volcanism has been observed by Voyager 1 and 2, Galileo, Cassini, New Horizons, and most recently Juno, which made close flybys in 2023–2024, along with Earth-based telescopes.
