The organistrum is an early form of the hurdy-gurdy, in use in Europe from roughly the 10th to the 13th century. Like the later hurdy-gurdy, it produced sound by a hand-crank-turned, rosined wheel rubbing against strings, yielding a continuous drone; melody strings were shortened by tangents operated by keys or pegs.

Description

The organistrum was large — often around a metre or more in length — and was typically played by two people sitting with it across their laps: one turned the crank while the other operated the tangents that changed the pitch of the melody strings, accompanied by drone strings. The instrument is depicted in Romanesque sculpture, most famously on the Portico de la Gloria of Santiago de Compostela Cathedral in Spain, where an organistrum appears among the instruments of the elders of the Apocalypse.

History and use

The instrument's name combines organum — the earliest form of polyphony — with instrumentum. It was used in monasteries and churches, possibly for teaching and accompanying chant and early polyphony, and in secular court music. As design improved, smaller one-player versions emerged, known variously as the symphonia, and the organistrum's descendants evolved into the hurdy-gurdy, which became a popular folk instrument across Europe.
