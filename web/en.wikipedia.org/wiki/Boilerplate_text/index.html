Boilerplate text

Boilerplate text, or simply boilerplate, is any written text (copy) that can be reused in new contexts or applications without significant changes to the original. The contemporary term gained popularity in the field of computer programming — where "boilerplate code" is code repeated in many places — but it also applies to standardised contract clauses, press releases, mission statements and other documents.

Origin of the term

"Boilerplate" originally referred to the curved steel plates used in steam boilers, and then to the stereotype plates — resembling boiler plate — delivered to small American newspapers from the 1850s onward. Syndicates such as the Western Newspaper Union shipped ready-to-print plates containing syndicated stories, advertisements and filler; editors derided the pre-set material as "boilerplate", and the word came to mean any standardised, unoriginal text.

Usage

In law, boilerplate clauses are standard provisions (governing law, severability, entire-agreement clauses) placed at the end of contracts; although individually negotiated rarely, they can be legally significant. In public relations, a boilerplate is the standard paragraph at the end of a press release describing a company. In software engineering, boilerplate code is the repetitive code a framework requires (such as HTML document skeletons or getters and setters); modern frameworks and code generation aim to reduce it. In journalism the word retains its pejorative sense of padded, formulaic copy — distinct from specialist jargon, though both may be labelled "boilerplate" by critics.
