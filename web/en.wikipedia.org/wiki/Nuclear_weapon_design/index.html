Nuclear weapon design

Nuclear weapons design are the physical, chemical, and engineering arrangements that cause the physics package of a nuclear weapon to detonate. There are three basic design types:


Schematic of the three main nuclear weapon design types.
  • Gun-type fission weapons assemble a supercritical mass by firing one subcritical piece of fissile material into another. This is the simplest design and was used in the Little Boy bomb dropped on Hiroshima.
  • Implosion-type fission weapons use conventional explosives arranged in lenses to compress a subcritical sphere of fissile material, increasing its density until it becomes supercritical. This design was used in the Fat Man bomb dropped on Nagasaki and in the Trinity test device.
  • Thermonuclear weapons (hydrogen bombs) use a fission primary to ignite a secondary stage containing fusion fuel such as lithium deuteride, typically in the Teller–Ulam design, producing yields far greater than pure fission weapons.

History

The first nuclear weapons were developed by the Manhattan Project during the Second World War. The gun-type design was considered so reliable that it was used at Hiroshima without a prior full-scale test, while the more complex implosion design was proven at the Trinity test on 16 July 1945. After the war, design work continued at laboratories such as Los Alamos and Lawrence Livermore in the United States, and analogous institutions in the Soviet Union, the United Kingdom, France, and China.

Fission weapon designs

Gun-type assembly

In a gun-type weapon, a conventional explosive charge propels one subcritical mass of uranium-235 down a barrel into another subcritical mass. The combined mass exceeds the critical mass, and a neutron initiator triggers the chain reaction. Because assembly is relatively slow, gun-type designs are vulnerable to predetonation with plutonium, whose spontaneous fission rate is high; they are therefore practical only with uranium.

Implosion assembly

Implosion designs surround a plutonium or composite pit with precisely shaped explosive lenses. When detonated simultaneously, the lenses produce a converging spherical shock wave that compresses the pit to several times its normal density, rendering it supercritical. Implosion is far more efficient than gun-type assembly and permits the use of plutonium-239 produced in reactors.

Boosted fission weapons

In a boosted fission weapon, a small amount of fusion fuel — a deuterium–tritium gas mixture — is placed at the centre of the pit. When the fission reaction begins, the fusion fuel ignites and releases a burst of high-energy neutrons, which greatly increase the fraction of fissile material that fissions before the device disassembles. Boosting raises yield and allows more compact designs.

Thermonuclear weapons

The Teller–Ulam design, demonstrated by the United States in the Ivy Mike test of 1952, uses radiation from a fission primary, channelled within a radiation case, to compress and ignite a physically separate secondary containing fusion fuel. Radiation implosion allows multiple stages and yields in the megaton range; the largest weapon ever tested, the Soviet Tsar Bomba of 1961, yielded about 50 megatons.

Other designs

Other concepts include neutron bombs (enhanced radiation weapons), which maximise lethal neutron output relative to blast; "clean" weapons designed to minimise fallout; and hypothesised pure fusion weapons, which no state is known to have built. Design information is among the most closely guarded secrets of nuclear-weapon states, and its spread is controlled by the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons.
