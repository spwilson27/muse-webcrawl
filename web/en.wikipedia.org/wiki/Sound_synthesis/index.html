Sound synthesis

Sound synthesis is the electronic or computational generation of sound, typically as the first step in creating music with synthesizers, samplers, and digital audio workstations. A synthesizer generates electrical signals that are converted into sound through loudspeakers or headphones.

History

Electronic sound synthesis began with instruments such as the Telharmonium (1897) and the Theremin (1920). The RCA Mark II Sound Synthesizer (1957) was an early programmable instrument. In the 1960s Robert Moog and Don Buchla developed modular voltage-controlled synthesizers, and the Minimoog (1970) made synthesis portable. Digital synthesis arrived with the Yamaha DX7 (1983), whose FM synthesis sold over 200,000 units.

Methods

  • Subtractive synthesis — rich waveforms from oscillators are shaped by filters that remove harmonics.
  • Additive synthesis — individual sine-wave partials are summed to build timbre.
  • Frequency modulation (FM) synthesis — one oscillator modulates the frequency of another, producing complex spectra.
  • Wavetable synthesis — single-cycle waveforms are scanned and interpolated.
  • Granular synthesis — sounds are built from tiny grains of sampled audio.
  • Physical modelling — mathematical models simulate vibrating strings, tubes, and membranes.
  • Sampling — recorded sounds are played back, transposed, and processed.

Components

Classic synthesizer voices consist of oscillators (VCO/DCO), a filter (VCF), an amplifier (VCA), envelope generators (typically ADSR), and low-frequency oscillators (LFOs) for modulation. Modern software synthesizers reproduce and extend these architectures in plug-in form.
