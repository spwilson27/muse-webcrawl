Strong interaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of quarks exchanging gluons inside a proton and neutron bound in a nucleus]
Gluon exchange binds quarks; a residual force binds nucleons.

The strong interaction (also called the strong force or strong nuclear force) is the strongest of the four known fundamental interactions. It acts over distances of about a femtometre (10⁻¹⁵ m) — the size of an atomic nucleus — binding quarks into hadrons such as protons and neutrons and, as a residual effect, binding those nucleons into atomic nuclei despite their electric repulsion.

The theory of the strong interaction is Quantum chromodynamics (QCD): quarks carry colour charge, and gluons mediate the force. Its defining peculiarities — confinement (force roughly constant with distance, so quarks can never be isolated) and asymptotic freedom (weakening at very short distances) — follow from gluons themselves carrying colour. At the nuclear scale the strong force is some 100 times stronger than electromagnetism, though the weak force and gravity are far weaker still.

History

Rutherford's nucleus (1911) demanded a force stronger than Coulomb repulsion to hold protons together; Chadwick's neutron (1932) identified the second nuclear constituent; Yukawa (1935) modelled the force as meson exchange, predicting the pion. The strong interaction's true nature emerged only with the quark model (1964) and QCD (1973): the nuclear force is a leftover, like the van der Waals force between molecules, of the colour force within nucleons — a picture made quantitative by effective field theories of nuclei.
