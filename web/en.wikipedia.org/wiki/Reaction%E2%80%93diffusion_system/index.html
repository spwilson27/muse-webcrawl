Reaction–diffusion system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A reaction–diffusion system is a mathematical model describing how the concentrations of one or more substances distributed in space change under two competing processes: local chemical reactions, which create or consume substances, and diffusion, which spreads them through space. The model takes the form of a system of parabolic partial differential equations.

Pattern formation

Reaction–diffusion systems can generate rich self-organised patterns from nearly uniform initial conditions. Alan Turing's 1952 paper "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" showed that two chemicals reacting and diffusing at different rates can produce stable spatial patterns, an instability now called the Turing instability. Such mechanisms have been proposed to explain animal coat markings, seashell patterns, and developmental structures.

Examples and applications

Well-known examples include the Fisher–KPP equation for gene spread and combustion fronts, the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction with its oscillating waves, and models of epidemics, ecology, and tumour growth. Reaction–diffusion algorithms are also used in computer graphics to synthesise natural-looking textures.
