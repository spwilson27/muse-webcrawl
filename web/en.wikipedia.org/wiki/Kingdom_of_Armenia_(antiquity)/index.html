The Kingdom of Armenia was an ancient monarchy in the Armenian Highlands of western Asia, existing from 321 BC to AD 428. Ruled successively by the Orontid, Artaxiad, and Arsacid dynasties, it was for centuries a buffer state and battleground between Rome (and later Byzantium) in the west and Persia (Parthian, then Sasanian) in the east. Armenia is notable as the first state in the world to adopt Christianity as its official religion, traditionally in AD 301.

Orontid and Artaxiad periods

The Orontid (Yervanduni) satraps of the Achaemenid Empire became hereditary kings after Alexander the Great's conquests. In 189 BC, following Seleucid defeat by Rome, Artaxias I founded the Artaxiad dynasty. The kingdom reached its zenith under his descendant Tigranes the Great (r. 95–55 BC), who built the new capital Tigranocerta, conquered Syria, Phoenicia, and parts of Cilicia and Mesopotamia, and briefly made Armenia an empire "from sea to sea." Defeated by the Roman generals Lucullus and Pompey, Tigranes retained Armenia proper as a Roman ally.

Arsacid period and Christianization

From AD 52/66, a branch of the Parthian Arsacid dynasty ruled Armenia under a compromise between Rome and Parthia: the king would be an Arsacid prince crowned by the Roman emperor. Tiridates III (the Great) proclaimed Christianity the state religion around 301–314, under the influence of Gregory the Illuminator, making Armenia the first Christian kingdom. Around 405, Mesrop Mashtots created the Armenian alphabet, ushering in a golden age of literature.

Partition and end

In 387 Rome and Sasanian Persia partitioned Armenia; the smaller western part went to Rome and the larger east ("Persarmenia") to Persia. The Persians abolished the Arsacid monarchy in 428, ruling through marzbans. Armenian resistance culminated at the Battle of Avarayr (451), where Vardan Mamikonian's rebels died defending the Christian faith against forced Zoroastrianization, securing eventual religious toleration.

Legacy

The ancient kingdom shaped Armenian national identity, church, and culture; its memory was revived by the later Bagratid kingdom and by modern Armenia.
