Durio

Durio is a genus of flowering plants in the mallow family, Malvaceae, native to the rainforests of Borneo and Sumatra in Southeast Asia. The genus comprises around 30 recognised species of trees, several of which produce edible fruit known as durians, most famously Durio zibethinus, the common durian.

Description

Durio trees are large rainforest trees, sometimes exceeding 40 m in height, with oblong leaves that are glossy green above and scaly golden or silvery beneath. Their flowers are borne in clusters on the trunk and larger branches (cauliflory), are strongly scented, and are pollinated by bats. The fruits are large, woody capsules covered in sharp spines, splitting into segments of soft, pungent flesh.

The durian fruit

The fruit of Durio zibethinus is celebrated in Southeast Asia as the "king of fruits" and notorious for its powerful odour, which has led to it being banned from many hotels and public transport systems. Other edible species include D. graveolens (red-fleshed durian), D. dulcis and D. kutejensis (lai). Wild species are an important food source for animals such as orangutans, elephants and hornbills.

Taxonomy

The genus was named by Michel Adanson in 1763, the name deriving from the Malay word duri, meaning "thorn". Some botanists have split the genus, placing species in groups such as Boschia, but Durio in the broad sense remains the usual treatment.
