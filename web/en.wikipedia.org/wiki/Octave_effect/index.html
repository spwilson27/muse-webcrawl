Octave effect

The octave effect is a phenomenon reported in bioelectromagnetics in which exposure to sinusoidal magnetic fields alternating at specific low frequencies is said to influence ion transport in biological systems. The closely related concept, sometimes called the octave bandwidth effect, holds that biological systems respond to electromagnetic fields in a frequency-dependent, nonlinear manner, so that only certain frequency "windows" — often spaced an octave apart — produce measurable effects.

Background

The effect grew out of research beginning in the 1970s by W. Ross Adey, Suzanne Bawin, and others on calcium efflux from chick brain tissue exposed to amplitude-modulated radiofrequency fields. They reported that efflux changed only for certain modulation frequencies, suggesting windowed rather than linear dose–response relationships. Later work by Carl Blackman and colleagues expanded the idea of frequency and intensity windows.

Interpretation and criticism

Proponents connect the phenomenon to theories of ion cyclotron resonance and ion parametric resonance, in which ions in static and oscillating magnetic fields interact preferentially at particular frequency combinations. The existence and reproducibility of such window effects have been disputed, and mainstream reviews regard the evidence for low-level electromagnetic bioeffects of this kind as inconclusive. The octave effect remains a niche and controversial topic within biophysics.
