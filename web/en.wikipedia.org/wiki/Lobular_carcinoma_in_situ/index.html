Lobular carcinoma in situ

Lobular carcinoma in situ
SpecialtyOncology, pathology
ClassificationICD-10: D05.0; ICD-O: 8520/2
Related termsLobular neoplasia, atypical lobular hyperplasia
Risk significanceMarker of increased bilateral breast cancer risk

Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) is an incidental microscopic finding characterised by a proliferation of malignant-appearing but non-invasive cells within the lobules of the breast. Despite its name, LCIS is not generally considered a true cancer or an obligate precursor of invasive carcinoma; rather, it is regarded as a marker of a substantially increased risk of developing invasive breast cancer in either breast — on the order of seven to twelve times that of the general population, with an absolute risk of roughly 1–2% per year.

Pathology

LCIS, described by Foote and Stewart in 1941, consists of distended acini filled with relatively uniform, dyscohesive cells that have lost expression of the cell-adhesion molecule E-cadherin — the same molecular feature characteristic of invasive lobular carcinoma. Classic LCIS is divided into type A and type B forms by cytology. Pleomorphic LCIS, a less common variant with high-grade nuclei and sometimes necrosis, is treated more aggressively, usually like ductal carcinoma in situ. LCIS together with atypical lobular hyperplasia forms the spectrum termed lobular neoplasia.

Diagnosis

LCIS produces no palpable mass and is generally invisible on mammography, though it may be associated with calcifications. It is usually discovered incidentally in a biopsy performed for another reason. Because it is often multicentric and bilateral, finding it in one breast indicates risk in both.

Management

Contemporary management of classic LCIS found on core biopsy (when imaging and pathology are concordant) is usually active surveillance rather than surgery, combined with risk-reducing strategies: regular clinical and imaging follow-up, and endocrine chemoprevention with tamoxifen, raloxifene or aromatase inhibitors, which roughly halves subsequent cancer risk. Bilateral prophylactic mastectomy is now rarely chosen for classic LCIS alone. Pleomorphic LCIS and LCIS with comedonecrosis are generally excised with clear margins, as for DCIS.
