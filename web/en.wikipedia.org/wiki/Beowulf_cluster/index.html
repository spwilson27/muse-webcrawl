Beowulf cluster

A Beowulf cluster is a computer cluster built from commodity off-the-shelf hardware — ordinary personal computers or servers — connected by a local network and running free and open-source software such as Linux, in order to perform parallel computations cheaply. The concept originated in 1994 at NASA, and "Beowulf" has since become a generic term for this do-it-yourself style of supercomputing.

History

The first Beowulf was assembled in 1994 by Thomas Sterling and Donald Becker at NASA's Center of Excellence in Space Data and Information Sciences. It consisted of 16 Intel 486 DX4 processors connected by channel-bonded Ethernet and ran Linux. The project demonstrated that useful supercomputing performance could be obtained for a fraction of the cost of a traditional supercomputer. Donald Becker's networking drivers and the community's documentation spread the design widely.

Architecture

A Beowulf cluster typically has one master (head) node that controls and distributes work, and many compute nodes that communicate over a private network. Nodes are usually homogeneous commodity machines. Software libraries such as the Message Passing Interface (MPI) let programs split a task across nodes. Cluster management tools handle provisioning, scheduling and monitoring; popular systems included Scyld, Rocks Cluster Distribution and OSCAR.

Legacy

Beowulf clusters transformed high-performance computing in the late 1990s and 2000s, enabling universities and small labs to build supercomputers in-house. The commodity-cluster architecture they pioneered underlies most modern Top500 supercomputers, which are overwhelmingly Linux-based clusters of standard components.
