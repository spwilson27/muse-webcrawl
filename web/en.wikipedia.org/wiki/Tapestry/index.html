Tapestry

Tapestry is a form of textile art, traditionally woven by hand on a loom. Normally it is used to create images rather than patterns. Tapestry is relatively fragile, and difficult to make, so most historical pieces are intended to hang vertically on a wall (or sometimes in tents), or sometimes horizontally over a piece of furniture such as a table or bed. Some periods made smaller pieces, often long and narrow and used as borders.

Technique

Tapestry is a weft-faced plain weave: all the warp threads are hidden in the completed work. Weavers work from the back of the piece, following a full-size design called a cartoon, building up areas of colour with discontinuous wefts so that pictorial detail can be rendered. Because the image is formed during weaving itself, tapestry differs from embroidery, where decoration is stitched onto an existing ground cloth — the famous Bayeux Tapestry is, strictly, an embroidery.

History

Woven hangings were made in ancient Egypt, pre-Columbian Peru and China. Tapestry reached its European height in the late Middle Ages and Renaissance, when workshops in Flanders — notably Brussels, Tournai and Arras (whose name became a synonym for tapestry in English) — supplied courts across Europe. Famous cycles include the Apocalypse Tapestry at Angers (c. 1377–1382), The Lady and the Unicorn (c. 1500), and the Raphael-designed Acts of the Apostles woven in Brussels for the Sistine Chapel.

The Gobelins Manufactory in Paris, organised under Louis XIV, dominated production in the 17th and 18th centuries. In the 19th century William Morris revived hand tapestry weaving in Britain, and the 20th century saw artists such as Jean Lurçat renew the medium, which continues today in workshops and contemporary art practice.

Function

Besides decoration, tapestries insulated draughty stone rooms, could be rolled and transported between residences, and displayed the wealth and lineage of their owners through heraldry and narrative scenes from history, mythology and the Bible.
