Supercharger

A supercharger is an air compressor that increases the pressure or density of air supplied to an internal combustion engine. This gives each intake cycle of the engine more oxygen, letting it burn more fuel and do more work, thus increasing the power output.

A Roots-type supercharger, showing the twin intermeshing rotors

Power for the supercharger can be provided mechanically by means of a belt, gear, shaft, or chain connected to the engine's crankshaft. When power is provided by a turbine powered by exhaust gas, a supercharger is known as a turbosupercharger – typically referred to simply as a turbocharger.

History

In 1860, brothers Philander and Francis Marion Roots of Connersville, Indiana, patented the design for an air mover for use in blast furnaces, later known as the Roots blower. The supercharger's early automotive use came in the 1920s, when manufacturers such as Mercedes-Benz fitted Roots-type blowers to production cars. Aircraft engines adopted supercharging widely during the Second World War to maintain power at high altitude, where the air is thinner.

Types

Superchargers are commonly classified by their method of air delivery. Positive-displacement types — including the Roots, twin-screw (Lysholm), and sliding vane designs — deliver a nearly constant volume of air per revolution. Dynamic compressors, such as the centrifugal supercharger, rely on accelerating the air with an impeller and do not deliver significant boost at low engine speeds.

Comparison with turbocharging

Because a supercharger is driven directly by the crankshaft, it consumes some of the engine's power, but provides boost immediately with no lag. A turbocharger, driven by exhaust energy that would otherwise be wasted, is generally more efficient but suffers from turbo lag — a delay in boost delivery while the turbine spools up. Some engines, such as the Volkswagen 1.4 TSI "Twincharger", combine both systems.

Applications

Superchargers are used in production automobiles, drag racing (where Roots blowers are iconic on Top Fuel dragsters), aircraft piston engines, and marine engines. Electrically driven superchargers, powered by 48-volt systems rather than the crankshaft, have also entered production in mild-hybrid vehicles.
