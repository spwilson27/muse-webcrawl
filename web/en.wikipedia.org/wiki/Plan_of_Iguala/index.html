Plan of Iguala

The Plan of Iguala, proclaimed on 24 February 1821 in the town of Iguala (in present-day Guerrero), was the programme that achieved Mexican independence from Spain. Drafted by the royalist officer Agustín de Iturbide in agreement with the insurgent leader Vicente Guerrero, it united former enemies behind three guarantees: religion, independence and union.

The Three Guarantees

The plan's twenty-four articles rested on three principles, symbolised by the colours of the new national flag (green, white and red) and defended by the Army of the Three Guarantees:

  • Religion: Roman Catholicism would be the sole, official religion of the new state.
  • Independence: Mexico would be a sovereign, constitutional monarchy, its crown offered to Ferdinand VII of Spain or another Bourbon prince.
  • Union: all inhabitants — Spaniards, creoles, mestizos, indigenous people and Africans — would be equal citizens of the new nation, ending the caste system in law.

Course of events

The plan won rapid support across New Spain, reconciling conservative royalists alarmed by liberal revolution in Spain with the remaining insurgents. Town after town adhered; the new viceroy, Juan O'Donojú, recognised the inevitable and signed the Treaty of Córdoba on 24 August 1821 accepting Mexican independence. Iturbide entered Mexico City on 27 September, and the Act of Independence was signed the following day. When no Bourbon prince came, Iturbide was himself proclaimed Emperor Agustín I in 1822.

Legacy

The Plan of Iguala is remembered as the founding compromise of the Mexican state: independence achieved with minimal further bloodshed, though its guarantees of Catholic exclusivity and monarchy were soon overtaken by republican and liberal developments.
