Ecclesiology

Ecclesiology
FieldChristian theology
MeaningThe study of the Church: its nature, origins, structure, and mission
From Greekekklēsia (assembly/church) + logos (study)
Related topicsEcclesiastical polity, sacraments, ministry, ecumenism

Ecclesiology is the theological study of the Christian Church — its origins, its relationship to Jesus Christ, its role in salvation, its discipline, its destiny, and its leadership. Within Christianity there are a variety of perspectives regarding each of these matters, and ecclesiological differences have historically been among the most divisive issues between denominations, because the question "what is the Church?" determines where the Church is found and who belongs to it.

Key questions

Ecclesiology addresses the nature of the Church (often explored through biblical images such as the Body of Christ, the People of God, the Bride of Christ, and the temple of the Holy Spirit), its marks or identifying attributes — classically the four marks of the Nicene Creed: one, holy, catholic, and apostolic — and the relation between the visible, institutional church and the invisible communion of all believers. It also covers the Church's mission, its sacraments or ordinances, worship, and polity and ministry.

Denominational perspectives

Roman Catholic ecclesiology centres on the Church as a visible, hierarchical society in communion with the Pope, developed notably at the Second Vatican Council in Lumen gentium, which also described the Church as the People of God and taught that the Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church. Eastern Orthodox ecclesiology emphasises the local eucharistic community gathered around its bishop and the communion of autocephalous churches. Protestant traditions vary widely: confessional churches ground ecclesiology in the preaching of the Word and right administration of the sacraments (Augsburg Confession VII), while free-church traditions stress the gathered, voluntary community of believers.

Modern developments

The twentieth century saw ecclesiology transformed by the ecumenical movement, which produced convergence texts such as the World Council of Churches' Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (1982), and by liberation, feminist, and global theologies that reframed the Church's mission in social and contextual terms.
