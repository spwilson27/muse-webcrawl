Disc jockey

A disc jockey, more commonly abbreviated as DJ, is a person who plays recorded music for an audience. Types of DJs include radio DJs, club DJs (who work in nightclubs or music festivals), mobile DJs (hired for weddings and parties), and turntablists, who use turntables to manipulate sounds and create new music.

Disc jockey
Occupation: Music performer / broadcaster
Tools: Turntables, CDJs, mixer, controller, software

History

The term was coined in the 1930s for radio announcers who introduced phonograph records on air; Walter Winchell is credited with popularising it in 1935. In the 1940s and 1950s radio DJs became influential hit-makers. In the 1970s, disco and then hip hop transformed the club DJ's role: Jamaican sound systems, Bronx pioneers such as DJ Kool Herc, Afrika Bambaataa and Grandmaster Flash developed breakbeat DJing, scratching and beatmatching.

Techniques

DJs select and sequence tracks, beatmatch tempos, mix with crossfaders and equalisers, and may scratch, loop, sample and apply effects. Modern DJs often use digital vinyl systems, CDJs, controllers and software such as Serato, Rekordbox, Traktor and Ableton Live.

Types

  • Radio DJs — present music programmes and talk between songs.
  • Club / festival DJs — perform sets for dancing crowds; headline electronic dance music acts command large fees.
  • Mobile DJs — provide music and MC services at private events.
  • Turntablists — treat the turntable as an instrument, competing in battles such as the DMC World Championships.

Culture

DJ culture underpins disco, house, techno, drum and bass, dubstep and most electronic dance music. Magazines such as DJ Mag publish an annual Top 100 DJs poll. Notable DJs include Larry Levan, Frankie Knuckles, Carl Cox, Daft Punk, Tiësto, Calvin Harris and Peggy Gou.
