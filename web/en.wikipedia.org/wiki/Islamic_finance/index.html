Islamic finance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Islamic finance refers to financial activities that conform to Sharia (Islamic law). Its core prohibitions are riba (interest or usury), gharar (excessive uncertainty in contracts) and investment in forbidden sectors (alcohol, pork, gambling, conventional interest-based finance, armaments in some standards). In their place the industry uses risk-sharing and asset-backed structures: mudaraba (trust financing), musharaka (joint venture), murabaha (cost-plus sale), ijara (leasing) and sukuk (certificates representing shares in assets, often compared to bonds).

The modern industry dates from the 1960s–70s (Mit Ghamr savings bank in Egypt, 1963; the Islamic Development Bank and Dubai Islamic Bank, 1975), grew with Gulf oil wealth, and spread to Malaysia and to Western financial centres (London sukuk issues; Islamic windows of conventional banks). Global assets are commonly estimated in the trillions of dollars (on the order of US$3–4 trillion in the early 2020s, concentrated in Iran, Saudi Arabia and Malaysia). Scholars debate whether the instruments differ substantively from conventional finance — “Sharia arbitrage” — or express a genuine alternative ethic of risk-sharing; Sharia boards of scholars certify compliance.
