Tow hitch

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tow hitch (or tow bar in British English) is a device attached to the chassis of a vehicle for towing, or a towbar is a device using a removable trailing arm together with a hitch that mounts to the vehicle. A tow hitch is not required to allow towing; it allows safer towing in the case of large trailers. Tow hitches are also used to mount accessories such as bicycle racks and cargo carriers.

Types

Receiver hitch

The most common type in North America is the receiver hitch, consisting of a square receiver tube mounted to the vehicle frame into which a removable ball mount, pintle hook or accessory shank is inserted and secured with a pin and clip. Receiver openings are standardised in sizes of 1¼ inch, 2 inch, 2½ inch and 3 inch.

Fixed drawbar and tow ball

In Europe and Australasia, the tow ball — commonly 50 mm in diameter — is usually fixed to a tow bar by two bolts or formed as a swan-neck or detachable neck. The trailer coupling fits over the ball and is secured by a lever or screw mechanism. Retractable and detachable tow bars are common, both to protect pedestrians and because some regulations restrict protruding fittings.

Fifth wheel and gooseneck

Heavy trailers are commonly towed with a fifth-wheel coupling mounted in the bed of a pickup truck, or a gooseneck hitch in which a ball in the truck bed couples to a downward tube on the trailer. These place the pivot point over or slightly ahead of the rear axle, improving stability. See also trailer.

Weight classes

In North America, receiver hitches are divided into classes by towing capacity: Class I (up to 2,000 lb gross trailer weight), Class II (up to 3,500 lb), Class III (up to 8,000 lb), Class IV (up to 10,000 lb) and Class V (above 10,000 lb). The tongue weight — the downward force exerted by the trailer coupler — is normally around 10–15% of the gross trailer weight and must also fall within the hitch and vehicle ratings.

Tow balls and couplers

North American tow balls are made in diameters of 1⅞ in, 2 in, 2⁵⁄₁₆ in and 3 in. The 50 mm ball is standard in Europe, Australia and New Zealand. The coupler must match the ball size; using a mismatched coupler risks detachment. Safety chains are crossed under the coupler to cradle the tongue if the coupling fails, and a breakaway cable applies the trailer brakes if the trailer separates from the tow vehicle.

Electrical connections

Towing also requires an electrical connection for trailer lights and, where fitted, electric brakes. Common connectors include the 4-way flat plug (tail, brake and turn lights and ground) and the 7-way blade connector used in North America, which adds electric brake, reverse and auxiliary power circuits; Europe uses 7-pin (ISO 1724) and 13-pin (ISO 11446) connectors.

Regulation

In the European Union, tow bars for passenger cars must be type-approved under Directive 94/20/EC and later regulations, and many vehicles require dedicated wiring kits so that trailer stability programs and parking sensors function correctly. In the United States, hitch ratings follow SAE J684 standards, and manufacturers' towing ratings must not be exceeded.
