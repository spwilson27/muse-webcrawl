Cytogenetics is the branch of genetics and cell biology that studies the structure, number, and function of chromosomes, traditionally by examining them under a microscope. It bridges cytology and genetics and is a mainstay of clinical diagnosis, cancer characterization, and evolutionary biology.

History

The field grew from late-19th-century observations of chromosomes; Walther Flemming's work on mitosis and the Boveri–Sutton chromosome theory laid the groundwork. A landmark came in 1956, when Tjio and Levan established that humans have 46 chromosomes. Within a few years, trisomy 21 was identified as the cause of Down syndrome (1959) and the Philadelphia chromosome was described in chronic myeloid leukemia (1960).

Techniques

Classical cytogenetics prepares a karyotype: dividing cells are arrested in metaphase, stained (for example G-banding with Giemsa), and chromosomes are arranged in pairs to detect aneuploidies and structural rearrangements such as deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations. Molecular cytogenetics adds fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with locus-specific probes, multicolor karyotyping (SKY/M-FISH), and chromosomal microarray analysis for submicroscopic copy-number changes.

Clinical applications

Cytogenetic testing is used in prenatal diagnosis (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling), investigation of developmental disorders and recurrent pregnancy loss, and extensively in hematologic malignancies, where specific translocations — such as t(9;22) BCR–ABL1 — define diagnosis and guide therapy.
