Royal Proclamation of 1763

The Proclamation Line along the Appalachian divide

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 was issued by King George III on 7 October 1763, following the British acquisition of French territory in North America at the end of the Seven Years' War. It reorganized Britain's new North American empire and regulated relations with Indigenous peoples.

Provisions

The proclamation created four new colonies — Quebec, East Florida, West Florida and Grenada — and drew a boundary line along the crest of the Appalachian Mountains. Colonists were forbidden to settle or purchase land west of the line; the territory beyond was reserved as "Indian Territory" for Native American nations. Crucially, it established that only the Crown could purchase Indigenous lands, and only through public meetings — a framework that recognized Indigenous title to their lands.

Consequences

Land-hungry colonists, speculators and veterans who had been promised western grants deeply resented the restrictions, and the proclamation became one of the grievances feeding the American Revolution. In Canada, by contrast, it remains a foundational document: it is referenced in Section 25 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, and First Nations regard it as a charter of their rights, sometimes called an "Indian Magna Carta". Its principles were elaborated in the Treaty of Niagara of 1764.
