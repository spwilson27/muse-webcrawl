The Comitium (Latin: comitium, from coire, "to come together") was the original open-air public meeting place of ancient Rome, occupying the north-western part of the Roman Forum below the Capitoline Hill. It was the venue for the comitia curiata and later the tribal assemblies in their early form, for trials, public speeches and the ceremonial business of the Roman people. The word became the generic term in Roman law for a duly convened political assembly (comitia).

History and layout

Tradition placed the Comitium's origins in the regal period, when Rome's thirty curiae met there to ratify kings and laws. The space was oriented to the cardinal points and treated as an inaugurated templum. Adjacent stood the Curia Hostilia, the senate house, and from the mid-Republic the Rostra, the speaker's platform decorated with the prows (rostra) of captured ships from Antium (338 BC). Nearby landmarks included the Lapis Niger, a black marble pavement over an archaic shrine complex (possibly associated with Romulus or the early kings), the Vulcanal, and the Graecostasis, where foreign envoys waited.

As Rome grew, popular assemblies increasingly met in the Forum proper or on the Campus Martius, and Julius Caesar's reorganisation of the Forum (from 44 BC) paved over much of the Comitium, replacing the old Rostra and moving the Curia (Curia Julia). Excavations from 1899 onward under Giacomo Boni revealed the Lapis Niger pavement and the underlying archaic cippus with one of the oldest Latin inscriptions.
