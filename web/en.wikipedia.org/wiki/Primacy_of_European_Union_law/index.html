The primacy of European Union law (sometimes also referred to as the supremacy of European law) is a legal principle establishing that European Union law takes precedence over the national law of member states, including their constitutions. It is a judge-made doctrine of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU), not written into the treaties themselves; a declaration annexed to the Lisbon Treaty merely recalls the case law.

Development

In Van Gend en Loos (1963) the Court held that the Community constituted a "new legal order" whose rules could confer rights on individuals enforceable in national courts (direct effect). A year later, in Costa v ENEL (1964), the Court reasoned that member states had limited their sovereign rights and created a body of law binding on themselves and their nationals, so that a later unilateral national measure could not prevail against Community law. In Internationale Handelsgesellschaft (1970) primacy was extended over national constitutional fundamental-rights provisions, and in Simmenthal (1978) the Court held that every national court must set aside, of its own motion, any conflicting national law — even law adopted later — without waiting for a constitutional court to strike it down.

Tension with national courts

National constitutional courts have generally accepted primacy in practice while reserving limits: the German Federal Constitutional Court's Solange jurisprudence conditions acceptance on equivalent fundamental-rights protection, and ultra-vires and constitutional-identity review. Conflicts sharpened in the 2010s–2020s: the German court's 2020 PSPP judgment declared a CJEU ruling ultra vires (the first such finding), and the Polish Constitutional Tribunal's 2021 ruling rejected primacy over the Polish constitution for parts of the EU treaties, prompting infringement proceedings and withheld EU funds. Similar reservations have been voiced by constitutional courts in Denmark (Ajos), the Czech Republic, Romania, and France.
