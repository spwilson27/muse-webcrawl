Arena rock

Arena rock (also known as stadium rock, pomp rock, and corporate rock) is a style of rock music that emerged in the mid-1970s, designed for performance in large arenas and stadiums. It is characterised by slick production, anthemic choruses, power ballads, prominent keyboards and guitars, and mass sing-along appeal, and was dominated by commercially successful acts such as Journey, Boston, Foreigner, Styx, REO Speedwagon, Toto, and Kansas.

Origins and characteristics

As rock concerts moved from theatres to sports arenas during the 1970s, bands adapted their sound to fill huge spaces: songs became simpler and more emphatic, with big drums, layered harmonies, and choruses built for crowd participation. The development of album-oriented rock (AOR) radio rewarded the polished, melodic style. Signature recordings include Boston's debut album (1976), Journey's Escape (1981) with "Don't Stop Believin'", Foreigner's 4 (1981), and Styx's Paradise Theatre (1981). Queen, Led Zeppelin, The Who, and Pink Floyd are often cited as earlier or parallel exemplars of stadium-scale rock.

Criticism

"Corporate rock" was coined as a pejorative by critics who saw the music as formulaic, overproduced, and calculated for maximum sales, in contrast to the rawness of punk and the experimentation of progressive rock. The arena rock sound was a common target of the alternative and grunge movements of the early 1990s, which defined themselves partly in opposition to it.

Legacy

Despite the criticism, arena rock songs have proved enduringly popular, propelled by film, television, sports events, and streaming — "Don't Stop Believin'" became one of the best-selling digital tracks of the twentieth century. The style influenced later stadium acts and the big-chorus approach of much mainstream pop-rock.
