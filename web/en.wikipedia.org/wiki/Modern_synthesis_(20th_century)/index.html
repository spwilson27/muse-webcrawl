Modern synthesis (20th century)

The modern synthesis united Darwinian selection with Mendelian genetics

The modern synthesis was the early-20th-century reconciliation of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection with Gregor Mendel's theory of genetics. Achieved mainly between about 1918 and the 1940s, it established the population-genetic framework that still underpins evolutionary biology.

Development

Around 1900, Mendelian genetics and Darwinian gradualism were widely seen as incompatible, and mutationism and other alternatives flourished. Mathematicians Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane and Sewall Wright showed in the 1910s–1930s that Mendelian inheritance is fully consistent with continuous variation and selection; Fisher's The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection (1930) was foundational. Field naturalists and systematists then integrated the mathematics: Theodosius Dobzhansky's Genetics and the Origin of Species (1937), Ernst Mayr's work on speciation, Julian Huxley's Evolution: The Modern Synthesis (1942) — which gave the synthesis its name — and G. G. Simpson's palaeontological synthesis completed the unification.

Core ideas

The synthesis holds that evolution is the change in allele frequencies within populations, driven by selection, mutation, gene flow and genetic drift; that most heritable variation is genetic; that speciation usually occurs through the gradual accumulation of differences in geographically separated populations; and that macroevolutionary patterns can be explained by these microevolutionary processes.

Later developments

Since the 1970s, findings on neutral drift, punctuated equilibrium, evo-devo, horizontal gene transfer and epigenetics have prompted calls for an "extended evolutionary synthesis," though the extent to which the framework needs revision remains debated.
