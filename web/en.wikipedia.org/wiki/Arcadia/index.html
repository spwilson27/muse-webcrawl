Arcadia (Greek: Ἀρκαδία, Arkadia) is a region in the central Peloponnese. It takes its name from the mythological figure Arcas, and in Greek mythology it was the home of the gods Hermes and Pan. In European Renaissance arts, Arcadia was celebrated as an unspoiled, harmonious wilderness; as such, it was referenced in popular culture.

The modern regional unit of Arcadia covers part of the historical region and is situated in the central and eastern part of the Peloponnese peninsula. Its capital is Tripoli.

History

Arcadia was a region in the central Peloponnese whose inhabitants, the Arcadians, were regarded in antiquity as an aboriginal people who had lived in the area before the arrival of other Greek tribes. The mountainous terrain meant that Arcadia took no dominant role in Greek history; its towns, such as Tegea and Mantineia, fought mainly local wars. The region later formed the core of the Arcadian League, founded in the 370s BC after the Spartan defeat at Leuctra, with the new city of Megalopolis as its capital.

During the Roman period Arcadia declined, and in the Middle Ages its castles were contested by Byzantines, Franks and Ottomans. The region played a notable part in the Greek War of Independence in the 1820s.

Arcadia in culture

Arcadia has remained a subject in pastoral poetry, sometimes referred to in English poetry as Arcady. The pastoral ideal of Arcadia — a vision of pastoralism and harmony with nature — was developed in classical antiquity and revived in the Renaissance. The Roman poet Virgil's Eclogues set idealized shepherd life in Arcadia, and the image was taken up by poets such as Jacopo Sannazaro, Sir Philip Sidney and Torquato Tasso. The Latin phrase Et in Arcadia ego ("Even in Arcadia, I [Death] exist"), famously painted by Nicolas Poussin, became a meditation on mortality within the pastoral paradise.

Geography

Arcadia is almost landlocked and mountainous, ringed by Mount Erymanthus, Mount Cyllene, Mount Maenalus, Mount Parnon and the Taygetus range. Rivers include the Alpheios, the Ladon and the Erymanthos. Its rugged isolation in antiquity gave rise to the proverbial image of the Arcadians as rustic shepherds.
