Mummers' plays are folk plays performed by troupes of amateur actors, traditionally all male, known as mummers, guisers or pace-eggers, in villages and towns across Britain and Ireland — and, carried by emigration, in Newfoundland and elsewhere. The performances traditionally take place around Christmas and the New Year, when the troupe goes from house to house, pub to pub or performs in the street, collecting money. Costumes are often rough and emblematic — strips of paper or rags, tall hats, blackened or masked faces — and the texts, passed down orally and in chapbooks, are delivered in broad rhyming declamation.

The hero-combat plot

The most widespread type is the hero-combat play. A presenter (often Father Christmas) introduces the hero — usually Saint George — who boasts and then fights an adversary such as the Turkish Knight, a dragon or a rival champion. One combatant is killed, to general lamentation, whereupon a comic Doctor enters, boasts of his travels and marvellous cures, and revives the corpse with a magic medicine. The play closes with songs, further characters such as Beelzebub or Little Devil Doubt demanding money, and a collection. Other types include the sword-dance plays of northern England, in which a character is symbolically "beheaded" by a lock of swords and similarly restored.

History and revival

Although earlier antiquarians attributed the plays to pagan fertility ritual, documentary evidence is mostly early modern and later: texts survive in quantity from the 18th and 19th centuries, and related customs appear in early records of mumming as seasonal disguise-visiting. The plays declined with the First World War and rural depopulation but were extensively collected by folklorists — the work of collectors such as Alex Helm documented hundreds of local versions — and revived from the mid-20th century by folk clubs and morris teams; many villages now perform their own recovered play each year as a fixture of the Christmas season.
