Ghost Dance

The Ghost Dance (Caddo: Nanissáanah, also called the Ghost Dance of 1890) was a religious movement incorporated into numerous Native American belief systems. According to the teachings of the Northern Paiute spiritual leader Wovoka (renamed Jack Wilson), proper practice of the circle dance would reunite the living with the spirits of the dead, bring the spirits to fight on their behalf, end American westward expansion, and bring peace, prosperity and unity to Native American peoples throughout the region. The movement spread rapidly among the peoples of the Great Plains, and fear of it among US officials contributed to the events culminating in the Wounded Knee Massacre of 1890.

Origins: Wodziwob and Wovoka

An earlier Ghost Dance movement arose in 1869–70 around the Northern Paiute prophet Wodziwob in Nevada, part of a longer pattern of prophetic movements in the Great Basin that included the teachings of Tavibo. In 1889 Wovoka, Tavibo's son, fell ill during a solar eclipse and reported a vision in which he was taken to heaven and shown a renewed land where the dead lived happily; he returned teaching that if Native peoples lived peacefully, worked, refrained from violence and performed the round dance at intervals, the earth would be renewed, the buffalo would return and the dead would be resurrected.

Spread among the Plains peoples

Delegations from many tribes — including Lakota, Cheyenne, Arapaho, Kiowa and Caddo — travelled to Nevada to learn the dance and carried it home in 1890. Among the Lakota, the teachings were adapted by emissaries such as Kicking Bear and Short Bull to their desperate situation after the destruction of the buffalo herds and the loss of land: some Lakota dancers wore "ghost shirts" believed to repel bullets. The anthropologist James Mooney investigated the movement for the Bureau of Ethnology and published a sympathetic classic study in 1896.

Wounded Knee and aftermath

Alarmed Indian agents demanded suppression; the arrest of Sitting Bull on 15 December 1890 ended with his killing by Indian police. Two weeks later, on 29 December 1890, US cavalry killed some 250–300 Lakota, many of them women and children, at Wounded Knee Creek — an event often taken as the end of the Ghost Dance movement in its 1890 form. The dance nonetheless persisted in some communities and fed into later Native religious life, including elements of the Native American Church.
