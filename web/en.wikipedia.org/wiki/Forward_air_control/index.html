Forward air control

Forward air control is the provision of guidance to close air support (CAS) aircraft intended to ensure that their attack hits the intended target and does not injure friendly troops. This task is carried out by a forward air controller (FAC; in modern U.S. usage on the ground, a Joint Terminal Attack Controller, or JTAC). A primary function of the forward air controller is to ensure the safety of friendly troops during close air support: because friendly and enemy positions may be only metres apart ("danger close"), the FAC identifies targets, marks them, coordinates the attack and authorises weapons release.

History

Air–ground coordination began in the First World War, with infantry-contact patrols and ground panels and flares signalling positions to aircraft. The role matured in the Second World War — British and Commonwealth forces in North Africa and Burma, and US "Rover" controllers in Italy, vectored fighter-bombers onto targets. In the Korean War, airborne controllers in T-6 "Mosquito" aircraft pioneered the modern FAC system. The Vietnam War made the FAC central: slow, light aircraft such as the Cessna O-1 Bird Dog and O-2 Skymaster, and later the OV-10 Bronco and fast FAC jets, directed strikes in close support of troops and along the Ho Chi Minh trail, including the famous Raven FACs in Laos. From the 1990s onward, precision-guided munitions, laser designators and digital data links reshaped the task, and the ground role was standardised as the JTAC within NATO.

Methods

The controller identifies and marks the target — historically with smoke rockets, today more often with laser designation, infrared pointers or coordinates transmitted digitally — passes the attack aircraft a standardised brief (the "nine-line"), conducts a "talk-on" from known landmarks to the target, and issues clearance ("cleared hot") only when satisfied the correct target is identified and friendly forces are safe. Airborne FACs (FAC-A) fly dedicated or dual-role aircraft to control strikes in areas without ground controllers.
