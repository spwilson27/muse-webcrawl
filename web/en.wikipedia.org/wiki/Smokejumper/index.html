Smokejumper

A smokejumper is a wildland firefighter who parachutes into a remote area to combat wildfires. Smokejumpers are highly trained and experienced firefighters who can reach inaccessible fires quickly, often being the first responders to new ignitions in backcountry terrain. They are employed in the United States by the U.S. Forest Service and the Bureau of Land Management, and similar units exist in Russia (where the practice originated) and Canada.

History

Aerial firefighting with parachutists was first developed in the Soviet Union in the 1930s. In the United States, the Forest Service experimented with parachute delivery of firefighters beginning in 1939, with the first operational jumps made in 1940 in the Nez Perce and Chelan National Forests. The program expanded during the Second World War, when conscientious objectors in the Civilian Public Service served as smokejumpers.

The 1949 Mann Gulch fire in Montana, in which thirteen firefighters died (twelve of them smokejumpers), became famous through Norman Maclean's book Young Men and Fire.

Operations

Smokejumpers typically work in small crews of two to ten. After parachuting in, they receive their tools and supplies by cargo drop and attack the fire, aiming to contain it while it is still small. Once the fire is controlled, they may hike out or be picked up by helicopter. Smokejumpers must pass rigorous physical fitness standards and complete intensive training in parachuting, fire behaviour and wilderness survival.

In the United States there are smokejumper bases in Montana, Idaho, Oregon, California, Alaska, Washington and Arizona, employing several hundred jumpers each fire season.
