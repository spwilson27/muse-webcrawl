Legalism (theology)

Legalism is contrasted with grace in Christian theology

In Christian theology, legalism is a usually pejorative term for the belief — or the pattern of practice — that salvation or right standing before God is earned by strict adherence to moral and ritual law, or the imposition of rules beyond what Scripture requires. It is typically contrasted with the doctrine of salvation by grace through faith, and sometimes paired with its opposite error, antinomianism (the rejection of moral law altogether).

Usage

The term is applied in several related senses. First, it can mean attempting to be justified by works of the law — the position Paul of Tarsus is traditionally read as opposing in his epistles to the Galatians and Romans. Second, it describes adding human traditions to divine commands, the charge brought against the Pharisees in the Gospels for "teaching as doctrines the commandments of men". Third, in pastoral and popular usage, it labels any rigid, rule-centred approach to the Christian life that measures spirituality by external conformity — dress, diet, Sabbath observance, or abstinence rules — rather than by faith and love.

Debates

Because "legalism" is a term of criticism, its application is contested. Movements emphasising holiness, obedience, and discipline often respond that obedience is the fruit, not the root, of salvation, and that the label unfairly pathologises ordinary moral teaching. The New Perspective on Paul, associated with scholars such as E. P. Sanders and N. T. Wright, has further complicated the picture by arguing that Second Temple Judaism was not itself a legalistic religion of works-righteousness, prompting re-examination of what Paul was actually opposing. Analogous debates about law, grace, and merit occur in other religious traditions, though the term itself is chiefly Christian.
