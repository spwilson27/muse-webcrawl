Metascience

Metascience (also known as meta-research) is the use of scientific methodology to study science itself. Metascience seeks to increase the quality of scientific research while reducing waste. It studies how research is done, reported, rewarded, and evaluated, and seeks to improve practices across all fields of science.

History

The roots of metascience lie in the sociology and philosophy of science, in scientometrics — the quantitative study of science, including citation analysis and bibliometrics — and in mid-20th-century concerns about the reliability of published research. The field gained prominence in the 2010s amid the replication crisis, when large-scale attempts to reproduce published findings in psychology, economics, and biomedical research found that many results could not be replicated.

A landmark contribution was John Ioannidis's 2005 paper "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False," which argued mathematically that small sample sizes, small effect sizes, flexibility in analysis, and publication bias combine to make most positive findings in many fields false positives. Metascience institutes and centres were subsequently founded at several universities.

Areas of study

Metascience is commonly divided into five overlapping areas:

  • Methods — how studies are performed, including experimental design and statistics;
  • Reporting — how research is communicated, including selective reporting and incomplete methods sections;
  • Reproducibility — whether results can be verified by independent researchers;
  • Evaluation — how science is peer-reviewed and assessed;
  • Incentives — how reward structures (funding, hiring, promotion) shape researcher behaviour.

Problems identified

Metascientists have documented problems including publication bias (the tendency for positive results to be published over null results), p-hacking and HARKing (hypothesising after results are known), low statistical power, lack of data and code sharing, and poor peer review. Proposed remedies include preregistration of studies, registered reports, open data and open materials, larger collaborative replications, and reform of research assessment to reward rigour rather than novelty alone.
