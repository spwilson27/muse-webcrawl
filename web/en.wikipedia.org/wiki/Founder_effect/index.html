Founder effect

In population genetics, the founder effect is the loss of genetic variation that occurs when a new population is established by a very small number of individuals from a larger population. First described by Ernst Mayr in 1942, it is a special case of genetic drift: because the founders carry only a sample of the original gene pool, allele frequencies in the new population can differ markedly from the source population, and rare alleles may be lost or become unusually common.

Consequences

Founder populations often show reduced genetic diversity and an increased frequency of otherwise rare heritable disorders. Well-known human examples include the high incidence of certain genetic diseases among the Amish, Ashkenazi Jewish populations, and the inhabitants of Tristan da Cunha. In island biology, the founder effect is thought to contribute to rapid speciation, as small colonising groups diverge genetically from their parent populations — a mechanism central to peripatric speciation and to Mayr's ideas about evolutionary change.

Serial founder effect

A series of founder events, in which each new settlement is founded from the previous one, produces a gradient of declining diversity. This pattern is seen in human genetic data, where diversity decreases with distance from Africa, consistent with successive founder events during the expansion of modern humans.
