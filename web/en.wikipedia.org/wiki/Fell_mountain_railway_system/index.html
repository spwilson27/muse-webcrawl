Fell mountain railway system

The Fell mountain railway system is a system of mountain railway propulsion and braking invented by the English engineer John Barraclough Fell. It uses a raised centre rail between the two running rails, gripped by horizontal driving wheels on the locomotive to provide additional traction and braking on steep gradients.

Design

In the Fell system, smooth horizontal wheels press against both sides of a central rail, supplementing the adhesion of the ordinary driving wheels. The arrangement allowed trains to climb gradients as steep as 1 in 15, far beyond the capability of conventional adhesion working at the time, without the expense of a rack railway.

Applications

The system was first tested in the 1860s and used commercially on the Mont Cenis Pass Railway in the Alps, which operated from 1868 to 1871 as an interim route until the Fréjus Rail Tunnel opened. Its longest-lived application was the Rimutaka Incline in New Zealand (1877–1955), where Fell locomotives hauled trains over the Rimutaka Ranges. The Snaefell Mountain Railway on the Isle of Man still uses the centre rail for braking.
