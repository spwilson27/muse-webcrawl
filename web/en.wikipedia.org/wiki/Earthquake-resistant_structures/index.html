Earthquake-resistant structures

Earthquake-resistant or aseismic structures are designed to protect buildings and other structures to some degree from earthquakes. While no structure can be entirely immune to damage from earthquakes, the goal of earthquake engineering is to erect structures that perform better during seismic activity than their conventional counterparts. According to building codes, earthquake-resistant structures are intended to withstand the largest earthquake of a certain probability that is likely to occur at their location.

Design principles

Seismic design generally provides for ductility — the ability of a structure to deform without collapsing — and a continuous load path that transfers earthquake forces safely to the ground. Key strategies include:

  • Base isolation: supporting the building on flexible bearings (such as lead-rubber bearings) that decouple it from ground motion, lengthening its natural period.
  • Energy dissipation: adding dampers — viscous, friction, or yielding devices — that absorb seismic energy, analogous to automobile shock absorbers.
  • Shear walls and braced frames: stiff vertical elements that resist lateral forces.
  • Regular configuration: simple, symmetrical plans and elevations that avoid torsional irregularities and soft storeys.
  • Lightweight construction: reducing mass reduces the inertial forces the earthquake induces.

Materials and construction

Reinforced concrete detailed with closely spaced confinement steel, structural steel with ductile moment-resisting connections, and confined masonry are widely used in seismic regions. Traditional timber and bamboo construction in parts of Asia has also shown good earthquake performance because of its lightness and flexibility.

Retrofitting

Existing vulnerable buildings can be strengthened by seismic retrofitting: adding shear walls, steel bracing, column jacketing, or base isolators. Such programmes are common in California, Japan, Italy, Turkey and other seismic regions, particularly for unreinforced masonry buildings, hospitals, and schools.

History

Modern seismic design developed after destructive earthquakes of the early 20th century, such as the 1906 San Francisco and 1923 Great Kantō earthquakes, and codes have been progressively strengthened after events including the 1971 San Fernando, 1989 Loma Prieta, 1994 Northridge, 1995 Kobe, and 2011 Christchurch earthquakes.
