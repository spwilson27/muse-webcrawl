Abelian variety


A complex torus as an abelian variety

In mathematics, particularly in algebraic geometry, complex analysis and algebraic number theory, an abelian variety is a projective algebraic variety that is also an algebraic group, i.e., has a group law that can be defined by regular functions. Abelian varieties are at the same time among the most studied objects in algebraic geometry and indispensable tools for much research on algebraic curves and their geometry.

Definition

A complex abelian variety of dimension g is a complex torus of dimension g that admits an embedding into projective space; equivalently, it is a complex torus carrying a Riemann form, a Hermitian form whose imaginary part takes integer values on the period lattice. Over an arbitrary field, an abelian variety is defined as a complete connected algebraic group — completeness being the algebraic analogue of compactness.

Examples and theory

The fundamental one-dimensional example is an elliptic curve. In higher dimensions, the Jacobian variety of an algebraic curve and the Prym variety are central constructions. The theory was developed in the 19th century by Niels Henrik Abel, Carl Jacobi and Bernhard Riemann in connection with the inversion of elliptic and hyperelliptic integrals, and was placed on rigorous foundations in the 20th century by Solomon Lefschetz, André Weil and others. Abelian varieties carry a dual abelian variety, support a rich theory of endomorphisms and polarizations, and figure prominently in the arithmetic of number fields, class field theory and the Langlands program. A celebrated result is the Faltings theorem (Mordell conjecture), proved using the geometry of moduli spaces of abelian varieties.
