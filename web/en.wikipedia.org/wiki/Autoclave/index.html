Autoclave

An autoclave is a machine used to carry out industrial and scientific processes requiring elevated temperature and pressure in relation to ambient pressure and temperature. In medicine and microbiology, autoclaves are used to sterilize equipment and supplies by subjecting them to pressurized saturated steam at 121 °C (250 °F) for around 15–20 minutes, depending on the size of the load and the contents.

History

The steam digester, a prototype of the autoclave, was invented by Denis Papin in 1679. The modern autoclave was developed by Charles Chamberland in 1879 while working with Louis Pasteur. The name comes from the Greek auto- (self) and Latin clavis (key), referring to a self-locking door that pressure seals shut.

Operation

A typical sterilization cycle purges air from the chamber, raises saturated steam to the set temperature and pressure (commonly 121 °C at about 1 bar above atmospheric, or 134 °C for pre-vacuum cycles), holds for a validated time, then exhausts and dries the load. Air removal is critical, since trapped air insulates surfaces from steam. Chemical indicator tape and biological indicators using spores of Geobacillus stearothermophilus verify sterilisation.

Applications

Beyond hospitals and laboratories, autoclaves cure composites in aerospace manufacturing, vulcanise rubber, treat timber, process food in retorts, and sterilise waste. Large composite autoclaves can be many metres long and operate at several hundred degrees Celsius and pressures above 10 bar.
