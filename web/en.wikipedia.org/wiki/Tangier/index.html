Tangier (Arabic: طنجة, Ṭanja) is a city in north-western Morocco, on the coast where the Mediterranean Sea meets the Atlantic Ocean at the western entrance to the Strait of Gibraltar. One of the oldest cities in North Africa, it has been a strategic port and crossroads of cultures for more than two millennia.

History

Founded by Phoenician and Carthaginian settlers as Tingis, the city later became a Roman colony — capital of Mauretania Tingitana — and passed through Vandal, Byzantine and Arab hands; it was conquered for Islam in the early 8th century. Portugal held Tangier from 1471, and it passed to England in 1661 as part of the dowry of Catherine of Braganza, before being abandoned to Morocco in 1684. From 1923 to 1956 Tangier was an International Zone administered jointly by European powers, famous as a haven for spies, writers, smugglers and bohemians, attracting figures such as Paul Bowles, William S. Burroughs and Tennessee Williams.

Modern city

Since the 2000s Tangier has undergone rapid development, centred on Tanger Med, one of Africa's busiest ports, and the Tangier–Casablanca high-speed railway (Al Boraq, opened 2018, the first of its kind in Africa). The city's economy combines shipping, industry (including automotive manufacturing), tourism and its historic medina and kasbah. Its literary and artistic heritage — associated with painters such as Matisse and Delacroix, who stayed here, and with the Beat generation — remains a major draw.
