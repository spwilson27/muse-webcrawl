Documentary photography

Documentary photography usually refers to a popular form of photography used to chronicle events or environments that are relevant and important to history, historical events, and everyday life. It is typically undertaken as professional photojournalism, but may also be an amateur, artistic, or academic pursuit.

Documentary photography
Field: Photography / photojournalism
Key figures: Dorothea Lange, Jacob Riis, Walker Evans

History

Early social documentation includes Jacob Riis's How the Other Half Lives (1890) on New York tenements and Lewis Hine's photographs of child labour, which aided reform. In the 1930s the US Farm Security Administration employed Dorothea Lange (whose "Migrant Mother" became iconic), Walker Evans, Gordon Parks and others to document the Great Depression. Magnum Photos, founded 1947 by Robert Capa, Henri Cartier-Bresson and others, defined post-war humanist documentary.

Characteristics and ethics

Documentary work aims at truthful witness, though staging, selection and captioning raise ethical questions. Distinct from pure photojournalism by its longer-term projects and social intent, it overlaps with street photography and visual anthropology. The rise of smartphones and social media democratised documentary practice while intensifying debates about manipulation and consent.

Notable photographers

Sebastião Salgado, Mary Ellen Mark, James Nachtwey, Steve McCurry, Don McCullin and contemporary practitioners continue the tradition in books, exhibitions and online long-form projects.
