Mu (philosophy)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The character 無 (mu in Japanese, wú in Chinese), "not; nothing".

Mu (Japanese: 無; Korean: 무) or Wu (traditional Chinese: 無; simplified Chinese: 无; pinyin: wú) is a word and concept in East Asian philosophy and religion meaning "not", "nothing", "without", or "non-existence". The character combines the senses of negation and emptiness and appears in Taoist, Buddhist, and especially Zen thought. It is best known in the West as the answer in a famous Zen kōan.

The kōan of Zhaozhou's dog

In the first case of the 13th-century collection The Gateless Barrier (Mumonkan), a monk asks the Chinese master Zhaozhou Congshen (Jōshū): "Has a dog Buddha-nature or not?" Zhaozhou answers: "Mu" — "No", "Not", or simply a refusal of the question's terms. The commentary of Wumen Huikai urges the student to concentrate the whole body and mind on this single word until dualistic thinking is exhausted. In the Rinzai Zen tradition, "Mu" is often the first kōan assigned to a student, and penetrating it is considered an initial awakening (kenshō).

Interpretations

Because Buddha-nature is elsewhere taught to be universal, commentators have read "Mu" not as a plain negative answer but as a pointer beyond affirmation and denial: the question rests on mistaken assumptions, and the proper response is to "unask" it. In this sense Mu has been compared to the answer "the question does not apply". The concept also appears in the opening of Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance by Robert M. Pirsig and throughout popular discussions of Zen.

In Taoism and Chinese thought

The Chinese wú is central to the Tao Te Ching, where the interplay of being (you) and non-being (wu) is said to generate the world: "All things under heaven are born of being; being is born of non-being." Related concepts include wu wei (effortless action) and the Zhuangzi's sceptical emptiness of distinctions.
