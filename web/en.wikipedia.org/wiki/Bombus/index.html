Bombus is a genus of bees, the only extant genus in the tribe Bombini. Its members are known as bumblebees (or humble-bees), of which more than 250 species have been described. They are found primarily in higher latitudes and altitudes in the Northern Hemisphere, although they also occur in South America, where a few lowland tropical species live. European bumblebees have been introduced to New Zealand and Tasmania.

Description

Bumblebees are large, hairy, social bees characterised by black and yellow (sometimes orange or red) warning coloration in bands. The dense pile insulates them, allowing flight at lower temperatures than most bees. Queens are the largest caste; workers are smaller females, and males (drones) appear late in the season. Females carry pollen in a smooth, concave corbicula ("pollen basket") fringed with hairs on the hind leg. Unlike honey bees, a bumblebee's sting is not barbed, so it can sting repeatedly, though it rarely does.

Biology and colonies

Most Bombus species nest socially in colonies founded each spring by a single queen that has overwintered alone. Colonies are annual and typically contain only 50–400 individuals. The queen lays eggs in wax pots stocked with pollen and nectar; workers forage, tend larvae and defend the nest, which is often in an abandoned rodent burrow or tussock. Late in summer the colony produces new queens and males, which mate; the old colony dies out. A minority of species, the cuckoo bumblebees (subgenus Psithyrus), are brood parasites that invade other bumblebees' nests.

Pollination and agriculture

Bumblebees are important pollinators of wild plants and crops. They can "buzz pollinate" (sonicate) flowers such as tomato and blueberry that require vibration to release pollen, and they forage in cooler, wetter weather than honey bees. Since the 1980s, colonies of the buff-tailed bumblebee (B. terrestris) have been reared commercially for greenhouse pollination, especially of tomatoes; escapees have established invasive populations in Japan, South America and elsewhere.

Conservation

Many species have declined through habitat loss, pesticide exposure, disease spillover from managed bees and climate change. Some, such as the rusty patched bumblebee (B. affinis) of North America and Franklin's bumblebee (B. franklini), have become critically endangered; B. franklini has not been recorded since 2006.
