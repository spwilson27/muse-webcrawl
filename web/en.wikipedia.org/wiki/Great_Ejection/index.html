Great Ejection

The Great Ejection followed the Act of Uniformity 1662 in England. Several thousand clergy, dons and schoolmasters — some 2,000 ministers in England and Wales — were ejected from their posts if they did not conform to the requirements of the Act, which required assent to everything contained in the Book of Common Prayer and episcopal ordination for those not already ordained by a bishop.

Background

Following the Restoration of Charles II in 1660, the Cavalier Parliament sought to re-establish the Church of England on the terms favoured by the returning Anglican establishment. The Savoy Conference of 1661 failed to reconcile Presbyterian objections to the Prayer Book. Ministers were required to declare their conformity before St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August 1662; those who refused resigned or were deprived of their livings. Famous ejected ministers included Richard Baxter, John Howe, and Matthew Poole.

Consequences

The Great Ejection marks a foundational event in English Nonconformity: the ejected ministers and their congregations formed the nucleus of the Presbyterian, Congregational, Baptist and Quaker dissenting traditions. Subsequent penal laws, collectively known as the Clarendon Code — including the Conventicle Act and the Five Mile Act — restricted their worship and movement. Full relief came only gradually, with the Toleration Act 1689 permitting most dissenting worship. The date is still commemorated by some English churches descended from the ejected.
