Magnetic susceptibility

Magnetic susceptibility
Symbolχ (chi)
UnitsDimensionless (SI)
DefinitionM = χ H
SignNegative: diamagnetic; Positive: paramagnetic

In electromagnetism, the magnetic susceptibility (Latin: susceptibilis, "receptive"; denoted χ, chi) is a measure of how much a material will become magnetised in an applied magnetic field. It is the ratio of magnetisation M (magnetic moment per unit volume) to the applied magnetic field intensity H: M = χH. Magnetic susceptibility is dimensionless in SI units.

Definition and types

If χ is positive, the material is called paramagnetic: the magnetic field is strengthened inside it, and the magnetisation aligns with the applied field. If χ is negative, the material is diamagnetic: the field is weakened, and the magnetisation opposes the field. Most materials respond weakly; in ferromagnetic materials the susceptibility is very large, positive, and depends nonlinearly on the field, so the simple linear definition applies only approximately.

Measurement

Susceptibility is measured with instruments such as the Gouy balance, Faraday balance, SQUID magnetometers, and Evans balance. It is widely used in geology and archaeology for rapid characterisation of sediments and soils, in chemistry for determining the number of unpaired electrons in complexes, and in medical imaging, where susceptibility-weighted MRI exploits differences in tissue susceptibility.

Temperature dependence

For paramagnetic materials, susceptibility follows Curie's law, varying inversely with absolute temperature; ferromagnetic materials lose their spontaneous magnetisation above the Curie temperature and become paramagnetic.
