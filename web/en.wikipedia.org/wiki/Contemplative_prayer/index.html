Contemplative prayer

Contemplative prayer is a form of Christian prayer and meditation in which the practitioner seeks a direct, restful awareness of God's presence, beyond concepts, words and images. In Catholic tradition it is considered the highest form of prayer: the Catechism of the Catholic Church, following St Teresa of Ávila, defines contemplation as a "gaze of faith, fixed on Jesus", a "silent love" in which the soul rests in God. Contemplative prayer is distinguished from vocal prayer (spoken words) and from discursive meditation (reflection on Scripture or doctrine), though the forms are often seen as stages in a deepening life of prayer.

History

The roots of contemplative prayer lie in the Desert Fathers and Mothers of the 3rd and 4th centuries, who practised stillness (hesychia) in the Egyptian desert, and in monastic traditions of lectio divina. The anonymous 14th-century English work The Cloud of Unknowing taught that God may be reached by love but not by thought. In the 16th century St John of the Cross and Teresa of Ávila gave classic accounts of the contemplative path, including the "dark night of the soul". Eastern Orthodox spirituality developed the Jesus Prayer and the theology of hesychasm, defended by Gregory Palamas.

Modern practice

Twentieth-century writers such as Thomas Merton popularised contemplative prayer beyond monasteries. The Centering Prayer movement, developed by Trappist monks Thomas Keating, William Meninger and Basil Pennington in the 1970s, proposes a simple method: sitting in silence for about twenty minutes, gently returning to a chosen "sacred word" whenever thoughts intrude. The World Community for Christian Meditation, founded from the teaching of John Main, similarly advocates the repetition of a mantra (such as maranatha) in twice-daily periods of meditation. Contemplative practices have also been studied scientifically for their effects on stress and attention, and have drawn both ecumenical interest and criticism from some theologians who question their relation to traditional doctrine.
