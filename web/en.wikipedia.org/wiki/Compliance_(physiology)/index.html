Compliance (physiology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physiology, compliance is a measure of the tendency of a hollow organ to distend — to stretch and increase its volume — in response to an increase in pressure. It is defined as the change in volume divided by the change in pressure:

C = ΔV / ΔP

Compliance is the reciprocal of elastance. It is a key concept in the physiology of the lungs, the cardiovascular system, and other distensible structures such as the bladder and the eye.

Pulmonary compliance

Lung compliance measures the distensibility of the lungs and chest wall. Total pulmonary compliance in a healthy adult is about 200 mL per cmH₂O. Compliance is high in emphysema, where elastic tissue is destroyed, and low in conditions that stiffen the lung, such as pulmonary fibrosis, pneumonia, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), where surfactant deficiency raises surface tension. On a pressure–volume curve, compliance is the slope of the curve; hysteresis between inflation and deflation limbs reflects surface forces and tissue viscoelasticity.

Vascular compliance

Arterial compliance describes how much the volume of the arterial system expands for a given rise in pressure; it buffers the pulsatile output of the heart (the Windkessel effect). Arterial compliance falls with age and arteriosclerosis, contributing to widened pulse pressure and isolated systolic hypertension. Venous compliance is much greater than arterial compliance, so the veins act as a capacitance reservoir holding most of the blood volume.

Other examples

Bladder compliance is measured in urodynamics: a poorly compliant bladder stores urine at high pressures, endangering the kidneys. Intracranial compliance describes the brain's ability to accommodate added volume without a steep rise in intracranial pressure. Ocular compliance relates to changes in intraocular pressure with volume.
