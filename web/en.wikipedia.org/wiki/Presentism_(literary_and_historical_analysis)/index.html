Presentism (literary and historical analysis)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Presentism
FieldHistoriography, literary criticism
TypeAnachronistic interpretation; historiographical fallacy
RelatedAnachronism, Whig history, historicism

In literary and historical analysis, presentism is the anachronistic introduction of present-day ideas, values and perspectives into depictions or interpretations of the past. Historians generally regard it as a fallacy: by attributing modern attitudes, knowledge or moral standards to people who could not have shared them, presentist readings distort historical understanding. The term should not be confused with the philosophical presentism in the philosophy of time, nor with the finance concept of present value.

Discussion

David Hackett Fischer's Historians' Fallacies (1970) catalogued the "fallacy of presentism" (also called the fallacy of nunc pro tunc) — judging the past with the knowledge and values of the present. Herbert Butterfield's earlier critique of the "Whig interpretation of history" (1931) made a related point: narratives that divide past actors into heroes and villains according to how far they anticipated the present obscure what the past was actually like.

Presentism is also a central concern in literary criticism, especially in the study of Shakespeare and early modern texts. Critics differ over whether a work should be read strictly within its historical moment (historicism) or whether present concerns inevitably and legitimately shape interpretation — E. M. W. Tillyard's picture of an "Elizabethan world picture" exemplified the historicist approach, while the "presentist" school associated with scholars such as Hugh Grady and Terence Hawkes argues openly that Shakespeare is read from, and made meaningful in, the critic's own present.

"The past is a foreign country; they do things differently there." — L. P. Hartley, The Go-Between (1953), often invoked in discussions of anachronistic interpretation.

Criticism and defence

Critics of an uncompromising anti-presentism note that historians always write from the present, that questions about the past are unavoidably framed by current concerns, and that moral judgement of historical actors — for instance slaveholders — need not be suspended in the name of contextual understanding. In public debates, accusations of presentism frequently arise over monuments, curricula and commemorations, where contemporary values are applied to historical reputations.
