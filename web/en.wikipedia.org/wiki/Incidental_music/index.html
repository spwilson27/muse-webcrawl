Incidental music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: theatre orchestra]

Incidental music is music written to accompany a play, radio drama, television programme, video game or other non-musical production. In theatre it may include an overture, entr'actes, scene-change music, songs within the action and background scoring that underscores dialogue and mood — the ancestor of the modern film score.

History

Music accompanied drama from Greek tragedy onward, but the genre flowered in the 19th century, when major composers wrote for the stage: Beethoven for Egmont, Mendelssohn for A Midsummer Night's Dream (whose "Wedding March" became famous), Schubert for Rosamunde, Bizet for L'Arlésienne, Grieg for Ibsen's Peer Gynt, and Sibelius for Pelléas et Mélisande. Such scores were often extracted as concert suites, in which form they are best known today.

Modern usage

In film and television, the analogous term is the score (or "underscore"), contrasted with source music heard by the characters. Radio drama and podcasts retain live-style sound design, and video games use adaptive incidental music that responds to gameplay. Composers may record cues without seeing final cuts, and theatre productions increasingly commission original scores or use recorded libraries.
