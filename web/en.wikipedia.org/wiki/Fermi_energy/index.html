Fermi energy

The Fermi energy is a concept in quantum mechanics and condensed matter physics: the energy difference between the highest and lowest occupied single-particle states in a quantum system of non-interacting fermions at absolute zero temperature. In a metal, it is the energy of the highest occupied electronic state at 0 K; states below it are filled and those above are empty, the boundary forming the Fermi surface in momentum space.

Definition and formula

Because fermions obey the Pauli exclusion principle, each quantum state holds at most one particle, so a system of N fermions fills the N lowest-energy states. For a free electron gas in three dimensions, the Fermi energy is EF = (ħ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3, where n is the electron number density. The corresponding Fermi temperature TF = EF/kB is tens of thousands of kelvin for typical metals, which is why the electron gas in a metal is strongly degenerate at room temperature.

Significance

Only electrons within about kBT of the Fermi energy can be thermally excited, which explains why the electronic contribution to the heat capacity of metals is far smaller than classical theory predicted, and why electrical and thermal conductivities are governed by the Fermi velocity rather than average thermal speeds. The related Fermi level (chemical potential) extends the concept to finite temperatures and to semiconductors and insulators, where it may lie within a band gap.

Typical Fermi energies are a few electronvolts: about 7.0 eV for copper and 11.7 eV for aluminium. The concept, named after Enrico Fermi, also applies to neutron stars and white dwarfs, where Fermi pressure (degeneracy pressure) resists gravitational collapse.
