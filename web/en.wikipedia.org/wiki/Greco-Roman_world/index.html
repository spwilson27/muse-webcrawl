Greco-Roman world

The Greco-Roman world (also Greco-Roman civilization, Greco-Roman culture or Greco-Latin culture; spelled Graeco-Roman in British English) comprises the interrelated cultures of ancient Greece and ancient Rome, their extensive cultural spheres of influence across the Mediterranean world and beyond, and the civilisations they in turn influenced — cultures that were heavily interdependent in language, politics, education, architecture, philosophy, science and religion.

Overview

The term denotes a cultural and historical complex in which Greek culture, spread first by the colonies of the Greek city-states and then by the conquests of Alexander the Great, merged with Roman political power following Rome's conquest of the Hellenistic kingdoms in the last two centuries BC. Within the Roman Empire, Latin dominated administration and law in the West while Greek remained the lingua franca of the educated East; Roman literature, architecture and thought were saturated with Greek models even as Roman law, engineering and statecraft gave the synthesis its distinctive character.

The legacy of the Greco-Roman world underlies much of later European and Mediterranean civilisation — its languages, alphabets, literature, philosophy, science, architecture (the classical orders), political concepts such as citizenship and republic, and much of the tradition of law. Through the Renaissance revival of classical learning, its influence was transmitted to the modern world.
