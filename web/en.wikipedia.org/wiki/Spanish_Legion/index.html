The Spanish Legion (Spanish: Legión Española) is an elite light infantry unit of the Spanish Army, founded in 1920 by Lieutenant Colonel José Millán-Astray on the model of the French Foreign Legion. Its soldiers are known as legionarios and are famous for their distinctive traditions, rapid marching pace, and esprit de corps.

History

The Legion was created by royal decree of Alfonso XIII on 28 January 1920 to provide professional troops for Spain's colonial war in Morocco, reducing reliance on conscripts. Under Millán-Astray and Francisco Franco, who served as its deputy commander and later commander, the Legion fought in the Rif War, including the relief of Melilla after the Disaster of Annual (1921). During the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) the Legion formed part of the Army of Africa and was among the Nationalists' most effective units. It later served in the Ifni War, in the Spanish Sahara, and in peacekeeping and combat deployments to Bosnia, Kosovo, Iraq, Afghanistan, and Lebanon.

Traditions

The Legion is famed for the Credo Legionario, a code of twelve "spirits" emphasizing comradeship and a cult of sacrifice, and for the Christ of Good Death (Cristo de la Buena Muerte), a crucifix carried in procession by legionaries each Holy Week in Málaga. Legionaries march at 160–180 paces per minute, far faster than other Spanish units, and wear a distinctive green shirt uniform with an open collar. Its mascots have traditionally included a goat that marches with the troops. Although originally open to foreigners, the Legion today recruits overwhelmingly Spanish citizens.
