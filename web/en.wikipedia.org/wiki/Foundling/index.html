Foundling

A foundling is an infant or young child who has been abandoned by their parents and discovered and cared for by others. Child abandonment has been practised throughout history, often driven by poverty, illegitimacy, disability, or social stigma. Foundlings are a recurring figure in literature and folklore, where the child's unknown origins and eventual discovery of identity drive the plot.

Foundling hospitals

Institutions dedicated to the care of abandoned children appeared in medieval Europe. Florence's Ospedale degli Innocenti, opened in 1445, was among the first purpose-built foundling hospitals. In London, Thomas Coram established the Foundling Hospital in 1739 after years of campaigning; it received children at a hatch or through a petition process and gave each a new name, keeping tokens left by mothers as means of identification.

Many hospitals used a foundling wheel (ruota), a revolving cylindrical hatch set into the wall that allowed a child to be left anonymously: the baby was placed in the wheel, which was turned so the infant passed inside, and a bell was rung to alert staff.

Modern practice

Today, many jurisdictions provide "safe haven" laws and baby hatches or baby boxes, allowing a parent to surrender an infant anonymously at a hospital, fire station, or similar facility without prosecution. These measures aim to prevent dangerous abandonment. In law, a foundling may be granted citizenship of the country in which they were found if their parentage and place of birth cannot be established, in order to prevent statelessness.

In literature

Foundlings feature prominently in fiction, from Moses in the Book of Exodus to Henry Fielding's The History of Tom Jones, a Foundling (1749) and the works of Charles Dickens, whose Oliver Twist begins life in a workhouse.
