Finite verb

A finite verb is a form of a verb that has a specific tense, person, and number, and that can serve as the root of an independent clause. Finite verbs are distinguished from non-finite verbs, such as infinitives, participles, and gerunds, which are not marked for tense and person in the same way.

Characteristics

In many languages, including English, a finite verb agrees with its subject in person and number and marks tense. For example, in the sentence "She writes every day", the verb writes is finite: it is present tense and third person singular. In "She wanted to write a letter", wanted is finite, while to write is a non-finite infinitive.

A complete independent clause normally requires a finite verb; verb phrases lacking one form dependent constructions. In English, modal auxiliary verbs (such as can, must) are finite in form — the first verb in an English verb chain carries tense and agreement.

In other languages

In highly inflected languages, finiteness is often clearly marked by inflection. Some linguistic traditions instead characterise finite forms by their ability to occur in root clauses. The grammatical notion of finiteness also plays a role in analyses of subject licensing and clause structure.
