The Charter of the French Language (French: Charte de la langue française), also known as Bill 101 (Loi 101), is a law in the province of Quebec, Canada, first adopted in 1977, which defends and promotes the French language, making it the official language of Quebec and the normal language of work, instruction, communications, commerce and business in the province.

History

The Charter was introduced by Camille Laurin in the government of Premier René Lévesque and adopted by the National Assembly on 26 August 1977. It succeeded the Official Language Act of 1974 (Bill 22) and built on the work of the Gendron Commission, which had documented the subordinate status of French in Quebec's economy despite francophones forming the large majority of the population.

Main provisions

The Charter declares French the language of legislation and of the courts in Quebec, of public administration, and of labour relations. It francized businesses, required French or predominantly French commercial signage, and restricted access to English-language public schooling largely to children with a parent educated in English in Canada — the "Quebec clause" later challenged under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms. The Office québécois de la langue française oversees enforcement.

Amendments and legal challenges

Parts of the Charter's sign provisions were struck down by the Supreme Court of Canada in Ford v. Quebec (1988), after which Quebec used the notwithstanding clause to maintain a French-only sign law for a period. Later amendments (Bill 86, 1993) permitted bilingual signs with French predominant. In 2022 the National Assembly adopted Bill 96, a major strengthening of the Charter extending French requirements to smaller businesses and federal undertakings in Quebec.
