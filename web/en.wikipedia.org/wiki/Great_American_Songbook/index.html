The Great American Songbook is the loosely defined canon of significant 20th-century American jazz standards, popular songs, and show tunes. The term does not refer to a single book but to a body of songs — mostly written between the 1920s and the early 1950s for Broadway theatre, Hollywood musical films, and Tin Pan Alley publishers — that have remained enduringly performed and recorded, especially by jazz musicians and traditional pop vocalists.

Principal songwriters

The core repertoire is dominated by a small group of songwriters: George Gershwin (with lyricist brother Ira Gershwin), Cole Porter, Irving Berlin, Jerome Kern, Richard Rodgers (with Lorenz Hart and later Oscar Hammerstein II), Harold Arlen, Hoagy Carmichael, Duke Ellington, and lyricists such as Johnny Mercer, Dorothy Fields, and Yip Harburg. Songs like "Summertime", "Night and Day", "The Way You Look Tonight", "My Funny Valentine", "Over the Rainbow", and "All the Things You Are" are canonical examples.

Interpreters

Ella Fitzgerald's series of "Song Book" albums for Verve (1956–1964), each devoted to a single songwriter, did much to define the canon. Frank Sinatra, Tony Bennett, Nat King Cole, Billie Holiday, and Sarah Vaughan are among the vocalists most associated with the repertoire, and jazz instrumentalists have long used its chord changes (for example, the "rhythm changes" of Gershwin's "I Got Rhythm") as vehicles for improvisation. Later revivals include Linda Ronstadt's albums with Nelson Riddle in the 1980s, Rod Stewart's best-selling It Had to Be You series in the 2000s, and recordings by Diana Krall, Michael Bublé, and Lady Gaga with Tony Bennett.

Characteristics

Songbook songs typically employ a verse–chorus (AABA or ABAC) form of 32 bars, sophisticated harmony influenced by European art song and jazz, and witty, literate lyrics. Their combination of melodic memorability and harmonic richness explains their persistence as jazz standards.
