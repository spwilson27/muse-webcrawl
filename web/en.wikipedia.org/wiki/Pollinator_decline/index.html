Pollinator decline

Pollinator decline is the reduction in abundance and diversity of insects and other animal pollinators documented in many ecosystems worldwide during the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Pollinators — including bees, butterflies, moths, flies, beetles, bats, and hummingbirds — are essential to the reproduction of most flowering plants and to roughly a third of global food production by volume, including many fruits, vegetables, nuts, and oilseeds.

Evidence

Long-term monitoring in Europe and North America has recorded steep losses: many bumblebee species have contracted in range, the western honey bee suffers elevated colony losses (including the phenomenon termed colony collapse disorder), and studies of insect biomass in German protected areas found declines of more than 75% in flying insect biomass over 27 years. The 2016 IPBES assessment concluded that large proportions of pollinator species, particularly wild bees and butterflies, face elevated extinction risk.

Causes

  • Habitat loss and fragmentation from agriculture, urbanization, and intensive land management.
  • Pesticides, especially neonicotinoid insecticides, which can impair bee navigation, learning, and reproduction.
  • Parasites and disease, notably the Varroa destructor mite and associated viruses in honey bees.
  • Climate change, shifting the ranges of species and the timing of flowering.
  • Invasive species and competition from introduced pollinators.

Consequences and responses

Declines threaten crop yields, wild plant reproduction, and ecosystem stability; researchers have also documented a parallel decline in wind-pollinated plant pollen production and in insect-pollinated wildflowers in some surveys. Responses include restrictions on neonicotinoids in the European Union, pollinator-friendly planting and habitat corridors, integrated pest management, and national pollinator strategies, alongside citizen-science monitoring programs.
