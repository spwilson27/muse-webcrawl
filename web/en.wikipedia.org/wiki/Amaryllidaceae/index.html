Amaryllidaceae

Scientific classification
KingdomPlantae
CladeMonocots
OrderAsparagales
FamilyAmaryllidaceae J.St.-Hil.
Type genusAmaryllis L.
SubfamiliesAgapanthoideae; Allioideae; Amaryllidoideae

The Amaryllidaceae are a family of herbaceous, mainly perennial and bulbous (rarely rhizomatous) flowering plants in the monocot order Asparagales. The family takes its name from the genus Amaryllis and is commonly known as the amaryllis family. In the APG IV system the family comprises about 79 genera and over 1,600 species in three subfamilies: Agapanthoideae (agapanthus), Allioideae (onions, garlic and chives) and Amaryllidoideae (amaryllis, daffodils and snowdrops).

Members of the family are distributed mainly in tropical and subtropical regions, with centres of diversity in South America, the Mediterranean and southern Africa. Many are prized ornamentals, and the Allioideae include some of the world's most important vegetable and flavouring crops.

Description

Plants of the family typically grow from bulbs (or, in the onion subfamily, bulbs or rhizomes), with linear leaves and flowers borne in umbels on leafless stalks (scapes). Flowers usually have six tepals and six stamens; in the Amaryllidoideae the ovary is inferior, a classic distinguishing character, while in Allioideae it is superior. Many species produce distinctive alkaloids, including lycorine and galantamine.

Classification

The family's circumscription changed greatly with molecular phylogenetics: the APG systems merged the former families Alliaceae and Agapanthaceae into an expanded Amaryllidaceae, a settlement reflected in the three-subfamily structure used today.

Uses

The family supplies staples such as onion, garlic, leek and chives; ornamentals including daffodils, snowdrops, Amaryllis and Hippeastrum; and the pharmaceutical alkaloid galantamine, obtained from snowdrops and daffodils and used in the treatment of Alzheimer's disease.
