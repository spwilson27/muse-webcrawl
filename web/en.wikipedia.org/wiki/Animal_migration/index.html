Animal migration is the regular, usually seasonal, movement of animals from one region to another, typically along well-defined routes and often over very long distances. It is found in all major groups — birds, mammals, fish, reptiles, amphibians, insects and crustaceans — and is driven by the seasonal distribution of food, breeding sites and tolerable climate.

Notable migrations

The Arctic tern makes the longest known migration, travelling between Arctic breeding grounds and Antarctic seas, some 70,000 km a year. Bar-tailed godwits fly non-stop across the Pacific for over 11,000 km. On land, around 1.3 million wildebeest circulate through the Serengeti–Mara ecosystem. Pacific salmon return from the ocean to their natal streams to spawn, and monarch butterflies migrate between North America and overwintering sites in Mexico over several generations.

Navigation

Migrants navigate using a variety of cues: the sun and stars, landmarks, smell, and sensitivity to the Earth's magnetic field (magnetoreception), sometimes combined with an inherited programme of direction and distance. Tracking with rings, satellite tags and geolocators has revolutionised the study of these journeys.

Triggers and conservation

Migration is timed largely by day length and internal circannual rhythms, and prepared for by hyperphagia (heavy feeding) and fat storage. Migratory species face threats at breeding grounds, wintering grounds and stopover sites alike; habitat loss, barriers such as dams and fences, and climate change are major concerns.
