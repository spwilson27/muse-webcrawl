Deal with the Devil
MotifBargain with the Devil; "selling one's soul"
Classic versionsTheophilus of Adana; Faust legend

Typical rewardsKnowledge, wealth, youth, fame, power, talent

Selling your soul, more formally a deal with the Devil or Faustian bargain, is a cultural motif in which a person offers their soul to Satan or another malevolent being in exchange for diabolical favours — such as knowledge, wealth, youth, power, or extraordinary talent — with the soul forfeit to damnation after a set term or at death. The motif has been a staple of Western folklore, theology, and literature for over a millennium, and survives today as a metaphor for trading long-term integrity or salvation for short-term advantage.

Origins

One of the earliest versions is the sixth-century legend of Theophilus of Adana, a cleric who, passed over for a bishopric, signed a pact with the Devil to obtain the office, then repented and was saved by the intercession of the Virgin Mary — a pattern of pact and penitence common in medieval exempla. Similar tales attached themselves to historical figures reputed to possess uncanny knowledge, including Pope Sylvester II, and to early modern accounts of witchcraft, in which accused witches were said to have made pacts with the Devil.

The Faust legend

The motif's most famous form is the legend of Johann Georg Faust, the German astrologer and alchemist whose story was popularised by the 1587 chapbook Historia von D. Johann Fausten and Christopher Marlowe's play Doctor Faustus (c. 1592). In Goethe's Faust (1808/1832), the scholar wagers his soul with Mephistopheles but is ultimately redeemed through striving and grace — a reversal of the damnation ending. Thomas Mann's Doktor Faustus reworked the bargain as an allegory of Germany's fate.

In popular culture

The bargain recurs across modern media: blues legend holds that Robert Johnson sold his soul at a Mississippi crossroads for his guitar mastery — a story also dramatised in the film Crossroads (1986) and echoed in the Charlie Daniels song "The Devil Went Down to Georgia" (1979). Variations include Washington Irving's "The Devil and Tom Walker," Stephen Vincent Benét's "The Devil and Daniel Webster" (in which Webster argues the soul back in court), the musical Damn Yankees, and numerous films and television episodes. Figuratively, to "sell one's soul" now usually means compromising one's principles for money, fame, or advancement, with no supernatural party required.
