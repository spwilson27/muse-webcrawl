Two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy

Two-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (2D NMR) is a set of nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy methods which give data plotted in a space defined by two frequency axes rather than one. Types of 2D NMR include correlation spectroscopy (COSY), J-spectroscopy, exchange spectroscopy (EXSY), and the nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy (NOESY). Heteronuclear experiments such as HSQC and HMBC correlate different nuclear species. 2D NMR provides more structural information about a molecule than one-dimensional spectra and is especially useful for determining the structure of molecules too complex for 1D NMR, including proteins and other biomolecules.

Basic concepts

Each experiment consists of a sequence of radiofrequency pulses with delay periods: preparation, an evolution period (t1) during which coherence evolves, an optional mixing period, and acquisition (t2). Repeating the experiment with incremented t1 and applying a two-dimensional Fourier transform yields a spectrum in which diagonal peaks correspond to the ordinary 1D spectrum and cross-peaks reveal couplings or spatial proximity between nuclei.

Common experiments

COSY identifies nuclei that are spin–spin coupled, usually through two or three bonds. NOESY detects nuclei close in space (within about 5 Å) via cross-relaxation, making it central to determining three-dimensional structures of proteins in solution. HSQC correlates a proton with the heteroatom (¹³C or ¹⁵N) to which it is directly bonded, and HMBC reveals longer-range correlations. The concept was proposed by Jean Jeener in 1971 and developed by Richard Ernst, who shared the 1991 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
