The International Refugee Organization (IRO) was an intergovernmental organisation founded by the United Nations on 20 August 1946 to deal with the massive refugee problem created by the Second World War. It took over the functions of the earlier United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA) on 1 July 1947 and operated until 1952, when it was superseded by the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR).

Mandate

The IRO's constitution defined refugees and displaced persons eligible for its assistance and set out its tasks: repatriation, identification, registration and classification, care and assistance, legal and political protection, transport, and resettlement and re-establishment in countries willing to receive them. In practice, as Cold War tensions grew, repatriation to countries under communist rule fell away and resettlement in the West became the organisation's central activity.

Operations

The IRO cared for displaced persons in camps, principally in Germany, Austria and Italy, providing food, shelter, medical care and vocational training while arranging emigration. Over its lifetime it resettled more than a million people: the largest receiving countries were the United States (which admitted displaced persons under the Displaced Persons Act of 1948), Australia, Israel, Canada and various Latin American and Western European states. The organisation chartered ships for mass movements of refugees and ran an extensive tracing and documentation service.

Dissolution and legacy

Eighteen states became members of the IRO; the Soviet bloc did not join, objecting to resettlement rather than repatriation. When the IRO wound up in 1952, residual functions passed to UNHCR, established in 1950–51, and its resettlement machinery informed the later work of the Intergovernmental Committee for European Migration (now the International Organization for Migration).
