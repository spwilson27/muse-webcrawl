Spanish Golden Age

The Spanish Golden Age (Spanish: Siglo de Oro, "Golden Century") is a period of flourishing in arts and literature in Spain, coinciding with the rise of the Spanish Empire under the Habsburg dynasty. It is conventionally dated from the end of the Reconquista and the publication of Antonio de Nebrija's Gramática de la lengua castellana in 1492 to the death of the playwright Pedro Calderón de la Barca in 1681. Politically the era spanned the reigns from the Catholic Monarchs through Philip IV, even as Spain's power declined in its later decades.

Literature

The era produced Miguel de Cervantes' Don Quixote (1605, 1615), frequently called the first modern novel, as well as the picaresque tradition beginning with the anonymous Lazarillo de Tormes (1554). Lope de Vega, the "Phoenix of Wits," codified the Spanish comedia nueva and is credited with hundreds of plays; his successor Calderón wrote philosophical dramas such as Life Is a Dream. Poetry was dominated by the rivalry of Luis de Góngora's ornate culteranismo and Francisco de Quevedo's dense conceptismo. Mystical writing flourished in Teresa of Ávila and John of the Cross.

Visual arts

Painting reached its apex with Diego Velázquez, court painter to Philip IV and creator of Las Meninas. El Greco, Francisco de Zurbarán, Jusepe de Ribera and Bartolomé Esteban Murillo defined Spanish Baroque painting, marked by tenebrism, naturalism and intense religious feeling.

Music and theatre

The polyphonists Tomás Luis de Victoria and Cristóbal de Morales were among Europe's leading Renaissance composers. Public theatres (corrales de comedias) in Madrid drew audiences from all classes, and the era closed as Spain's political decline deepened even while its culture set European fashions.
