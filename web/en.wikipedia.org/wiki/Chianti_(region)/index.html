Chianti (region)

Chianti is a hilly rural region of central Tuscany, Italy, lying between the provinces of Florence, Siena and Arezzo. It is internationally famous for the red wine of the same name, produced chiefly from the Sangiovese grape, and for a landscape of vineyards, olive groves, cypress-lined roads, stone farmhouses and small medieval towns.

Geography

The Chianti hills (Monti del Chianti) form a low mountain chain running between the Arno and Ombrone valleys. The historic core, known as Chianti storico, comprises the communes of Greve, Radda, Castellina and Gaiole, once organised in the medieval League of Chianti, whose symbol — the black rooster (gallo nero) — still marks bottles of Chianti Classico wine.

Wine

Wine has been made in Chianti since Etruscan times. In 1716 the Grand Duke Cosimo III de' Medici issued one of the world's earliest legal delimitations of a wine region. The modern appellations include Chianti DOCG and the more prestigious Chianti Classico DOCG, produced in the historic zone between Florence and Siena and based principally on Sangiovese, with permitted additions of grapes such as Canaiolo, Colorino, Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot.

History

Contested between Florence and Siena in the Middle Ages, Chianti was a frontier of castles and fortified villages. After the Second World War, the sharecropping (mezzadria) system collapsed and many farms were abandoned, before wine-led tourism and foreign buyers revived the region from the 1970s onwards — a phenomenon sometimes nicknamed "Chiantishire" for its popularity with British residents.
