Sierra Leone Creole people

The Sierra Leone Creole people (also called Krio) are an ethnic group native to Sierra Leone in West Africa, descended principally from freed African American, Afro-Caribbean and liberated African slaves who settled the Freetown Peninsula between 1787 and about 1885. Although they form a small minority of Sierra Leone's population — on the order of a few per cent — the Creoles historically exercised an influence in education, law, the civil service and commerce far beyond their numbers, and their language, Krio, serves as the lingua franca of the whole country.

Origins

The community coalesced from successive groups of settlers in the British "Province of Freedom": the Black Poor of London (1787); the Nova Scotians, formerly enslaved people who had fought for the British in the American Revolutionary War and settled in Nova Scotia before emigrating in 1792; the Jamaican Maroons deported after 1800; and, throughout the 19th century, tens of thousands of "Liberated Africans" or "recaptives" rescued by the Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron from slave ships and landed at Freetown. These Yoruba (Aku), Igbo, Congolese and many other peoples were gradually absorbed into a single Creole society shaped by Christianity, English education and British institutions.

Language and culture

Krio is an English-based creole language with strong influences from Yoruba and other African languages; it is spoken as a first language by the Creoles and as a second language by the great majority of Sierra Leoneans. Creole culture is known for its distinctive board houses (timber architecture with Caribbean affinities), its emphasis on formal education — Fourah Bay College, founded in 1827, was the first Western-style university institution in West Africa — and for producing many of the doctors, lawyers, clergy and administrators of colonial British West Africa, where Sierra Leonean Creoles were nicknamed the "Black Englishmen". Creole cuisine includes dishes such as plasas and pepper soup, and the community preserves festivals and societies with roots in both African and settler traditions.

Modern position

Relations between the Creoles of the former Crown Colony and the peoples of the Protectorate (the hinterland) were a central theme of Sierra Leonean politics in the colonial period and after independence in 1961. Although their political dominance faded, Creole professionals, judges and academics remain prominent, and Krio continues to function as the country's unifying informal language in media, markets and everyday speech.
