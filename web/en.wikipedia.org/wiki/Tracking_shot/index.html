Tracking shot

Tracking shot
Type: Camera movement
Equipment: Dolly, tracks, Steadicam, gimbal
Related: Pan, zoom, crane shot, long take

In cinematography, a tracking shot is any shot where the camera physically moves through space — typically following beside, behind or in front of the subject — as distinct from a pan (rotation in place) or a zoom (focal-length change).

Technique

Traditionally the camera rides on a dolly along laid tracks, pushed by a dolly grip. Since the 1970s Steadicam, and later handheld gimbals and drones, have freed the tracking shot from rails. A 'tracking shot' moving forward is sometimes called a push-in; lateral movement is trucking or crabbing.

Famous examples

Celebrated tracking shots include the opening of Orson Welles's Touch of Evil (1958), the Copacabana entrance in Scorsese's Goodfellas (1990), the trench sequences of Paths of Glory, and the extended takes of Children of Men and 1917. Alfred Hitchcock's Rope (1948) disguised cuts to simulate one continuous shot. See Long take and Steadicam.
