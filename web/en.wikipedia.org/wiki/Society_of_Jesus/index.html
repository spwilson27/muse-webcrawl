The Society of Jesus (Latin: Societas Iesu; abbreviated SJ), commonly known as the Jesuits, is a religious order of the Catholic Church headquartered in Rome. It was founded in 1540 by Ignatius of Loyola and six companions, with the approval of Pope Paul III. The society is engaged in evangelization and apostolic ministry in 112 nations. Jesuits work in education, research, and cultural pursuits, and are known for their commitment to education and intellectual life.

History

Ignatius, a Basque soldier wounded at the Siege of Pamplona in 1521, underwent a spiritual conversion and composed the Spiritual Exercises. In 1534 he and six companions, including Francis Xavier and Peter Faber, took vows at Montmartre in Paris to live in poverty and chastity and to go to the Holy Land. Pope Paul III approved the order in the bull Regimini militantis Ecclesiae (1540), and the Constitutions were adopted in 1558.

Missions and education

Jesuit missionaries spread worldwide — Xavier to India and Japan, Matteo Ricci to China, and others to the Americas, where they founded the Paraguay reductions. Education became a hallmark of the order: the Ratio Studiorum (1599) standardized a humanistic curriculum, and Jesuit schools and universities were founded across Europe and beyond.

Suppression and restoration

Under pressure from Bourbon monarchies, Pope Clement XIV suppressed the Society in 1773 with the brief Dominus ac Redemptor. It survived in Russia and Prussia, where monarchs refused to promulgate the brief, and was formally restored by Pope Pius VII in 1814.

Modern era

In 2013, Jorge Mario Bergoglio became Pope Francis, the first Jesuit pope. The order today numbers around 15,000 priests, brothers, and scholastics.
