Gene therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gene therapy is a medical approach that treats or prevents disease by modifying a person's genes: adding a working copy of a gene, silencing or repairing a faulty one, or editing DNA in the patient's cells. It is used mainly for inherited disorders, some cancers and certain viral infections, and represents one of the most significant new treatment strategies of recent decades.

Approaches

Therapies may work ex vivo — cells are removed, corrected in the laboratory (as in CAR T-cell cancer therapy and treatments for blood disorders) and returned — or in vivo, delivering genes directly in the body, commonly with engineered adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors. Genome editing tools, especially CRISPR-Cas9, enable precise corrections; the first CRISPR therapy (for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia) was approved in 2023. Approved gene therapies treat conditions including spinal muscular atrophy, inherited blindness and haemophilia.

Challenges and ethics

Obstacles include immune reactions to vectors, delivering genes to enough cells, durability, rare off-target edits, and extremely high prices (often over a million dollars per patient). Altering embryos or germ cells (heritable editing) is widely prohibited after the 2018 scandal of gene-edited babies in China; somatic (body-cell) therapy, which affects only the patient, is the accepted field.
