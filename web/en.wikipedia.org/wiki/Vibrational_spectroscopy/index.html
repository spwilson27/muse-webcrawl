Vibrational spectroscopy

Vibrational spectroscopy is a branch of spectroscopy concerned with the vibrational motions of molecules. Its principal techniques are infrared (IR) spectroscopy, in which a molecule absorbs infrared light at frequencies matching its bond vibrations, and Raman spectroscopy, in which vibrational transitions are observed through inelastic scattering of monochromatic light.

Principles

A molecule with N atoms has 3N−6 normal modes of vibration (3N−5 if linear). A vibration is infrared-active if it changes the molecule's dipole moment, and Raman-active if it changes its polarisability; the two techniques are therefore complementary, and in centrosymmetric molecules mutually exclusive for a given mode. Frequencies, expressed in wavenumbers (typically 400–4000 cm⁻¹ for IR), are characteristic of bond types and functional groups — carbonyl stretches near 1700 cm⁻¹, O–H stretches near 3600 cm⁻¹ — making vibrational spectra a fingerprint for identifying substances and studying structure, bonding and environment.
