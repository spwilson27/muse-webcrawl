RNA interference

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
RNA interference
Discovered1998, Andrew Fire and Craig Mello (in C. elegans)
MechanismDouble-stranded RNA → siRNA → RISC silences mRNA
AwardsNobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (2006)
[Image: Diagram of RNA interference: double-stranded RNA processed by Dicer into siRNA, loaded into RISC, cleaving target mRNA]
The RNAi pathway.

RNA interference (RNAi) is a biological process in which RNA molecules are involved in sequence-specific suppression of gene expression by double-stranded RNA, through translational or transcriptional repression. Historically it went by other names, including co-suppression, post-transcriptional gene silencing and quelling.

The phenomenon was pinned down in 1998 by Andrew Fire and Craig Mello, who showed that double-stranded RNA — not single-stranded — silences genes in the worm Caenorhabditis elegans with extraordinary potency; they received the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. RNAi is a natural defence against viruses and transposons, a regulator of development (via microRNAs), a standard laboratory tool for knocking down genes, and increasingly a drug class: the first RNAi therapeutic, patisiran, was approved in 2018.

Mechanism

Long double-stranded RNA is cut by the enzyme Dicer into short interfering RNAs (~21 nucleotides); one strand is loaded into the RISC complex (built around an Argonaute protein), which uses it as a guide to find and cleave complementary messenger RNA, destroying the message before it can be translated. Because the machinery amplifies the signal (in worms and plants), a few molecules of dsRNA can silence a gene throughout an organism.
