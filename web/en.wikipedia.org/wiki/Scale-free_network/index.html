Scale-free network

A scale-free network is a network whose degree distribution follows a power law: the fraction of nodes with k connections decays roughly as P(k) ~ k−γ, typically with 2 < γ < 3. Such networks contain a few highly connected "hubs" and many nodes with few links, unlike random networks whose degrees cluster around a mean. The term was popularised by Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert in 1999 on the basis of measurements of the World Wide Web and other real networks.

A scale-free network: a few hub nodes hold most of the connections.

Formation: preferential attachment

The Barabási–Albert model explains the emergence of scale-free structure through two ingredients: growth (networks add nodes over time) and preferential attachment (new nodes link preferentially to already well-connected nodes — "the rich get richer"). The model reproduces a power-law degree distribution with exponent γ = 3 and has become a standard model in network science, anticipated in earlier work by Herbert Simon and others on skewed distributions.

Properties

Scale-free networks are remarkably robust to random node failures — removing nodes at random almost never fragments them — but vulnerable to targeted attacks on hubs. They typically have very short average path lengths (the "ultra-small world" effect) and, for γ ≤ 3, a vanishing epidemic threshold, meaning infections or information can spread persistently even at low transmission rates. Examples claimed in the literature include citation networks, metabolic and protein-interaction networks, the Internet at the autonomous-system level, and social networks, though the strict power-law character of some empirical networks has been debated.
