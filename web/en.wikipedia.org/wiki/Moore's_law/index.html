Moore's law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: An exponential curve of transistor counts rising across five decades on a log scale]

Moore's law is the observation that the number of transistors in an integrated circuit doubles at regular intervals — roughly every two years — a trend that held from the 1970s into the 2010s and drove the computer revolution.

Gordon Moore, co-founder of Intel, made the prediction in a 1965 paper in Electronics magazine, initially forecasting yearly doubling, and revised it in 1975 to about every two years. It was never a law of nature: it functioned as a target the semiconductor industry organised itself to meet — a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Limits

Since roughly the mid-2000s, clock-speed scaling stalled (the end of Dennard scaling) and gains came from multiple cores instead; by the 2010s–2020s the transistor-doubling pace itself slowed as features approached atomic scales and fabrication costs soared.

Progress continues through new architectures, 3D stacking and specialised accelerators, but as an exponential in cheap general-purpose power, Moore's law is generally considered to be ending or ended.
