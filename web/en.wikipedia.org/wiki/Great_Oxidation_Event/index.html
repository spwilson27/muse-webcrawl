Great Oxidation Event

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Great Oxidation Event
Whenc. 2.4–2.0 billion years ago (Paleoproterozoic)
Also calledGreat Oxygenation Event, Oxygen Catastrophe
CauseOxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria
EffectsFree O2 in atmosphere; Huronian glaciation; mass extinction of anaerobes

The Great Oxidation Event (GOE), also called the Great Oxygenation Event or Oxygen Catastrophe, was a period early in Earth's history when the atmosphere and shallow oceans first experienced a sustained rise of free oxygen (O2). It began roughly 2.4 billion years ago during the Paleoproterozoic era, most likely triggered by oxygen-producing cyanobacteria whose photosynthetic output finally overwhelmed the planet's chemical oxygen sinks.

Mechanism

Cyanobacteria had probably evolved oxygenic photosynthesis hundreds of millions of years before the GOE, but the oxygen they released was consumed by dissolved iron in the oceans — precipitating out as the banded iron formations common in rocks of this age — and by reduced volcanic gases. Only once these sinks were saturated could oxygen accumulate in the air. Geological evidence includes the disappearance of easily oxidised minerals such as uraninite from sediments, the appearance of red beds, and the loss of mass-independent fractionation of sulfur isotopes around 2.4 billion years ago.

Consequences

For the anaerobic organisms that dominated early life, oxygen was a poison: the GOE likely caused one of the largest mass extinctions in Earth history. Rising oxygen also destroyed atmospheric methane, a potent greenhouse gas, probably triggering the Huronian glaciation — possibly a global "Snowball Earth." In the long term, oxygen enabled aerobic respiration, the ozone layer, and ultimately the evolution of complex multicellular life.
