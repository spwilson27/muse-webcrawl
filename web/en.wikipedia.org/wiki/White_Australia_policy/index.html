White Australia policy

The White Australia policy was a set of racial policies that aimed to forbid people of non-European ethnic origin — especially Asians (primarily Chinese) and Pacific Islanders — from immigrating to Australia, in order to create a "white" / Anglo-Celtic society. It was a founding policy of the Commonwealth of Australia after federation in 1901, beginning with the Immigration Restriction Act 1901, and was progressively dismantled between 1949 and the early 1970s.

The dictation test was the policy's main administrative tool for over 50 years.

Origins and mechanisms

Anti-Chinese agitation from the gold-rush era and fears of cheap Kanaka labour in Queensland preceded federation. Rather than naming races (which would embarrass the British Empire and its Japanese alliance), the Immigration Restriction Act used a dictation test: an officer could require 50 words in any prescribed language, ensuring almost any unwanted entrant could be failed. The Pacific Island Labourers Act 1901 led to the deportation of most South Sea Islanders. Prime Minister Edmund Barton declared that the doctrine of human equality was never intended to apply to relations between Britain and India or other non-white peoples — expressing the era's official racism bluntly.

Dismantling

After World War II, "Populate or Perish" drove mass European migration; Arthur Calwell used the phrase "two Wongs don't make a white" amid controversy over Asian war brides. Reforms under Menzies (abolition of the dictation test, 1958), Holt (1966) and finally the Whitlam government's Racial Discrimination Act 1975 ended race-based selection. Multicultural Australia — with large Asian-Australian communities — is its repudiation. Debates over asylum policy sometimes invoke the policy's legacy.
