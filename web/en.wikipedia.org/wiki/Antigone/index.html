Antigone (Greek: Ἀντιγόνη) is a character in Greek mythology, the daughter of Oedipus and his mother Jocasta, and the sister of Polynices, Eteocles, and Ismene. She is best known as the heroine of Antigone, a tragedy by the Athenian playwright Sophocles, first performed about 441 BC. In the play she defies the decree of King Creon of Thebes, who has forbidden the burial of her brother Polynices, and performs funeral rites for him, accepting death as the consequence. The play has become a canonical exploration of the conflict between divine or natural law and the law of the state, and of civil disobedience.

Mythology

Antigone was born of the incestuous union of Oedipus, king of Thebes, and his mother Jocasta. After Oedipus discovered the truth of his birth, blinded himself, and went into exile, Antigone accompanied him as his guide. In Sophocles' late play Oedipus at Colonus she stays with her father until his mysterious death near Athens. She then returned to Thebes, where her brothers Eteocles and Polynices, who had agreed to share the throne in alternate years, fell out; Polynices raised an army against the city (the expedition of the Seven against Thebes), and the brothers killed each other in single combat.

Sophocles' Antigone

Creon, the new ruler of Thebes, ordered that Eteocles be buried with full honours but that Polynices, as a traitor, be left unburied on pain of death. Antigone, holding that the unwritten laws of the gods oblige her to bury her brother, performs the rite — in the play she is caught sprinkling dust and pouring libations over the corpse — and is arrested. Her sister Ismene, who had refused to help, tries to share the guilt and is rejected. Creon sentences Antigone to be walled up alive in a rock tomb. The prophet Tiresias warns Creon that the gods are angered; Creon relents too late: Antigone has hanged herself in the tomb, his son Haemon, betrothed to Antigone, kills himself over her body, and his wife Eurydice dies on hearing the news, leaving Creon broken.

Themes and reception

The play's central conflict has been read in many ways: as divine law versus human law, family versus state, or individual conscience versus political authority. Hegel famously read it as a collision between two equally justified ethical powers. The figure of Antigone has been retold by many later writers, including Jean Anouilh, whose 1944 Antigone was performed in occupied Paris, and Bertolt Brecht. She remains an emblem of principled resistance to unjust authority.
