Permian–Triassic extinction event

The Permian–Triassic extinction event, colloquially known as the Great Dying, was the most severe mass extinction in the history of life on Earth. It occurred about 251.9 million years ago, marking the boundary between the Permian and Triassic periods and between the Paleozoic and Mesozoic eras. An estimated 81% of marine species and about 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species went extinct, along with huge numbers of insects — the only known mass extinction of insects. Recovery of complex ecosystems took millions of years, far longer than after later extinctions.

Causes

The leading explanation links the extinction to the eruption of the Siberian Traps, one of the largest volcanic events known, which flooded about seven million square kilometres of Siberia with basaltic lava and released immense quantities of carbon dioxide and other gases. The resulting cascade of environmental effects is thought to include rapid global warming, ocean acidification, widespread ocean anoxia (loss of dissolved oxygen), ozone destruction, and acid rain. Evidence includes mercury anomalies, carbon-isotope excursions, and temperature records from conodont apatite indicating tropical sea-surface temperatures rose by as much as 8–10 °C. Methane release from hydrates or from coal seams baked by intrusions may have amplified the warming.

Effects on life

Marine communities were devastated: trilobites, which had survived two earlier mass extinctions, disappeared entirely, as did blastoids, rugose and tabulate corals, and most brachiopods, ammonoids, and crinoids. On land, the dominant synapsid fauna collapsed; the disaster taxon Lystrosaurus came to dominate early Triassic ecosystems to an extraordinary degree. Reef ecosystems vanished for several million years.

Timing

High-precision uranium–lead dating places the main extinction pulse at about 251.94 million years ago, with the marine extinction possibly unfolding over tens of thousands of years or less — geologically abrupt — while the terrestrial crisis may have begun slightly earlier. The event defines the Permian–Triassic boundary, located by the first appearance of the conodont Hindeodus parvus at the Meishan section in China.
