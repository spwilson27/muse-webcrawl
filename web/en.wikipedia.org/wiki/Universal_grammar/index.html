Universal grammar

Universal grammar (UG), in modern linguistics, is the theory that the ability to learn grammar is hard-wired into the brain as part of human genetic endowment. It is principally associated with Noam Chomsky, whose work from the 1950s onward transformed linguistics.

Universal grammar
Key figure: Noam Chomsky
Field: Linguistics, cognitive science
Related: Principles and parameters, poverty of the stimulus

Theory

Chomsky argued from the poverty of the stimulus: children acquire complex, rule-governed language rapidly from limited, imperfect input, implying innate linguistic structure — a language acquisition device. In principles-and-parameters theory, UG consists of universal principles with parameters set by exposure (for example, head direction).

Development

Earlier ideas of universal grammar appear in Roger Bacon and the Port-Royal Grammar. Chomsky's Syntactic Structures (1957) and Aspects of the Theory of Syntax (1965) launched generative grammar; later versions include Government and Binding and the Minimalist Program.

Criticism

Usage-based linguists argue language diversity is greater than UG predicts and that general learning mechanisms plus social interaction can explain acquisition. The debate remains active.
