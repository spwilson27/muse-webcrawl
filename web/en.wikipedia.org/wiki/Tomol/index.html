Tomol

TypePlank canoe
UsersChumash and Tongva peoples

A tomol (Chumash: tomolo) is a type of plank-built canoe used by the Chumash and neighbouring Tongva peoples of the Santa Barbara Channel region of California. It is one of the oldest examples of a plank-constructed watercraft in North America.

Construction

Tomols were built from planks of driftwood — most prized was redwood washed down from the north — split, shaped and sanded, then sewn together with cordage made from plant fibre and sealed with yop, a heated mixture of pine pitch and asphaltum (tar) that occurs naturally in the region. The seams were caulked and the hull coated to make it watertight. Boats were typically between about 8 and 30 feet (2.4–9 m) long and could carry several passengers and substantial cargo.

Use

Tomols allowed the Chumash to travel between mainland villages and the Channel Islands, supporting an extensive maritime trade network in shell-bead money, obsidian, fish and other goods. Ownership of a tomol conferred status, and builders and owners were organised in a hereditary guild-like association, the Brotherhood of the Tomol. In modern times, Chumash community groups have built replica tomols, such as the 'Elye'wun ("Swordfish"), launched in 1976, and use them for crossings to Santa Cruz Island.
