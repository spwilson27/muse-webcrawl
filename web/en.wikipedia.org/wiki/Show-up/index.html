Show-up

A show-up, also called a one-person show-up or single-suspect identification, is an identification procedure in criminal law in which a single suspect is presented to an eyewitness shortly after a crime so the witness can confirm or deny that the suspect is the perpetrator. Unlike a lineup or photo array, which present several people, a show-up presents only one.

Legal issues

Because show-ups are inherently suggestive—the witness is shown only the person police have detained—they raise due process concerns. The US Supreme Court addressed pretrial identifications in Stovall v. Denno (1967), which permitted a prompt hospital show-up under exigent circumstances, and later cases applying the reliability factors of Neil v. Biggers and Manson v. Brathwaite. Courts generally permit show-ups conducted soon after the crime (when witness memory is fresh and investigation is urgent) but scrutinize those staged long afterward or conducted suggestively.

Practice and criticism

Police typically conduct show-ups at or near the crime scene within minutes to a few hours, sometimes by bringing the witness to the detained suspect ("drive-by" identification). Eyewitness-identification researchers criticize show-ups as more error-prone than properly constructed lineups, and several jurisdictions and police departments restrict their use or require documenting the witness's confidence immediately.
