Han Kitab

The Han Kitab (Chinese: 漢克塔布; from Arabic kitāb, "book") are a collection of Chinese Islamic texts written by Chinese Muslim scholars (the Hui people) during the Ming and Qing dynasties. Written in Chinese — and occasionally in the Xiao'erjing Arabic-based script — these works explained Islam using the terminology of Confucianism, Buddhism, and Taoism.

History

The Han Kitab tradition flourished from the late 16th to the 18th centuries, centred on the scholarly networks of Nanjing and Shandong. Its authors sought to demonstrate that Islam was compatible with the Confucian social and ethical order, presenting the Prophet Muhammad as a sage in the Chinese sense.

Major authors

Leading figures include Wang Daiyu (c. 1570–1660), author of the Zhengjiao Zhenquan ("True Commentary on the Right Teaching"); Ma Zhu (1640–1710); and Liu Zhi (c. 1660–1739), whose Tianfang Xingli ("The Philosophy of Islam") is considered the tradition's culminating work.

Significance

The Han Kitab represent one of the most sustained attempts at cross-cultural religious translation in pre-modern China, and they remain an important resource for the study of Islam in China and of Hui identity.
