Essential tremor

Field: Neurology
Definition: Bilateral action tremor of the upper limbs, without other neurological signs
Frequency: roughly 4–8 Hz
Prevalence: about 1% overall; up to ~5% over age 65
Aggravated by: Stress, fatigue, caffeine; often eased briefly by alcohol
First-line drugs: Propranolol, primidone

Essential tremor (ET) is a neurological disorder characterised by involuntary, rhythmic shaking (tremor) that occurs during voluntary movement (action tremor) and when holding a posture against gravity. It most often affects the hands and arms, but may also involve the head, voice, and, less commonly, the legs. Essential tremor is one of the most common movement disorders and must be distinguished from the resting tremor of Parkinson's disease.

Signs and symptoms

The tremor is typically bilateral and symmetric, present when the arms are held outstretched and during activities such as writing, drinking from a cup, or using utensils — which can make eating, shaving, and buttoning clothes difficult. Head tremor may be of a "yes-yes" (vertical) or "no-no" (horizontal) type, and the voice may quaver. Tremor frequency is usually between 4 and 12 Hz and amplitude tends to increase slowly over years. Many patients report that small amounts of alcohol temporarily suppress the tremor. Stress, fatigue, strong emotions, and caffeine worsen it.

Cause

The exact cause is unknown. About half of cases appear to be inherited, often in an autosomal-dominant pattern (sometimes called familial tremor); genetic loci and candidate genes (such as LINGO1 associations) have been reported but no single cause explains most cases. Research has focused on abnormal oscillation in the cerebello-thalamo-cortical circuit and on changes in the cerebellum, including loss or dysfunction of Purkinje cells and GABAergic signalling — one reason alcohol and GABA-related drugs can reduce the tremor.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, based on the history and examination: a bilateral action tremor of the upper limbs for at least three years without other neurological signs such as dystonia, ataxia, or parkinsonism. There is no confirmatory laboratory test; thyroid disease and drug-induced tremor are excluded as needed.

Treatment is symptomatic. First-line medications are the beta-blocker propranolol and the anticonvulsant primidone, each effective in roughly half of patients; other options include topiramate, gabapentin, and botulinum toxin injections for head or voice tremor. For disabling, drug-refractory tremor, deep brain stimulation of the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus and MRI-guided focused ultrasound thalamotomy are established surgical options. Occupational therapy, weighted utensils, and wrist weights help some patients.

Not to be confused with

Essential tremor is an unrelated condition to essential thrombocythemia, a blood disorder; the shared word "essential" in medicine means "of unknown cause / primary" in both cases.
