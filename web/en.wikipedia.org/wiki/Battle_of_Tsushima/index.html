The Battle of Tsushima (27–28 May 1905) was the decisive naval battle of the Russo-Japanese War. The Japanese Combined Fleet under Admiral Tōgō Heihachirō intercepted Russia's Second and Third Pacific Squadrons — the former Baltic Fleet, which had sailed some 18,000 nautical miles from the Baltic — in the Tsushima Strait and annihilated them. It remains the last decisive fleet action fought by battleship fleets and the only one of its scale in the dreadnought era's approach.

Background

After the fall of Port Arthur, Russia sent the Baltic Fleet under Admiral Zinovy Rozhestvensky around Africa and Asia to relieve the Far East. Forewarned, Tōgō positioned his well-trained fleet, which had been refitted while the Russians endured an exhausting seven-month voyage, across the strait the Russians had to pass to reach Vladivostok.

The battle

On the afternoon of 27 May, Tōgō executed his famous turn in succession across the head of the Russian line — "crossing the T" — and Japanese gunnery, using high-explosive Shimose shells, overwhelmed the Russian battleships. The flagships Oslyabya and Knyaz Suvorov were sunk early; Rozhestvensky was wounded. Night torpedo attacks added to the losses, and on 28 May the surviving Russian squadron under Rear Admiral Nebogatov surrendered. Only a handful of Russian ships reached Vladivostok or internment.

Aftermath

The destruction of Russia's navy, following defeats on land at Mukden, forced Russia to the peace table; the Treaty of Portsmouth, mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt, ended the war in September 1905. Tsushima announced Japan's arrival as a great naval power and influenced naval thinking worldwide.
