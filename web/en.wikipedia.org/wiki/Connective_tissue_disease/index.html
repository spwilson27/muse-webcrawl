Connective tissue: collagen and elastin fibres in an extracellular matrix

A connective tissue disease (also called connective tissue disorder or collagen vascular disease) is any disease that has the connective tissues of the body as a target of pathology. Connective tissue is any type of biological tissue with an extensive extracellular matrix that supports, binds together, and protects organs; these tissues form a framework, or matrix, for the body and are rich in collagen and elastin. Connective tissue diseases are characterised by disturbances in the immune system or in the genes coding for matrix proteins, and can affect joints, skin, blood vessels, muscles and internal organs throughout the body.

Classification

Connective tissue diseases fall into two broad groups.

Autoimmune (acquired) disorders, in which the immune system attacks the body's own tissues:

  • Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  • Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma)
  • Sjögren's syndrome
  • Polymyositis and dermatomyositis
  • Mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) — an overlap syndrome with features of lupus, scleroderma and myositis associated with anti-U1-RNP antibodies
  • Undifferentiated connective tissue disease (UCTD), when criteria for a defined disease are not met.

Hereditary disorders of connective tissue, caused by defects in genes for collagen, fibrillin, elastin and related proteins:

  • Marfan syndrome (fibrillin-1 defect)
  • Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (collagen defects)
  • Osteogenesis imperfecta ("brittle bone disease")
  • Stickler syndrome, Alport syndrome, pseudoxanthoma elasticum

Signs and symptoms

Features shared across many of these diseases include arthritis and arthralgia, rashes and photosensitivity, Raynaud's phenomenon, sicca symptoms (dry eyes and mouth), serositis, and involvement of kidneys, lungs, heart and nervous system. Laboratory investigation often reveals antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and disease-specific autoantibodies such as anti-dsDNA (lupus), anti-CCP (rheumatoid arthritis), anti-centromere and anti-Scl-70 (systemic sclerosis).

Management

Treatment of the autoimmune diseases aims to control inflammation and immune activity with corticosteroids, conventional immunosuppressants (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate) and biologic agents, alongside organ-specific supportive care and screening for complications such as pulmonary hypertension and interstitial lung disease. Hereditary disorders are managed supportively and with condition-specific surveillance, such as aortic monitoring in Marfan syndrome.
