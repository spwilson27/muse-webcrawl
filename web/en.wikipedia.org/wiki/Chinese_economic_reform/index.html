Overview

The Chinese economic reform, known in Chinese as "Reform and Opening Up" (改革开放), is the programme of market-oriented economic changes introduced in the People's Republic of China beginning in 1978 under Deng Xiaoping. Moving away from the Mao-era command economy, the reforms introduced market mechanisms in stages while the Communist Party retained political control — a system officially termed the "socialist market economy" (from 1992–93).

Course

Reform began in agriculture with the household responsibility system (pioneered secretly by farmers at Xiaogang in 1978), which decollectivised farming and unleashed output growth. Special Economic Zones (Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou, Xiamen, 1980) attracted foreign investment; township and village enterprises boomed; price reform proceeded by dual tracks; state-enterprise restructuring in the 1990s (under Zhu Rongji) closed many SOEs with large layoffs, and WTO accession (2001) integrated China into world trade. The result was four decades of near-double-digit growth, the largest poverty reduction in history (some 800 million people by World Bank counts) and China's rise to the world's second-largest economy — alongside widening inequality, local-government debt and environmental costs that later policy ("common prosperity", dual circulation) seeks to address.
