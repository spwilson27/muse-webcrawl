Sparta

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bronze statue of a Spartan hoplite warrior with Corinthian helmet and shield]

Sparta was a prominent city-state in ancient Greece, in Laconia on the banks of the Eurotas River, famed for its unique social system and its military — for centuries the finest hoplite army in the Greek world. Spartan citizens (Spartiates) were a small warrior elite ruling over a large subject population of helots; boys entered the communal agoge training at seven, and the whole of Spartan life was organised for war. Sparta led the Greek resistance coalition by land against Persia, defeated Athens in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC), and then declined after its defeat by Thebes at Leuctra in 371 BC.

Society

Spartan tradition credited the lawgiver Lycurgus with the city's constitution: two hereditary kings, a council of elders (gerousia), five annually elected ephors, and an assembly of citizens over thirty. Spartiate men lived in barracks and ate in common messes; women, unusually for Greece, were educated, physically trained and could own property. The helots — state-owned serfs who farmed the land — outnumbered citizens many times over, and fear of their revolt dominated Spartan policy, including the secret police (krypteia) and the annual declaration of war on them.

Decline

Sparta's victory over Athens brought an empire it could not sustain; corruption, shrinking citizen numbers (oliganthropia) and the loss of Messenia after Leuctra ended Spartan power. The city dwindled to a provincial town under Macedon and Rome, later romanticised as an austere ideal — the 'Spartan mirage' that still shapes its modern image.
