Modernism (music)

Modernism in music is a philosophical and artistic movement that arose in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, characterised by a deliberate break with the tonal traditions of the common practice period and a search for new languages of musical expression. Composers associated with musical modernism sought to extend or abandon tonality, explored new rhythmic, melodic and harmonic resources, and often embraced a self-conscious attitude toward innovation and progress.

Characteristics

Musical modernism is associated with features such as extended chromaticism, atonality, the twelve-tone technique, increased rhythmic complexity, irregular metre, and the incorporation of folk music, jazz and non-Western influences. Arnold Schoenberg's emancipation of the dissonance and Igor Stravinsky's rhythmic innovations, notably in The Rite of Spring (1913), are commonly cited as defining moments. Other major figures include Claude Debussy, Béla Bartók, Anton Webern, Alban Berg, Charles Ives and Edgard Varèse.

Origins and development

The movement grew out of late-Romantic developments in the music of Richard Wagner, Gustav Mahler and Richard Strauss, whose expanding chromaticism strained the limits of functional tonality. Schoenberg's progression from late-Romantic tonal works through free atonality to the twelve-tone method of the 1920s provided one influential model; Stravinsky's neoclassicism provided another. After the Second World War, modernist ideas were extended by the Darmstadt school and composers such as Pierre Boulez, Karlheinz Stockhausen and Milton Babbitt under the banner of total serialism and the avant-garde.

Reception and legacy

Modernism's dominance in academic and institutional composition after 1945 was later challenged by minimalism, postmodernism and a renewed interest in tonality. Nevertheless, its techniques — serialism, electronic music, extended instrumental techniques and new conceptions of form — permanently enlarged the resources available to composers.
