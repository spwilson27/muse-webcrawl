Fulling

Fulling (also known as tucking or waulking) is a step in woollen clothmaking that cleanses and thickens the newly woven fabric. In the process, oils, dirt and other impurities are removed, and the cloth is beaten and shrunk so that the fibres interlock — a mild felting — producing a denser, warmer, more weather-resistant textile. It was traditionally one of the finishing stages of the woollen industry, performed by workers called fullers, walkers, or tuckers.

Process

Historically, cloth was pounded in a trough of water mixed with fuller's earth — a clay-like material that absorbs grease — originally by hand or by trampling underfoot; the Gaelic waulking tradition of Scotland preserved the communal foot-fulling of tweed, accompanied by waulking songs. From the Middle Ages, water-powered fulling mills, in which heavy wooden hammers (fulling stocks) were lifted by a cam on a waterwheel axle and dropped onto the cloth, mechanised the work. Fulling mills were among the first industrial uses of water power in medieval England and helped the woollen trade spread from towns into the countryside.

History

Fulling was known in the Roman world — the fullones of Pompeii ran prominent workshops, famously using stale urine as a degreasing agent — and remained a key craft guild occupation through the medieval period. After fulling, cloth was stretched on tenters (giving rise to the phrase "on tenterhooks"), raised with teasels, and sheared smooth.
