Mental Health Act 1983

The Act is the principal legislation governing compulsory mental health care in England and Wales.

The Mental Health Act 1983 (c. 20) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It is the main piece of legislation covering the assessment, treatment and rights of people with a mental health disorder in England and Wales, and provides the legal framework for detaining and treating people against their will — commonly known as "sectioning" — in a psychiatric hospital.

History

The Act replaced the Mental Health Act 1959, following the Percy Commission of the 1950s and a growing emphasis on the rights of patients. It came into force on 30 September 1983. It was substantially amended by the Mental Health Act 2007, which introduced Community Treatment Orders, widened the definition of mental disorder, and created new professional roles such as the Approved Mental Health Professional (AMHP) and Responsible Clinician.

Key provisions

The Act is divided into parts covering, among other things:

Part II — civil admissions. Section 2 allows detention for assessment for up to 28 days; Section 3 allows detention for treatment for up to six months in the first instance; Section 4 provides emergency admission for up to 72 hours. Applications are usually made by an AMHP or the patient's nearest relative, supported by recommendations from two doctors (one specially approved).

Part III — patients involved in criminal proceedings, including hospital orders made by courts (Section 37) and restriction orders (Section 41).

Part IV — consent to treatment, including safeguards for medication after three months and second-opinion mechanisms for electroconvulsive therapy and other specified treatments.

Section 135/136 — police powers to remove a person from a private place (135) or a public place (136) to a place of safety for assessment.

Review and criticism

The Act has long attracted debate about its compatibility with human rights, racial disparities in detention rates, and the balance between compulsion and autonomy. In 2018 an independent review chaired by Sir Simon Wessely recommended extensive reform. The Mental Capacity Act 2005, by contrast, covers decision-making for people who lack capacity for any reason and is a separate statutory scheme.
