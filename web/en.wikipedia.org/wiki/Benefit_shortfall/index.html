Benefit shortfall

A benefit shortfall arises in social security systems when the amount of a cash benefit a person is entitled to is less than the amount needed to cover a specific cost that the benefit system assumes it meets, or less than the full amount payable because of caps, limits, or deductions. The term is particularly used in the United Kingdom, where local authorities may make Discretionary Housing Payments to cover a shortfall between Housing Benefit (or the housing element of Universal Credit) and a claimant's actual rent.

Causes

Common causes of a housing benefit shortfall include:

  • The Local Housing Allowance being set at a percentile of local rents (such as the 30th percentile), so many rents exceed the maximum payable.
  • The "bedroom tax" (spare room subsidy removal), which reduces benefit by 14% for one extra bedroom and 25% for two or more.
  • The household benefit cap, which limits total benefits to a fixed ceiling.
  • Non-dependant deductions, assuming contributions from other adults in the household.
  • Benefit freezes, under which rates are not uprated with inflation or rent increases.

Responses

Claimants facing a shortfall must make up the difference from other income. Local authorities in Great Britain can award Discretionary Housing Payments, funded by central government, to help with housing costs where a shortfall threatens homelessness. Advice charities note that persistent shortfalls are a driver of rent arrears, debt, and eviction risk.

The concept is related to the broader "benefits gap" discussed in welfare economics: the difference between statutory entitlements and the income needed for a minimum acceptable standard of living.
