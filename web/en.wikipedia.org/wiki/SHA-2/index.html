SHA-2 (Secure Hash Algorithm 2) is a set of cryptographic hash functions designed by the United States National Security Agency and first published in 2001. The family is standardised in FIPS PUB 180-4 and consists of functions with digests that are 224, 256, 384, or 512 bits long: SHA-224, SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512, and the truncated variants SHA-512/224 and SHA-512/256.

Design

The algorithms process the padded message in blocks through a compression function built from bitwise operations — rotations, shifts, XOR, majority and choice functions — following the Merkle–Damgård construction. SHA-256 and SHA-224 use 32-bit words; SHA-512 and its variants use 64-bit words and different initial values and round constants (derived from the fractional parts of cube roots of primes). Truncated variants such as SHA-384 (a truncated SHA-512 with different initial values) resist length-extension attacks better than plain truncation.

Security and use

As of the mid-2020s, no practical collision or preimage attacks against the full SHA-2 functions are known, in contrast to the broken MD5 and SHA-1. SHA-2 is ubiquitous: it secures TLS certificates and digital signatures, verifies software downloads, underlies the Bitcoin proof-of-work and address scheme (SHA-256, often applied twice), and is used in protocols such as IPsec, SSH, and DNSSEC.
