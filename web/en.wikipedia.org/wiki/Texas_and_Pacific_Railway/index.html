Texas and Pacific Railway

The Texas and Pacific Railway Company (reporting mark TP), commonly known as the T&P, was one of the major Class I railroads in the United States. It was incorporated by an act of Congress in 1871 to build a southern transcontinental railroad between Marshall, Texas, and San Diego, California.

History

Construction began in the 1870s, but the company struggled financially and never completed its line to the Pacific coast on its own; it reached Sierra Blanca, Texas, in 1881, where it met the Southern Pacific, forming a through route. Under financier Jay Gould, the T&P came under the influence of the Missouri Pacific Railroad, which eventually acquired control. The railroad served the oil fields of West Texas, an important source of traffic in the 20th century.

The T&P was merged into the Missouri Pacific in 1976, and the Missouri Pacific in turn became part of the Union Pacific Railroad in 1982. The landmark Texas and Pacific Station in Fort Worth, an Art Deco building opened in 1931, still stands and serves as a commuter rail station.

Legacy

The T&P's lines remain important freight corridors, including the route through Dallas–Fort Worth, Abilene, and El Paso. Several depots and the road's distinctive speed-lettering herald are preserved by museums and historical societies.
