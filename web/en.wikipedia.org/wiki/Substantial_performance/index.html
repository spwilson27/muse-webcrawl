Substantial performance
Area of lawContract law
Key caseJacob & Youngs v. Kent, 230 N.Y. 239 (1921)
Related doctrineMaterial breach; perfect tender rule (distinguished)

Substantial performance is a doctrine in contract law under which a party that has performed a contract in good faith, with only minor or inadvertent deviations from its terms, may still enforce the contract against the other party, subject to damages for the defects. The doctrine prevents the harsh result of forfeiture when strict, literal performance has not occurred but the essential purpose of the contract has been accomplished.

Leading case

The classic illustration is Jacob & Youngs v. Kent (1921), decided by Judge Benjamin Cardozo. A builder had installed Reading pipe in most of a house but, by oversight, used pipe of another manufacturer of equal quality where the contract specified Reading pipe. The court held the deviation to be inadvertent and trivial, found substantial performance, and awarded the builder the contract price less the (minimal) difference in value.

Factors

Courts consider the extent to which the injured party is deprived of the benefit reasonably expected, the adequacy of compensation for the shortfall, the hardship to the breaching party if performance is denied, the likelihood the defect can be cured, and whether the breaching party acted in good faith. If the deviation is wilful or goes to the root of the bargain, it is a material breach and substantial performance does not apply. The doctrine is most frequently invoked in construction contracts, and contrasts with the Uniform Commercial Code's "perfect tender" rule for the sale of goods.
