Functionalism (architecture)

In architecture, functionalism is the principle that buildings should be designed based solely on their purpose and function. The principle crystallised in the early 20th century within modernist architecture and is summed up by Louis Sullivan's 1896 dictum that "form follows function".

Principles

Functionalist architects argued that ornament was superfluous and even dishonest, and that a building's appearance should be the direct expression of its structure, materials and use. Characteristic results include flat roofs, smooth white surfaces, ribbon windows, open floor plans, pilotis (supports) lifting the building off the ground, and standardised, industrially produced components.

History

The idea has roots in the writings of Eugène Viollet-le-Duc and in the utilitarian design of 19th-century industrial buildings. It became a programme in the work of the Bauhaus, De Stijl and the architects of the International Style. Le Corbusier's "Five Points of Architecture" (1926) and his declaration that a house is "a machine for living in" epitomised the doctrine; in Scandinavia, functionalism ("funkis") shaped housing and public buildings from the 1930 Stockholm Exhibition onward.

Criticism and legacy

Postmodern critics charged functionalism with producing monotonous, alienating environments, and argued that buildings also serve symbolic and social functions. Nevertheless, functionalist thinking remains foundational to modern architectural education and to the design of industrial, medical and transport buildings, where performance requirements dominate.
