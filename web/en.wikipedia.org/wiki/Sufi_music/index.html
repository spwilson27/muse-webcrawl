Sufi music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sufi music is the devotional music of Sufism, the mystical tradition within Islam, performed as part of spiritual practice (sama, "listening") intended to bring listeners and performers closer to God. It encompasses a wide variety of regional forms, united by their use of poetry praising God, the Prophet Muhammad, and Sufi saints, often expressing themes of divine love, longing, and the annihilation of the self.

Forms

Among the best-known forms is qawwali, the energetic devotional music of South Asia performed at shrines of Chishti saints, popularised worldwide by Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan. In Turkey, the Mevlevi order's ayin ceremonies accompany the whirling of dervishes, with compositions for ney (reed flute), tanbur, and kudüm. Arab traditions include inshad hymns and the Andalusian-influenced muwashshah; Persian Sufi music features the daf frame drum and setar, and West African orders such as the Tijaniyya and Mourides have their own chant and praise-singing traditions. Ghazal and kafi singing in South Asia also draw heavily on Sufi poetry by figures such as Rumi, Bulleh Shah, and Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai.

Practice and controversy

The place of music in Islam has long been debated. Many Sufi orders regard sama as a legitimate and powerful spiritual discipline, sometimes producing ecstatic states (wajd), while more legalistic scholars have condemned music and ecstatic practice as innovations. The philosopher-poet al-Ghazali defended conditional listening in his Revival of the Religious Sciences, and the debate continues in modern Muslim societies.

Modern performers

Sufi music has reached global audiences through artists such as Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan, Abida Parveen, Sabri Brothers, Mercan Dede, and Sain Zahoor, and through fusion and world-music collaborations that blend traditional forms with electronic and popular styles.
