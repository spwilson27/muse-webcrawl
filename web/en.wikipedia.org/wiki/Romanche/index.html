Romansh (also spelled Romansch, Rhaeto-Romance or Romanche) is a Romance language spoken predominantly in the southeastern Swiss canton of the Grisons (Graubünden), where it has co-official status with German and Italian. It descends from the Vulgar Latin spoken by Roman settlers in the province of Raetia and is Switzerland's fourth national language, recognised in the federal constitution since 1938.

Varieties

Romansh comprises five regional written idioms, each with its own orthographic tradition: Sursilvan (in the Vorderrhein valley), Sutsilvan, Surmiran (in the Albula and Gelgia valleys), Puter (Upper Engadine) and Vallader (Lower Engadine and Val Müstair). In 1982 the linguist Heinrich Schmid created Rumantsch Grischun, a unified standard language intended for federal and cantonal administration; its adoption has been debated by local communities, several of which voted to return to regional idioms in schools.

History

Latin arrived in Raetia after the Roman conquest of 15 BC and gradually replaced the Raetic and Celtic languages of the region. Isolated by Alpine geography, the language developed distinct features while retreating over the centuries before German-speaking settlers. The first printed Romansh books appeared in the 16th century during the Reformation. In the 2010s, roughly 60,000 people in the Grisons reported Romansh as a language of regular use, and about 40,000 as their main language.

Status and media

Romansh is supported by the liaison organisation Lia Rumantscha (founded 1919), by the broadcaster Radiotelevisiun Svizra Rumantscha (RTR), and by federal and cantonal subsidies. Despite institutional support, the language is classified as endangered, as German continues to gain ground in most Romansh valleys.
