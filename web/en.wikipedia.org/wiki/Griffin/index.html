Griffin

A griffin as commonly depicted in heraldry

The griffin, griffon or gryphon is a legendary creature with the body, tail and back legs of a lion, and the head and wings of an eagle, with its front feet often bearing eagle's talons. As the lion was traditionally considered the king of the beasts and the eagle the king of the birds, the griffin was thought to be an especially powerful and majestic creature, and a guardian of treasures and gold.

Origins and history

Hybrid creatures combining leonine and aquiline features appear in the art of the ancient Near East and Egypt from the second millennium BCE, and the griffin became a familiar motif in the art of Persia, Greece and Rome. Greek writers such as Herodotus and Aeschylus placed griffins in the far north, in Scythia, where they were said to guard gold deposits from the one-eyed Arimaspians. Griffins were popular in medieval bestiaries and became one of the most common charges in heraldry, symbolising strength, vigilance and military courage.

Symbolism and later use

In medieval Christian thought the griffin could carry complex symbolism: Dante placed a griffin drawing the chariot of the Church in the Purgatorio, its dual nature reflecting the dual nature of Christ. The creature appears in the heraldry of many families, cities and nations, on coinage, and in architecture as paired guardian figures. Griffins remain popular in modern fantasy literature, games and film, often portrayed as proud, fierce mounts or protectors.

Possible real-world inspirations

It has been suggested that travellers' tales of griffins guarding gold in Central Asia may have been inspired by fossils of the beaked dinosaur Protoceratops, which are common in gold-bearing regions of the Gobi Desert and whose remains combine a parrot-like beak, four legs and shoulder blades that could be mistaken for wing bones. The theory, advanced by folklorist Adrienne Mayor, remains a matter of debate.
