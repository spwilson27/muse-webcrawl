Operation Cyclone was the code name for the United States Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) programme to arm and finance the Afghan mujahideen in their war against the Soviet Union and the Soviet-backed Democratic Republic of Afghanistan. Beginning in 1979, shortly before the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, the programme grew into one of the longest and most expensive covert operations in CIA history, with funding matched in large part by Saudi Arabia and distribution channelled through Pakistan's Inter-Services Intelligence (ISI).

Background

In July 1979, President Jimmy Carter signed a presidential finding authorising covert support to opponents of the pro-Soviet regime in Kabul. After Soviet forces intervened in December 1979 to sustain the Afghan communist government, the Carter and then Reagan administrations massively expanded the programme, viewing Afghanistan as an opportunity to give the USSR "its Vietnam".

The programme

Weapons — initially obsolete rifles, later including heavy machine guns, mortars, and, from 1986, the FIM-92 Stinger shoulder-fired anti-aircraft missile — were purchased from third countries and shipped via Pakistan, where the ISI decided which of the seven recognised mujahideen parties received them, favouring commanders such as Gulbuddin Hekmatyar. The Stingers' introduction is widely credited with blunting Soviet air superiority and contributing to Moscow's decision to withdraw, completed in February 1989.

Aftermath and debate

The operation is the classic case study in the debate over "blowback": after the Soviet withdrawal, US attention and funding lapsed, Afghanistan descended into civil war, and some of the networks and Arab volunteers drawn to the conflict later formed the milieu from which al-Qaeda emerged. Historians note that direct US funding went to Afghan factions rather than to the foreign Arab volunteers, but the long-term destabilising consequences of the programme remain widely discussed. The operation was dramatised in the book and film Charlie Wilson's War.
