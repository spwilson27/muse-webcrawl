Hubert van Eyck (also Huybrecht van Eyck; c. 1385–90 – September 18, 1426) was an Early Netherlandish painter and the elder brother of Jan van Eyck. Along with Robert Campin, the van Eyck brothers are regarded as the founders of the Early Netherlandish school and of the new, oil-based, intensely naturalistic style that transformed Northern European painting in the fifteenth century.

Life

Little is documented of Hubert's life. He was probably born in Maaseyck (now Maaseik, Belgium), and by the mid-1420s he was working in Ghent, where a payment record of 1424–25 connects him to a major work for the city's magistrates. He died in Ghent in 1426 and was buried in St. Bavo's Cathedral, in the chapel of Joost Vijd — the very chapel for which the Ghent Altarpiece was made.

The Ghent Altarpiece

Hubert is chiefly known from the inscription on the frame of the Ghent Altarpiece (the Adoration of the Mystic Lamb), a quatrain in Latin identifying "Hubert van Eyck, the greatest painter" as the artist who began the work, and his brother Jan, "second in art," as the one who completed it in 1432 for patrons Joost Vijd and Elisabeth Borluut. The altarpiece — a monumental polyptych of twelve panels with 258 figures — is one of the most influential and most frequently stolen artworks in history.

Attribution problems

No securely signed or documented independent work by Hubert survives, and the division of hands within the Ghent Altarpiece remains one of art history's enduring puzzles. A group of works once assigned to the anonymous "Hand G" of the Turin–Milan Hours was attributed to Hubert by some scholars, though most attributions are contested. Early sources, including Giorgio Vasari and Karel van Mander, credited the brothers jointly with the invention or perfection of oil painting — a claim modern research has moderated, since oil media predated them, but their exploitation of translucent oil glazes revolutionized what painting could depict.
