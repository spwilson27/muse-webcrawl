People of the Book or Ahl al-Kitāb (Arabic: أهل الكتاب) is an Islamic term referring to followers of monotheistic religions that predate Islam and possess a revealed scripture. In the Quran, the term is used most often of Jews and Christians, and sometimes of Sabians. People of the Book were granted a protected status, known as dhimma, in Muslim-governed societies: in return for paying a tax called jizya, they were permitted to practise their religion, keep their places of worship, and govern their internal communal affairs.

The concept is rooted in the Islamic view that God sent revelations to many peoples before Muhammad, including the Tawrat (Torah) given to Moses, the Zabur (Psalms) given to David, and the Injil (Gospel) given to Jesus. Because these communities received genuine, if in the Muslim view later distorted, revelation, they occupy an intermediate position between Muslims and polytheists in classical Islamic law.

Usage in the Quran

The phrase Ahl al-Kitāb appears 31 times in the Quran. Its tone varies with context: some verses praise the piety of righteous Jews and Christians, affirm that they will have their reward with God, and permit Muslims to eat their food and marry their women (Quran 5:5), while others criticise doctrinal positions such as the Christian doctrines of the Trinity and the divinity of Christ, or accuse some Jews of altering scripture (tahrif).

The identity of the Sabians, mentioned alongside Jews and Christians in Quran 2:62, has long been debated; candidates have included the Mandaeans of southern Iraq and the pagans of Harran, who reportedly adopted the name to gain protected status.

In Islamic law

Classical jurists developed the dhimma contract from Quran 9:29 and from agreements attributed to Muhammad and the early caliphs, such as the Pact of Umar. Under it, People of the Book paid jizya, accepted certain restrictions (on building new houses of worship, public display of religion, and bearing arms), and received protection of life, property, and worship in return. The Hanafi school extended the category generously, treating almost all non-Arab religious communities as eligible, while the Shafi'i and Hanbali schools restricted it more narrowly to Jews, Christians, Sabians, and Zoroastrians.

Extension to other religions

Zoroastrians were included early, on the basis of a saying of Muhammad instructing that they be treated as People of the Book. In Mughal India, Hindus were in practice granted dhimmi-like status by many rulers, and some jurists analogised the Vedas to a revealed book. In the modern period, reformist scholars have extended the term's logic to Buddhists, Sikhs, and others.
