The Jersey is a British breed of small dairy cattle from Jersey, in the Channel Islands. It is one of the world's most widespread dairy breeds, valued for milk of exceptionally high butterfat and protein content and for the breed's efficiency, fertility, and tolerance of heat. Jerseys are recognizable by their fawn coat, dished face, and dark eyes with a light ring around the muzzle.

History

Cattle on Jersey were bred in isolation for centuries; an island law of 1763 prohibited the importation of foreign cattle to protect the breed's purity and reputation. Jerseys were exported in large numbers in the 19th century, especially to the United States, Australia, and New Zealand, where herd books and breed societies were established (the Royal Jersey Agricultural and Horticultural Society dates from 1833; the American Jersey Cattle Association from 1868). Crossbreeding and selection abroad, particularly in New Zealand and Denmark, produced regional types, while the island itself maintains the original "Isle of Jersey" population.

Characteristics

Jerseys are among the smallest dairy breeds: cows typically weigh 400 to 500 kilograms and bulls 540 to 820 kilograms. They mature early, calve easily, and are notably fertile and long-lived, with efficient feed conversion partly owing to their smaller maintenance requirements. The breed handles heat better than larger European breeds, which has made it popular in tropical and subtropical dairying and in crossbreeding programs with Holsteins and with zebu cattle.

Milk

Jersey milk averages around 5 percent butterfat and 3.8 percent protein — the highest of the major dairy breeds — with fat globules that give it a rich, creamy character and a naturally yellow tint from carotene. The composition makes Jersey milk especially suitable for butter, cheese, and premium drinking milk, and under component-based payment systems its yield of fat and protein per cow rivals that of much larger breeds.
