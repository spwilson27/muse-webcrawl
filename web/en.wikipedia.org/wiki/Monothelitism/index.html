Monothelitism

Monothelitism (from Greek mono, "one", and thelema, "will") is a Christological doctrine that holds that Jesus Christ has two natures, divine and human, but only one will. It was promoted in the 7th century by the Byzantine emperor Heraclius and Patriarch Sergius I of Constantinople as a compromise intended to reconcile Chalcedonian Christians with the non-Chalcedonian (Miaphysite) churches of Egypt and Syria, whose support the empire needed against Persian and then Arab invasions.

Background

The doctrine grew out of the related teaching of monoenergism — that Christ had one "energy" or mode of activity — advanced during negotiations with non-Chalcedonian leaders in the 620s and 630s. After objections from the monk Sophronius, later Patriarch of Jerusalem, Sergius forbade discussion of energies and proposed instead the formula of one will. Emperor Heraclius promulgated the doctrine in the Ecthesis of 638, and his successor Constans II enforced silence on the controversy through the Typos of 648.

Opposition and condemnation

The leading opponent of monothelitism was Maximus the Confessor, who argued that a complete human nature must include a human will, and that Christ therefore had two natural wills, divine and human, in harmony. Pope Martin I condemned monothelitism at the Lateran Council of 649; both Martin and Maximus were arrested by the emperor, tried for treason, and died in exile after mutilation and imprisonment. The doctrine was definitively condemned as heresy by the Third Council of Constantinople (the Sixth Ecumenical Council, 680–681), which affirmed dyothelitism — two wills in Christ — and anathematised the monothelite patriarchs and Pope Honorius I.

Legacy

The controversy illustrates the entanglement of theology and imperial politics in Byzantium. The Maronite Church was historically alleged to have monothelite origins, a claim the Maronites themselves reject. Dyothelitism remains the standard teaching of the Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches.
