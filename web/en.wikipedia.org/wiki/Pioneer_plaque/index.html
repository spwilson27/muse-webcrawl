The Pioneer plaques are a pair of gold-anodised aluminium plaques that were placed on board the 1972 Pioneer 10 and 1973 Pioneer 11 spacecraft, depicting a message from humanity for any extraterrestrial civilisation that might one day intercept them. The plaques were the idea of journalist Eric Burgess, developed by astronomers Carl Sagan and Frank Drake, and drawn by Sagan's wife, Linda Salzman Sagan. Pioneer 10 was the first human-made object sent on a trajectory out of the Solar System.

Symbolism

The design is intended to be decipherable using universal physical constants. At top left, the hyperfine transition of neutral hydrogen provides a unit of time and length. A radial "pulsar map" of lines pointing from the Sun gives the periods of known pulsars, allowing the Sun's position in the galaxy — and the epoch of launch — to be deduced. At the bottom is a diagram of the Solar System showing the spacecraft's trajectory from Earth, the third planet. On the right, a man and a woman are drawn to scale against an outline of the spacecraft; the woman's height is indicated in binary using the hydrogen unit. The man's hand is raised in greeting.

Placement and fate

The plaque is mounted on the spacecraft's antenna support struts, in a position shielded from interstellar dust erosion. Pioneer 10 and 11 will drift for billions of years; because the plaque is made of gold-anodised aluminium, its message is expected to remain legible far longer than most human artefacts endure on Earth.

Criticism

The plaque drew contemporary criticism for the nudity of its human figures, and later commentators have questioned how easily its conventions — such as the arrow and the raised hand — could be understood by a non-human intelligence. The concept was developed further on the Voyager Golden Record carried by Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 in 1977.
