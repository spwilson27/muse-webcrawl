The dime novel was a form of late 19th-century and early 20th-century American popular literature, a cheap paperback novel usually priced at five or ten cents, sold in huge quantities to a working-class and youthful readership. The term became generic for any sensational, melodramatic cheap fiction — frontier tales, detective stories, romances and adventure series — and later for the story papers and "nickel weeklies" that succeeded the original format. Dime novels are a direct ancestor of pulp magazines, paperback originals and comic books.

Beadle and Adams

The publishing house of Erastus and Irwin Beadle launched the format on 9 June 1860 with Malaeska: The Indian Wife of the White Hunter by Ann S. Stephens, issued as "Beadle's Dime Novels No. 1" in distinctive orange-salmon wrappers. It sold some 300,000 copies in its first year. Civil War soldiers were a captive market, and Beadle's pocketable novels — about 100 pages, roughly 6 × 4¼ inches — travelled in knapsacks across the country. Edward S. Ellis's Seth Jones (1860) was another early blockbuster.

Content and characters

Early dime novels retold frontier and Revolutionary War adventures; after the Civil War they mythologised the West. Edward L. Wheeler's Deadwood Dick (from 1877) and the exploits of Buffalo Bill — written chiefly by Ned Buntline and Prentiss Ingraham — created the enduring popular image of the cowboy West. Urban settings followed: detective heroes such as Old Sleuth and, from 1886 in Street & Smith's Nick Carter stories, the archetypal series detective. Other lines served young readers with school and sport stories, notably Gilbert Patten's Frank Merriwell (as "Burt L. Standish") from 1896.

Formats and publishers

Competition from story papers (weekly newspaper-format fiction) and cheaper "nickel novels" changed the format in the 1870s–80s; publishers such as Street & Smith, George Munro and Frank Tousey issued vast series — libraries of complete novels, half-dime novels for boys, and reprint lines. Authors were typically paid by the story, wrote at speed to house formulas, and often published under house names. Print runs in the hundreds of thousands made dime novels the first true mass medium of American fiction.

Moral panic and decline

Clerics, librarians and moral reformers — Anthony Comstock campaigned against them as corrupting trash — blamed dime novels for juvenile crime, while literacy campaigners privately conceded they taught millions to read. Rising production costs, competition from pulps after 1896, public libraries, and finally cinema and radio eroded the market; Street & Smith converted its remaining weeklies into pulps such as Western Story Magazine around 1915–1930.

Legacy

Dime novels fixed the stock characters and plots of American genre fiction — the scout, the outlaw, the detective, the boy inventor — and their series economics shaped the pulp and comic-book industries. Major collections are held by the Library of Congress, Stanford, and the University of Minnesota, and scholars such as Michael Denning read them as a window on working-class culture.
