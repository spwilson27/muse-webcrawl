Oil well

An oil well is a boring in the Earth that is designed to bring petroleum oil hydrocarbons to the surface. Usually some natural gas is released as associated petroleum gas along with the oil. A well designed to produce primarily gas may be termed a gas well.

History

The earliest known oil wells were drilled in China in 347 CE, using bits attached to bamboo poles to depths of up to about 240 metres (790 ft). In the modern era, the well drilled by Edwin Drake in 1859 near Titusville, Pennsylvania, is widely regarded as the first successful modern oil well, launching the petroleum industry.

Drilling and completion

Modern oil wells are drilled with a drilling rig that rotates a drill bit, while drilling mud circulates to cool the bit and carry rock cuttings to the surface. Steel casing is cemented into the borehole to stabilize it and protect groundwater. Once the target reservoir is reached, the well is "completed": the casing is perforated so oil can flow in, and production tubing is installed.

Production

Initially, reservoir pressure may drive oil to the surface naturally. As pressure declines, artificial lift methods are used, most famously the nodding donkey or pumpjack. Techniques such as water injection and hydraulic fracturing can increase recovery. Wells are also drilled offshore from platforms, and directionally or horizontally to reach reservoirs from a distance.

Abandonment

When a well is no longer economically productive, it is plugged and abandoned. Poorly maintained abandoned ("orphan") wells can leak methane and other contaminants and are an environmental concern.
