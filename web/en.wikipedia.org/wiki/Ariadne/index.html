Ariadne


Ariadne with the thread that guided Theseus out of the Labyrinth.

In Greek mythology, Ariadne (Greek: Ἀριάδνη) was a Cretan princess, the daughter of King Minos of Crete and his wife Pasiphaë. She is mostly associated with the Labyrinth, which she helped the hero Theseus escape after he killed the Minotaur, her half-brother.

Mythology

Minos placed the Minotaur, the creature with the head of a bull and the body of a man born to Pasiphaë, in the Labyrinth built by Daedalus. Athens was compelled to send a tribute of seven youths and seven maidens to be devoured by the Minotaur. When Theseus arrived in Crete as one of the sacrificial youths, Ariadne fell in love with him and gave him a ball of thread (the famous "Ariadne's thread"), instructing him to unwind it as he went into the Labyrinth so that he could find his way out again after killing the monster.

Theseus slew the Minotaur and escaped, taking Ariadne with him, but on the island of Naxos he abandoned her as she slept. In most versions of the myth, the god Dionysus found her there, fell in love with her, and married her. He placed her wedding crown among the stars as the constellation Corona Borealis. In some traditions, Ariadne was made immortal by Zeus.

Worship

Ariadne was worshipped on Naxos, Cyprus and in Athens; on Naxos two festivals honoured two aspects of her, one joyful (as Dionysus's bride) and one mournful (as the abandoned maiden who died there). Her figure may preserve memories of a Minoan goddess.

In later culture

Ariadne's story has inspired countless works of art, music and literature, from Titian's painting Bacchus and Ariadne to Richard Strauss's opera Ariadne auf Naxos. Her name is given to the "Ariadne's thread" method of problem-solving through exhaustive exploration, and to the European Ariane family of space launch vehicles.
