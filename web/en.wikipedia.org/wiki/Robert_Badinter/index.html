Born: 30 March 1928, Paris, France
Died: 9 February 2024, Paris, France
Occupation: Lawyer, academic, politician

Robert Badinter (30 March 1928 – 9 February 2024) was a French lawyer, scholar, and politician, best known for leading the abolition of the death penalty in France in 1981 while serving as Minister of Justice under President François Mitterrand.

Early life and legal career

Badinter was born in Paris to a Jewish family originally from Bessarabia; his father was deported and murdered at Sobibor during the Second World War. Badinter studied law in Paris and at Columbia University, and became a prominent Parisian trial lawyer and professor of law. As a defence lawyer he took part in capital cases — including the defence of Roger Bontems, executed in 1972, and the 1977 trial of Patrick Henry, in which his plea against the death penalty helped secure a life sentence — experiences that deepened his abolitionist convictions, set out in his book L'Exécution (1973).

Minister of Justice and abolition

Appointed Minister of Justice in 1981, Badinter presented the bill abolishing capital punishment to the National Assembly, declaring that France would remove "the shadow of the guillotine" from its law; the abolition law was promulgated on 9 October 1981. His tenure also advanced prisoners' rights, abolished special courts, and decriminalised homosexual relations between consenting adults in the discriminatory form then in force. From 1986 to 1995 he presided over the Constitutional Council, and he later served as a senator for Hauts-de-Seine (1995–2011).

Later work and death

Badinter remained a leading voice on human rights, international criminal justice, and the worldwide abolition of the death penalty, chairing bodies concerned with the former Yugoslavia's constitution and serving on international panels. He died in Paris on 9 February 2024, aged 95, and was honoured with a national tribute; it was announced that he would be honoured at the Panthéon.
