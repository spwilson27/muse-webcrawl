Berthe Morisot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Cradle (1872): a young mother gazing at her sleeping infant through a veil of muslin, Morisot's most famous canvas]

Berthe Marie Pauline Morisot (14 January 1841 – 2 March 1895) was a French painter, a leading member of the circle of Impressionists in Paris and one of 'les trois grandes dames' of Impressionism alongside Mary Cassatt and Marie Bracquemond. Descended from the Rococo painter Fragonard, she exhibited in all but one of the Impressionist exhibitions from 1874 to 1886, and her intimate scenes of women's lives, painted with feathery, luminous brushwork, are now ranked among the movement's finest.

Her best-known works include The Cradle (1872), The Butterfly Chase (1874), Young Woman at a Ball (1875), Summer's Day (1879) and The Cherry Tree (1891–92).

From the Salon to the Impressionists

Morisot trained privately — unusually for a woman she was taught by Corot among others — and showed at the official Salon from 1864. She modelled for Édouard Manet, married his brother Eugène in 1874, and that same year joined the 'refused' artists in the first Impressionist exhibition, selling thereafter through the dealer Durand-Ruel. Critics long patronised her subjects as feminine charm; her radical cropping, sketch-like freedom and refusal of finish were at the movement's cutting edge.

Legacy

Morisot's reputation, maintained by a 1896 memorial exhibition organised by her artist friends, slipped in the early twentieth century but has risen steadily since; a major retrospective toured internationally in 2018–19, and her paintings now command prices among the highest for women artists of her century.
