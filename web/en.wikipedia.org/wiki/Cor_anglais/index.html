The cor anglais (UK: /ˌkɔːr ˈɒŋɡleɪ/, US: /- ɑːŋˈɡleɪ/; French for "English horn"), or English horn in North America, is a double-reed woodwind instrument in the oboe family. It is about one and a half times the length of an oboe, so it sounds a perfect fifth lower, in F. Its distinctive features are a pear-shaped (bulb) bell, which gives it a more mellow, plaintive tone, and a curved metal bocal (crook). The name is thought to be a corruption of the French cor anglé ("angled horn"), referring to the instrument's originally bent shape — it is neither English nor a horn.

History and construction

The instrument evolved in Silesia around 1720 from the curved oboe da caccia, acquiring its straight body with bulb bell in the mid-18th century in Vienna. It is usually made of grenadilla or other dense wood, occasionally with a straight upper joint and a variety of bell shapes. The cor anglais player in an orchestra is usually the third oboist doubling on the instrument. Its music is written a perfect fifth above sounding pitch (transposing in F), except in some older French scores written at pitch or in the alto clef.

Repertoire

The instrument is famed for expressive solos: the "Scene aux champs" of Berlioz's Symphonie fantastique, the largo of Dvořák's "New World" Symphony (whose theme became the song "Goin' Home"), Sibelius's The Swan of Tuonela, the shepherd's pipe in Wagner's Tristan und Isolde, and Rossini's William Tell overture. Concertante works are rarer; notable examples include concertos and chamber works by Honegger, Hindemith (sonata) and Carter.
