International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) is a multilateral treaty that commits nations to respect the civil and political rights of individuals, including the right to life, freedom of religion, freedom of speech, freedom of assembly, electoral rights and rights to due process and a fair trial. Adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1966 and in force from 1976, it forms, with the Universal Declaration and the companion Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, the International Bill of Human Rights; the great majority of states (over 170) are parties.

Content and monitoring

Article 1 proclaims peoples' self-determination; the substantive articles protect life (with death-penalty restrictions and an abolitionist Second Optional Protocol of 1989), freedom from torture and slavery, liberty of movement and person, humane detention, fair trial and presumption of innocence, privacy, conscience, expression, assembly, association, family and children's rights, political participation, equality before the law and minority cultural rights. The Human Rights Committee — 18 independent experts — reviews periodic state reports, issues General Comments that function as the Covenant's case law, and, under the First Optional Protocol, decides individual communications against accepting states. Reservations (notably broad ones by the United States on ratification in 1992) and weak enforcement keep the gap between the Covenant's text and practice at the centre of human-rights politics.
