Google Earth

Google Earth is a computer program that renders a 3D representation of Earth based primarily on satellite imagery. The program maps the Earth by superimposing satellite images, aerial photography, and GIS data onto a 3D globe, allowing users to see cities and landscapes from various angles. Users can explore rich geographical content, save toured places and share them with others.

History

The core technology was created by Keyhole, Inc., a company founded in 1999 and funded in part by the CIA's venture arm In-Q-Tel. Keyhole's EarthViewer 3D was released in 2001. Google acquired Keyhole in 2004 and relaunched the product as Google Earth in June 2005, making a free version widely available and dramatically popularising virtual globes. The related two-dimensional service Google Maps shares much of the same imagery pipeline.

Features

Google Earth offers satellite and aerial imagery at resolutions from about 15 metres down to a few centimetres in some urban areas, along with elevation data, 3D buildings in many cities, roads and place labels, historical imagery with a time slider, and layers of user-generated content such as photographs and Wikipedia links. Integrated tools include Street View at ground level; Google Sky, Mars and Moon modes; a flight simulator; ocean-floor exploration; and Voyager, a showcase of guided tours. Google Earth Engine, a separate platform, provides planetary-scale analysis of satellite archives for scientists and NGOs.

Versions

The original free desktop application was accompanied by Google Earth Pro, which added measurement, GIS import and printing tools and became free in 2015. A rewritten web version for Chrome and other browsers, plus apps for Android and iOS, launched in 2017, followed by the collaborative Google Earth Studio animation tool and the redesigned Earth with creation tools for user maps and stories.

Uses and controversy

Google Earth is used in education, journalism, environmental monitoring, archaeology and humanitarian work — for example to document deforestation, disaster damage and human-rights abuses. It has also drawn criticism from governments over the visibility of sensitive military sites, and some imagery is censored or outdated at official request. Users have made notable discoveries, including potential archaeological sites and previously unmapped terrain features.
