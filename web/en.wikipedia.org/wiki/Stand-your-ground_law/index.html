Stand-your-ground law

A stand-your-ground law is a law in the United States that allows an individual to use force, including deadly force, to defend themselves without any requirement to evade or retreat from a dangerous situation. Such laws remove the duty to retreat that applies in many jurisdictions before lethal force may be used in self-defense outside the home. The term derives from the phrase "stand one's ground"; the concept is related to, but broader than, the castle doctrine, which concerns defense of the home. Florida enacted the first modern stand-your-ground statute in 2005, and more than half of U.S. states have since adopted similar laws by statute or case law.

Background

At common law, a person facing attack outside the home traditionally had a duty to retreat if safely possible before using deadly force, except in jurisdictions following the American "true man" doctrine, under which a person without fault need not flee. Within the home, the castle doctrine generally imposes no duty to retreat. Stand-your-ground statutes extend the no-retreat principle to any place a person is lawfully present, and often add presumptions about reasonable fear and immunity from prosecution and civil suit when force is used lawfully.

Spread and debate

Florida's 2005 law, supported by the National Rifle Association and modelled in part by the American Legislative Exchange Council, was followed by similar statutes in more than two dozen states. The laws became nationally prominent after the 2012 shooting of Trayvon Martin, although the defense in that case did not formally invoke the statute at trial. Supporters argue the laws protect law-abiding people who defend themselves from prosecution and affirm a right to self-defense. Critics, including many law-enforcement and civil-rights groups, argue they encourage violence and complicate prosecutions. Empirical studies are contested: several analyses have associated stand-your-ground laws with increases in homicide and firearm homicide, while other research finds deterrent effects or no clear effect; a 2020 RAND review characterised the evidence that such laws increase violent crime as moderate.

Beyond the United States

Most other common-law countries retain some form of retreat consideration within a general reasonableness test for self-defense rather than a categorical no-retreat rule; Canada and England and Wales, for example, treat the opportunity to retreat as one factor among others rather than an absolute duty.
