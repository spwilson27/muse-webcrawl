Buprenorphine is a semi-synthetic opioid derived from thebaine, used to treat opioid use disorder, acute and chronic pain, and, in animals, as a veterinary analgesic. It is a mixed agonist–antagonist: a partial agonist at the μ-opioid receptor and an antagonist at the κ-opioid receptor. Its partial agonism produces a "ceiling effect" on respiratory depression, making overdose less likely than with full agonists such as heroin or fentanyl when used alone, though dangerous sedation can still occur when it is combined with alcohol or benzodiazepines.

Medical uses

For opioid use disorder, buprenorphine is most commonly given as a combination tablet or film with naloxone (brand name Suboxone), taken under the tongue; the naloxone is included to deter injection misuse, since it is poorly absorbed sublingually but precipitates withdrawal if injected. Long-acting monthly injections (Sublocade) and six-month implants (Probuphine) are also available. Treatment reduces mortality and illicit opioid use and, since changes to US law in 2023, may be prescribed by any clinician with standard controlled-substance registration. Low-dose transdermal patches and buccal films are used for chronic pain.

Pharmacology

Buprenorphine binds very tightly to the μ-opioid receptor and dissociates slowly, giving a long duration of action and the ability to block or displace other opioids — which can precipitate withdrawal in people dependent on full agonists if given too soon after their last use. Poor oral bioavailability from first-pass metabolism leads to its sublingual and other non-oral formulations. It is metabolised in the liver, mainly by CYP3A4, to norbuprenorphine.

History

Buprenorphine was synthesised in the 1960s by Reckitt & Colman researchers seeking a safer opioid, patented in 1969, and first marketed as an injectable analgesic (Temgesic) in the late 1970s. Sublingual formulations for addiction treatment were approved in France in the 1990s and in the United States in 2002. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
