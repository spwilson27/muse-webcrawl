Lava dome

In volcanology, a lava dome is a circular mound-shaped protrusion resulting from the slow extrusion of viscous lava from a volcano. Dome-building eruptions are common, particularly in convergent plate boundary settings. Around 6% of eruptions on Earth form lava domes. Because the lava is too viscous to flow far, it piles up over and around the vent, sometimes growing as endogenous lobes inflate from within or as exogenous spines and lobes emerge at the surface.

Characteristics

Lava domes are typically composed of high-silica, viscous magmas such as rhyolite and dacite, though andesitic domes also occur. They grow over timescales of months to years and range from tens of metres to over a kilometre across. Types include low lava domes or coulees (which grade into thick flows), Peléan domes with steep spines, and cryptodomes, in which magma intrudes and uplifts the ground without erupting — as happened on the north flank of Mount St. Helens before its 1980 eruption.

Hazards

Growing domes are unstable: their steep flanks and spines frequently collapse, generating pyroclastic flows and surges that are among the deadliest volcanic phenomena. Dome collapse at Unzen (Japan) in 1991 killed 43 people, including volcanologists Katia and Maurice Krafft and Harry Glicken; at Soufrière Hills (Montserrat), repeated dome collapses from 1995 onward destroyed the capital, Plymouth. The 1902 eruption of Mount Pelée, which destroyed Saint-Pierre, Martinique, involved a growing dome and spine. Dome growth can also seal a vent and allow pressure to build toward an explosive eruption.

Notable examples

  • The dome complex in the crater of Mount St. Helens, Washington, built 1980–1986 and again 2004–2008
  • The lava spine of Mount Pelée, Martinique, 1902–1903
  • The dome of the Soufrière Hills volcano, Montserrat
  • The dome at the summit of Mount Merapi, Indonesia
  • Novarupta dome, Alaska, formed after the 1912 eruption
  • Lassen Peak, California — one of the largest lava domes on Earth
