Dirty Harry problem

The Dirty Harry problem is a dilemma in criminal justice and constitutional law, named after the fictional San Francisco police inspector Harry Callahan of the 1971 film Dirty Harry. It describes the tension faced by law-enforcement officers between following legal and constitutional procedures and achieving a morally compelling outcome — catching and punishing a dangerous offender — when the two conflict, for example when evidence obtained unconstitutionally is the only way to convict a guilty person or to save a victim.

Origin of the term

The legal scholar Carl B. Klockars analysed the climactic scene of the film, in which Callahan tortures the captured killer Scorpio to learn where a kidnapped girl is buried, in a widely cited 1980 essay, "The Dirty Harry Problem". Klockars argued that officers who use "dirty" means for good ends confront an enduring moral problem: the goodness of the end does not erase the dirtiness of the means, and repeated resort to such means corrupts both the officer and the institution.

Legal dimensions

In United States law the problem appears in debates over the exclusionary rule, which bars evidence obtained through unconstitutional searches or coerced confessions; over exceptions such as inevitable discovery and good faith; and over the interrogation of suspects under the public-safety exception to Miranda warnings. Post-2001 debates about coercive interrogation and so-called ticking-time-bomb scenarios revived the Dirty Harry problem in discussions of torture warrants and emergency exceptions to constitutional rights.

In ethics and policing studies

Police ethicists use the problem to teach that noble-cause corruption — violating rules to secure convictions of the apparently guilty — is a distinct and corrosive form of misconduct, because its practitioners believe themselves justified. The dilemma remains a standard case study in criminal-justice curricula.
