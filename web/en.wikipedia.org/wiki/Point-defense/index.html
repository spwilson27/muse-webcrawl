Point-defense

Point-defense (or point-defence) is the defence of a single object or a limited area, e.g. a ship, building, or airfield, against attack. It is distinguished from area defence, which protects a broad region. The term is used particularly for short-range weapons systems that form the last layer of defence against anti-ship missiles, aircraft, rockets, artillery shells, and mortars that have penetrated longer-range defences.

Examples

Naval point-defence is exemplified by close-in weapon systems (CIWS) such as the American Phalanx, a radar-guided 20 mm Gatling cannon, and the Dutch Goalkeeper system built around a 30 mm cannon, as well as short-range missiles such as the RIM-116 Rolling Airframe Missile and Sea Ceptor. On land, counter rocket, artillery, and mortar (C-RAM) systems — including the land-based Phalanx variant — and active protection systems for armoured vehicles, such as Trophy, which intercepts incoming anti-tank missiles and rocket-propelled grenades with a small kinetic projectile, perform an analogous role.

Characteristics

Point-defence weapons are characterized by short range (typically a few kilometres or less), very rapid reaction times, and a high degree of automation, because the closing speeds of modern missiles leave little time for human decision-making. They are usually integrated with the defended platform's sensors and combat system, and serve as a final backstop: area and medium-range systems are expected to engage most threats, with point-defence handling the "leakers".
