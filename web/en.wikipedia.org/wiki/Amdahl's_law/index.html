Amdahl's law

Amdahl's law (or Amdahl's argument) is a formula in computer science and engineering that gives the theoretical speedup in latency of the execution of a task at fixed workload when resources are added to a system. Named after computer scientist Gene Amdahl, who presented it in 1967, it is most often used in parallel computing to predict the maximum improvement obtainable by parallelising a program: the speedup is limited by the fraction of the task that must remain serial.

Amdahl's law
Named afterGene Amdahl (1967)
FieldComputer architecture, parallel computing
FormulaS = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/s)

Formula

If a fraction p of a task can be accelerated by a factor s, and the remaining fraction (1 − p) cannot, the overall speedup is:

S = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/s)

As s tends to infinity, the maximum speedup approaches 1/(1 − p): if 5% of a program is inherently serial, no amount of parallel hardware can speed it up more than twentyfold. In the parallel case, s is the number of processors N, giving S = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/N).

Implications

Amdahl's law highlights diminishing returns from adding processors and the importance of reducing the serial fraction of software. It is sometimes contrasted with Gustafson's law, which notes that in practice problem sizes grow with available resources, keeping the parallel fraction effectively larger. The argument also generalises to other engineering improvements: optimising a component yields returns bounded by that component's share of the whole task.

History

Gene Amdahl presented the argument at the 1967 AFIPS Spring Joint Computer Conference as a case against the then-fashionable enthusiasm for massively parallel machines for general-purpose workloads, though its quantitative form has since become a standard tool in computer architecture.
