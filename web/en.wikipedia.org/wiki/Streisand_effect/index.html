The Streisand effect is a social phenomenon that occurs when an attempt to hide, remove, or censor a piece of information has the unintended consequence of publicising the information more widely, usually facilitated by the Internet.

Origin

The term is named after American singer and actress Barbra Streisand, whose attempt in 2003 to suppress photographs of her residence in Malibu, California, inadvertently drew far greater public attention to the photographs. Streisand sued photographer Kenneth Adelman and Pictopia.com for violation of privacy over an aerial photograph of her house from the California Coastal Records Project, which documented coastal erosion. The suit was dismissed, and Streisand was ordered to pay Adelman's legal fees. Before the lawsuit, the image had reportedly been downloaded only six times; afterwards it was viewed hundreds of thousands of times.

Techdirt editor Mike Masnick coined the name in 2005, writing: "How long is it going to take before lawyers realize that the simple act of trying to repress something they don't like online is likely to make it so that something that most people would never, ever see... is now seen by many more people?"

Examples

Frequently cited instances include cease-and-desist letters that are themselves published and ridiculed; efforts to remove leaked documents that are then mirrored across many sites; and lawsuits or takedown demands against parody, criticism or unflattering photographs that transform obscure material into news stories. The phenomenon is related to the psychological concept of reactance—people value information more when they perceive it is being withheld.
