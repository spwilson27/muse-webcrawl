Apostles in the New Testament

In Christian theology and ecclesiology, the apostles, particularly the Twelve Apostles (also called the Twelve Disciples or simply the Twelve), were the primary disciples of Jesus according to the New Testament. The word "apostle" derives from the Greek apostolos (ἀπόστολος), "one who is sent off".

The Twelve

Lists of the Twelve appear in the Synoptic Gospels (Matthew 10, Mark 3, Luke 6) and in Acts 1. The lists agree on the core names, with some variation — Thaddeus in Matthew and Mark corresponds to "Judas son of James" in Luke. Simon Peter is always named first and Judas Iscariot last in the Gospel lists. After Judas's death, the book of Acts records that the remaining eleven chose Matthias by lot to restore the number twelve, before the day of Pentecost.

Other apostles

The New Testament also applies the title more broadly. Paul of Tarsus calls himself "an apostle to the Gentiles" on the basis of his vision of the risen Christ on the road to Damascus, and his letters name other apostles including Barnabas, James the brother of Jesus, and (in Romans 16:7) Junia. The distinction between the Twelve and the wider circle of apostles is a recurring topic in New Testament scholarship.

Commission and legacy

According to the Gospels and Acts, the risen Jesus commissioned the apostles to preach the gospel "to all nations" (the Great Commission, Matthew 28) and they received the Holy Spirit at Pentecost in Jerusalem. Christian tradition associates each apostle with missionary journeys and martyrdoms across the Mediterranean world and beyond, and with the founding of early churches; the Apostles' Creed and the doctrine of apostolic succession derive their authority from them.
