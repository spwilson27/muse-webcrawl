Work–life balance

Work–life balance is the equilibrium between an individual's professional work and their personal life — including family, leisure, and personal development. The concept emerged in the late 1970s and 1980s in the United Kingdom and United States, alongside rising dual-earner households and lengthening working hours, and has since become a major theme in human resource management, labour policy, and occupational health research.

Concepts and theory

Work–life balance is closely related to the study of work–family conflict — the inter-role conflict in which pressures from work and family domains are mutually incompatible — and work–life enrichment, in which experiences in one role improve the quality of life in the other. Because individuals differ in priorities, there is no single ideal balance; researchers increasingly prefer the term "work–life interface" or "work–life integration" to reflect that boundaries between work and home are permeable, especially with remote and mobile work.

Factors and consequences

Long working hours, inflexible schedules, and high job demands are associated with poorer balance, burnout, and health problems, whereas schedule control, supportive supervision, and manageable workloads are protective. Organisations adopt policies such as flexible working hours, telework, job sharing, compressed workweeks, and parental leave to improve balance and retention.

Policy

Several countries legislate in this area: the European Union's Working Time Directive limits weekly hours, France recognises a "right to disconnect" from work communications outside working hours, and a number of jurisdictions have trialled or adopted four-day working weeks.

Criticism

Critics note that "balance" implies a simple trade-off and can place responsibility on individuals rather than on workplace structures; others point out that the concept historically centred dual-career professional households and may not fit all cultures, occupations, or life stages.
