Cement is a binder — a powder that sets and hardens by reacting with water (hydration) — used to make concrete and mortar. Ordinary Portland cement, the world's dominant type, is made by heating limestone and clay to about 1,450 °C to form clinker, ground with a little gypsum.

Mixed with water, sand and gravel, cement produces concrete, humanity's most consumed material after water (on the order of tens of billions of tonnes per year). Cement-making releases roughly 7–8% of global CO₂ emissions — from both fuel and the chemistry of calcining limestone — driving research into low-carbon alternatives.

Setting chemistry

Hydration of calcium silicates in clinker produces calcium silicate hydrate gel (the glue) and calcium hydroxide, releasing heat; the paste stiffens over hours and gains strength over weeks as curing proceeds.

History

The Romans made superb hydraulic cement from volcanic ash (pozzolana) — the Pantheon's dome still stands. The secret was lost, then recovered: John Smeaton's experiments for the Eddystone Lighthouse (1750s) and Joseph Aspdin's 1824 'Portland cement' patent founded the modern material.
