Emotional eating

Energy-dense "comfort foods" are commonly chosen during emotional eating episodes.

Emotional eating is the tendency to eat in response to emotions — particularly negative emotions such as stress, sadness, boredom, loneliness, or anxiety — rather than in response to physiological hunger. It is considered a form of disordered eating behaviour and is associated with overeating, weight gain, and psychological distress, although it occurs in people across the weight spectrum.

Characteristics

Unlike homeostatic hunger, which builds gradually and can be satisfied by a range of foods, emotional hunger tends to come on suddenly, to demand specific foods (often sweet, fatty, or salty "comfort foods"), and to persist after fullness. Emotional eaters may eat mindlessly, experience guilt afterwards, and use food to regulate mood, a pattern sometimes explained by affect-regulation and escape theories: eating blunts awareness of distressing feelings, at least temporarily.

Causes and mechanisms

Proposed contributors include childhood learning (food used as reward or soothing), stress physiology involving the hormone cortisol, difficulty identifying and labelling emotions (alexithymia), dietary restraint — chronic dieters are more prone to disinhibited eating under stress — and individual differences in reward sensitivity. The influential psychosomatic theory of Hilde Bruch held that emotional eating arises when internal hunger and satiety cues are confused with emotional arousal, often rooted in early feeding experiences.

Assessment

Emotional eating is commonly measured with self-report instruments such as the Emotional Eating Scale and the emotional-eating subscale of the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ). It is a feature associated with binge eating disorder but is not itself a formal diagnosis in the DSM-5.

Management

Approaches include cognitive-behavioural therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, emotion-regulation skills from dialectical behaviour therapy, keeping food diaries to identify triggers, and developing non-food coping strategies. Addressing underlying stress, sleep problems, and restrictive dieting patterns is also recommended.
