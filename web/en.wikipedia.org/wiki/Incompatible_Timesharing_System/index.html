Incompatible Timesharing System

The Incompatible Timesharing System (ITS) is a time-sharing operating system developed principally by hackers at the MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory in the late 1960s. The name is a joking contrast with MIT's earlier Compatible Time-Sharing System (CTSS). ITS ran on DEC PDP-6 and PDP-10 mainframes and became the centre of the early hacker culture.

History

Work began around 1967 under the direction of figures in the AI Lab community, including code contributed by Richard Greenblatt, Tom Knight, and others. ITS was written entirely in assembly language and was continually modified by its users, who were expected to fix bugs and add features themselves. The system continued in use at MIT until about 1990, and installations also ran in Sweden and elsewhere.

Features

ITS was notable for its openness and unusual design decisions: it had no passwords in its original form, all files were publicly accessible, and users could observe and even interfere with one another's sessions. It pioneered or popularised features such as a full-screen text editor (an early version of Emacs evolved on ITS), the DDT debugger, device-independent I/O, and networked access via the ARPANET. The Jargon File, documenting hacker slang, was maintained on ITS.

Influence

Richard Stallman was among the last of the ITS hackers, and the system's collapse as the AI Lab community dispersed helped motivate the founding of the GNU Project and the free software movement. Much early Lisp Machine and Macsyma development also took place on ITS machines.
