Regular space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In topology and related fields of mathematics, a regular space is a topological space in which, intuitively, a point and a closed set that does not contain the point can be separated by neighbourhoods. More precisely, a space X is regular if, given any closed set F and any point x not in F, there exist disjoint open sets containing F and x respectively.

Definitions

A topological space X is called regular if for every point x and every closed set F not containing x, there are open neighbourhoods U of x and V of F that are disjoint. Equivalently, every point has a neighbourhood base consisting of closed neighbourhoods. A regular space that is also a T1 space (equivalently, in which points are closed) is called a T3 space.

Properties and examples

Every metric space is regular (indeed completely normal). Every compact Hausdorff space is regular, and every regular Lindelöf space is normal. Regularity is hereditary for subspaces and is preserved under products. A standard example of a regular space that is not normal is provided by constructions such as the Sorgenfrey plane's relatives; an example of a T1 space that is not regular is the K-topology on the real line. Regularity sits between the Hausdorff (T2) and normal (T4) separation axioms.
