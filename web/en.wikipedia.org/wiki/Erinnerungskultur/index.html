Erinnerungskultur

Erinnerungskultur (German for "culture of remembrance" or "culture of memory") is a term describing the ways in which a society engages with its past — the ensemble of public, institutional and private practices by which history is commemorated, debated and transmitted. The concept has been used above all in Germany, where remembrance of the crimes of National Socialism and the Holocaust occupies a central, state-supported place, and where the term anchors ongoing debates about what should be remembered and how.

Development in Germany

Public memory of the Nazi era in West Germany moved from relative silence in the 1950s through the trials, generational conflicts and media events of the 1960s–1980s (including the Eichmann and Auschwitz trials and the Historikerstreit of 1986–87) to a dense memorial landscape: concentration-camp memorial sites, the Memorial to the Murdered Jews of Europe in Berlin (opened 2005), documentation centres, and grassroots projects such as Gunter Demnig's Stolpersteine. After reunification, a second strand addressed the history of the East German dictatorship. Remembrance is embedded in school curricula, official commemoration days (such as 27 January), and political rhetoric about German responsibility.

Debates

Erinnerungskultur is continually contested: debates concern the place of German suffering (expulsions, bombing) in public memory, the singularity of the Holocaust, remembrance of colonial crimes, the integration of migrant perspectives into a memory culture built around ethnic Germans, and charges — from across the political spectrum — of ritualisation or, conversely, of attempted closure ("drawing a line" under the past).
