Fender

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fender is the part of an automobile, motorcycle, or other vehicle body that frames a wheel well (the fender underside). Its primary purpose is to prevent sand, mud, rocks, liquids, and other road spray from being thrown into the air by the rotating tire. In British English the corresponding part is usually called a wing, while "fender" in British usage refers to the bumper bar.

History

Early automobiles, derived from horse-drawn carriages, used flat or gently curved splash guards over their wheels, often following the pattern of bicycle mudguards. Through the 1920s and 1930s, fenders became flowing, separate "pontoon" elements — a defining feature of Art Deco-era streamlining. In the late 1940s, designs such as the 1949 Ford integrated the fenders into the body sides, producing the "ponton" full-width body that dominates car design today. The fender's edge — the wheel arch — remains a key styling line.

Design and materials

Fenders must be stiff enough to resist stone impacts yet, on modern cars, are also part of pedestrian-protection and crash-energy management strategies. They are traditionally pressed from steel or aluminium sheet; some vehicles use plastic (such as thermoplastic olefin) fenders for weight saving and dent resistance — notable examples include the Saturn S-Series and certain off-road vehicles. Truck and trailer wheels may use simple flat "quarter fenders" behind the rear tires.

Related meanings

  • On a bicycle or motorcycle, the equivalent component is a mudguard.
  • On boats and ships, a fender is a cushion — of rope, rubber, or foam — hung over the side to prevent damage when berthing against a pier or another vessel.
  • A fireplace fender is a low metal guard placed before a hearth to keep logs and embers from rolling onto the floor.
  • A minor car collision is colloquially called a fender bender.
