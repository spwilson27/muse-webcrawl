Empty tomb

The Garden Tomb in Jerusalem, one of the sites associated with Jesus's burial.

The empty tomb is the traditional account, found in all four canonical Gospels, that the tomb of Jesus was found empty on the Sunday morning after his crucifixion. According to the narratives, women followers — prominently Mary Magdalene — came to the tomb to anoint Jesus's body and found the stone rolled away and the body gone; in several accounts they encounter an angel or angels (or a young man) who announce that Jesus has been raised. In Christian theology the empty tomb, together with the resurrection appearances, is foundational evidence for the resurrection of Jesus.

The Gospel accounts

The earliest account, Mark 16:1–8 (c. 70 AD), has Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome find the stone moved and a young man in white announcing the resurrection; the women flee in fear and say nothing — the abrupt original ending. Matthew adds a guard at the tomb, an earthquake, and an angel descending; he also reports a Jewish counter-claim that the disciples stole the body (Matthew 28:11–15), the earliest evidence of an ancient debate over the tomb. Luke describes multiple women, two angels, and Peter running to inspect the tomb. John focuses on Mary Magdalene, then Peter and the beloved disciple, who see the folded burial cloths. Paul's earlier summary in 1 Corinthians 15 mentions the burial and resurrection but does not explicitly mention the empty tomb or the women.

Historical discussion

Scholars debate the historicity of the empty tomb. Arguments in favour include the prominent role of women as discoverers (argued to be unlikely inventions, given women's low status as witnesses in the culture), the early date of the tradition, and the fact that the proclamation began in Jerusalem, where a occupied tomb could have been produced. Arguments against include the absence of a tomb narrative in Paul, variations among the accounts, and the possibility that Mark created the story. Many critical scholars regard the burial by Joseph of Arimathea and the empty tomb as plausible; others hold them legendary. Regardless of the scholarly verdict, the empty tomb remains central to Christian Easter faith and liturgy, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem enshrines the traditional site.
