Polyphony

In music, polyphony is a texture consisting of two or more simultaneous melodic lines of roughly equal importance, in contrast to monophony (a single line) and homophony (a melody supported by chords). Polyphonic writing is governed by counterpoint — the rules for combining independent voices harmoniously.

History

European polyphony developed in medieval church music from organum — chant with an added voice — through the Notre Dame school of Léonin and Pérotin (12th–13th centuries) to the elaborate motets and masses of the Renaissance. Composers such as Josquin des Prez, Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, and William Byrd brought imitative polyphony to a peak in the 15th and 16th centuries. In the Baroque era, J. S. Bach's fugues and canons represented a culmination of contrapuntal technique.

Polyphony worldwide

Independent polyphonic traditions exist in many cultures, including the vocal polyphony of Georgian and Balkan singing, Lithuanian sutartinės, and the interlocking instrumental textures of Central African music and Indonesian gamelan, each with its own rules and aesthetics.

In other fields

The term is also used in literature and linguistics: Mikhail Bakhtin described Dostoevsky's novels as "polyphonic," with multiple unmerged voices; in phonetics, "polyphony" has also been a brand name historically used for multi-voice synthesisers, where it denotes how many notes can sound at once.
