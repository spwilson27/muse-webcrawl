Free-culture movement

The free-culture movement is a social movement that promotes the freedom to distribute and modify the creative works of others in the form of free content or open content, without consent from or compensation to the original creators, by using the Internet and other media. It grew out of the free software movement and opposes what its supporters see as overly restrictive copyright laws.

History

The movement's foundations lie in the free and open-source software world: Richard Stallman's GNU Project (1983) and the copyleft concept showed that licensing could guarantee, rather than restrict, users' freedoms. Lawrence Lessig extended these ideas to culture at large in his book Free Culture (2004) and by co-founding Creative Commons in 2001, which provides licences allowing creators to share works with some rights reserved rather than all.

Institutions and projects

Free-culture projects include Wikipedia and its sister projects, whose content is freely licensed; Wikimedia Commons; the Internet Archive; and open-access publishing initiatives. A 2008 "Definition of Free Cultural Works" drafted by Erik Möller and others specifies the freedoms required for a work or licence to count as free. Critics of the movement include collecting societies and some artists, such as Jaron Lanier, who argue that free content undermines creators' livelihoods and devalues creative work.
