Jacob Cohen (statistician)

Jacob Cohen

Born: April 20, 1923, New York City

Died: January 20, 1998

Fields: Psychology, statistics

Institutions: New York University

Known for: Cohen's kappa, Cohen's d, statistical power analysis

Jacob Cohen (April 20, 1923 – January 20, 1998) was an American psychologist and statistician best known for his work on statistical power and effect size. His contributions — including Cohen's kappa, Cohen's d, and the conventions for small, medium, and large effects — reshaped how behavioral scientists design studies and interpret results.

Career

Cohen earned his doctorate in clinical psychology from New York University in 1948 after serving in the U.S. Army during World War II. He worked at the Department of Veterans Affairs and from 1959 taught at NYU, where he remained for the rest of his career, becoming a central figure in quantitative psychology.

Contributions

In 1960 Cohen introduced Cohen's kappa, a statistic measuring inter-rater agreement corrected for chance, now used across medicine, content analysis, and machine learning. His 1962 survey of a leading abnormal psychology journal showed that published studies had on average only about a coin-flip's chance of detecting medium-sized effects, igniting interest in statistical power. His book Statistical Power Analysis for the Behavioral Sciences (1969; second edition 1988) provided the tables and effect-size conventions — d = 0.2, 0.5, 0.8 for small, medium, and large mean differences — that remain standard teaching material. Cohen was also a prominent critic of ritualized null-hypothesis significance testing, arguing in papers such as "The Earth Is Round (p < .05)" (1994) for effect sizes and confidence intervals; he helped introduce these reforms into the American Psychological Association's publication guidelines.

Legacy

Cohen's emphasis on power and effect size underpins modern research methods courses, meta-analysis, and the reporting standards adopted during the replication-crisis era. The Society for Multivariate Experimental Psychology's early-career award is named in his honor.
