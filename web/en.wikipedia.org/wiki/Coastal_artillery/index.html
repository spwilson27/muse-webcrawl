Coastal artillery

A coastal artillery gun guarding a harbour entrance.

Coastal artillery is the branch of the armed forces concerned with operating anti-ship artillery or fixed gun batteries in coastal fortifications. Its purpose is to defend harbours, naval bases, straits, and other stretches of coast against attack from the sea, and to deny an enemy the use of waters within range of its guns.

History

Shore batteries appeared as soon as cannon became effective in the early modern period, and purpose-built sea forts multiplied in the nineteenth century as rifled artillery made wooden ships vulnerable. The classic era of coastal artillery ran from about 1880 to 1950: nations built elaborate networks of forts with large-calibre guns on disappearing carriages or in armoured turrets, supported by searchlights, rangefinding towers, plotting rooms, minefields, and anti-submarine nets. Examples include the American Endicott and Taft board fortifications, the British Palmerston Forts, and Germany's Atlantic Wall of the Second World War.

Decline and modern role

After 1945 the fixed gun battery was largely rendered obsolete by aircraft, guided missiles, and the declining threat of surface bombardment. Most coastal artillery branches were disbanded or converted to other roles, and their forts decommissioned — some having never fired a shot in anger, though others, such as the batteries at Singapore, Oslofjord, and Corregidor, saw famous actions. In the twenty-first century the coastal anti-ship mission is performed chiefly by mobile truck-mounted missile batteries, a form sometimes described as the successor to coastal artillery, operated by countries such as Sweden, Norway, Poland, and China.
