Cimetidine, sold under the brand name Tagamet among others, is a histamine H2 receptor antagonist that inhibits stomach acid production. It is used to treat peptic ulcer disease, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, heartburn, and hypersecretory conditions such as Zollinger–Ellison syndrome. Cimetidine was the first H2 blocker marketed and transformed the treatment of acid-related disorders, largely replacing surgery for peptic ulcers; it became one of the first true "blockbuster" drugs.

History

Cimetidine was developed in the 1960s and 1970s at Smith, Kline & French laboratories in Britain by a team including James Black, John Duncan, and Robin Ganellin, using a rational drug-design programme that began from the structure of histamine itself. Earlier candidate compounds (burimamide, metiamide) had shown the concept worked but had problems with oral activity and toxicity; replacing metiamide's thiourea group with a cyanoguanidine yielded cimetidine. It was approved in the United Kingdom in 1976 and in the United States in 1977. Black shared the 1988 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in part for this work.

Pharmacology and adverse effects

By competitively blocking H2 receptors on gastric parietal cells, cimetidine reduces both basal and stimulated acid secretion. It is also a notable inhibitor of several cytochrome P450 enzymes, so it interacts with many other drugs (including warfarin, theophylline, and phenytoin), and it has weak antiandrogenic effects that can cause gynaecomastia with long-term high-dose use. In modern practice it has been largely superseded by later H2 antagonists such as famotidine and by proton pump inhibitors, though it remains available, including over the counter in some countries.
