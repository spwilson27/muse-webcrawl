Dressage

Dressage (from French dresser, "to train") is a form of horse riding and an equestrian sport defined by the International Equestrian Federation (FEI) as "the highest expression of horse training", where horse and rider perform a series of predetermined movements in an arena from memory. Dressage competitions are held at all levels, from amateur to the Olympic Games and World Equestrian Games, and the discipline is one of the three Olympic equestrian sports, along with eventing and show jumping.

History

Systematic horse training dates to the ancient Greek writer Xenophon, whose work On Horsemanship remains influential. Dressage developed through Renaissance riding academies — notably in Naples, and later at the Spanish Riding School in Vienna (founded in the 16th–18th centuries) and the Cadre Noir in Saumur, France — which codified classical principles. Dressage first appeared as an Olympic sport at the 1912 Stockholm Games, originally restricted to commissioned military officers; civilian and female competitors were admitted in the mid-20th century.

Competition

In competition, horse and rider perform a test of prescribed movements in a standard arena (60 m × 20 m for senior tests), judged on accuracy, impulsion, submission, and the quality of the gaits. Levels progress from basic tests to Grand Prix, the highest level, which includes advanced movements such as piaffe, passage, extended gaits, pirouettes, and flying changes of lead in sequence. Musical freestyle (kür) competitions, in which tests are choreographed to music, are popular features of championships.

Training

Classical dressage training follows a systematic scale (the German Ausbildungsskala): rhythm, suppleness, contact, impulsion, straightness, and collection. The goal is a horse that responds to nearly invisible aids, carrying more weight on its hindquarters and moving with elasticity and balance. Warmblood breeds, particularly German and Dutch lines, dominate modern competition, though many breeds can succeed at the lower levels.
