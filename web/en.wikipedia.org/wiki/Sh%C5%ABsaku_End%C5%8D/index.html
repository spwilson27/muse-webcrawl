Shūsaku Endō

Shūsaku Endō (遠藤 周作, Endō Shūsaku; March 27, 1923 – September 29, 1996) was a Japanese author, one of the most prominent members of the "Third Generation" of postwar Japanese writers. A Roman Catholic, he is best known for exploring the tension between his faith and Japanese culture, most famously in his novel Silence (1966), about the persecution of hidden Christians in 17th-century Japan. Graham Greene counted him among the finest novelists of the 20th century, and Endō was repeatedly discussed as a candidate for the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Life

Endō was born in Tokyo and spent part of his childhood in Dalian, Manchuria. After his parents' divorce he returned to Japan, where his mother converted to Catholicism and he was baptized at about age eleven. He studied French literature at Keio University and in France at the University of Lyon (1950–1953). His career was repeatedly interrupted by illness, including tuberculosis and major lung surgery, experiences that fed into novels such as The Sea and Poison (1957), about wartime medical experiments, and Deep River (1993).

Works

  • White Man (1955) and Yellow Man — early prize-winning novellas
  • The Sea and Poison (1957)
  • Wonderful Fool (1959)
  • Silence (1966) — winner of the Tanizaki Prize; adapted for film by Masahiro Shinoda (1971) and by Martin Scorsese (2016)
  • The Samurai (1980) — about the envoy Hasekura Tsunenaga
  • Scandal (1986)
  • Deep River (1993)

His recurring themes include the "swamp" of Japan that he believed transformed foreign faiths, the maternal image of God and of Christ as a suffering companion, apostasy and weakness, and the hidden Christians (Kakure Kirishitan). Alongside his serious works he wrote humorous essays under the persona "Fox".

Honours

Endō received the Akutagawa Prize (1955), the Tanizaki Prize, the Noma Literary Prize, and the Order of Culture (1995), Japan's highest cultural honour. The Endō Shūsaku Literary Museum stands in Sotome, Nagasaki, a setting associated with Silence.
