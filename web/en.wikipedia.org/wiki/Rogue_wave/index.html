Rogue wave

Rogue waves (also known as freak waves, monster waves, episodic waves, killer waves, extreme waves, and abnormal waves) are unusually large and unpredictable surface waves that can be extremely dangerous to ships and isolated structures such as lighthouses. They are typically defined as waves whose height is more than twice the significant wave height of the surrounding sea state.

Evidence

Long dismissed as sailors' folklore, rogue waves were scientifically confirmed on 1 January 1995, when a wave of 26 metres (85 ft) struck the Draupner oil platform in the North Sea and was measured by laser sensors. In subsequent years satellite radar surveys detected individual waves up to 30 metres high, and ships such as the MS München, lost in 1978, have been retrospectively attributed to rogue waves.

Causes

Proposed mechanisms include constructive interference of multiple wave trains, focusing by currents (as in the Agulhas Current off South Africa), and nonlinear effects described by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, which can produce "breather" solutions analogous to optical rogue waves observed in fibre-optic experiments.

Impact

Rogue waves exert enormous pressures on hulls and offshore structures, far beyond the loads vessels are normally designed to withstand. Modern ship design standards and satellite monitoring now take their statistics into account.
