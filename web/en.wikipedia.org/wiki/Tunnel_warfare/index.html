Tunnel warfare

Tunnel warfare is warfare conducted in tunnels and other underground cavities, whether natural (such as caves) or artificially constructed. It encompasses mining under fortifications to collapse their walls, the use of tunnel networks for shelter, movement and surprise attack, and combat fought inside underground complexes themselves.

Ancient and medieval mining

Attackers have dug under walls since antiquity: Roman armies faced mining and counter-mining at sieges such as Dura-Europos, where a Persian mine and a Roman countermine met underground. Medieval siegecraft formalised the practice as "sapping" — digging a mine beneath a curtain wall, propping the cavity with timbers, then burning the props (sometimes with pig carcasses as fuel) so the wall collapsed into the void.

First World War

Static trench warfare on the Western Front revived military mining on an industrial scale. Specialist tunnelling companies drove deep galleries beneath enemy lines and packed them with explosives. At the Battle of Messines in June 1917, the British detonated 19 enormous mines under the German positions, killing thousands in one of the largest non-nuclear explosions then recorded.

Modern tunnel warfare

In the Pacific theatre of the Second World War, Japanese forces defended islands such as Iwo Jima and Okinawa from extensive cave and tunnel systems. During the Vietnam War, the Viet Cong's tunnel networks — most famously at Củ Chi — sheltered fighters from overwhelming American firepower, and US "tunnel rats" were sent in to clear them. North Korea dug infiltration tunnels under the Demilitarised Zone, and militant groups in the Middle East have used cross-border and urban tunnels extensively, prompting dedicated counter-tunnel units, sensors and robotics.
