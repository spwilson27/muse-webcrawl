Musica universalis

Musica universalis (literally "universal music"), also called the music of the spheres or harmony of the spheres, is an ancient philosophical concept holding that the movements of the planets and other celestial bodies create a form of music—not literally audible, but a harmonic, mathematical order governing the cosmos.

Origins

The idea is associated with Pythagoras (6th century BCE) and his school, who taught that the distances of the planets bore the same ratios as those producing musical consonances. Plato developed the notion in the Republic (the Myth of Er) and the Timaeus, where the world-soul is constructed according to musical ratios.

Medieval and Renaissance thought

Boethius, in his De institutione musica (c. 510 CE), divided music into musica mundana (the music of the cosmos), musica humana (the harmony of body and soul), and musica instrumentalis (sounding music). This scheme shaped Western music theory for a thousand years. Johannes Kepler's Harmonices Mundi (1619) sought literal musical scales in planetary motions, even as it announced his third law of planetary motion. Marsilio Ficino and other Renaissance thinkers linked cosmic harmony to medicine and astrology.

Modern echoes

Though abandoned as astronomy, the concept persists as a metaphor in art, literature, and popular science, and it influenced composers from the medieval era to Gustav Holst (The Planets) and beyond.
