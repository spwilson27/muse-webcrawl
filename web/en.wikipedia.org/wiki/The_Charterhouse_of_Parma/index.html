The Charterhouse of Parma

The Charterhouse of Parma (French: La Chartreuse de Parme) is a novel by the French writer Stendhal (Marie-Henri Beyle), published in 1839. Written in just 52 days, it follows Fabrice del Dongo, a young Italian nobleman, through the Battle of Waterloo and the intrigues of the petty court of Parma in Restoration Italy. It is generally regarded, along with The Red and the Black, as Stendhal's masterpiece.

Fabrice experiences the Battle of Waterloo in bewilderment — a landmark of literary realism.

Plot

Fabrice, the younger son of the reactionary Marchese del Dongo, is secretly fathered — the novel hints — by a French officer billeted at the family castle on Lake Como. Idolising Napoleon, Fabrice runs away to join him in 1815 and experiences Waterloo as mud, theft, and confusion, unable to tell whether he has been in a battle at all. Returning to Italy, protected by his adoring aunt Gina, Duchess Sanseverina, he is pushed into a church career at the court of Parma, where Gina and her lover, the prime minister Count Mosca, plot against their enemies. Fabrice kills a rival in a duel over an actress, is imprisoned in the Farnese Tower, and falls in love with the governor's daughter, Clelia Conti, whom he courts from the tower by lamplight signals. After a daring escape engineered by Gina, scandal, exile, and Clelia's marriage to another intervene. Their love continues in secret; their child dies, Clelia dies of grief, and Fabrice withdraws to the Charterhouse (Carthusian monastery) of Parma, where he soon dies, followed within the year by Gina.

Themes and style

The Waterloo chapters are celebrated as the first truly realistic depiction of battle in fiction — Tolstoy acknowledged their influence on War and Peace. The novel blends romance and irony, political satire of the Restoration courts, and Stendhal's characteristic psychological acuity and rapid, detached prose. Honoré de Balzac championed the book in a famous 1840 review, praising it lavishly while suggesting structural revisions.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted for film (1948, with Gérard Philipe), television, and opera (Henri Sauguet's La Chartreuse de Parme, premiered 1939).
