Burden of proof

Burden of proof
LatinOnus probandi
TypesBurden of production; burden of persuasion
StandardsBeyond reasonable doubt; clear and convincing; preponderance; reasonable suspicion

The burden of proof (Latin: onus probandi) is the obligation placed on a party in a dispute — legal, philosophical or everyday — to produce evidence sufficient to establish the truth of their claim. If the party bearing the burden fails to meet the applicable standard, the claim fails by default: the burden thus determines who loses when the evidence is evenly balanced or absent. The general rule in most traditions is that the burden lies on the party making an assertion ("he who asserts must prove"), and in criminal cases on the prosecution.

Two burdens

Lawyers distinguish the burden of production (the duty to introduce enough evidence to get an issue before the fact-finder, which may shift back and forth during trial) from the burden of persuasion (the duty to convince the fact-finder to the required standard, which usually stays fixed). In criminal trials in common-law systems the prosecution bears both burdens for every element of the offence, subject to limited exceptions: a defendant raising an affirmative defence (such as insanity in some jurisdictions) may bear the burden for that defence, and statutes occasionally impose a "reverse onus", scrutinised under human-rights law.

Standards of proof

  • Beyond a reasonable doubt — the standard in criminal cases, reflecting the presumption of innocence (articulated in the English case Woolmington v DPP, 1935, as the "golden thread" of criminal law). Its constitutional status in the US was confirmed in In re Winship (1970).
  • Clear and convincing evidence — an intermediate standard used in US civil matters of special gravity, such as fraud, termination of parental rights and civil commitment.
  • Preponderance of the evidence — the ordinary civil standard: a claim is proved if it is more likely than not true (greater than 50%).
  • Lower thresholds — "probable cause" for arrest and indictment, "reasonable suspicion" for brief investigative stops, "some evidence" and "substantial evidence" in administrative law.

Philosophical usage

In philosophy and debate, the burden of proof lies on the person making a claim — especially a positive existential claim — and cannot be shifted by demanding that opponents prove a negative. Bertrand Russell's teapot analogy illustrates an unprovable, undisprovable claim whose burden rests on its proponent. The principle connects with the presumption of innocence, with retroactivity bars such as the ban on ex post facto laws, and with rules allocating presumptions throughout the legal system.
