Physical theatre

Physical theatre is a genre of theatrical performance that encompasses storytelling primarily through physical movement. Although several performance theatres use some "physical" acting, the distinguishing aspect of physical theatre is a reliance on the performers' physical motion rather than, or combined with, text to convey the story. Performers use techniques including mime, gesture, dance, object manipulation, and ensemble movement.


Physical theatre emphasises the body as the primary storytelling instrument.

Origins

Physical theatre has roots in ancient performance traditions including Greek theatre, commedia dell'arte, mime, mask work, and non-Western forms such as Japanese Noh and Kathakali. In the 20th century its modern form was shaped by practitioners including Jacques Copeau, Étienne Decroux (corporeal mime), Jacques Lecoq, Jerzy Grotowski, Eugenio Barba, and Vsevolod Meyerhold, whose biomechanics trained actors' bodies as expressive instruments.

Modern developments

Companies associated with physical theatre include Complicité, DV8 Physical Theatre, Frantic Assembly, Théâtre de Complicité's successors in devised performance, Gecko, and the SITI Company. The form often overlaps with devised theatre, dance theatre (Tanztheater), circus, and visual theatre. Training is offered at schools such as the École Internationale de Théâtre Jacques Lecoq in Paris and LISPA, emphasising neutral mask, clown, melodrama, and ensemble creation.

Techniques

Common techniques include mime and illusionary object work; contact improvisation and lifts; choral movement and ensemble synchronisation; mask performance (neutral, larval, expressive, and commedia half-masks); clown and bouffon; and the integration of text, song, and multimedia as secondary elements. Devising is often collaborative, with material generated from improvisation rather than a pre-written script.
