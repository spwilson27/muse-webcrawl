Apple cider

Fresh-pressed, unfiltered apple cider.

Apple cider (also called sweet cider, soft cider, or simply cider in North America) is the name used in the United States and Canada for an unfiltered, unsweetened, non-alcoholic beverage made from apples. Though typically referred to simply as "cider" in the United States, it is not to be confused with the alcoholic beverage known as cider in other parts of the world, which is called "hard cider" in North America.

Production

Cider is made by grinding apples into a pulp, or pomace, and pressing the pulp to extract the juice. The resulting liquid is raw apple cider, which is typically bottled without filtration, giving it a cloudy, opaque appearance distinct from clear, filtered apple juice. Because it is unpreserved, fresh cider is perishable and is traditionally sold seasonally in autumn at orchards and farmers' markets. In the United States, many states require cider sold commercially to be treated — usually by pasteurization or ultraviolet irradiation — to kill harmful bacteria such as E. coli.

Variations

Spiced or mulled cider is heated with spices such as cinnamon, cloves, and allspice. Cider may also be carbonated, or fermented into hard cider, applejack, or apple cider vinegar. Sweet cider played an important role in early American history: in colonial and frontier times it was a staple beverage, and presidential candidate William Henry Harrison famously campaigned in 1840 with log-cabin-and-hard-cider imagery.
