Plain language

Plain language rewrites complex documents so readers can find, understand and use the information the first time they read it.

Plain language is writing designed to ensure the reader understands as quickly, easily, and completely as possible. Plain language strives to be easy to read, understand, and use. It avoids verbose, convoluted language and jargon. In many countries, laws mandate that public agencies use plain language to increase access to programs and services.

The United States Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires federal agencies to use "clear Government communication that the public can understand and use." The International Plain Language Federation defines a communication as plain language "if its wording, structure, and design are so clear that the intended readers can easily find what they need, understand what they find, and use that information."

Principles

Plain language guidelines generally recommend: writing for the intended audience; organising material logically with informative headings; using short sentences and paragraphs; preferring everyday words over technical terms, and defining terms that must be used; using the active voice and personal pronouns ("we" and "you"); and presenting information in lists, tables, or question-and-answer formats where helpful. Plain language is not "dumbing down": accuracy and completeness are retained while obscurity is removed.

History and movement

The modern plain language movement grew from consumer and citizens' rights campaigns of the 1970s, when banks, insurers and governments began simplifying contracts and forms. In the United Kingdom, the Plain English Campaign, founded by Chrissie Maher in 1979, has campaigned against "gobbledygook" and awards its Crystal Mark to documents meeting clarity standards. Plain language requirements have since spread through government, law, medicine and finance in many countries.
