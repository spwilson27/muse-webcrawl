Cycladic culture


Cycladic folded-arm figurine (placeholder image)

Period: c. 3300–1100 BC (Early Bronze Age focus c. 3300–2000 BC)
Region: Cyclades islands, Aegean Sea

Cycladic culture (also known as the Cycladic civilization) is the Bronze Age culture of the Cyclades islands in the Aegean Sea, flourishing roughly from 3300 to 1100 BC. Alongside the Minoan civilization of Crete and the Helladic culture of mainland Greece, it forms one of the three principal cultures of the Aegean Bronze Age.

Art

The culture is most famous for its marble figurines — slender, abstract, mostly female figures with folded arms, carved from the islands' abundant white marble. Their stark forms influenced modern artists such as Brâncuși, Modigliani and Moore. Cycladic art also includes marble vessels, bronze tools and weapons, pottery (including the enigmatic "frying pan" vessels), and fortified settlements such as Kastri.

History

The Early Cycladic period saw prosperous trading communities exporting marble and obsidian. By the Middle and Late Bronze Age the islands fell increasingly under Minoan, then Mycenaean, influence. Major collections are held by the Museum of Cycladic Art in Athens; extensive looting of early graves to supply the antiquities market has destroyed much archaeological context.
