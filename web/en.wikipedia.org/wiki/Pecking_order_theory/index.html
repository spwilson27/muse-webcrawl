Pecking order theory

The pecking order theory is a theory of capital structure in corporate finance. It proposes that companies prioritise their sources of financing — first internal funds (retained earnings), then debt, and only as a last resort new equity — because of the costs of asymmetric information between managers and outside investors.

Development

The idea was first suggested by Gordon Donaldson in 1961 and was developed into a formal theory by Stewart C. Myers and Nicolas Majluf in their 1984 paper "Corporate Financing and Investment Decisions When Firms Have Information That Investors Do Not Have". Myers coined the term in his 1984 presidential address to the American Finance Association, "The Capital Structure Puzzle".

Logic

Managers know more about the firm's prospects than outside investors. If the firm issues new shares, investors may suspect the shares are overvalued — why else would managers sell ownership cheaply? — and demand a discount, which can make even profitable projects unattractive to finance with equity. Debt is less sensitive to private information, and internal cash carries no issuance costs or information penalty at all. The resulting hierarchy is: (1) retained earnings, (2) debt, (3) equity. Under the theory there is no well-defined target debt ratio; observed leverage simply reflects the firm's cumulative need for external finance.

Evidence and criticism

The theory helps explain why profitable firms tend to borrow less (they generate internal funds) and why share prices often fall on announcements of equity issues. Empirical tests are mixed: Shyam-Sunder and Myers (1999) found strong support for large mature firms, while Frank and Goyal (2003) argued the theory performs poorly for small, high-growth firms, which issue equity frequently. The pecking order is usually contrasted with the trade-off theory of capital structure, which assumes firms balance the tax benefits of debt against bankruptcy costs.
