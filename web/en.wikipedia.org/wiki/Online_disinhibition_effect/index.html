Online disinhibition effect

Coined byJohn Suler (2004)
FieldCyberpsychology
FormsBenign and toxic disinhibition

The online disinhibition effect refers to the tendency of people to behave online with less restraint than they would in face-to-face interaction — expressing themselves more openly, disclosing more personal information, or, conversely, becoming more hostile than they would be offline. The concept was systematically described by the psychologist John Suler in his 2004 paper "The Online Disinhibition Effect."

Suler's six factors

Suler identified six interacting features of online communication that loosen inhibitions:

  • Dissociative anonymity — the sense that one's online persona is separate from one's real identity.
  • Invisibility — others cannot see you, so their reactions carry less weight.
  • Asynchronicity — replies need not be immediate, weakening the sense of a live conversation governed by social feedback.
  • Solipsistic introjection — readers "hear" others' text in their own inner voice, projecting characteristics onto them.
  • Dissociative imagination — cyberspace is experienced as a kind of game or separate world with different rules.
  • Minimization of authority — status cues are flattened online, so people address equals and superiors alike.

Benign and toxic disinhibition

Suler distinguished benign disinhibition — unusually deep self-disclosure, acts of kindness and generosity, the seeking of help and support in forums and therapy groups — from toxic disinhibition: flaming, harassment, threats, and the compulsive visiting of disturbing content. The same factors can drive both, depending on the person and setting. The effect is frequently invoked to explain online harassment, trolling, and also the striking candour of support communities.

Applications

The framework informs the design of moderation systems, the use of real-name policies (which aim to reduce dissociative anonymity), online counselling practice, and research into cyberbullying. Later scholarship has qualified the model, noting that platform design, community norms, and persistent identities can restrain or redirect disinhibition.
