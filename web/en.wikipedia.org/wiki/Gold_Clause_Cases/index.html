The Gold Clause Cases were a series of decisions of the U.S. Supreme Court in 1935 upholding the Roosevelt administration's abrogation of "gold clauses" in contracts and its withdrawal of the United States from the gold standard during the Great Depression.

Background

Many private and public contracts — railroad bonds, mortgages, and United States Liberty Bonds — contained gold clauses requiring payment in gold coin or its equivalent value, protecting creditors against devaluation. In 1933 Congress, by Joint Resolution, declared gold clauses unenforceable, and the government devalued the dollar, raising the official price of gold from $20.67 to $35 per ounce. Bondholders sued, claiming enormous losses and unconstitutional impairment of contracts and taking of property.

Decisions

In Norman v. Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co. (1935), the Court (5–4) upheld the abrogation for private contracts as a valid exercise of Congress's power over the currency. In Nortz v. United States, a holder of gold certificates was denied recovery of the devaluation windfall. In Perry v. United States, the Court held that Congress could not constitutionally repudiate the government's own obligations — but that the plaintiff, unable to show actual damages because gold coin could not lawfully be held or exported, could recover nothing.

Reception

The four dissenters (the "Four Horsemen") denounced the decisions; Justice McReynolds declared from the bench that "the Constitution is gone." The decisions were a key victory for New Deal monetary policy and, together with the devaluation, are credited by economists with aiding recovery by ending deflation.
