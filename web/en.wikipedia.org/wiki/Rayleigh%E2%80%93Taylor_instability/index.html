Rayleigh–Taylor instability


Simulation of Rayleigh–Taylor instability: heavy fluid penetrating light fluid.

The Rayleigh–Taylor instability is an instability of an interface between two fluids of different densities which occurs when the lighter fluid pushes the heavier fluid. Equivalently, it arises when a heavy fluid is supported above a lighter fluid under gravity (or an accelerating frame), so that small perturbations of the interface grow. It is named after Lord Rayleigh and G. I. Taylor.

Development

The instability develops in stages: initially, small sinusoidal perturbations of the interface grow exponentially (linear stage). In the nonlinear stage, the heavy fluid forms descending "spikes" and the light fluid forms rising "bubbles," which interact, merge, and eventually produce a turbulent mixing layer whose width grows in time.

Examples

Rayleigh–Taylor instability occurs in many natural and engineering settings: the expansion of supernova remnants such as the Crab Nebula, atmospheric and oceanographic flows, volcanic and geological processes (salt domes), inertial confinement fusion (where it degrades implosion symmetry), and everyday phenomena such as turning a glass of water upside down.
