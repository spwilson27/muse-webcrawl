Cravat

A cravat is a neckband, the forerunner of the modern tailored necktie and bow tie, originating from a style worn by members of the 17th-century Croatian unit known as the Cravats. The word derives from the French cravate, itself a corruption of Croate ("Croat").

History

Croatian mercenaries serving in France during the Thirty Years' War wore distinctive cloth bands around their necks, which caught the attention of the Parisian court. The style was adopted by King Louis XIV's court in the 1660s and quickly became a fashionable item of male dress throughout Europe, replacing the lace collars of the earlier period. By the late 17th century, the cravat — often of fine linen or lace tied in elaborate arrangements — was an essential element of a gentleman's costume.

In the 18th and early 19th centuries cravats grew more elaborate. The dandy Beau Brummell famously devoted great care to tying his plain white cravat, and manuals were published describing dozens of named knots and styles. The industrial revolution brought simpler, ready-tied versions, and during the 19th century the cravat evolved into the necktie, the bow tie and the stock. A looser, tucked-in form of the cravat survives in the day cravat or Ascot tie worn at formal daytime events such as Royal Ascot.

Military use

Cravats remain part of ceremonial uniform for some regiments descended from Croatian units, and 18 October is celebrated in Croatia as Cravat Day; the Academia Cravatica promotes the cravat as part of Croatian cultural heritage, and in 2003 a giant red cravat was tied around the Roman arena in Pula.
