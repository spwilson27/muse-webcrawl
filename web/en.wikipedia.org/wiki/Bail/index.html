Bail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bail is a set of pre-trial restrictions imposed on a suspect to ensure that they will not hamper the judicial process, most commonly the payment of money (or pledge of property) that is forfeited if the defendant fails to appear in court or violates conditions. Bail balances the presumption of innocence and liberty against flight risk, public safety and interference with witnesses; alternatives include release on recognisance (a promise), sureties, curfews, reporting duties and electronic monitoring.

Systems and reform

The United States' commercial bail industry — defendants pay bondsmen a non-refundable premium (typically 10%) — is nearly unique (with the Philippines) and is the focus of sustained reform criticism for detaining the poor while releasing the affluent accused; several jurisdictions (New Jersey 2017, Illinois 2023, New York in stages) have curtailed or eliminated cash bail, with effects on jail populations and court-appearance rates under study. Most other systems abandoned commercial bail centuries ago, using judicial detention decisions within statutory presumptions (the UK's Bail Act 1976 presumes bail except for specified risks). Excessive bail is constitutionally prohibited in the US Eighth Amendment; in practice, bail amounts and pre-trial detention populations — a large share of local jail inmates are unconvicted — drive the policy debate.
