Tzitzit

Tzitzit (Hebrew: צִיצִית, ṣīṣīt; plural tzitziyot) are specially knotted ritual fringes, or tassels, worn by observant Jews attached to the four corners of a garment — most visibly the tallit (prayer shawl) and the tallit katan, a fringed undershirt worn throughout the day. The wearing of tzitzit is commanded in the Torah and is regarded in Judaism as a constant physical reminder of the commandments.

Tzitzit are wound and knotted in a prescribed pattern on each corner of the garment.

Biblical source

The commandment appears in Numbers 15:37–41, where the Israelites are told to make fringes on the corners of their garments, including a thread of tekhelet (blue-violet dye), "and you shall look at it and remember all the commandments of the LORD and do them"; Deuteronomy 22:12 likewise commands tassels on the four corners of one's covering. The passage is recited daily as the third paragraph of the Shema.

Construction

Each fringe consists of four threads passed through a hole near the corner of the garment, doubled to eight strands, then wound and double-knotted according to custom — commonly a pattern of windings of 7, 8, 11, and 13 between five double knots, totalling 39 windings; the numbers are traditionally made to allude to the divine name and to the word tzitzit itself, whose numerical value (600), plus the eight strands and five knots, yields 613, the number of the commandments. Strings are traditionally of wool or of the same material as the garment.

The tekhelet thread

The Torah's blue thread was dyed with a substance from a sea creature called the ḥillazon. The identity of the dye source was lost in antiquity, and for many centuries tzitzit were made entirely white. Since the late nineteenth century, scholars and rabbis have argued that the source was the banded dye-murex snail (Hexaplex trunculus), whose dye turns blue-violet in sunlight; a growing number of Jews now wear a tekhelet thread produced from this snail, while others maintain the all-white custom.

Practice

Observant Jewish men (and, in some communities, women) wear the tallit katan under clothing all day and the large tallit at morning prayers. A blessing is recited when donning the garment. Tzitzit are not worn at night by custom, and special care is taken that the strings remain untangled and of proper length.
