Folk costume
Traditional clothing expressing the identity of a place, people or period.

A folk costume (also regional costume, national costume, traditional clothing, or traditional regalia) is the traditional, distinctive dress of a region, people, or historical period, expressing local or national identity through characteristic garments, embroidery, colours, and accessories. Folk costumes can be everyday dress, preserved in rural life, or special-occasion clothing worn for festivals, weddings, dances, and national celebrations.

Characteristics

Folk costumes typically developed from the clothing worn by rural populations over centuries, adapted to local materials (linen, wool, leather), climate, and occupations, and elaborated for festive use with embroidery, lace, metal ornaments, and distinctive headwear. Particular colours, patterns, and items could signal the wearer's village, marital status, age, or wealth. During the 19th century, Romantic nationalism across Europe encouraged the collection, codification, and sometimes invention of folk costumes as symbols of national identity, a process that fixed many regional variants in the form in which they are still known.

Examples

  • Germany and Austria: the dirndl (bodice, blouse, full skirt and apron) and men's lederhosen, worn especially in Bavaria and at festivals such as Oktoberfest
  • Scandinavia: the Norwegian bunad and Swedish folkdräkt, with silver jewellery (sølje)
  • Scotland: Highland dress — the tartan kilt with sporran, jacket and accessories
  • Japan: the kimono
  • Korea: the hanbok
  • India: the sari and related regional dress
  • Ukraine: the embroidered vyshyvanka shirt
  • Mexico: china poblana dress; charro suits
  • Netherlands: Volendam and Marken costumes with distinctive caps and wooden shoes
  • Romania: the embroidered ie blouse and regional ensembles
  • Saami people: the gákti of northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia

Contemporary use

Today folk costumes are worn mainly for folk dance ensembles, festivals, religious and national holidays, weddings, tourism, and official representation — for example, Icelandic women wear the upphlutur at formal occasions, and Bhutan retains traditional dress (gho and kira) as everyday clothing required by the Driglam Namzha code. Museums of folk culture preserve and document historic garments, and revivals of weaving, embroidery, and natural dyeing traditions accompany renewed interest in heritage dress.
