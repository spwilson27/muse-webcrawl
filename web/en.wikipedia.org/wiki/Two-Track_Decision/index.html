Date12 December 1979

SubjectIntermediate-range nuclear forces in Europe

The NATO Double-Track Decision (German: NATO-Doppelbeschluss) was a decision by NATO on 12 December 1979 to modernize the alliance's theatre nuclear forces in Western Europe while simultaneously pursuing arms-control negotiations with the Soviet Union.

Background

During the 1970s the Soviet Union deployed the RSD-10 Pioneer (NATO designation SS-20 Saber), a mobile intermediate-range ballistic missile carrying three warheads, targeted at Western Europe. Western European leaders, notably West German Chancellor Helmut Schmidt in his 1977 Alastair Buchan Memorial Lecture, voiced concern that the Euro-strategic balance was shifting and that the credibility of the United States' extended deterrent might be "decoupled" from the defence of Europe.

The decision

The decision had two "tracks". The first track called for the deployment in Europe, beginning in late 1983, of 572 new American single-warhead missiles: 108 Pershing II ballistic missiles and 464 BGM-109G ground-launched cruise missiles, to be stationed in West Germany, the United Kingdom, Italy, the Netherlands, and Belgium. The second track committed NATO to negotiate with the Soviet Union on limiting intermediate-range nuclear forces, with the deployments to be reduced or cancelled if an agreement made them unnecessary.

Protests and deployment

The decision triggered some of the largest protest movements in post-war Western European history, with hundreds of thousands demonstrating in Bonn, London, Rome, and other cities in the early 1980s. Despite the protests, deployments began in late 1983 after West Germany's Bundestag voted in favour in November 1983. Negotiations eventually led to the 1987 Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty (INF Treaty), which eliminated the entire class of missiles on both sides.
