Weed control

Weed control is a type of pest control that attempts to stop or reduce growth of weeds, especially noxious weeds, with the aim of reducing their growth to a level acceptable to farmers, foresters, conservationists and other land managers. Weeds compete with crops and desirable plants for light, water and nutrients, and can significantly reduce yields if left unmanaged.

Methods

Weed control methods fall into several broad categories:

  • Cultural control – practices such as crop rotation, cover cropping, mulching, stale seedbeds and adjusting planting density to favour the crop over the weed.
  • Mechanical control – tillage, hoeing, mowing, hand-pulling, and thermal methods such as flame weeding and soil solarisation.
  • Chemical control – the application of herbicides, which may be selective (killing particular groups of plants) or non-selective, and applied pre-emergence or post-emergence.
  • Biological control – the use of natural enemies, including insects, pathogens and grazing animals, to suppress weed populations.

Integrated weed management

Integrated weed management (IWM) combines multiple control tactics with knowledge of weed biology and ecology, aiming to keep weed populations below economic injury thresholds while minimising environmental impact and slowing the evolution of herbicide resistance. Herbicide-resistant weeds, including glyphosate-resistant Palmer amaranth and black-grass resistant to multiple modes of action, have become a major challenge in modern agriculture.

History

For most of history, weeds were controlled by hand and by hoe. The development of synthetic herbicides in the 1940s, beginning with 2,4-D, transformed weed management, and herbicide-tolerant genetically modified crops in the 1990s further increased reliance on chemical control in some farming systems.
