Ferdinand von Lindemann

Carl Louis Ferdinand von Lindemann (1852–1939), who proved that π is transcendental.

Carl Louis Ferdinand von Lindemann (12 April 1852 – 6 March 1939) was a German mathematician, noted for his proof, published in 1882, that π (pi) is a transcendental number, meaning it is not a root of any polynomial with rational coefficients.

Life and career

Lindemann was born in Hanover, the son of a language teacher. He studied mathematics at Göttingen, Erlangen, and Munich, receiving his doctorate in 1873 under Felix Klein, with a dissertation on non-Euclidean geometry. He subsequently held professorships at Freiburg (from 1877), Königsberg (from 1883), and finally Munich (from 1893), where he remained for the rest of his career. Among his doctoral students were David Hilbert, Hermann Minkowski, and Arnold Sommerfeld.

Proof that π is transcendental

Charles Hermite had proved in 1873 that e is transcendental. Building on Hermite's methods, Lindemann proved in 1882 that ea is transcendental for every non-zero algebraic number a. Applying Euler's identity, this implies that π cannot be algebraic, since otherwise iπ would give a contradiction. His result—the special case of what is now called the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem, generalised by Karl Weierstrass in 1885—settled the ancient problem of squaring the circle: because π is transcendental, it cannot be constructed with compass and straightedge.

Other work

Lindemann also worked on geometry, number theory, and the history of mathematics, and he edited and expanded the lectures of Clebsch on geometry. He was ennobled (receiving the "von") in 1918.
