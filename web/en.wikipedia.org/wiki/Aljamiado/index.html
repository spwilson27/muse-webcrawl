Aljamiado

Aljamiado (Spanish: [alxaˈmjaðo]; from Arabic al-ʿajamiyya, "foreign/non-Arabic") refers to manuscripts written in Romance languages — chiefly Spanish, but also Portuguese, Aragonese, Catalan and Ladino — using the Arabic alphabet. The practice flourished among the Mudéjars and Moriscos, the Muslim populations of Christian Spain, from roughly the 14th to the 17th century.

Characteristics

Because the Arabic script lacks letters for several Romance sounds, Aljamiado scribes adapted the alphabet with modified characters and diacritics to represent vowels and consonants such as /p/ and /tʃ/. Texts were typically written right-to-left and include Qur'an translations, legal and ritual manuals, medical treatises, poetry, and narrative works such as the Poema de Yúçuf.

Historical significance

Aljamiado literature allowed crypto-Muslim communities to preserve Islamic practice after the fall of Granada (1492) and the forced conversions of the 16th century. Manuscripts were hidden in walls, ceilings and floors of Morisco houses; caches discovered in the 19th and 20th centuries — notably at Almonacid de la Sierra — transformed scholarly understanding of the community's inner life. After the expulsion of the Moriscos in 1609–1614, the tradition survived briefly in North African exile.
