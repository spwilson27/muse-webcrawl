Eradication of infectious diseases

The eradication of infectious diseases is the reduction of the prevalence of an infectious disease in the global host population to zero. It is the ultimate goal of disease control, beyond elimination (reduction to zero in a defined geographic area) and control (reduction to a locally acceptable level). Eradication implies that intervention measures are no longer needed and the disease is gone worldwide, apart from samples retained in laboratories.

Successful eradications

Two human diseases have been eradicated:

  • Smallpox — certified eradicated by the World Health Organization in 1980, following the Intensified Eradication Programme begun in 1967 under D. A. Henderson. The last natural case occurred in Somalia in 1977.
  • Rinderpest — a disease of cattle (not humans), declared eradicated in 2011, the first animal disease to be eradicated.

Ongoing campaigns

Poliomyelitis has been the target of the Global Polio Eradication Initiative since 1988. Wild poliovirus cases fell from about 350,000 per year to a handful; wild poliovirus type 2 was certified eradicated in 2015 and type 3 in 2019, while type 1 transmission persisted in Afghanistan and Pakistan, alongside outbreaks of vaccine-derived poliovirus. Dracunculiasis (Guinea worm disease) has been reduced from 3.5 million cases in 1986 to only a small number of human cases annually, without any vaccine or drug, through water filtration and case containment. Programmes also target yaws, measles and rubella (regional elimination achieved in the Americas), malaria (elimination in a growing number of countries), and lymphatic filariasis.

Criteria for eradicability

Features that make a disease eradicable include: humans are the essential reservoir (no animal or environmental reservoir); an effective intervention exists, such as a vaccine conferring lasting immunity; infection is easy to diagnose and cases are visible; and there is sustained political and financial commitment. The 1997 Dahlem Workshop and the International Task Force for Disease Eradication set out formal biological, technical and socioeconomic criteria.

Challenges

Obstacles include conflict and inaccessible populations, vaccine hesitancy and misinformation, asymptomatic transmission, waning funding as cases approach zero ("the last mile" is the most expensive per case), and the risk of reintroduction from laboratory stocks or synthetic biology. Eradication also raises ethical questions about diverting resources from broader health services to a single-disease vertical programme.
