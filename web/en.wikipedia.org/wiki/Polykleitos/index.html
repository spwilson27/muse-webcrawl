Polykleitos
Activec. 460–410 BC
OriginArgos / Sicyon, Greece
Known forThe Canon; Doryphoros; Diadumenos

Polykleitos (Greek: Πολύκλειτος) was an ancient Greek sculptor of the High Classical period, regarded alongside Phidias as one of the greatest sculptors of antiquity. Working mainly in bronze, he created a famous treatise and statue known as the Canon, embodying his system of ideal proportions for the human body.

The Doryphoros (Spear-Bearer), known through Roman marble copies.

The Canon

Polykleitos' lost treatise set out the mathematical ratios — symmetria — by which each part of the body should relate to the whole. His statue the Doryphoros (c. 440 BC) demonstrated the system: a standing athlete in contrapposto, weight on one leg, with the body's parts balanced in a chiastic arrangement of tensed and relaxed limbs. Later writers treated the work itself as "the Canon."

Works

Other attributed works include the Diadumenos (a youth tying a victory fillet), the Discophoros, and the colossal gold-and-ivory statue of Hera in the Heraion of Argos. His school at Argos influenced Greek sculpture for generations; his sons and pupils continued the workshop tradition.

Legacy

No original bronze by Polykleitos survives; his works are known from Roman marble copies and literary descriptions by authors such as Pliny the Elder and Galen. The Canon's ideal of measured proportion profoundly shaped Western art theory.
