Partial pressure

In a mixture of gases, each constituent gas has a partial pressure which is the notional pressure of that constituent gas as if it alone occupied the entire volume of the original mixture at the same temperature. The total pressure of an ideal gas mixture is the sum of the partial pressures of the gases in the mixture (Dalton's law).

ptotal = p1 + p2 + … + pn

The partial pressure of a gas is proportional to its mole fraction in the mixture: pi = xi · ptotal. It is a measure of the thermodynamic activity of the gas's molecules and determines, for example, the rate at which gases dissolve in liquids (Henry's law).

Applications

Partial pressures are fundamental in respiratory physiology: oxygen and carbon dioxide move between air and blood by diffusion down partial pressure gradients, and arterial blood gas analysis is reported in partial pressures (PaO2, PaCO2). In diving, the partial pressure of oxygen determines the risk of oxygen toxicity, which is why deep divers use helium-diluted mixtures; the partial pressure of nitrogen governs narcosis and decompression obligations. Chemists use partial pressures to express the equilibrium constant Kp for gas-phase reactions.

Dalton's law

John Dalton observed in 1801 that the total pressure of a mixture of non-reacting gases equals the sum of the pressures each would exert if it occupied the container alone. The law is exact for ideal gases and a good approximation for real gases at ordinary conditions.
