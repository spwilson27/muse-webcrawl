Baihua

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Baihua (traditional Chinese: 白話; simplified Chinese: 白话; pinyin: báihuà; lit. 'plain speech') is a written form of Chinese based on the spoken vernacular, as opposed to Classical Chinese (wenyan), the formal written language used from the era of Confucius until the early 20th century. Written vernacular Chinese is also called Written Vernacular Chinese and is the basis of modern Standard Written Chinese.

History

Although Classical Chinese remained the prestige written language for two millennia, texts written close to the spoken language appeared from the Tang and Song dynasties onward — in Buddhist sermons, Chan (Zen) records, and later in the great Ming and Qing vernacular novels such as Water Margin, Journey to the West and Dream of the Red Chamber.

The decisive shift came with the literary revolution of the New Culture Movement. In 1917 the scholar Hu Shih published proposals for literary reform arguing that literature should be written in the living vernacular; Lu Xun's "Diary of a Madman" (1918) became the first major modern short story in baihua. In 1920 the Ministry of Education of the Republic of China decreed that school textbooks be written in the vernacular, and within a generation baihua had displaced Classical Chinese as the standard written medium.

Features

Baihua is based primarily on the grammar and vocabulary of northern Mandarin dialects. Unlike Classical Chinese, whose words are mostly monosyllabic, baihua freely uses disyllabic compounds, grammatical particles such as 的, 了 and 嗎, and explicit pronouns and conjunctions, making texts longer but far more accessible to ordinary readers. Modern written Chinese has since absorbed vocabulary and some structures from Classical Chinese, Western languages and Japanese.
