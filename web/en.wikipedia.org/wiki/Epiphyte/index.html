Epiphyte

An epiphyte is a plant or plant-like organism that grows on the surface of another plant and derives its moisture and nutrients from the air, rain, water (in marine environments) or from debris accumulating around it. The plants on which epiphytes grow are called phorophytes. Epiphytes take part in nutrient cycles and add to both the diversity and biomass of the ecosystem in which they occur, like any other organism. In addition, they are an important source of food for many species.

Diversity and ecology

There are an estimated 89,000 species of vascular epiphytes, most diverse in tropical moist forests, especially cloud forests, where they may carpet branches. The largest families are the orchids (Orchidaceae), bromeliads (Bromeliaceae) and ferns; well-known examples include Spanish moss, staghorn ferns, and many aroids. Non-vascular epiphytes — mosses, liverworts, lichens and algae — are abundant on trees in both tropical and temperate rainforests.

Adaptations

Because they lack contact with soil, epiphytes depend on specialised adaptations: bromeliads form water-holding tanks with their leaf rosettes; many orchids have velamen-covered aerial roots that absorb moisture rapidly; others use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) to limit water loss. Unlike parasitic plants, epiphytes make no physical extraction from their host's vascular system, though a heavy epiphyte load can shade or weigh down host branches.
