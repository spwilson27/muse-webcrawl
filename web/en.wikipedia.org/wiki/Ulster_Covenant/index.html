Ulster Covenant

The Ulster Covenant, also known as the Solemn League and Covenant, was a declaration signed on 28 September 1912 by nearly half a million men and women from Ulster, in protest against the Third Home Rule Bill introduced by the British government the same year. It pledged its signatories to resist the introduction of Home Rule for Ireland by any means necessary.

Background

By 1912 the question of Irish Home Rule dominated British politics. H. H. Asquith's Liberal government, dependent on the votes of the Irish Parliamentary Party, introduced a bill to establish a devolved parliament in Dublin. Ulster unionists, led by Edward Carson and James Craig, feared that an all-Ireland parliament would be dominated by Irish nationalists and Roman Catholics, and that Ulster's industrial economy would suffer from being severed from the rest of the United Kingdom.

Ulster Day

The Covenant was launched on "Ulster Day", Saturday 28 September 1912, after a series of rallies across the province. Carson signed first at Belfast City Hall, using a silver pen, on a table draped with the Union Flag. In total, 237,368 men signed the Covenant and 234,046 women signed a parallel declaration, giving a combined total of 471,414 signatures. Some signatories are said to have signed in their own blood, in imitation of the Scottish Covenanters of the seventeenth century.

Text

The Covenant was modelled on the Scottish Solemn League and Covenant of 1643. Its signatories declared themselves "convinced in our consciences that Home Rule would be disastrous to the material well-being of Ulster as well as of the whole of Ireland, subversive of our civil and religious freedom, destructive of our citizenship, and perilous to the unity of the Empire", and pledged to "stand by one another in defending... our cherished position of equal citizenship in the United Kingdom, and in using all means which may be found necessary to defeat the present conspiracy to set up a Home Rule Parliament in Ireland".

Aftermath

The Covenant was followed in January 1913 by the formation of the Ulster Volunteer Force, a militia sworn to resist Home Rule by force if necessary. The Larne gun-running of April 1914 armed the force. Although the Home Rule Act was passed in September 1914, its implementation was suspended for the duration of the First World War, and it never came into effect. The eventual settlement, the Government of Ireland Act 1920, partitioned Ireland, with the six north-eastern counties forming Northern Ireland.
