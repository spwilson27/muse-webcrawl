A kite buggy is a light, purpose-built vehicle powered by a traction kite (power kite). It is usually single-seated and has one steerable front wheel and two fixed rear wheels. The driver sits in the seat located in the middle of the vehicle and accelerates and slows down by applying steering manoeuvres in coordination with flying manoeuvres of the kite. Kite buggying is practised on beaches, fields, and dry lake beds.

History

Although wind-powered land vehicles date back centuries — including the Chinese wind-wheel carriages and George Pocock's "Charvolant" of 1826 — the modern kite buggy was developed in the late 1970s. Peter Lynn of New Zealand is generally credited with popularising the sport in its modern form, building three-wheeled buggies pulled by large controllable kites. The sport spread rapidly in the 1990s with the development of efficient four-line traction kites.

Equipment and technique

The buggy itself is typically built from stainless steel or aluminium tubing, with a wide rear axle and low centre of gravity. The pilot wears a harness connected to the kite's lines through a pulley system, transferring the kite's pull to the buggy. Steering is done with the feet on the front wheel's foot pegs. Speeds of over 100 km/h (62 mph) have been recorded; the recognised land speed record for a kite buggy exceeded 130 km/h.

Racing and safety

Kite buggy racing is organised under bodies such as the International Kite Buggy Association and national federations. Races are held on marked courses on beaches and airfields. Safety equipment includes helmets, and buggies are often fitted with side rails and mudguards. Because the sport depends on wide open spaces with smooth wind, venues such as Camber Sands in England, dry lakes in Nevada, and beaches in Germany and the Netherlands are popular.
