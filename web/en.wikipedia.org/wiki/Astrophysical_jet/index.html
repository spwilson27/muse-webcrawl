Astrophysical jet

The jet of M87, first observed in 1918, extends thousands of light-years.
Astrophysical jet
NatureCollimated outflow of ionised matter along an accretion disk's rotation axis
SourcesActive galactic nuclei, quasars, microquasars, gamma-ray bursts, young stars, pulsars
SpeedUp to relativistic (a large fraction of light speed)
MechanismsBlandford–Znajek and Blandford–Payne processes (magnetohydrodynamic)

An astrophysical jet is an astronomical phenomenon in which outflows of ionised matter are emitted as extended beams along the axis of rotation of a compact object accreting material from a disk. Jets occur across an enormous range of scales, from protostars a few light-years long to the megaparsec-scale jets of giant radio galaxies.

Formation

Jets are thought to be launched and collimated by twisted magnetic fields anchored in the accretion disk and, for black holes, possibly in the hole's ergosphere. The Blandford–Znajek mechanism extracts rotational energy from a spinning black hole electromagnetically; the Blandford–Payne mechanism flings disk material outward along inclined field lines. Jets are typically bipolar, though one side often appears brighter because of relativistic beaming (Doppler boosting) toward the observer.

Observations

The jet of the elliptical galaxy M87 was noted by Heber Curtis in 1918 as a "curious straight ray." Radio galaxies display twin lobes where jets terminate in intergalactic space; blazars are active nuclei whose jets point almost directly at Earth. Gamma-ray bursts produce the most extreme, short-lived relativistic jets. Superluminal motion — apparent faster-than-light expansion — is a projection effect of near-light-speed jets aimed near the line of sight.
