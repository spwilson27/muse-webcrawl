The Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) is a mainline Protestant Christian denomination in North America. It grew out of the Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement of the early 19th century, which sought to restore Christian unity and apostolic practice on the basis of the New Testament alone.

History

The movement originated with Barton W. Stone's "Christian" movement in Kentucky and Thomas and Alexander Campbell's "Disciples of Christ" in Pennsylvania; the two joined in 1832. Over the following century its churches divided: the more conservative Churches of Christ were separately listed in the 1906 US census, and further conservative "independent" congregations departed in the mid-20th century. The remaining movement adopted a denominational structure as the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ) in 1968.

Beliefs and practices

Disciples practice believer's baptism by immersion and celebrate the Lord's Supper weekly, open to all Christians. Congregations are autonomous; there is no creed — the traditional formula is "No creed but Christ, no book but the Bible." The chalice is the denomination's symbol. The church participates in the ecumenical movement and ordains women.
