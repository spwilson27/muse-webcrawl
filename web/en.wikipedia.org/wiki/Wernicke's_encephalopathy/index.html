Wernicke's encephalopathy

Wernicke's encephalopathy is an acute neurological disorder caused by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It was described by Carl Wernicke in 1881. It most commonly occurs in people with alcohol use disorder and malnutrition, but also follows prolonged vomiting (including hyperemesis gravidarum), bariatric surgery, starvation, and other causes of thiamine depletion.

Signs and symptoms

The classic triad is confusion (encephalopathy), ophthalmoplegia (eye movement abnormalities such as nystagmus and lateral rectus palsy) and gait ataxia. In practice the full triad is present in only a minority of patients, which contributes to frequent under-diagnosis. Autopsy studies show the characteristic lesions — symmetrical damage around the third and fourth ventricles and aqueduct — in far more cases than are diagnosed in life. Untreated, the condition may progress to Korsakoff syndrome, coma or death.

Treatment

Wernicke's encephalopathy is a medical emergency treated with high-dose parenteral thiamine, given before intravenous glucose (which can precipitate or worsen the condition by consuming remaining thiamine). With prompt treatment, eye signs improve within hours to days, while confusion and ataxia recover more slowly and may be incomplete. Because the diagnosis is clinical and treatment is safe and cheap, guidelines recommend empirical thiamine for at-risk patients with compatible signs.
