History of the Jews in the Czech lands

The history of the Jews in the Czech lands — Bohemia, Moravia, and Czech Silesia — dates back more than a thousand years. Jewish merchants and settlers are documented in Prague by the tenth century, and Prague became one of the most important Jewish centres in Central Europe.

Middle Ages

Jewish communities in the Czech lands experienced alternating periods of royal protection and persecution. Crusaders attacked Prague's Jews in 1096, and the community suffered during the Black Death persecutions of 1348–1350. The Prague ghetto, later known as Josefov, developed as a walled quarter. In 1389 a pogrom at Easter killed thousands of Prague Jews.

Renaissance and the Maharal

The late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries were a golden age. Under Emperor Rudolf II, Prague's Jewish community flourished; Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal, became associated with the legend of the Golem. Financier Jacob Bassevi received a noble title, and Mordecai Meisel funded synagogues and public buildings.

Habsburg era

The Familiants Laws of 1726–1727 capped Jewish marriages in Bohemia and Moravia, prompting emigration. The reforms of Joseph II in the 1780s relaxed some restrictions, and full emancipation came in 1867 within Austria-Hungary. Czech Jews contributed prominently to culture; writers such as Franz Kafka wrote in German, while others embraced Czech national culture or Zionism.

Twentieth century

In interwar Czechoslovakia, Jews enjoyed full civic equality; the 1930 census counted about 356,000 Jews in the republic, including some 118,000 in the Czech lands. After the Munich Agreement (1938) and the German occupation of March 1939, anti-Jewish measures escalated. Most Czech Jews were deported, many through the Theresienstadt (Terezín) ghetto, to extermination camps; roughly 78,000 Jews from Bohemia and Moravia were murdered in the Holocaust. After the war, survivors faced renewed emigration pressures under communist rule, and the Slánský trial of 1952 had antisemitic overtones. Since 1989 the small community, centred on Prague's Jewish Museum and synagogues, has revived communal life.
