Diminished fifth

In music theory, a diminished fifth is an interval produced by narrowing a perfect fifth by a chromatic semitone. In twelve-tone equal temperament it spans six semitones and is enharmonically equivalent to the augmented fourth; together they make up the tritone.

Characteristics

For example, the interval from C up to G♭ is a diminished fifth, while the inverse interval, G♭ up to C, is an augmented fourth. The diminished fifth is a dissonant interval and a defining component of the diminished triad and of dominant seventh chords, in which the interval between the third and seventh drives resolution to the tonic. In just intonation several different ratios approximate it, such as 64/45 and 36/25, none as simple as the perfect fifth's 3/2.

History

The tritone was historically nicknamed diabolus in musica ("the devil in music") for its instability, though claims that it was banned in medieval church music are largely mythical. It became a deliberate expressive device from the Baroque era onward and is fundamental to jazz harmony through tritone substitution.
