Corvée

Corvée
DefinitionUnpaid, forced labour owed to a lord, state or public authority
FromFrench corvée, from Latin corrogata (opera), "requisitioned work"
RelatedSerfdom, statute labour, mit'a

Corvée (French: [kɔʁve]) is a form of unpaid, forced labour that is intermittent in nature and lasts for limited periods: typically only a certain number of days each year. Statute labour is a corvée imposed by a state for the purposes of public works. As such, corvée differs from chattel slavery in that the worker is not owned outright, and from taxation in that it is paid in labour rather than in money or kind.

History

Corvée systems existed in ancient Egypt, China, and the Inca world (the mit'a). In feudal Europe, peasants owed labour services on the lord's demesne in addition to rents. In France, the royal corvée for road building was greatly expanded under Louis XV and became one of the most resented burdens of the Ancien Régime; finance minister Turgot's attempt to abolish it in 1776 contributed to his dismissal. The French corvée was finally abolished during the Revolution in 1789.

Later examples

Corvée labour was used to build railways and infrastructure in colonial Africa and Asia, and persisted in parts of the world into the 20th century. In British North America and the early United States, "statute labour" required residents to work a set number of days on public roads. Modern international law largely prohibits forced labour, with narrow exceptions such as military service and emergency work under ILO conventions.
