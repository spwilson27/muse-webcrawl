Nerve growth factor

Nerve growth factor (NGF) is a neurotrophic factor and neuropeptide primarily involved in the regulation of growth, maintenance, proliferation, and survival of certain target neurons. It is perhaps the prototypical growth factor, in that it was one of the first to be described. Since it was first isolated by Nobel laureates Rita Levi-Montalcini and Stanley Cohen in 1956, numerous biological processes involving NGF have been identified, two of them being the survival of pancreatic beta cells and the regulation of the immune system.

Structure of the NGF protein.

Structure

NGF is initially synthesized in a precursor form, proNGF, which is cleaved to yield the mature protein. The biologically active form is a noncovalently linked homodimer of two identical 118-amino-acid chains, belonging to the cystine knot superfamily of growth factors. NGF is a member of the neurotrophin family, which also includes brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), neurotrophin-3, and neurotrophin-4.

Function

NGF binds with at least two classes of receptors: the low-affinity nerve growth factor receptor (LNGFR/p75NTR) and the high-affinity receptor TrkA, a transmembrane tyrosine kinase. Through TrkA signaling, NGF promotes the survival and differentiation of sympathetic and sensory neurons during development and maintains their phenotype in adulthood. NGF is also a mediator of pain: it is released by mast cells and other cells at sites of injury and inflammation, sensitizing nociceptors.

History

Rita Levi-Montalcini and Viktor Hamburger discovered NGF in the 1950s through experiments showing that mouse sarcoma tissue implanted into chick embryos caused explosive growth of sensory and sympathetic nerve fibers. Stanley Cohen purified the factor, found it in snake venom and mouse submaxillary glands, and identified it as a protein. Levi-Montalcini and Cohen shared the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.

Clinical significance

Because of its role in pain signaling, monoclonal antibodies that block NGF, such as tanezumab, have been investigated as analgesics for osteoarthritis and chronic low back pain. Mutations in the NGF gene or its receptor TrkA cause congenital insensitivity to pain with anhidrosis (CIPA). NGF has also been studied for possible therapeutic roles in neurodegenerative disease and corneal healing; recombinant human NGF (cenegermin) is approved for neurotrophic keratitis.
