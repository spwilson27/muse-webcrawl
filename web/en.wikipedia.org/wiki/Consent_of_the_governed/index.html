Consent of the governed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Consent of the governed is a phrase from democratic political philosophy denoting that a government's legitimacy and moral right to use state power is justified only when derived from the authority of the people it governs. The phrase is canonical in the United States Declaration of Independence (1776): governments are instituted among men "deriving their just powers from the consent of the governed".

Theory

Social-contract theorists grounded consent differently: Locke in individuals' express or tacit consent (tacit consent — enjoying the protection of the laws — being the theory's contested workhorse), Rousseau in the general will, and modern writers in fair democratic procedures, hypothetical consent or acceptance under conditions of freedom and equality. Critics from Hume onward objected that actual consent is rarely given and cannot bind dissenters or descendants; anarchist and sceptical traditions take the objection further. In practice, the phrase functions less as a literal mechanism than as the legitimating norm of modern politics: elections, constitutional ratification and revolution are its rituals and its rupture, and every regime since 1776 — including authoritarian ones with managed plebiscites — finds it necessary to claim consent.
