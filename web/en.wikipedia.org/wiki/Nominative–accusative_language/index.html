Nominative–accusative language

A nominative–accusative language is one in which the subject of an intransitive verb is treated grammatically in the same way as the agent of a transitive verb, while the object of a transitive verb is treated differently. In such languages the shared subject/agent case is the nominative and the distinct object case is the accusative.

Alignment typology

Linguists describe morphological alignment using three core arguments: S (intransitive subject), A (transitive agent) and P (transitive patient/object). Nominative–accusative languages group S and A together against P. This contrasts with ergative–absolutive languages, which group S with P, and with tripartite and active–stative systems.

Examples

Most Indo-European languages with case marking are nominative–accusative, including Latin, Ancient Greek, German, Russian and Sanskrit, as are the Uralic and Turkic languages and Japanese and Korean (using nominative and accusative particles). English retains the pattern in its pronouns: "he" serves for both S and A, versus accusative "him" for P.
