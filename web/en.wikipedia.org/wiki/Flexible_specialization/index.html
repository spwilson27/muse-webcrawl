Flexible specialization

Flexible specialization is a model of industrial organization based on networks of small, technologically sophisticated firms that use general-purpose equipment and skilled workers to produce varied, customized goods in shifting varieties. The concept was developed by Michael J. Piore and Charles F. Sabel in their book The Second Industrial Divide (1984), where they presented it as a historically grounded alternative to mass production.

The argument

Piore and Sabel argued that economic history offered two paths of technological development: mass production, based on dedicated machinery, semi-skilled labor, and standardized products for large markets; and craft or flexible specialization, based on skilled workers using flexible equipment to make a wide and changing range of goods. Mass production triumphed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries not because it was inherently superior but because of particular institutional and political conditions. The crises of the 1970s — volatile markets, oil shocks, and saturated demand for standardized goods — reopened the possibility of the craft path.

Industrial districts

The empirical model was the industrial districts of the "Third Italy," such as the textile firms of Prato, the footwear producers of the Marche, and the machine-tool and ceramics districts of Emilia-Romagna, where dense networks of small firms cooperated and competed simultaneously, sharing services, information, and skilled labor. Cooperation was underpinned by local institutions — municipal governments, trade associations, families, and political parties — that regulated competition and prevented destructive undercutting on wages.

Influence and criticism

Flexible specialization influenced research on industrial districts, regional development, post-Fordism, and later concepts such as mass customization and lean production. Critics argued that the model romanticized small firms, that many districts relied on low wages and precarious work, and that mass production proved more durable and adaptable (through flexible automation) than the theory predicted. Others questioned whether the apparent success regions could be replicated elsewhere by policy.
