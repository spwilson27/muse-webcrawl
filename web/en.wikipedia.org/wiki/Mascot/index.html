Mascot

A costumed mascot entertaining a crowd at a sporting event.

A mascot is any human, animal, or object thought to bring luck, or anything used to represent a group with a common public identity, such as a school, sports team, society, military unit, or brand name. Mascots are also used as fictional, representative spokescharacters for consumer products.

In the world of sports, mascots are also used for merchandising. Team mascots are often related to their respective team nicknames, especially in American professional sports. They are usually anthropomorphized versions of animals or objects, chosen to symbolize traits desired by the team, such as strength, ferocity, or speed. Mascots have also been used to promote brands, schools, corporations, and other organizations since the late 19th century.

Etymology and origins

The word mascot originates from the French term mascotte, meaning lucky charm, and is derived from the Provençal word mascòto, meaning "spell" or "charm," and ultimately from the medieval Latin masca, meaning "witch" or "specter." The term gained popularity following the 1880 premiere of Edmond Audran's operetta La mascotte, which was an immediate success and introduced the word into the English language.

Sports mascots

Mascots are a staple of organized sports, from youth leagues to the professional level. The first mascots were live animals: in the early 20th century, universities and baseball clubs kept live bears, goats, dogs, and other creatures on the sidelines as good-luck charms. Costumed mascots became widespread in the 1970s and 1980s, aided by the influence of Jim Henson's Muppet-style character design.

Notable examples

Famous sports mascots include the Phillie Phanatic of the Philadelphia Phillies, widely regarded as one of the best ballpark mascots; the San Diego Chicken (originally the KGB Chicken), who became a sensation in the 1970s; and Youppi!, who moved from the Montreal Expos to the Montreal Canadiens. In collegiate athletics, live mascots remain popular, such as Uga, the bulldog of the University of Georgia, and Ralphie the Buffalo of the University of Colorado.

Olympic mascots

Since the 1968 Winter Olympics in Grenoble introduced Schuss, an abstract skier figure, every Olympic Games has had an official mascot. Olympic mascots are usually animals native to the host region or figures drawn from local culture, and they play a major role in merchandising and promotion. Waldi, the dachshund mascot of the 1972 Munich Games, was the first officially named Olympic mascot; Misha, the bear of the 1980 Moscow Games, became an enduring cultural icon.

Military mascots

Military units have long kept mascots, both official and unofficial, including animals such as goats, dogs, and ponies. The Welsh Guards keep a goat, and the Norwegian Royal Guard has a king penguin, Nils Olav, who holds the rank of colonel-in-chief and was knighted in 2008. Mascots are believed to raise morale and serve as symbols of unit identity.

Commercial mascots

Companies use mascots as brand ambassadors in advertising. Well-known examples include the Michelin Man (Bibendum), introduced in 1898; Tony the Tiger for Kellogg's Frosted Flakes; Ronald McDonald; and the GEICO gecko. In Japan, local governments and companies employ cute character mascots known as yuru-chara, such as Kumamon of Kumamoto Prefecture.
