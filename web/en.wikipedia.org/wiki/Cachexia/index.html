Cachexia

SpecialtyOncology, internal medicine
SymptomsWeight loss, muscle atrophy, fatigue, weakness, loss of appetite
CausesCancer, AIDS, COPD, heart failure, chronic kidney disease
ICD-10R64

Cachexia is a complex metabolic syndrome associated with underlying illness, characterised by loss of muscle mass with or without loss of fat mass. Its defining feature is weight loss in adults (or growth failure in children) that cannot be reversed simply by increased caloric intake, distinguishing it from ordinary starvation, in which fat is lost preferentially and lean tissue is relatively spared.

Cachexia is frequently seen in patients with cancer, AIDS, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), multiple sclerosis, chronic heart failure, tuberculosis, and chronic kidney disease. Roughly half of all cancer patients experience cachexia, and it is estimated to contribute directly to a substantial fraction of cancer deaths.

Signs and symptoms

The most prominent features are involuntary weight loss, muscle wasting (sarcopenia), fatigue, weakness, and significant loss of appetite (anorexia). Patients may also develop anaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, and a systemic inflammatory response.

Diagnostic criteria

An international consensus definition published in 2011 (Fearon and colleagues) diagnoses cachexia when a patient shows weight loss greater than 5% over the previous six months (in the absence of simple starvation), or a body mass index below 20 kg/m² with weight loss greater than 2%, or appendicular skeletal muscle loss consistent with sarcopenia together with weight loss greater than 2%. The syndrome is staged as precachexia, cachexia, and refractory cachexia, the last being unresponsive to treatment and associated with a short life expectancy.

Mechanism

Cachexia results from a combination of reduced food intake and abnormal metabolism driven by the host's inflammatory response to the underlying disease. Tumour- and host-derived cytokines — including tumour necrosis factor alpha (once called "cachectin"), interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and interferon gamma — promote proteolysis in skeletal muscle via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway, stimulate lipolysis, and act on the hypothalamus to suppress appetite. Tumour-derived factors such as proteolysis-inducing factor (PIF) and lipid-mobilising factor have also been described. Unlike starvation, the metabolic response is not switched off by feeding.

Treatment

The primary treatment is management of the underlying disease. Nutritional support alone does not reverse cachexia, though it may help in combination with other measures. Appetite stimulants such as megestrol acetate and corticosteroids produce modest short-term gains, mainly in fat and water rather than muscle. Anamorelin, a ghrelin receptor agonist, increases body weight and lean body mass and is approved for cancer cachexia in Japan. Exercise, anti-inflammatory agents, and multimodal therapy are under investigation.

Etymology

The word derives from the Greek κακός (kakos, "bad") and ἕξις (hexis, "condition") — literally, a "bad condition" of the body.
