Rite of passage

A rite of passage is a ceremony or ritual marking an individual's transition from one social status to another, typically at important stages of life such as birth, puberty, adulthood, marriage, and death. The concept was popularized by the French ethnographer Arnold van Gennep in his 1909 work Les rites de passage, which analysed such ceremonies across cultures.

Van Gennep identified a common three-part structure: separation (preliminal rites), in which the individual is detached from their previous status; transition (liminal rites), an in-between phase in which the initiate has left the old status but not yet entered the new one; and incorporation (postliminal rites), in which the individual is received into society with their new identity. The middle, liminal phase was later developed extensively by anthropologist Victor Turner, who emphasized the experience of communitas among initiates.

Examples

Rites of passage occur in virtually all societies. Examples include coming-of-age ceremonies such as the Jewish bar and bat mitzvah, Christian confirmation, the Japanese seijinshiki, quinceañera celebrations in Latin America, and Aboriginal walkabout traditions; initiation into religious or military orders; graduation ceremonies; weddings; and funerals. In many traditional societies, male and female initiation involves seclusion, instruction by elders, ordeals, scarification or circumcision, and the revelation of sacred knowledge.

Types

Van Gennep distinguished rites accompanying pregnancy and childbirth, birth and childhood, initiation (usually at puberty), betrothal and marriage, and funerals, as well as territorial passage—crossing boundaries or entering new spaces—all sharing the underlying tripartite pattern. Later scholarship, including Turner's work on liminality, extended the framework to pilgrimage, carnival, and modern phenomena such as military basic training and fraternity hazing.
