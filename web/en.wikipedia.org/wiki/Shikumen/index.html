Shikumen (Chinese: 石庫門; pinyin: shíkùmén, "stone[-framed] warehouse gate") is a traditional Shanghainese architectural style combining Western and Chinese elements, first appearing in the 1860s and 1870s. Shikumen residences are two- or three-storey terraced houses of brick and timber, each fronted by a high brick wall and entered through a distinctive stone gate frame, from which the style takes its name. The houses are arranged along straight lanes called lilong (里弄), and at their peak in the early 20th century they made up the great majority of Shanghai's housing.

Origins and development

The style emerged in the foreign concessions of treaty-port Shanghai, where developers combined the British terraced house with the courtyard plan of the traditional Jiangnan dwelling, adapting housing to expensive, densely settled land. Early shikumen kept a clear central axis and courtyard; later generations became smaller and more ornate, their stone gate frames carved with Chinese motifs, European floral scrolls, or Art Deco geometry. From the 1910s to the 1930s, "new-style" lilong houses with modern sanitation and wider lanes were built for the growing middle class.

Layout

A typical shikumen house is entered through the stone gate into a small front courtyard, then a reception room, with a kitchen and back court behind and bedrooms above. A tiny, dark mezzanine room over the kitchen — the tingzijian or "pavilion room" — became famous as the cheap lodging of impoverished writers and artists in the 1930s, giving its name to a whole school of Shanghai literature.

Decline and preservation

After 1949 most shikumen houses were subdivided among several families, with kitchens and bathrooms shared. From the 1990s, rapid redevelopment demolished most surviving neighbourhoods; it is often estimated that a large share of the old stock has been lost. The redevelopment of Xintiandi as an upmarket leisure district, preserving shikumen exteriors around a museum house where the First Congress of the Chinese Communist Party met in 1921, sparked debate over the preservation of the remaining examples, some of which are now protected as heritage.
