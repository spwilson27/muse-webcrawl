Tullus Hostilius (r. 673–642 BC) was the legendary third king of Rome. He succeeded Numa Pompilius and was in turn succeeded by Ancus Marcius. Unlike his peaceable predecessor, Tullus was a warlike king who, in the account of the Roman historian Livy, doubled the population of Rome and defeated the Albans and Sabines.

War with Alba Longa

The centrepiece of the traditional account is Rome's war with Alba Longa, settled — in Livy's famous story — by single combat between two sets of triplets: the Roman Horatii and the Alban Curiatii. After Rome's victory, Alba's dictator Mettius Fufetius agreed to an alliance but betrayed Rome in a subsequent war against Fidenae and Veii; Tullus had him executed by being torn apart by two chariots driving in opposite directions, and Alba Longa was destroyed, its population transferred to Rome and settled on the Caelian Hill.

Death

According to legend, Tullus neglected the worship of the gods for much of his reign and, during a plague, attempted a secret and improperly performed rite to Jupiter Elicius; he and his household were struck by lightning and burned to ashes — a divine punishment that the tradition contrasts with the piety of Numa. He is credited with building the Curia Hostilia, the first senate house of Rome.

Historicity

Like all of Rome's early kings, Tullus is generally regarded as a legendary figure, though the tradition may preserve memories of Rome's early expansion and the destruction of Alba Longa in the 7th century BC.
