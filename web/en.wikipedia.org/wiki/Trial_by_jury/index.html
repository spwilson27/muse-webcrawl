Trial by jury

A trial by jury, or jury trial, is a legal proceeding in which a jury makes a decision or findings of fact rather than a judge alone. It is a distinctive feature of the common law system, used in countries including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada and Australia. The jury — ordinarily a group of citizens drawn from the community — hears the evidence, deliberates, and returns a verdict, while the judge supervises the law and procedure.

History

The jury has roots in the Frankish practice of royal inquiry and in Norman customs brought to England after 1066. Henry II's reforms in the twelfth century made juries central to English justice, and the Assize of Clarendon (1166) set out panels of twelve men to report crimes. Trial by jury replaced trial by ordeal after the Church forbade clerical participation in ordeals in 1215. The 1670 case of Bushel's Case established that jurors could not be punished for their verdicts, securing jury independence.

Procedure

Prospective jurors are selected during voir dire, in which attorneys question candidates and may strike some for cause or by peremptory challenge. Criminal juries in many jurisdictions must return unanimous verdicts; civil juries may sometimes decide by majority. Historically the standard size is twelve jurors, though smaller juries are permitted in some courts and for minor offences.

Constitutional status

In the United States, the right to a jury trial is guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment for criminal prosecutions, by the Seventh Amendment for many federal civil cases, and by most state constitutions. English law has restricted jury trials over time: most civil cases are now heard by a judge alone, and jury trial is largely confined to Crown Court criminal cases, certain fraud trials and some defamation and coroner's proceedings.

Criticism

Critics argue juries can be biased, swayed by rhetoric, or unfit to evaluate complex evidence; supporters regard the jury as a democratic check on state power and a means of importing community values into the law.
