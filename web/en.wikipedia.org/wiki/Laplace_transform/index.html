In mathematics, the Laplace transform, named after Pierre-Simon Laplace, is an integral transform that converts a function of a real variable (usually time, t) into a function of a complex variable s (the complex frequency). It is widely used in physics and engineering because it converts many types of differential equations into algebraic equations, and convolution into multiplication.

Definition

The Laplace transform of a function f(t), defined for all real numbers t ≥ 0, is the function F(s) defined by

F(s) = ∫0∞ f(t) e−st dt,

where s is a complex number. The integral converges for s with real part greater than some abscissa of convergence. The inverse Laplace transform recovers f(t) from F(s) via the Bromwich integral.

Properties

The transform is linear. It converts differentiation into multiplication by powers of s: the transform of f′(t) is sF(s) − f(0), incorporating initial conditions automatically. Other key properties include time shifting, frequency shifting, and the convolution theorem: the transform of a convolution is the product of the transforms.

Familiar transform pairs include 1 ↔ 1/s, eat ↔ 1/(s−a), sin(ωt) ↔ ω/(s²+ω²), and the unit step and Dirac delta.

Applications

The Laplace transform is a standard tool for solving linear ordinary differential equations with constant coefficients, analysing electrical circuits and mechanical systems, deriving transfer functions in control theory, and studying moment-generating functions in probability (where the transform of a density evaluated at −s gives the moment-generating function).

History

Laplace used the transform in his work on probability theory in the late 18th century. The operational calculus developed by Oliver Heaviside in the 1890s applied similar methods to electrical engineering, and the modern rigorous treatment was established in the early 20th century.
