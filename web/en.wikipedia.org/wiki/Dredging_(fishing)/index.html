Dredging (fishing)

For the excavation of waterways, see Dredging.

In fishing, dredging is the harvesting of shellfish — principally scallops, oysters, clams and mussels — by towing a dredge along the seabed. A fishing dredge is typically a heavy steel frame fitted with a chain-mesh or net collecting bag, often with a toothed bar that digs into the sediment to dislodge the catch.

Methods

Scallop dredges, such as the New Bedford-style dredge used off New England and the spring-loaded dredges used in the Isle of Man and Scottish fisheries, are towed in rows behind the vessel. Oyster dredges are worked in estuaries and bays. Because dredges contact the bottom, the method is a form of bottom fishing related to, but distinct from, beam and otter trawling.

Environmental impact

Scallop dredging is among the most physically disruptive fishing methods: the toothed bar ploughs the seabed, damaging slow-growing habitats such as maerl beds and seagrass. Many fisheries therefore regulate dredge width, tooth spacing and seasonal closures, and some areas are closed to dredging entirely to protect habitat.
