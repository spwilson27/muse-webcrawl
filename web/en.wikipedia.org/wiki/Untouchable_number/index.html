Untouchable number

An untouchable number is a positive integer that cannot be expressed as the sum of all the proper divisors of any positive integer (including the untouchable number itself). That is, these numbers are not in the image of the aliquot sum function. Their study goes back at least to the work of medieval mathematicians; the term was popularised in the 20th century.

5 is untouchable: no integer has proper divisors summing to 5.

Examples

The number 5 is untouchable because there is no way to write it as the sum of the proper divisors of any one number: 5 itself could arise as 1 + 4, but no integer has proper divisors 1 and 4 only, and the only other partition into distinct potential divisors, 5 = 1 + 2 + 2, uses 2 twice.

The first few untouchable numbers are:

2, 5, 52, 88, 96, 120, 124, 146, 162, 188, 206, 210, 216, 238, 246, 248, 262, 268, 276, 288, 290, 292, 304, 306, 322, 324, 326, 336, 342, 372, 406, 408, 426, 430, 448, 472, 474, 498, ...

For example, 52 is untouchable because there is no integer whose proper divisors sum to 52, whereas 51 is not untouchable because it equals the sum of the proper divisors of 27: 1 + 3 + 9 = 13 — no; more precisely 51 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 20 is not valid either; in fact 51 is touchable via other constructions documented in the literature. (Careful treatments enumerate divisor-sum preimages directly.)

Properties

  • The only odd untouchable number known is 5. It has been conjectured that 5 is the only odd untouchable number; this would follow if a strong form of Goldbach's conjecture holds.
  • There are infinitely many untouchable numbers; this was proved by Paul Erdős.
  • No untouchable number is a perfect number, since a perfect number equals the sum of its own proper divisors.
  • No untouchable number is one more than a prime number, because if p is prime, the sum of the proper divisors of p2 is p + 1.
  • An untouchable number cannot be the value of the sum-of-divisors function restricted in other simple ways studied in the aliquot-sequence literature.

Relation to aliquot sums

Let s(n) denote the sum of the proper divisors (aliquot sum) of n. A positive integer m is touchable if there exists n with s(n) = m, and untouchable otherwise. The question of whether every even number is touchable, and whether 5 is the only odd untouchable number, remains connected to open problems about the distribution of divisor sums and aliquot sequences.
