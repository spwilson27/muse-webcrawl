Cristero War

Cristero rebels fought the Mexican government from 1926 to 1929

The Cristero War (1926–1929), also known as the Cristiada, was a widespread popular uprising in central and western Mexico against the anti-clerical provisions of the Mexican Constitution of 1917 and their enforcement under President Plutarco Elías Calles. Catholic rebels, who rallied to the cries of "¡Viva Cristo Rey!" ("Long live Christ the King!") and "¡Viva la Virgen de Guadalupe!", took the name Cristeros.

Background

The Constitution of 1917 restricted the Catholic Church: it barred religious orders, limited the number of priests, prohibited public worship outside churches, and nationalised church property. When Calles strictly enforced these articles through the "Calles Law" of 1926, the Mexican bishops suspended public worship, and lay Catholics organised first economic boycotts and then armed resistance, coordinated in part by the National League for the Defense of Religious Liberty.

Course of the war

Fighting centred on the Bajío and Los Altos regions. The Cristeros, largely peasants, developed an effective guerrilla army that by 1928 numbered tens of thousands, commanded from 1928 by the hired professional general Enrique Gorostieta. The federal army responded with harsh counterinsurgency, including executions, concentration of populations, and destruction of villages. Priests such as Miguel Pro, executed in 1927 and photographed before a firing squad, became celebrated martyrs. Women supporters, including the "Feminine Brigades of St. Joan of Arc", smuggled ammunition and supplies.

Settlement and aftermath

In June 1929, with mediation by US ambassador Dwight Morrow, the government and the Church reached an arrangement (arreglos): public worship resumed and amnesty was offered, though the constitutional restrictions remained on paper. Many former Cristeros who laid down their arms were subsequently killed. Tensions persisted through the 1930s, with a smaller "Segunda" uprising, and the Church–state conflict eased only gradually; formal restrictions were substantially reformed in 1992. Numerous Cristero martyrs were later canonised by the Catholic Church, including Cristóbal Magallanes and companions in 2000.
