Man with a Movie Camera (Russian: Человек с кино-аппаратом, romanized: Chelovek s kino-apparatom) is a 1929 Soviet silent documentary film directed by Dziga Vertov and filmed by his brother Mikhail Kaufman. It presents urban life in the Soviet cities of Kyiv, Kharkiv, Moscow and Odesa over the course of a single day, from dawn to dusk.

The film is famous for its range of cinematic techniques — double exposure, fast motion, slow motion, freeze frames, split screens, Dutch angles, extreme close-ups, tracking shots and stop-motion animation — which Vertov used to advance his "Kino-Eye" theory: that the camera, freed from human limitations, could capture reality more truthfully than the naked eye. It has no actors, no sets, no script and no intertitles.

Structure and themes

The film follows a cameraman (Kaufman himself) as he films the city waking, working, and at leisure: factory labour, trams, childbirth, a wedding and a divorce registry, funerals, sport and cinema-going. Vertov repeatedly shows the filmmaking apparatus itself — the cameraman perched above traffic, the editor at her bench, an audience watching the very film being assembled — making the work a self-reflexive celebration of cinema and of Soviet modernity.

Reception and legacy

Initial Soviet reception was mixed; Sergei Eisenstein dismissed its montage as "pointless camera hooliganism". Its reputation grew steadily after Vertov's rediscovery in the West in the 1960s. In the 2012 Sight & Sound critics' poll it was ranked the eighth greatest film ever made, and in the magazine's 2022 poll it placed ninth; the companion documentary poll of 2014 named it the greatest documentary of all time. Numerous composers, including Michael Nyman and the Cinematic Orchestra, have written scores for it.
