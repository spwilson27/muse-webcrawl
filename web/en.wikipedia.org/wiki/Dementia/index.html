Dementia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Dementia
DefinitionUmbrella term for decline in memory, thinking, behaviour and ability to perform everyday activities
Most common causeAlzheimer's disease (about 60–70% of cases)
PrevalenceMore than 55 million people worldwide (early 2020s)
Risk factorsAge, genetics, cardiovascular disease, low education, hearing loss, smoking, inactivity

Dementia is an umbrella term for several diseases affecting memory, other cognitive abilities and behaviour that interfere significantly with a person's ability to maintain their activities and occupations. It mainly affects older people, but it is not a normal part of ageing. Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause, followed by vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia and frontotemporal dementia. The World Health Organization recognises dementia as a public health priority; more than 55 million people live with it worldwide, with nearly 10 million new cases a year, and it is a leading cause of disability and death among older people.

Symptoms and course

Early signs include memory loss (especially of recent events), difficulty finding words, getting lost in familiar places, and impaired judgement; later stages bring confusion, personality change, loss of language and mobility, and total dependence on carers. The course is progressive and irreversible for the neurodegenerative forms, though some causes (vitamin deficiency, thyroid problems, certain infections) are treatable. Diagnosis uses cognitive testing, brain imaging and, increasingly, biomarkers of amyloid and tau in spinal fluid or blood.

Treatment and prevention

There is no cure for Alzheimer's disease; cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine give modest symptomatic benefit, and anti-amyloid antibody drugs (lecanemab, donanemab) approved in the 2020s slow decline slightly in early disease at the cost of significant side-effect risks and expense — their value remains debated. Up to about 40–45% of dementia risk may be attributable to modifiable factors — the Lancet Commission lists education, hearing loss, hypertension, obesity, smoking, depression, inactivity, diabetes, alcohol, head injury, air pollution and social isolation — making prevention a major focus. Care falls heavily on families; the global cost of dementia exceeds US$1 trillion a year.
