Soil salinity

Soil salinity is the salt content in the soil; the process of increasing the salt content is known as salinization. Salts occur naturally in soil and water, but human activities — especially irrigation with insufficient drainage — can raise concentrations to levels that damage plant growth and degrade land. Salts in soil include sodium chloride, sulfates, carbonates, and bicarbonates of sodium, calcium, and magnesium.

Causes

Primary (natural) salinization arises from weathering of parent rock, deposition of sea salt by wind and rain, and inundation by seawater in coastal lowlands. Secondary (human-induced) salinization results mainly from irrigation: applied water always contains some dissolved salts, and as water evaporates or transpires, salts accumulate in the root zone. Rising water tables — for example, after clearing deep-rooted native vegetation in Australia — can bring saline groundwater to the surface ("dryland salinity").

Measurement and classification

Salinity is usually measured as the electrical conductivity of a saturation extract (ECe), expressed in decisiemens per metre (dS/m). Soils with ECe above about 4 dS/m are classed saline. Saline soils have an exchangeable sodium percentage (ESP) under 15, while sodic soils have high ESP (and pH often above 8.5) but lower soluble salts; saline-sodic soils combine both traits.

Effects on plants

Excess salts impose osmotic stress, making it harder for roots to extract water, and can cause specific ion toxicities (sodium, chloride, boron) and nutrient imbalances. Crop yields decline once a species-specific threshold is passed; plants adapted to saline conditions are called halophytes.

Management

Reclamation of saline soil involves leaching salts below the root zone with good-quality water plus adequate drainage. Sodic soils are treated with gypsum (calcium sulfate) to displace sodium before leaching. Long-term control uses efficient irrigation (such as drip systems), salt-tolerant crops, and maintaining drainage infrastructure. Globally, tens of millions of hectares of irrigated land are estimated to be salt-affected, making salinity a major constraint on food production.
