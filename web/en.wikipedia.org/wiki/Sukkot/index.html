Sukkot is a Torah-commanded Jewish holiday celebrated for seven days, beginning on the 15th day of the month of Tishrei. It is one of the Three Pilgrimage Festivals (Shalosh Regalim) during which the Israelites made pilgrimages to the Temple in Jerusalem. The holiday follows soon after the solemn Days of Awe (Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur) and is a joyous occasion. Outside Israel, the first two days — and in Israel, the first day — are celebrated as full holidays.

Sukkot has a double significance: it commemorates the forty years of wandering in the desert during which the Israelites lived in temporary shelters, and it is also an agricultural harvest festival, sometimes referred to as the "Feast of Ingathering" (Chag HaAsif). The holiday is commonly known in English as the Feast of Tabernacles or the Feast of Booths.

A sukkah, the temporary hut built for the festival, covered with branches through which the sky can be seen.

The sukkah

Observant Jews build a temporary hut called a sukkah (plural sukkot), in which meals are eaten throughout the festival and, weather and practice permitting, one may also sleep. The sukkah recalls the fragile dwellings in which the Israelites lived during their journey through the desert after the Exodus from Egypt. Its roof, called schach, must be made of material that grows from the ground — such as palm branches, bamboo, or pine boughs — and must be sparse enough that the stars can be seen through it at night, yet provide more shade than sunlight during the day. It is customary to decorate the sukkah with hangings, fruits, and children's artwork.

The Four Species

Another central observance is the taking of the Four Species (arba'at ha-minim): an etrog (citron), a lulav (frond of a date palm tree), three hadassim (myrtle twigs), and two aravot (willow branches). These are held together and waved in six directions — east, south, west, north, up, and down — during the Hallel prayers, symbolising that God is everywhere. The Four Species are carried each day of the festival except on Shabbat.

Intermediate days and Hoshana Rabbah

The days between the opening and closing festival days are known as Chol HaMoed, the intermediate days, during which work is partially permitted. On each morning, processions (hakafot) are made around the synagogue's bimah carrying the Four Species, accompanied by prayers called Hoshanot. The seventh day, Hoshana Rabbah, features seven processions and the beating of willow branches on the floor, and is traditionally regarded as the final day of judgment for the year's decrees.

Following festivals

Sukkot is immediately followed by Shemini Atzeret, the "Eighth Day of Assembly", on which the annual prayer for rain is recited. In Israel, Shemini Atzeret is combined with Simchat Torah, celebrating the conclusion and restart of the annual Torah reading cycle; in the Diaspora, Simchat Torah is observed on the following day.

Origins

The Torah commands the festival in Leviticus 23 and Deuteronomy 16, linking the booths to the Exodus: "You shall live in booths seven days... so that your generations may know that I made the people of Israel live in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt." Historically, Sukkot was the most important festival in ancient Israel, associated with the dedication of Solomon's Temple and, in the Second Temple period, with the water-drawing ceremony (Simchat Beit HaShoevah) celebrated with great rejoicing in Jerusalem.
