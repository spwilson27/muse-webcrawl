History of clothing and textiles

The history of clothing and textiles traces the development of textiles and clothing from the use of animal skins in prehistory, through the domestication of fibre plants and animals, the invention of weaving, and the mechanisation of production in the Industrial Revolution, to the global fast-fashion industry of today.

Linen, wool, cotton and silk formed the basis of clothing for most of recorded history.

Early textiles

Humans began wearing clothing tens of thousands of years ago; eyed needles appear around 40,000 years ago. The earliest known woven fibres are dyed flax from Dzudzuana Cave in Georgia, dated to about 30,000 BP. In the Neolithic, flax and later wool were spun and woven on simple looms. Egypt produced fine linen; Mesopotamia developed a large woollen textile industry; cotton was cultivated in the Indus Valley by about 5000 BC; and sericulture — silk production — began in China, where silk became a prized export along the Silk Road.

Ancient and medieval worlds

Roman clothing centred on the tunic and toga; Roman fullers and dyers produced purple-dyed cloth from murex snails for the elite. In the medieval Islamic world and Europe, guilds regulated weaving and dyeing; wool was England's great export, and Flemish and Italian cities grew rich on cloth. The horizontal treadle loom and the spinning wheel, spreading from Asia, greatly increased productivity. Sumptuary laws in many societies restricted who could wear silk, fur or certain colours.

Industrial Revolution

Textiles led the Industrial Revolution. The flying shuttle (1733), spinning jenny (1764), water frame, spinning mule and power loom mechanised cotton production in Lancashire mills, fed by raw cotton from India, Egypt and the slave plantations of the American South. The sewing machine (patented in various forms from the 1840s) transformed garment-making, and William Perkin's mauveine (1856) launched the synthetic dye industry.

Modern era

Rayon, nylon (1935) and polyester introduced synthetic fibres; after 1945 production shifted increasingly to Asia. The late 20th century saw the rise of ready-to-wear fashion and then "fast fashion", criticised for labour conditions — highlighted by the Rana Plaza collapse in Bangladesh in 2013 — and for its environmental footprint. Recent trends include technical textiles, recycled fibres and renewed interest in traditional crafts.
