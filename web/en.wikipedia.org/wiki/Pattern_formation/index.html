Pattern formation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The science of pattern formation deals with the visible, spatially ordered (or temporal) outcomes of self-organisation and the mathematical principles behind them. A pattern is characterised by a regular arrangement of structure in space or time, arising without a central controller or a pre-existing template, through interactions among the parts of a system.

In biology

In developmental biology, pattern formation is the process by which cells in an embryo acquire different identities according to their spatial position, so that tissues and organs form in the right places and proportions. Key concepts include:

  • Morphogens — signalling molecules that spread from a source and specify cell fate according to their local concentration, as in the "French flag model" proposed by Lewis Wolpert.
  • Positional information — the idea that cells read their location within a field and differentiate accordingly.
  • Reaction–diffusion systems — proposed by Alan Turing in his 1952 paper "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis," in which an activator and an inhibitor diffusing at different rates generate standing patterns such as spots and stripes. Turing patterns are now widely invoked to explain animal coat markings, feather and hair follicle spacing, and digit formation.

Well-studied experimental systems include the segmentation of the fruit fly Drosophila, patterned by gradients of Bicoid and other transcription factors; the development of the vertebrate limb; and the periodic patterning of feathers, scales and teeth.

In physics and chemistry

Pattern formation is also central to nonlinear science: examples include convection rolls in heated fluids (Rayleigh–Bénard convection), spiral waves in the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction, dune and ripple patterns in sand, snowflake growth, and the stripes of sand tiger convection in granular media. The unifying mathematics involves bifurcation, symmetry breaking and instabilities of uniform states.

In mathematics

Theoretical frameworks include reaction–diffusion equations, cellular automata, phase-field models, and Turing's instability analysis. Pattern formation overlaps with the study of fractals, chaos theory and emergent behaviour in complex systems.
