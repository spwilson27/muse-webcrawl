Walter Scott

Sir Walter Scott, 1st Baronet (15 August 1771 – 21 September 1832) was a Scottish novelist, poet, playwright, and historian. Many of his works remain classics of European and Scottish literature, notably the novels Ivanhoe (1819), Rob Roy (1817), The Lady of the Lake (1810), Waverley (1814), The Heart of Mid-Lothian (1818), and The Bride of Lammermoor (1819). He is often credited with inventing the modern historical novel and had a pervasive influence on European and American literature.

Life

Scott was born in Edinburgh, the son of a solicitor. A childhood attack of polio left him lame in his right leg, a condition he turned into legendary endurance as a walker and rider. He trained as an advocate, became sheriff-depute of Selkirkshire and a clerk of session — legal posts he kept all his life — while collecting Border ballads published as The Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border (1802–03). Narrative poems such as The Lay of the Last Minstrel (1805) and The Lady of the Lake (1810) made him Britain's most popular poet until Byron eclipsed him.

In 1814 he published Waverley anonymously; its story of the Jacobite rising of 1745 founded the historical novel as a genre. The "Waverley novels" followed in rapid succession, published as by "the Author of Waverley", and Scott's authorship was only publicly acknowledged in 1827. He built Abbotsford House, a baronial mansion that became a shrine of Romantic Scotland, and stage-managed George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822, inventing much of the modern tartan pageantry of Scotland. The crash of his publishing partners in 1826 left him personally liable for some £130,000 of debt; rather than accept bankruptcy, he wrote himself out of it, producing a stream of novels and a life of Napoleon before his death in 1832. His debts were paid off after his death from the sale of his copyrights.

Influence

Scott shaped the historical fiction of Balzac, Hugo, Pushkin, Tolstoy, Cooper, and Manzoni, and his romantic image of Scotland — clan tartans, Highland scenery, Jacobite nostalgia — profoundly influenced Scottish national identity and 19th-century tourism. The Scott Monument in Edinburgh, completed in 1844, is the largest monument to a writer in the world.
