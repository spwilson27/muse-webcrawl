An adjustable gastric band, commonly called a lap band, is an inflatable silicone device placed around the upper part of the stomach in a surgical procedure (laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding) used to treat obesity. The band creates a small stomach pouch above it, so that eating a modest amount of food produces a feeling of fullness. Its tightness can be adjusted after surgery by adding or removing saline through a port placed under the skin, allowing the degree of restriction to be titrated without further operations.

[Image: Diagram of an adjustable gastric band in place around the upper stomach]

Procedure and mechanism

The band is usually inserted laparoscopically and does not involve cutting or stapling the stomach or rerouting the intestines, making the operation shorter and reversible compared with gastric bypass or sleeve gastrectomy. Weight loss is purely restrictive: the narrow outlet slows the passage of food from the small upper pouch, and patients must eat slowly and chew thoroughly. Typical excess weight loss is around 40–50 per cent over several years, generally less than with bypass or sleeve procedures.

Complications and decline in use

Complications include band slippage, erosion of the band into the stomach, port and tubing problems, oesophageal dilatation, reflux, and inadequate weight loss; long-term studies report that a substantial fraction of patients eventually require band removal or conversion to another operation. Because of these issues and the superior durability of other procedures, use of the adjustable gastric band has declined steeply since about 2010 in the United States and many other countries.
