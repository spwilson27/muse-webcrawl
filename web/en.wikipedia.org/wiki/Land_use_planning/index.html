Land use planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Overview
Field:Urban and regional planning
Instruments:Zoning, master plans, development control, land-use regulation

Land use planning is the process of regulating the use of land by a central authority, usually a local or national government, in an effort to promote more desirable social and environmental outcomes as well as a more efficient use of resources. Goals of land use planning may include environmental conservation, restraint of urban sprawl, minimising transport costs, preventing land use conflicts, and reducing exposure to pollutants and natural hazards.

Practice

Land use planning often involves the designation of zones for particular uses — residential, commercial, industrial, agricultural, recreational and open space — enforced through zoning ordinances and development control. Comprehensive or master plans set out a community's long-term vision and the policies to achieve it. Other instruments include subdivision regulations, building codes, growth boundaries, transfer of development rights, and environmental impact assessment. In England and Wales, the modern system stems from the Town and Country Planning Act 1947; in the United States, zoning was constitutionally affirmed in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty (1926).

Criticism and approaches

Critics argue that rigid single-use zoning contributes to sprawl, car dependence, housing shortages and racial and economic segregation, and have proposed alternatives such as mixed-use development, form-based codes, transit-oriented development and New Urbanism. Strategic and participatory approaches increasingly involve communities directly in plan-making, and contemporary planning integrates land use with transport, climate adaptation and public health objectives.
