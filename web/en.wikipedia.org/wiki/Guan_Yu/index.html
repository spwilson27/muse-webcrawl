Guan Yu (died 220), courtesy name Yunchang, was a Chinese military general serving under the warlord Liu Bei during the late Eastern Han dynasty. Together with Zhang Fei, he shared a fraternal bond with Liu Bei formed, according to tradition, by the Oath of the Peach Garden. Guan Yu played a central role in the civil war that ended the Han dynasty and led to the establishment of Shu Han, one of the Three Kingdoms.

Life

Guan Yu fled his home in Hedong after killing a local bully and eventually joined Liu Bei. Captured by Cao Cao in 200, he briefly served the latter and repaid his generosity by slaying the enemy general Yan Liang at the Battle of Boma before leaving to rejoin Liu Bei — an act Cao Cao admired. In 219, as Liu Bei's governor of Jing Province, Guan Yu launched the Battle of Fancheng, besieging Cao Ren and flooding the armies of Yu Jin, capturing him. At the height of his fame, however, Sun Quan's general Lü Meng secretly invaded Jing Province in his rear; Guan Yu's army disintegrated, and he was captured and executed by Sun Quan's forces near Linju.

Deification and legacy

Guan Yu was progressively deified in Chinese folk religion: the Sui dynasty saw early temples, the Song dynasty bestowed noble titles, and by the Ming dynasty he had been elevated to the status of a god of war, Guandi ("Emperor Guan"). He is worshipped in Taoism, Confucianism, and Chinese Buddhism as a paragon of loyalty and righteousness. Temples and statues of the red-faced, long-bearded general, wielding his Green Dragon Crescent Blade (guandao), are found throughout China and its diaspora, including in police stations and businesses, where he symbolises trustworthiness and protection.

The 14th-century novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms greatly embellished his deeds, depicting feats such as slaying Hua Xiong and crossing five passes single-handedly.
