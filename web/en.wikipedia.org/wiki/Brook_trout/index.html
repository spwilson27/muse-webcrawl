Brook trout

The brook trout (Salvelinus fontinalis) is a species of freshwater fish in the char genus Salvelinus of the salmon family. Despite its name it is a char, not a true trout. Native to eastern North America, it inhabits clear, cold, well-oxygenated streams, rivers, lakes and ponds, and has been widely introduced elsewhere in the world.

Description

Brook trout have a dark green to brown back with distinctive light, worm-like markings (vermiculations) and red spots with blue halos along the flanks. The lower fins are edged in white followed by black. In autumn spawning season, males develop brilliant orange-red bellies. Most brook trout weigh under 2 kg, though lake-dwelling fish (and the sea-run "salters" of coastal streams) grow larger; the world record, caught in Ontario's Nipigon River in 1915, weighed 6.6 kg (14.5 lb).

Habitat and ecology

Brook trout require water that stays cool — usually below 20 °C — and are an indicator species for pristine headwater streams. They spawn in autumn over gravel beds (redds) fed by upwelling groundwater, feeding on aquatic insects, crustaceans and small fish.

Conservation

Native populations have declined across much of their range through habitat loss, warming water, acid rain and competition from introduced brown and rainbow trout. The brook trout is the state fish of nine US states, including New York, Pennsylvania and Michigan.
