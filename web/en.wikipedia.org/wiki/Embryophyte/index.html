The Embryophyta, or land plants, are the most familiar group of green plants, forming vegetation on Earth. They comprise the bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, and hornworts) and the vascular plants (ferns, lycophytes, gymnosperms, and flowering plants). Embryophytes are complex multicellular eukaryotes with specialized reproductive organs, descended from aquatic green algae.

Defining features

The name derives from their defining innovation: the young sporophyte develops as an embryo retained within the tissues of the parent gametophyte, from which it receives nourishment. Other characteristic features include an alternation of generations between a multicellular haploid gametophyte and a multicellular diploid sporophyte, multicellular reproductive structures (antheridia and archegonia), and spores protected by resistant sporopollenin walls.

Evolution

Evidence suggests land plants evolved from charophyte algae during the Ordovician period, roughly 450–500 million years ago, and transformed terrestrial ecosystems. Adaptations to life on land — cuticle, stomata, and later vascular tissue, seeds, and flowers — allowed embryophytes to dominate most land environments; there are roughly 300,000–400,000 known species.
