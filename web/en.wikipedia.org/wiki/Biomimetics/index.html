Biomimetics

Hero image: the lotus effect — water beading on a lotus leaf — inspired self-cleaning coatings.

Biomimetics or biomimicry is the emulation of models, systems, and elements of nature for the purpose of solving complex human problems. The term "biomimetics" was coined by Otto Schmitt in the 1950s; "biomimicry" was popularized by Janine Benyus's 1997 book Biomimicry: Innovation Inspired by Nature. The field treats evolved biological designs — refined by 3.8 billion years of evolution — as a library of solutions to engineering, materials, and design problems.

Famous examples

Velcro was invented by Swiss engineer George de Mestral in the 1940s after he examined burdock burrs clinging to his dog's fur and saw their tiny hooks under a microscope. The lotus effect, described by Wilhelm Barthlott, led to self-cleaning paints and fabrics that imitate the water-repellent microstructure of lotus leaves. The streamlined nose of Japan's Shinkansen bullet train was redesigned after the kingfisher's beak, reducing tunnel sonic booms and energy use. Shark skin's denticle structure inspired drag-reducing swimsuits and hull coatings, and the adhesive pads of geckos — which grip through van der Waals forces among millions of microscopic setae — have driven research into reusable dry adhesives and climbing robots.

Approaches

Biomimicry is practiced at three levels: imitating the form of an organism, the process by which it makes things (for example, low-temperature, water-based chemistry), and the ecosystem level, designing human systems that cycle materials as ecosystems do. Benyus's framework emphasizes sustainability as a design criterion, under the slogan "nature as model, nature as measure, nature as mentor."

Challenges

Not all biological traits are optimal — evolution produces workable compromises under historical constraints — and scaling biological solutions to industrial dimensions is often nontrivial. Nevertheless biomimetics is applied in architecture, robotics, materials science, medicine, and aerodynamics, with dedicated research institutes and databases of biological strategies.
