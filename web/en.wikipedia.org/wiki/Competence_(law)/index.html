Competence (law)

In law, competence has several related meanings. It can refer to the legal authority of a court or other body to hear and decide a matter (competent jurisdiction), to a person's mental capacity to participate in legal proceedings or make legally binding decisions, and, in European Union law, to the division of powers between the Union and its member states.

Competence of courts

A court is said to be competent when it has jurisdiction over the subject matter and the parties before it. Rules of competence allocate cases among courts by territory, value and subject, and a judgment rendered by an incompetent court may be void or voidable on appeal.

Mental competence

In criminal law, a defendant must be competent to stand trial: in the United States, the standard from Dusky v. United States (1960) asks whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with a lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding, and a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. Trials of incompetent defendants are stayed until competence is restored, if it can be. Competence also matters for executing wills and contracts, consenting to medical treatment, and waiving rights; a person lacking the relevant capacity may act through a guardian or under powers of attorney. Related concepts include the insanity defence, which concerns the defendant's mental state at the time of the offence rather than at trial.

European Union

In EU law, competences are the areas in which the Union may act. The treaties divide them into exclusive competence (for example, the customs union), shared competence (for example, the internal market and environment), and supporting competence, under the principle of conferral: the Union acts only within the powers conferred on it by the member states.
