Polyacetylene

Structures of cis- and trans-polyacetylene.

Polyacetylene (IUPAC name: polyethyne) usually refers to an organic polymer with the repeating unit (C2H2)n. The name also refers to any of a group of polymers made of a carbon backbone of alternating single and double bonds. Polyacetylene is of theoretical and historical importance as the prototype conducting polymer.

Structure

The polymer consists of a long chain of carbon atoms with alternating single and double bonds, each carbon bearing one hydrogen atom. Two isomeric forms exist: cis-polyacetylene and trans-polyacetylene, the latter being more thermodynamically stable.

Discovery of conductivity

Polyacetylene films were first prepared by Hideki Shirakawa's group in Japan. In 1977, Shirakawa, Alan Heeger and Alan MacDiarmid reported that exposing polyacetylene to iodine vapour increased its electrical conductivity by many orders of magnitude, effectively turning the plastic into a metal-like conductor. This work on "synthetic metals" earned the three scientists the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2000.

Properties and applications

Undoped polyacetylene is a semiconductor. Doping with electron acceptors or donors produces conductivities approaching those of metals. Although polyacetylene itself is unstable in air and difficult to process, its discovery launched the field of conducting polymers, leading to materials such as polypyrrole, polyaniline and polythiophene used in organic electronics, antistatic coatings and flexible displays.
