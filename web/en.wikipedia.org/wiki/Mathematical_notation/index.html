Mathematical notation

Mathematical notation consists of the symbols, characters, and conventions used to express mathematical ideas compactly and precisely. It includes Hindu–Arabic numerals and arithmetic signs, letters from the Latin and Greek alphabets used for variables and constants, special symbols for operations and relations (such as +, −, =, <, >, ∫, ∑, and ∈), and structured layouts such as fractions, exponents, matrices, and diagrams.

History

Early mathematics was written largely in prose ("rhetorical algebra"). The development of symbolic notation accelerated in the 16th and 17th centuries: Robert Recorde introduced the equals sign in 1557, François Viète systematised the use of letters for unknowns and parameters, René Descartes established conventions for exponents and coordinates, and Leibniz and Newton created the notation of calculus. Modern set-theoretic and logical notation dates largely from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with contributions from Peano, Frege, Russell, and Whitehead.

Characteristics

Mathematical notation is highly context-dependent: the same symbol may mean different things in different fields, and notation is defined locally in texts. Its compactness allows complex structures to be manipulated almost mechanically, which many historians consider essential to the progress of mathematics. Notation varies somewhat between traditions and countries, and specialised branches continually invent new symbols, which are increasingly standardised through typesetting systems such as TeX.
