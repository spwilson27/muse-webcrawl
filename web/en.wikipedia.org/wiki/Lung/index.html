Lung

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The lungs are the primary organs of the respiratory system in humans and most other animals that breathe air, extracting oxygen from the atmosphere and expelling carbon dioxide. Humans have two lungs — the right (three lobes) larger than the left (two lobes, with a notch for the heart) — enclosed in the chest by the rib cage and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm.

Air enters through the trachea, which branches into bronchi and ever-finer bronchioles ending in alveoli: microscopic sacs, several hundred million of them, where gas exchange with capillary blood occurs. Lungs are spongy and elastic; their inner surfaces are kept moist and are coated with surfactant that prevents collapse.

Volumes and control

A resting adult breathes 12–20 times a minute. Lung volumes measured by spirometry (tidal volume ~500 mL, vital capacity several litres) diagnose restrictive and obstructive disease. Breathing is automatic, paced by brainstem centres sensitive to carbon dioxide, though it can be voluntarily overridden.

Disease

Major lung diseases include pneumonia, Tuberculosis, Asthma, COPD (chronic bronchitis and emphysema, strongly linked to smoking), cystic fibrosis and lung cancer — for decades the commonest cause of cancer death worldwide.
