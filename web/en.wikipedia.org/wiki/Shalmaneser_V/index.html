Shalmaneser V

King of Assyria: 727–722 BC
Predecessor: Tiglath-Pileser III
Successor: Sargon II

Shalmaneser V (Akkadian: Salmānu-ašarēd, "the god Shulmanu is foremost") was king of the Neo-Assyrian Empire from 727 BC until his death or deposition in 722 BC. He was the son and successor of Tiglath-Pileser III. In Babylon he also ruled under the throne name Ululayu. His short reign is chiefly remembered for the siege and conquest of Samaria, the capital of the Kingdom of Israel, whose completion is disputed between him and his successor, Sargon II.

Reign

Little is securely known of Shalmaneser's reign because Sargon II, who apparently seized the throne in a coup and may have been Shalmaneser's brother, conducted a campaign of damnatio memoriae against his memory. Babylonian king lists confirm his rule in Babylonia as Ululayu for five years. Assyrian sources credit him with campaigns against Tyre — whose king Elulaeus he subjected after a long siege of the island city's mainland possessions — and with suppressing revolts among the western vassals.

Fall of Samaria

The Hebrew Bible (2 Kings 17–18) states that Hoshea, the last king of Israel, rebelled against Assyria by withholding tribute and appealing to Egypt; Shalmaneser then invaded and besieged Samaria for three years before the city fell in 722 BC, after which the Israelites were deported — the origin of the "Ten Lost Tribes." Sargon II's inscriptions, however, claim Samaria's capture for the beginning of his own reign and credit him with deporting 27,290 inhabitants. Most modern scholars conclude that Shalmaneser began and largely conducted the siege but died during or just before the fall of the city, allowing Sargon to claim the victory.

Death

Shalmaneser died in 722 BC under murky circumstances; the swift accession and propaganda of Sargon II, who moved the capital and denounced his predecessor as impious, suggest a violent palace coup. Genealogical tradition deriving from later sources sometimes made Sargon his brother; Sargon pointedly avoided naming his father in his inscriptions.
