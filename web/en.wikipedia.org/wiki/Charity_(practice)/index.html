Charity (practice)

Charity is the voluntary giving of help, typically in the form of money, goods, time or services, to those in need. It is a central practice or duty in many religions and ethical traditions, and in modern usage it also describes the organised sector of charitable organisations that pursue relief of poverty, education, health and other public-benefit purposes.

Religious traditions

In Christianity, caritas — translated "charity" in the King James Bible — is the theological virtue of love of God and neighbour, expressed practically in almsgiving and works of mercy. In Islam, zakat, one of the Five Pillars, requires giving a fixed proportion (usually 2.5%) of one's savings to the poor, supplemented by voluntary sadaqah. Judaism's tzedakah frames giving as an obligation of justice rather than optional generosity, systematised by Maimonides in his "Ladder of Tzedakah". Dāna is a foundational virtue in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism, where the Sikh practice of langar provides free meals to all comers.

History of organised charity

Medieval Europe saw charity institutionalised in monasteries, hospitals and almshouses. The English Poor Laws and the Statute of Charitable Uses 1601 laid foundations for modern charity law. The nineteenth century brought large philanthropic foundations and organised fund-raising societies; the twentieth and twenty-first centuries added professionalised international NGOs, mass-media appeals, online giving and movements such as effective altruism, which seeks to maximise the good done per donation using evidence and cost-effectiveness analysis.

Criticism and debate

Charity has been debated since Oscar Wilde argued in The Soul of Man under Socialism that it perpetuates the conditions it relieves. Modern discussion covers the efficiency and overheads of charities, the ethics of donor influence, international aid effectiveness, and whether charity should be a duty or a supererogatory act.
