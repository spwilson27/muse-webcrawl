Motet

The motet is a mainly vocal musical composition, of highly diverse form and style, from high medieval music to the present. The motet was one of the pre-eminent polyphonic forms of Renaissance music. According to the musicologist Margaret Bent, "a piece of music in several parts with words" is as precise a definition of the motet as will serve from the 13th to the late 16th century and beyond.

History

Medieval origins

The motet emerged in the early 13th century from the Notre Dame school of organum: words (mot in French, whence motet) were added to the upper voices of a discant clausula over a tenor drawn from plainchant. Because each added voice might carry a different text — often one in Latin and another in French, one sacred and one amorous — the medieval motet became a polytextual genre of great sophistication, cultivated by composers such as Pérotin and, in the Ars nova, Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut, who perfected the isorhythmic motet.

Renaissance

In the 15th and 16th centuries the motet became the principal vehicle for learned sacred polyphony in Latin, typically for four to six voices, written in a continuous imitative style. Josquin des Prez, Orlande de Lassus, Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, William Byrd, and Tomás Luis de Victoria composed motets that remain central to the choral repertoire. The Council of Trent's debates over the intelligibility of words in church music are famously associated with Palestrina's style.

Baroque and later

The Baroque era divided the motet between the conservative stile antico and concerted motets for voices and instruments, exemplified by Monteverdi and by the grand motets of the French court. J. S. Bach composed a small number of masterly motets, including Jesu, meine Freude and Singet dem Herrn ein neues Lied. Composers from Mozart and Mendelssohn to Brahms, Bruckner, and modern writers continued to use the term for short sacred choral works.
