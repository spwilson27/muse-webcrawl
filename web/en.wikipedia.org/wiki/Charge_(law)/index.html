Charge (law)

In legal usage, a charge may refer to several distinct concepts, all broadly connected with the formal imposition of an allegation, obligation or instruction.

Criminal charge

A criminal charge is a formal accusation made by a governmental authority, usually a prosecutor, asserting that somebody has committed a crime. A charging document, such as an indictment, information or complaint, contains the specific charges, sets out the alleged facts, and identifies the law said to have been violated. A person who has been charged is entitled, in common-law systems, to be informed promptly of the nature and cause of the accusation and is presumed innocent until proved guilty.

Charge to the jury

A "charge" also denotes the instructions given by a trial judge to a jury at the close of a case — a summing-up of the evidence and an explanation of the applicable law, sometimes called the jury charge. Errors in the charge are a frequent ground of appeal.

Charges over property

In property and company law, a charge is a form of security interest: an encumbrance over an asset (such as a company's book debts or a parcel of land) that secures payment of a debt without transferring ownership. Charges may be fixed, attaching to a specific asset, or floating, hovering over a class of assets until some event crystallises them into fixed charges. In land law, a "charging order" may be obtained by a judgment creditor against a debtor's property.

Other uses

Historically, "charge" also described a pastoral or ecclesiastical duty: a cleric's parish was his "charge", and a bishop's formal admonition to clergy could be delivered as a charge. In Scots law, a charge is also a formal demand for payment following a court decree.
