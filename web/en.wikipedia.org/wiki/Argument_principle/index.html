Argument principle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In complex analysis, the argument principle (or Cauchy's argument principle) is a theorem relating the number of zeros and poles of a meromorphic function inside a closed contour to the change in the argument of the function as the contour is traversed.

Statement

Let f be a meromorphic function on an open set containing a simple closed contour C and its interior, with no zeros or poles of f on C. Then

(1/2πi) ∮C f′(z)/f(z) dz = N − P,

where N is the number of zeros and P the number of poles inside C, each counted with multiplicity (order). Equivalently, N − P equals the winding number of the image curve f(C) around the origin — the net change in arg f(z) divided by 2π as z goes once around C.

Consequences and applications

The argument principle follows from the residue theorem, since the residues of f′/f at a zero of order m and a pole of order n are m and −n respectively. Its most famous corollary is Rouché's theorem, used to locate zeros of functions. In engineering, the principle underlies the Nyquist stability criterion for feedback control systems, where the winding of a frequency-response plot about a critical point determines closed-loop stability. Generalisations include the argument principle for functions on Riemann surfaces.

History

Augustin-Louis Cauchy published versions of the result in the 1820s–1830s; the geometric winding interpretation gave the principle its name, from the "argument" (angle) of a complex number.
