Chop suey (Chinese: 雜碎; Cantonese Yale: jaahpseui) is a dish in American Chinese cuisine and other forms of overseas Chinese cuisine, generally consisting of meat (usually chicken, pork, beef, shrimp or fish) and eggs, cooked quickly with vegetables such as bean sprouts, cabbage, and celery in a starch-thickened sauce, and typically served with rice or crispy fried noodles.

Origins

The origins of chop suey are disputed. One popular legend attributes its invention to the cooks of the Chinese envoy Li Hongzhang during his 1896 visit to the United States; food historians regard this as a myth. A stronger claim traces it to tsap seui ("miscellaneous leftovers"), a stir-fry of Taishanese origin in Guangdong, brought to North America by immigrants from Taishan who formed the backbone of early Chinese restaurant cooking. Whatever its origin, by the early 20th century chop suey had become so emblematic that "chop suey joints" was a generic term for Chinese restaurants in America.

Variations

"American chop suey" in New England is an unrelated pasta dish of macaroni, ground beef, and tomato sauce. In Germany, Chop Suey similarly denotes a stir-fry with soy-based dark sauce. Filipino chop suey features a wide variety of vegetables in a starch-thickened sauce.
