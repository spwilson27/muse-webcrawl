Self-sovereign identity

Self-sovereign identity (SSI) is an approach to digital identity that gives individuals control of their digital identities. Rather than identity being administered by a central authority or third-party provider, the user is the central authority and decides what identity data to disclose, to whom, and for what purpose.

Principles

In 2016 Christopher Allen articulated ten principles of self-sovereign identity: existence, control, access, transparency, persistence, portability, interoperability, consent, minimalisation and protection. The model typically involves three roles — issuer, holder and verifier — and is built on W3C standards for Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials, often recorded or anchored on distributed ledgers.

Implementations

Projects such as Hyperledger Indy, the Sovrin network and Microsoft's ION experiment with SSI infrastructure. The European Union's eIDAS 2.0 framework and European Digital Identity Wallet incorporate SSI-like principles, though purists debate whether government-issued wallets fulfil the self-sovereign ideal.

Criticism

Critics question the usability of key management for ordinary users, the scalability and governance of ledger-based systems, and whether "self-sovereignty" is achievable when issuers such as governments and employers retain the power to grant or revoke the underlying credentials.
