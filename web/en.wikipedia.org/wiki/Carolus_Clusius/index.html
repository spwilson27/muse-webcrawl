Carolus Clusius (born Charles de l'Écluse; 19 February 1526 – 4 April 1609) was a Flemish doctor and botanist, one of the most influential plant scientists of the Renaissance. He created one of Europe's first botanical gardens, wrote landmark illustrated floras of Spain and of Austria and Hungary, and is famous for introducing the tulip to the Netherlands—where the theft of bulbs from his Leiden garden helped ignite the Dutch tulip trade.

Life

Born in Arras, then in the Habsburg Netherlands, Clusius studied law and then medicine, and learned botany under Guillaume Rondelet at Montpellier. He traveled and collected plants in Spain and Portugal (1564–65), publishing the first flora of the Iberian Peninsula, Rariorum aliquot stirpium per Hispanias observatarum historia (1576). From 1573 to 1587 he directed the imperial medical garden in Vienna for Maximilian II and Rudolf II, exploring and describing the flora of Austria and Hungary. After a period in Frankfurt, he became professor at Leiden University in 1593 and laid out its Hortus Botanicus, one of the first botanical gardens in northern Europe.

Works and legacy

Clusius's Rariorum Plantarum Historia (1601) gathered his Spanish and Austro-Hungarian floras with hundreds of woodcuts; his Exoticorum libri decem (1605) described plants and animals from the wider world, including early accounts based on material from the Americas, Africa, and Asia. He corresponded with a vast network of scholars, gardeners, and apothecaries across Europe, through whom exotic plants—tulips, hyacinths, lilies, the potato, and the horse chestnut—spread through European gardens. The theft of his prized "broken" tulips from the Leiden garden is a celebrated episode in the prehistory of Tulip mania. The genus Clusia was later named in his honor.
