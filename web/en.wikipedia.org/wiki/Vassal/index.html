Vassal


A vassal performing homage to his lord

A vassal is a person regarded as having a mutual obligation to a lord or monarch, in the context of the feudal system in medieval Europe and analogous systems elsewhere. The obligations often included military support and mutual protection in exchange for certain privileges, usually including land held as a tenant or fief. The term derives from Medieval Latin vassallus, itself probably from a Celtic word for "servant" (compare Welsh gwas).

The feudal relationship

The relationship was created in a ceremony of commendation: the vassal performed homage, kneeling and placing his joined hands between the lord's, and swore an oath of fealty. The lord in return invested the vassal with a fief—typically land and its revenues, but sometimes an office or income. The vassal owed counsel (attendance at the lord's court), military service with a retinue for an agreed period, and financial "aids" on occasions such as the ransom of the lord or the knighting of his eldest son; the lord owed protection and justice.

Subinfeudation and vassal states

Vassals could themselves grant parts of their fiefs to rear-vassals, creating chains of lordship that could complicate loyalty—captured in the maxim that "the vassal of my vassal is not my vassal." In international relations, the term extends to vassal states: states subordinate to a more powerful neighbour, owing tribute or military support while retaining internal autonomy, as with many principalities under the Ottoman Empire or tributary states of imperial China.

Analogues outside Europe

Analogous lord–retainer relationships existed in many societies: in Japan, samurai served daimyo under the code later known as bushido; in the Islamic world, holders of iqta grants owed military service; and in pre-colonial Africa and the Andes, layered systems of tribute and service bound local rulers to kings and emperors.
