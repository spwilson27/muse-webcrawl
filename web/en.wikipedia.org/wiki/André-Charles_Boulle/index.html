André-Charles Boulle (11 November 1642 – 29 February 1732) was a French cabinetmaker and the pre-eminent artist in the field of marquetry. Appointed cabinetmaker to Louis XIV, he gave his name to Boulle work (also spelled Buhl), the technique of veneering furniture with marquetry of tortoiseshell and brass, often combined with ebony and gilt-bronze mounts.

Life

Boulle was born in Paris into a family of craftsmen of German Protestant origin. In 1672 the king granted him lodgings and a workshop in the galleries of the Louvre, an exceptional privilege that freed him from guild restrictions. From his workshop he supplied the court at Versailles with commodes, desks, clocks, chandeliers and floors of marquetry, including work for the Dauphin's apartments and the famous cabinet of the Miroirs.

Technique

In Boulle marquetry, sheets of tortoiseshell and brass are glued together and cut with a fret saw in a single operation, producing two complementary panels: the première partie, with a tortoiseshell ground and brass inlay, and the contre partie, reversing the materials. Boulle combined this with ebony veneers, engraved detail and sculptural gilt-bronze mounts, many designed in his own workshop.

Legacy

Boulle's workshop was continued by four of his sons, and his designs were published in a book of engravings. His style was revived repeatedly, notably under Napoleon III, and fine examples are held by the Louvre, the Wallace Collection, the Getty Museum and the Royal Collection. A disastrous fire in 1720 destroyed part of his workshop and his celebrated art collection.
