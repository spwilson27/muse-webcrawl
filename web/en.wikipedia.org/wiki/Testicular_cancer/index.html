Testicular cancer is cancer that develops in the testicles, a part of the male reproductive system. Most testicular cancers are germ cell tumours, divided into seminomas and nonseminomas (including embryonal carcinoma, teratoma, choriocarcinoma and yolk sac tumour); rarer types arise from supporting tissues, such as Leydig cell and Sertoli cell tumours. It is uncommon overall but is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in young adult men in many high-income countries, with incidence highest in European and New Zealand populations.

Signs and symptoms

The most common presentation is a painless lump or enlargement of one testicle; a minority of cases are painful. Other features may include a feeling of heaviness, fluid collection (hydrocele), gynaecomastia (especially with hormone-secreting tumours), and symptoms from metastases, such as back pain from retroperitoneal lymph-node spread or cough and breathlessness from lung involvement.

Risk factors

The strongest risk factor is an undescended testicle (cryptorchidism), particularly intra-abdominal. Other factors include a personal or family history of the disease, a fertility-impaired or atrophic testicle, testicular intraepithelial neoplasia (germ cell neoplasia in situ), white ethnicity and taller height. Incidence rose in many countries during the 20th century for reasons not fully understood; environmental hypotheses form part of the "testicular dysgenesis syndrome" framework.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis uses scrotal ultrasound and serum tumour markers (alpha-fetoprotein, β-hCG and lactate dehydrogenase), followed by radical inguinal orchiectomy, which is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Staging is by CT imaging. Management depends on type and stage and includes active surveillance, chemotherapy (commonly BEP: bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin), radiotherapy (chiefly for seminoma) and retroperitoneal lymph node dissection. The introduction of cisplatin-based chemotherapy in the 1970s transformed prognosis: five-year survival now exceeds 95% overall and metastatic disease is frequently curable. Former cyclist Lance Armstrong is a prominent survivor.
