Comparative anatomy

Comparative anatomy is the study of similarities and differences in the anatomy of different species. It is closely related to evolutionary biology and phylogeny (the evolution of species). The science began in the classical era, continuing in the early modern period with work by Pierre Belon, who noted the similarities of the skeletons of birds and humans.

Comparative anatomy has provided evidence for common descent, and has assisted in the classification of animals.

Key concepts

  • Homologous structures — structures that are similar in related organisms because they were inherited from a common ancestor. They may or may not perform the same function. An example is the forelimb structure shared by cats and whales.
  • Analogous structures — structures that are similar in unrelated organisms because they evolved to perform the same function in similar environments, a process called convergent evolution. An example is the streamlined body shape of sharks and dolphins.
  • Vestigial structures — anatomical features that have lost most or all of their original function in a species through evolution, such as the human appendix and the pelvic bones of whales.

History

Aristotle made early systematic comparisons of animal structure. In the sixteenth century Pierre Belon published a famous comparison of bird and human skeletons in his History of Birds (1555). Edward Tyson's dissection of a chimpanzee (1699) is often credited as the founding work of comparative anatomy in England. In the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, Georges Cuvier established the discipline in France, using anatomical comparison to reconstruct extinct animals from fragmentary fossils, while Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire emphasised unity of plan among organisms.

Richard Owen formalised the concepts of homology and analogy in the 1840s. After Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), similarities of structure were reinterpreted as evidence of descent with modification, and comparative anatomy became a central pillar of evolutionary biology. In the twentieth century it merged with embryology (evolutionary developmental biology, or "evo-devo") and molecular phylogenetics.

Applications

Comparative anatomy supports taxonomy and phylogenetics, informs veterinary medicine, helps interpret the fossil record, and guides biomedical research by identifying suitable model organisms whose anatomy illuminates human physiology and disease.
