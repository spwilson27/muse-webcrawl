Edmund Cartwright (24 April 1743 – 30 October 1823) was an English inventor and Church of England clergyman. He is best known for inventing the power loom, a key machine of the Industrial Revolution, which he patented in 1785.

Life

Cartwright was educated at University College, Oxford, became a fellow of Magdalen College, and took holy orders. He first gained notice as a poet — his poem Armine and Elvira (1771) was popular — and from 1779 he was rector of Goadby Marwood in Leicestershire. The story of his turn to invention is famous: on a visit to Matlock in 1784, in conversation about Arkwright's spinning machinery, he remarked that weaving could surely be mechanised too, and, though he barely understood loom construction, set out to do it.

The power loom

His first patent was taken out on 4 April 1785. The early machine was crude — operated with considerable force and needing workers to stop it to dress the warp — but successive patents in 1786 and 1787 improved it with a metal reed, better shedding and take-up motions, and the sizing of warps while still on the loom. Cartwright set up a factory at Doncaster to weave with his looms and even built a steam engine to drive them, but the venture failed commercially, and a mill at Manchester built to use his looms was burned, reportedly by handloom weavers fearing for their livelihoods.

The power loom only became practical through improvements by others, notably William Horrocks and the dressing-machine work of Thomas Johnson, but the factory weaving system that transformed textile production rested on Cartwright's fundamental idea. In 1809 Parliament voted him £10,000 in recognition of the national benefit of his invention.

Other work

Cartwright also invented a wool-combing machine (the "Big Ben" comb, patented 1790–1792), which brought him into conflict with manufacturers, and experimented with steam engines using alcohol, primitive motor vehicles, and agricultural machinery. In later life he was presented to the living of Broadway in Kent and died at Hastings in 1823; he is commemorated in Manchester and in the Science Museum's collections.
