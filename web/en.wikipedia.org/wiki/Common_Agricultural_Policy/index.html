Common Agricultural Policy

The CAP is the agricultural policy of the European Union, first implemented in 1962.

The Common Agricultural Policy (CAP) is the agricultural policy of the European Union. It implements a system of agricultural subsidies and other programmes, and has historically accounted for the largest single share of the EU budget — around a third or more in recent decades, and close to 70 percent in the 1980s. It was introduced in 1962 by the then European Economic Community.

Objectives

The objectives set out in the Treaty of Rome were to increase agricultural productivity, ensure a fair standard of living for farmers, stabilise markets, assure the availability of supplies, and ensure reasonable prices for consumers. The original system relied on price support: guaranteed intervention prices, import levies, and export refunds that insulated European farmers from world market prices.

Reforms

Price support produced chronic surpluses — the famous "butter mountains" and "wine lakes" — and strained relations with trading partners. Successive reforms, beginning with the MacSharry reforms of 1992, shifted support away from prices toward direct payments to farmers. The 2003 Fischler reform "decoupled" most payments from production through the Single Farm Payment. Later reforms added "greening" requirements tying payments to environmental practices, and the CAP for 2023–2027 emphasises climate action, eco-schemes, and support for young farmers.

Structure

The modern CAP rests on two "pillars": Pillar 1, direct payments and market measures funded by the European Agricultural Guarantee Fund; and Pillar 2, rural development programmes co-financed through the European Agricultural Fund for Rural Development. Payments are conditional on farmers meeting standards for the environment, food safety, and animal welfare, a system known as cross-compliance.

Criticism

Critics have long argued that the CAP is expensive, favours large landowners, harms farmers in developing countries through subsidised competition, and has damaged the environment by encouraging intensive farming. Defenders respond that it safeguards food security, supports rural communities, and increasingly funds environmental public goods.
