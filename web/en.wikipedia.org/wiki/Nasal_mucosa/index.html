Nasal mucosa

The nasal mucosa, also called the nasal mucous membrane, lines the nasal cavity. It is part of the respiratory mucosa and serves to warm, humidify, and filter inspired air before it reaches the lower airways. It also contains the olfactory epithelium responsible for the sense of smell in its upper region.

SystemRespiratory system
RegionsRespiratory; olfactory; vestibular

Structure

The nasal mucosa is continuous with the mucous membranes of the paranasal sinuses, the nasopharynx, and the lacrimal apparatus via the nasolacrimal duct. Three regions are distinguished:

  • Vestibular region – lined by skin with coarse hairs (vibrissae) near the nostrils.
  • Respiratory region – the largest part, lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium with goblet cells, overlying a lamina propria rich in seromucous glands and a cavernous venous plexus, especially over the nasal conchae. The cilia beat in coordinated waves, moving the mucus blanket toward the pharynx (mucociliary clearance).
  • Olfactory region – at the roof of the cavity, containing olfactory receptor neurons.

Function

The mucosa conditions inspired air: its rich blood supply warms the air, glandular secretions humidify it, and the sticky mucus layer traps dust, microbes, and other particulates, which the cilia sweep away. The erectile venous tissue of the inferior conchae swells and shrinks in the alternating "nasal cycle".

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the nasal mucosa is called rhinitis; viral infection causes the common cold, and allergic rhinitis produces sneezing, congestion, and watery discharge. Chronic inflammation can produce nasal polyps. The mucosa's rich vascularity is also relevant to nosebleeds (epistaxis), most commonly from Kiesselbach's plexus on the anterior septum.
