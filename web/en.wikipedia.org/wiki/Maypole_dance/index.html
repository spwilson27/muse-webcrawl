Maypole dance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The maypole dance is a ceremonial folk dance performed around a tall wooden pole garlanded with flowers and ribbons, primarily associated with May Day and spring and midsummer celebrations in Europe. Dancers, each holding the end of a ribbon attached to the top of the pole, circle in opposite directions so that the ribbons wind around the pole in a woven pattern, then reverse to unwind them.

History

Maypole customs are documented from the later Middle Ages in Britain, Germany, Sweden and other parts of Europe. In Britain, maypoles were a focus of parish festivities; they were suppressed by Puritans in the 1640s — the Long Parliament ordered them removed in 1644 — and revived after the Restoration. In Sweden, the midsommarstång raised at Midsummer remains a central national custom. The ribbon-plaiting dance in its modern form developed in the 18th and 19th centuries and was popularised in schools and by the folk-dance revival of the early 20th century.

The dance

Dancers stand in a circle around the pole, men and women alternating, ribbons in hand. Moving in opposite directions and passing alternately over and under neighbouring dancers' ribbons, they braid the ribbons down the pole forming a woven sheath; the figures have names such as the "single plait", "spider's web" and "gypsy's tent". A traditional mistake that knots the ribbons is said to bring bad luck until untangled by reversing the dance.

Symbolism

Folklorists once interpreted the maypole as a survival of pagan tree worship or a phallic fertility symbol; modern scholarship treats these readings cautiously, emphasising the documented courtly and communal festivities of the late medieval and early modern periods.
