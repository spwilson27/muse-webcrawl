Color theory

A traditional RYB color wheel.

Color theory, or colour theory, is the body of practical guidance about color mixing and the visual effects of color combinations, applied in art, design, and related fields. It organizes colors with a color wheel, categorizes them as primary, secondary, and tertiary, and describes harmonious relationships—complementary, analogous, triadic, and others—as well as the dimensions of hue, value (lightness), and saturation (chroma).

History

Early systems appear in the writings of Leon Battista Alberti and Leonardo da Vinci; Isaac Newton's Opticks (1704) arranged the spectral hues in a circle, founding the color wheel tradition. Goethe's Theory of Colours (1810) emphasized perceptual effects. In the twentieth century, teachers such as Johannes Itten at the Bauhaus and Josef Albers, whose Interaction of Color (1963) demonstrated the relativity of color perception, reshaped instruction. Albert Munsell's system described color three-dimensionally by hue, value, and chroma.

Color models and harmony

Traditional artist's mixing uses the RYB model (red, yellow, blue primaries), printing uses CMYK (subtractive), and screens use RGB (additive). Pairs of complementary colors—opposite on the wheel—intensify one another when juxtaposed and produce neutral grays or browns when mixed. Warm–cool contrast, simultaneous contrast, and afterimages illustrate that perceived color depends on context, a fact central to painting from Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast onward.

Applications

Color theory informs painting, graphic and interior design, branding, data visualization, and user-interface design, where palette choices affect legibility, mood, and accessibility (including for viewers with color-vision deficiencies).
