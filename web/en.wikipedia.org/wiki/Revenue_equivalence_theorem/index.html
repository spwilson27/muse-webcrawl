Revenue equivalence

Revenue equivalence
FieldAuction theory, mechanism design
Key contributorsWilliam Vickrey, Roger Myerson
Applies toFirst-price, second-price, English and Dutch auctions (under stated conditions)

The revenue equivalence theorem is a central result in auction theory: under a standard set of assumptions, any auction mechanism in which (i) the item is always allocated to the bidder with the highest valuation and (ii) a bidder with the lowest possible valuation expects zero surplus will yield the same expected revenue for the seller and the same expected payment for each bidder. A striking consequence is that four classic formats—a first-price sealed-bid auction, a second-price (Vickrey) sealed-bid auction, an English (ascending) auction, and a Dutch (descending) auction—all generate identical expected revenue when the assumptions hold.

Assumptions

  • Bidders are risk neutral.
  • Each bidder's valuation is drawn independently from a common probability distribution (independent private values).
  • Bidders are symmetric (their valuations come from the same distribution).
  • The auction efficiently allocates the item to the highest-value bidder, and the lowest type earns zero expected surplus.

Historical development

William Vickrey first demonstrated equivalence among standard auction formats in his landmark 1961 paper "Counterspeculation, Auctions, and Competitive Sealed Tenders." Roger Myerson (1981) and, independently, Riley and Samuelson (1981) generalised the result to a wide class of mechanisms and used it as a foundation for optimal auction design.

Intuition

In equilibrium, a bidder's bid in any of these auctions must balance the probability of winning against the payment made upon winning. Because the allocation rule (highest type wins) and the boundary condition (lowest type pays nothing in expectation) are the same across formats, the interim expected payment of every type is pinned down uniquely—hence revenue, which is the sum of expected payments, is identical. More precisely, the expected payment of a bidder with value v is determined by that bidder's expected allocation across all possible types of opponents.

Violations and limitations

Equivalence breaks down when its assumptions fail. Risk-averse bidders bid more aggressively in first-price auctions, which then raise more revenue than second-price auctions. Correlated or common values introduce the winner's curse and make open (English) auctions more informative and typically more lucrative. Asymmetries among bidders, collusion, budget constraints and entry costs likewise overturn the result—considerations of great practical importance in spectrum auctions, procurement and online advertising.
