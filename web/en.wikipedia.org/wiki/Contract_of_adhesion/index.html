Contract of adhesion

A contract of adhesion (French: contrat d'adhésion) is a contract drafted by one party (usually a business with greater bargaining power) and signed by another party (usually a weaker party, often a consumer) who must accept the contract as a whole — on a "take it or leave it" basis — or not at all. The term derives from French civil law, and the concept was popularised in Anglo-American jurisprudence by Edwin W. Patterson in 1919 and Friedrich Kessler in 1943.

A standard-form contract, the archetypal contract of adhesion.

Characteristics

Contracts of adhesion typically exhibit several features: they are prepared in advance, printed in large numbers, non-negotiable, and offered by a party who is the only supplier of the good or service in question, or who otherwise enjoys substantially superior bargaining strength. Common examples include insurance policies, residential leases, software end-user licence agreements, mobile phone contracts, mortgage documents, and airline tickets.

The adhering party's only options are to accept the contract in full or to refuse the transaction entirely; there is no genuine opportunity to bargain over individual terms.

Judicial treatment

United States

American courts generally enforce contracts of adhesion but subject them to heightened scrutiny. Doctrines used to police them include the rule that ambiguities are construed against the drafter (contra proferentem), the doctrine of reasonable expectations (associated with Robert Keeton), and the unconscionability doctrine codified in section 2-302 of the Uniform Commercial Code. The enforceability of arbitration clauses in adhesive consumer contracts was notably upheld in AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011).

Civil law jurisdictions

In French law, the concept was recognised by Raymond Saleilles in 1901, and the 2016 reform of contract law codified rules on clauses abusives (abusive terms) in contracts of adhesion at Article 1171 of the Civil Code. German law regulates standard business terms (Allgemeine Geschäftsbedingungen) in sections 305–310 of the Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch.

Criticism

Critics argue that contracts of adhesion undermine the classical premise of freedom of contract, because the adhering party gives no meaningful assent to most terms. Defenders respond that standardised terms reduce transaction costs and prices for consumers generally.
