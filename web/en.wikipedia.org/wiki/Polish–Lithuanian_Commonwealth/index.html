The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, formally known as the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, was a bi-confederal state — a dual monarchy of Poland and Lithuania ruled by a common monarch, in existence from 1569 to 1795. It was one of the largest and most populous countries of 16th- to 17th-century Europe. At its largest territorial extent, in the early 17th century, the Commonwealth covered almost 1,000,000 square kilometres (400,000 sq mi) and sustained a multi-ethnic population of almost 12 million.

Formation

The Commonwealth was created by the Union of Lublin (1 July 1569), forging a real union between the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. Lithuania's grand duke Vytautas had earlier expanded the duchy; the Jagiellonian dynasty had ruled both states in personal union since the Union of Krewo (1385).

Political system

The Commonwealth was characterised by the political power of the nobility (szlachta), who elected the king in free elections and constrained him through the Henrician Articles. Its doctrines included religious toleration — the Warsaw Confederation (1573) guaranteed freedom of worship — widespread political participation of the noble class, and, fatally, the liberum veto allowing any deputy to dissolve the Sejm.

Golden Age and decline

Under Sigismund III and Władysław IV the Commonwealth was a great power of Eastern Europe, intervening in Russia during the Time of Troubles and repelling Swedes, Ottomans and Cossacks. The mid-17th century brought catastrophe: the Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648) and the Swedish-Russian invasion known as the Deluge devastated the country. John III Sobieski's victory at the Battle of Vienna (1683) was a late triumph.

Partitions and legacy

Weakened internally and surrounded by rising Prussia, Russia and Austria, the Commonwealth was partitioned in 1772, 1793 and 1795 — the third partition erased it from the map despite reforms such as the Constitution of 3 May 1791, Europe's first modern codified national constitution. The Commonwealth's multi-ethnic, multi-confessional federation remains a shared historical legacy of Poland, Lithuania, Ukraine and Belarus.
