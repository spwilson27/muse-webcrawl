Ensatina

Ensatina (Ensatina eschscholtzii) is a species of salamander in the lungless family Plethodontidae, native to coniferous forests and oak woodlands of western North America, from British Columbia through California into northern Baja California. It is one of the most famous examples in evolutionary biology of a ring species: a chain of interbreeding populations encircling a geographic barrier, whose two ends meet again as forms too distinct to interbreed.

Description

The ensatina is a small, slender salamander, typically 7–12 cm long, with a characteristically constricted base to the tail. It lacks lungs and breathes through its moist skin. Colour varies dramatically across its range — plain reddish-brown in the north and along the coast, boldly blotched with orange and brown patches in the Sierra Nevada — a variation central to its scientific fame. When threatened it can detach its tail, which continues to writhe, and exude a milky defensive secretion.

Ring species

Around California's Central Valley, which is too dry for the salamander, the populations form a ring: down the coastal ranges one set of forms grades into the next, and down the Sierra Nevada another set grades likewise. Where the two arms of the ring meet again in southern California, the plain coastal form (eschscholtzii) and the blotched inland form (klauberi) coexist with little or no interbreeding — behaving as separate species despite being connected by a chain of interbreeding populations elsewhere. Described in detail by Robert C. Stebbins in the 1940s and popularised by Richard Dawkins and others, the ensatina is a textbook illustration of speciation as a gradual process. Modern genetic studies show the history is more complex than a single clean ring, but the case remains a cornerstone of the subject.

Ecology

Ensatinas live under logs, bark and leaf litter, emerging on rainy nights to hunt small invertebrates. Females lay small clutches of eggs in moist underground sites and guard them until they hatch directly into miniature salamanders, with no aquatic larval stage.
