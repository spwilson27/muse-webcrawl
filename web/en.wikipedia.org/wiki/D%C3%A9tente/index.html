Détente

Détente (from the French word meaning "relaxation") is the easing of strained relations, especially between nations. In the context of the Cold War, the term is most often applied to the period of improved relations between the United States and the Soviet Union from the late 1960s to the late 1970s, marked by arms-control agreements, expanded trade, and summit diplomacy.

Background

After the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 brought the superpowers close to nuclear war, both sides sought ways to manage their rivalry. By the end of the 1960s the USSR had achieved rough nuclear parity with the United States, while the US was mired in the Vietnam War and Soviet leaders wanted access to Western technology and grain.

Major achievements

The high point of détente came in May 1972, when US President Richard Nixon visited Moscow and signed the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I) agreement and the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty. The 1975 Helsinki Accords, signed by 35 nations, ratified post-war European borders while committing signatories to respect human rights. Détente also produced increased trade, joint space ventures such as the 1975 Apollo–Soyuz Test Project, and regular summit meetings.

Collapse

Détente came under attack in the United States from both liberals, who criticised its indifference to Soviet human-rights abuses, and conservatives, who saw it as appeasement. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 effectively ended the policy; the SALT II treaty was withdrawn from Senate consideration, and the early 1980s saw renewed confrontation sometimes called the "Second Cold War".
