Macbeth (full title The Tragedy of Macbeth) is a tragedy by William Shakespeare, estimated to have been first performed in 1606. It dramatises the damaging physical and psychological effects of political ambition on those who seek power. The play follows the Scottish general Macbeth, who receives a prophecy from three witches that he will one day become King of Scotland; consumed by ambition and spurred on by his wife, he murders King Duncan and takes the throne, then spirals into tyranny, guilt, and madness until his defeat and death.

Macbeth is Shakespeare's shortest tragedy, and among his most popular and frequently performed plays. Its sources include Holinshed's Chronicles (1587), and it is often linked to the accession of James I — a descendant of the historical Banquo — and to the Gunpowder Plot of 1605. In the theatre, the play is surrounded by superstition: actors traditionally avoid saying its name inside a theatre, calling it "the Scottish Play".

Plot

Returning victorious from battle, Macbeth and Banquo meet three witches who hail Macbeth as Thane of Cawdor and future king, and Banquo as father of kings. When Cawdor's title is promptly granted to him, Macbeth contemplates regicide. Lady Macbeth urges him on; Macbeth murders the sleeping Duncan and frames his guards. Duncan's sons Malcolm and Donalbain flee, and Macbeth is crowned. Fearing the prophecy about Banquo's line, Macbeth has Banquo murdered — though Banquo's son Fleance escapes — and Banquo's ghost haunts Macbeth at a banquet. The witches' new prophecies ("none of woman born shall harm Macbeth"; he is safe until Birnam Wood comes to Dunsinane) breed false confidence. Macbeth orders the slaughter of Macduff's family; Lady Macbeth, sleepwalking and guilt-ridden, dies. Malcolm's army advances camouflaged with branches from Birnam Wood, and Macduff — "from his mother's womb untimely ripped" — kills Macbeth in combat. Malcolm is proclaimed king.

Themes and legacy

The play explores ambition, fate versus free will, guilt, masculinity, and the corrupting nature of unchecked power. Famous lines include "Fair is foul, and foul is fair", "Is this a dagger which I see before me", "Out, damned spot!", and the "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" soliloquy. Notable adaptations include Verdi's opera Macbeth (1847), Orson Welles's 1948 film, Akira Kurosawa's Throne of Blood (1957), Roman Polanski's 1971 film, and Joel Coen's The Tragedy of Macbeth (2021).
