Solvolysis

Solvolysis is a type of nucleophilic substitution or elimination reaction in which the solvent itself acts as the nucleophile. The term is a blend of "solvent" and "lysis" (loosening). Specific cases are named after the solvent: reaction with water is hydrolysis, with an alcohol is alcoholysis (for example methanolysis), with ammonia is ammonolysis, and with acetic acid is acetolysis.

Mechanism

Solvolysis of alkyl halides and similar substrates commonly proceeds through the SN1 mechanism: the leaving group departs in the rate-determining step to form a carbocation, which is then attacked by a solvent molecule, followed by loss of a proton to give the neutral product. Because the solvent is present in enormous excess, such reactions follow pseudo-first-order kinetics. Tertiary and resonance-stabilised substrates solvolyse fastest, and polar protic solvents — which stabilise the developing charges — strongly accelerate the reaction.

Solvent effects

The sensitivity of solvolysis rates to solvent ionising power was quantified by the Grunwald–Winstein equation, which uses the solvolysis of tert-butyl chloride as a reference reaction. Solvent nucleophilicity also matters for less hindered substrates, where SN2 character may compete. Solvolysis frequently gives rearranged products when the intermediate carbocation can rearrange to a more stable form before capture.

Examples

Classic examples include the hydrolysis of tert-butyl chloride in aqueous ethanol, the acetolysis of tosylates studied by Winstein in stereochemical investigations, and the ammonolysis of esters. Solvolysis is also of practical importance in the degradation of materials and in the behaviour of reactive intermediates in synthesis.
