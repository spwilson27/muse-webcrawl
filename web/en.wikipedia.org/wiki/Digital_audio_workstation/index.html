A digital audio workstation (DAW) is an electronic device or application software used for recording, editing and producing audio files. DAWs come in a variety of configurations, from a single software program on a laptop to integrated stand-alone units, and are the central tool of modern music production, film scoring, podcasting and sound design. They combine multitrack recording and non-destructive editing of digital audio with MIDI sequencing of virtual instruments, mixing automation, and processing through plug-ins.

History

Early digital editing systems of the late 1970s, such as Soundstream's, were custom and costly. The landmark step was Digidesign's Sound Tools (1989) and then Pro Tools (1991), which brought multitrack hard-disk recording to professional studios; in parallel, sequencers such as Steinberg's Cubase (1989) and Emagic's Logic grew from MIDI-only programs into full audio workstations as home computers became powerful enough. By the 2000s, cheap computing and interfaces made near-studio-quality production possible in bedrooms, and free or inexpensive DAWs such as GarageBand (2004) and Audacity (for editing) reached mass audiences.

Components and workflow

A typical DAW presents a timeline ("arrangement") of tracks holding audio clips and MIDI clips, a mixer with channel strips (faders, pan, sends, inserts), editors for audio (waveform) and MIDI (piano roll), and browsers for loops and instruments. Recording uses an audio interface for analogue-to-digital conversion; low-latency drivers (such as ASIO or Core Audio) let performers monitor through effects in near real time. Editing is usually non-destructive: clips are trimmed, warped, pitch-corrected and crossfaded without altering source files, and every action can be undone.

Plug-ins

Third-party plug-ins — effects (equalisers, compressors, reverbs, delays) and software instruments (synthesisers, samplers) — are hosted in standard formats: Steinberg's VST (1996) and VST3, Apple's Audio Units, and Avid's AAX. Plug-in ecosystems from companies such as Native Instruments, Waves and Universal Audio are a major part of the DAW economy.

Major DAWs

  • Pro Tools (Avid) — long the standard in professional recording studios and post-production.
  • Logic Pro (Apple) — macOS-only, bundled instruments and effects.
  • Ableton Live — clip-launching Session View oriented to electronic music and live performance.
  • FL Studio — pattern-based workflow popular in hip-hop and EDM.
  • Cubase and Nuendo (Steinberg); Studio One (PreSonus); REAPER (Cockos) — low-cost, highly configurable; Bitwig Studio; Reason; and free/open options such as Ardour.

Impact

The DAW displaced the multitrack tape studio for most purposes, collapsing the cost of recording and shifting record production toward software skills: editing vocals, quantising performances and designing sounds in the box. It also changed aesthetics — loop-based composition, pitch-correction as an effect (following Auto-Tune), and loudness-driven mixing — and enabled the bedroom-producer era of popular music.
