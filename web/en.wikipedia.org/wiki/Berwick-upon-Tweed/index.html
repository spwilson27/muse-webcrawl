Berwick-upon-Tweed, sometimes known simply as Berwick, is a town and civil parish in Northumberland, England, 2.5 miles (4 km) south of the Anglo-Scottish border, and the northernmost town in England. The 2011 United Kingdom census recorded Berwick's population as 12,043.

History

Situated at the mouth of the River Tweed on the east coast, the town was founded as an Anglo-Saxon settlement during the time of the Kingdom of Northumbria. For more than 400 years the area was central to historic border wars between the Kingdoms of England and Scotland, and several times possession of Berwick changed hands between the two kingdoms — it changed hands thirteen times before finally being taken by the English in 1482, after which it remained English, though its identity long sat ambiguously between the two nations.

The town is famous for its Elizabethan ramparts, among the finest surviving examples of bastioned town walls in Britain, built from 1558 to repel Scottish and French attack. Three bridges cross the Tweed at Berwick: the Berwick Bridge (1626), the Royal Border Bridge — a railway viaduct designed by Robert Stephenson, opened by Queen Victoria in 1850 — and the Royal Tweed Bridge (1928). The artist L. S. Lowry holidayed frequently in Berwick and painted many scenes of the town.

Geography and economy

Berwick lies roughly 56 miles (90 km) south of Edinburgh and 64 miles (103 km) north of Newcastle upon Tyne, on the East Coast Main Line railway. Its economy rests on tourism, services, salmon fishing on the Tweed, and food processing. The town's football club, Berwick Rangers, is notable as an English club playing in the Scottish football league system.
