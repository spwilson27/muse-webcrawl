Quantifier (linguistics)

In linguistics, a quantifier is a type of determiner, such as all, some, many, few, much, and no, that indicates the quantity of the referents of a noun phrase. Quantifiers are a type of shifter and play a central role in formal semantics, where they are analyzed in terms of generalized quantifier theory.

Properties

Quantifier expressions typically combine with a noun (their restriction) and a predicate (their scope) to yield a proposition. For example, in "Every dog barked", the quantifier every takes dog as its restriction and barked as its scope. In generalized quantifier theory, developed by Jon Barwise and Robin Cooper drawing on Richard Montague's work, a quantifier denotes a relation between sets: "every A is B" is true when the set of As is a subset of the set of Bs.

Scope and ambiguity

Quantifiers interact with one another and with negation to produce scope ambiguities. "Every student read a book" can mean that each student read some book (possibly different ones) or that there is one book every student read. The analysis of quantifier scope — and phenomena such as quantifier raising — is a major topic in syntax and semantics.

Universals

Research on linguistic universals has identified properties common to quantifiers across languages, such as conservativity: a quantifier Q is conservative if Q(A)(B) is equivalent to Q(A)(A ∩ B). Natural language quantifiers are argued to be universally conservative, a property not shared by arbitrary set-theoretic relations.
