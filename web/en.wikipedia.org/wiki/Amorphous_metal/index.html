An amorphous metal (also known as a metallic glass or glassy metal) is a solid metallic material, usually an alloy, with a disordered, non-crystalline atomic structure. Most metals are crystalline, with atoms arranged in a regular lattice; amorphous metals instead freeze from the melt with a random arrangement resembling that of a liquid or glass.

Production

The first metallic glass, an alloy of gold and silicon, was produced in 1960 at Caltech by W. Klement, R. H. Willens and Pol Duwez using extremely rapid cooling (around a million kelvins per second) to prevent crystallisation. Techniques such as melt spinning onto a cold copper wheel produce thin ribbons of amorphous alloy, while compositions with high glass-forming ability — including zirconium-based alloys developed in the 1990s — can be cast in bulk sections millimetres to centimetres thick, known as bulk metallic glasses.

Properties and uses

Lacking grain boundaries and dislocations, amorphous metals are typically much stronger and more elastic than crystalline alloys of similar composition, with high corrosion resistance. Iron-based amorphous ribbons have very low magnetic losses and are widely used in high-efficiency electrical transformer cores. Bulk metallic glasses have been used in applications such as sporting goods, medical devices and electronic casings, though brittleness in tension remains a limitation.
