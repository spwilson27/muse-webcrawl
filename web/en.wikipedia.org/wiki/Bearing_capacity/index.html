Bearing capacity

In geotechnical engineering, bearing capacity is the capacity of soil to support the loads applied to the ground. The bearing capacity of soil is the maximum average contact pressure between a foundation and the soil which should not produce shear failure in the soil. It is a central concept in the design of shallow and deep foundations for buildings, bridges, and other structures.

Theory

The classical theory of bearing capacity was developed by Karl Terzaghi in 1943, who expressed the ultimate bearing capacity of a shallow strip foundation as a sum of three terms: contributions from soil cohesion, from the surcharge (overburden) at the foundation level, and from the weight of the soil within the failure zone. Each term includes a bearing capacity factor (Nc, Nq, and Nγ) that depends on the soil's angle of internal friction. Later researchers, including Meyerhof, Hansen, and Vesic, extended Terzaghi's equation with factors for foundation shape, depth, load inclination, and ground slope.

Failure modes

Three modes of shear failure are recognised. General shear failure occurs in dense or stiff soils, with a well-defined failure surface reaching the ground surface and sudden settlement. Local shear failure involves significant compression of the soil with failure surfaces that do not fully reach the surface. Punching shear failure, typical of loose or soft soils, involves vertical penetration of the foundation with little surface disturbance.

Allowable bearing capacity

In practice, engineers divide the ultimate bearing capacity by a factor of safety — commonly 2.5 to 3 — to obtain the allowable bearing pressure. Design must also ensure that total and differential settlements remain within tolerable limits, since settlement, rather than shear failure, often governs the design of foundations on clay or where structures are settlement-sensitive. Presumptive bearing values for common soil and rock types are given in building codes for preliminary design.
