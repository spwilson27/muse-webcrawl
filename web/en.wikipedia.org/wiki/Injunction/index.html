Injunction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An injunction is a legal and equitable remedy in the form of a special court order that compels a party to do or refrain from specific acts — "an injunction is a judicial process whereby a party is ordered by a court to refrain from doing or to do a particular act or thing". A party that fails to comply faces criminal or civil contempt sanctions, including fines and imprisonment.

Varieties

Injunctions issue at every stage: temporary restraining orders (emergency, days-long, sometimes ex parte), preliminary injunctions (holding the ring until trial, on showings of likely success, irreparable harm and the balance of equities, per Winter v. NRDC, 2008), and permanent injunctions after judgment (property-rule protection — the prevailing party's right to exclude enforced by the court rather than priced by damages). Equity's traditional reluctance (adequacy of legal remedies, clean hands, laches) persists as doctrine. Modern flashpoints: nationwide ("universal") injunctions by single US district judges against federal policy — a practice administrations of both parties have litigated and legislators proposed to curb, and which the Supreme Court substantially restricted in 2025 in the birthright-citizenship litigation, holding universal injunctions likely exceed courts' equitable authority (as the case was decided on that preliminary question). Injunctions also police protest, strikes (the labour injunction's notorious history), speech (as suspect prior restraints) and environmental and prison litigation, where structural injunctions make courts continuing administrators.
