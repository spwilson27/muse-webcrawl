Perovskite is a calcium titanium oxide mineral, CaTiO3, discovered in the Ural Mountains of Russia by Gustav Rose in 1839 and named after the Russian mineralogist Lev Perovski. The name has come to denote a large family of compounds — and a crystal structure — sharing the general formula ABX3; see perovskite (structure).

Occurrence

As a mineral, perovskite occurs in silica-undersaturated igneous rocks such as kimberlites, nepheline syenites and carbonatites, and in some metamorphosed limestones. Varieties rich in rare-earth elements, niobium or cerium have been mined as minor ores. Deep in the Earth, the structurally related silicate bridgmanite, (Mg,Fe)SiO3, is believed to be the most abundant mineral in the lower mantle, adopting the perovskite structure at high pressure.

The perovskite family

Compounds with the perovskite structure display a remarkable range of properties: ferroelectricity (barium titanate), piezoelectricity (lead zirconate titanate), colossal magnetoresistance (manganites), and superconductivity in related layered structures. Since 2009, hybrid organic–inorganic halide perovskites such as methylammonium lead iodide have become leading materials for perovskite solar cells, whose efficiency has risen rapidly to rival silicon, as well as for light-emitting diodes and photodetectors.
