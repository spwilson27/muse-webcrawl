Super Mario 64

Mario in the grounds of Peach's Castle, the game's hub world

Super Mario 64 is a 1996 platform game developed and published by Nintendo for the Nintendo 64. Directed by Shigeru Miyamoto, it was a launch title for the console and the first Super Mario game to feature 3D gameplay. Cited as one of the greatest and most influential video games ever made, it established the conventions of the 3D platformer — free-roaming worlds, an analog-controlled character and a dynamic camera — and sold more than 11 million copies.

Gameplay

Princess Peach's Castle serves as a hub world; Mario jumps into magical paintings to enter 15 courses, each an open 3D environment. In each course Mario collects Power Stars by completing objectives — defeating bosses, winning races, solving puzzles and gathering coins. Collecting stars unlocks new areas, leading to three battles with Bowser and, at 70 stars, the final confrontation; all 120 stars can be collected for a perfect completion. Mario's moveset was revolutionary: triple jumps, long jumps, wall kicks, backflips, punching and the use of caps granting flight, metal invincibility and invisibility.

Development and reception

Miyamoto had experimented with 3D Mario since the Super NES era, and the Nintendo 64's hardware and its controller's analog stick finally made free 3D movement practical. The camera system, operated by the character Lakitu, was a landmark attempt at an intelligent in-game camera. The game received near-unanimous acclaim on release for its graphics, controls and freedom of exploration, and it is regularly ranked at or near the top of "greatest games" lists. A remake, Super Mario 64 DS (2004), added playable characters, and the game was re-released in the Super Mario 3D All-Stars collection (2020).

Legacy

Super Mario 64 defined the template followed by Banjo-Kazooie, Spyro and countless other "collectathon" platformers, and it remains a pillar of speedrunning culture, famous for sequence-breaking techniques such as the backwards long jump. Its influence on game design — teaching players through open spaces rather than instructions — is still studied by developers.
