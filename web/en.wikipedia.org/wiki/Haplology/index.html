Haplology

Haplology
FieldPhonology, morphology
Coined byMaurice Bloomfield (1895)
ProcessDeletion of one of two identical or similar adjacent syllables

Haplology (from Greek haplóos, "single", and lógos, "speech") is a sound change in which one of two identical or similar successive syllables in a word is lost. The term was coined by the American linguist Maurice Bloomfield in 1895 — itself, fittingly, a haplological shortening of the expected "haplogology".

Examples

Classic examples include Latin nutritrix from underlying *nutritritrix ("foster mother"), and English pronunciations such as "probly" for probably, "libry" for library, and "Febuary" for February, though in connected speech these may reflect syncope rather than true haplology. The phenomenon also shapes morphology: English avoids sequences like *-lee-ly, so adverbs from adjectives in -ly (e.g. gentle) are typically formed with a flat adverb or a paraphrase ("in a gentle manner").

Related processes

Haplology is related to dissimilation and to the Obligatory Contour Principle in phonological theory, which disfavours adjacent identical elements. It should be distinguished from haplography in textual criticism, the accidental omission of repeated material in copying.
