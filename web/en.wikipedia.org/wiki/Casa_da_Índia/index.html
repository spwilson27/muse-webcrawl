Casa da Índia

PurposeCrown trading house and colonial administration for Asia (and, initially, Africa)

The Casa da Índia ("House of India") was the Portuguese crown institution that administered Portugal's overseas trade and colonial affairs in Asia in the 16th and 17th centuries. Based in the Ribeira Palace on the Lisbon waterfront, it functioned simultaneously as a trading company, customs house, treasury, and colonial office, and managed the crown's monopoly of the spice trade.

History

The Casa grew out of earlier institutions managing the Guinea and West African trade (the Casa da Guiné, founded in the 1440s–60s, later the Casa da Guiné e Mina). After Vasco da Gama's voyage opened the sea route to India (1498), King Manuel I organised the India trade under the Casa da Índia around 1500. It regulated the annual Carreira da Índia fleets, registered all cargoes, collected the royal fifth and customs duties, sold spices into European markets (notably through the Portuguese factory at Antwerp), charted and guarded secret maps (the padrão real), licensed private trade, and corresponded with officials across the Estado da Índia.

Decline and end

The Casa's records were largely destroyed in the Lisbon earthquake of 1755. As the Portuguese Asian empire contracted and trade was progressively liberalised, the institution dwindled; it was formally abolished in 1833, its remaining functions passing to other agencies. During the Iberian Union (1580–1640) it had operated alongside the Spanish Casa de Contratación in Seville, which administered Spain's American trade.
