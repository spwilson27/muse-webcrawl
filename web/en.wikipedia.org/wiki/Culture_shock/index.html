Culture shock

Culture shock
Unfamiliar language and customs can disorient newcomers
Term coined byKalervo Oberg (1954)
FieldCross-cultural psychology, anthropology
StagesHoneymoon, crisis, recovery, adjustment

Culture shock is the disorientation, anxiety and stress a person may experience when suddenly placed in an unfamiliar cultural environment — for example when moving, studying or working abroad. The term was popularised by anthropologist Kalervo Oberg in 1954 to describe the occupational and emotional difficulties faced by expatriates adjusting to life in a new country.

Stages

Oberg described a sequence of stages commonly summarised as a U-curve of adjustment:

  1. Honeymoon — the new culture is experienced as exciting and novel; differences are viewed positively.
  2. Crisis (culture shock proper) — language barriers, unfamiliar customs, homesickness and frustration produce irritability, fatigue and a sense of incompetence.
  3. Recovery — language and cultural competence grow; the newcomer learns to function and interprets differences with more humour and understanding.
  4. Adjustment — the person operates effectively in the new culture and may come to appreciate aspects of it.

Some models add a further phase: reverse culture shock on returning home, where the traveller finds their own culture unexpectedly strange. Peter Adler and others extended the model into a W-curve to account for this re-entry stage.

Symptoms and causes

Reported symptoms include homesickness, loneliness, irritability, changes in sleep and appetite, excessive concern with cleanliness or health, and withdrawal into expatriate enclaves. Causes include the loss of familiar cues and social signals, language difficulty, and the cognitive effort of constantly interpreting a new environment. Severity varies widely with personality, preparation, cultural distance and the support available.

Coping and significance

Preparation — language study, cultural training and realistic expectations — is used by universities, missionary organisations and multinational companies to reduce culture shock among exchange students and expatriate staff. Psychologists increasingly frame the process less as a pathology than as a normal, potentially growth-producing form of acculturative stress, managed through social support, routine-building and gradual engagement with the host culture.
