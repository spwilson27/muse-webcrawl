British Expedition to Abyssinia

The British Expedition to Abyssinia was a rescue mission and punitive expedition carried out in 1868 by the armed forces of the British Empire against the Ethiopian Empire. It was launched after Emperor Tewodros II imprisoned Britain's envoy and other Europeans at his mountain fortress of Magdala.

The fortress of Magdala, objective of the expedition.

Background

Tewodros II, seeking British help against his enemies and frustrated by the lack of reply to a letter he had sent to Queen Victoria, detained the British consul Charles Duncan Cameron in 1864, together with the envoy Hormuzd Rassam and a number of European missionaries and artisans. Diplomatic efforts failed, and in 1867 the British government resolved on a military expedition.

The expedition

A force of some 13,000 British and Indian troops under Lieutenant-General Sir Robert Napier landed at Zula on the Red Sea coast in December 1867 and advanced over 400 miles (640 km) of mountainous country, building roads, a railway from the coast and even importing elephants to carry the artillery. The army reached Magdala in April 1868; at the Battle of Arogee the Ethiopian assault was repulsed with heavy losses. When the fortress was stormed on 13 April 1868, Tewodros took his own life rather than surrender. The hostages were released unharmed.

Aftermath

The expedition cost an enormous sum — around £9 million — but achieved its objectives, and Napier was created Baron Napier of Magdala. British and Indian troops looted large numbers of Ethiopian manuscripts, crosses and treasures, many of which remain in British museums and the Royal Collection, subject to continuing restitution claims. The force withdrew without annexing any territory.
