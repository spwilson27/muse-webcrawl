The Amazon rubber boom (1879–1912) was an era of extraordinary wealth and social upheaval in the Amazon basin driven by the extraction of latex from wild rubber trees (Hevea brasiliensis), in high demand after the invention of vulcanisation and the bicycle and automobile tyre industries.

Boom years

River ports Manaus and Belém in Brazil and Iquitos in Peru became fabulously wealthy; Manaus gained electric lighting, trams and the ornate Teatro Amazonas opera house (1896). Rubber barons built fortunes on a labour system of debt peonage that trapped tappers (seringueiros). In the Putumayo region of Peru, the company of Julio César Arana committed atrocities against indigenous peoples — documented by Roger Casement's 1911 report — in which tens of thousands died.

Collapse and second boom

In 1876 Henry Wickham had taken 70,000 Hevea seeds from Brazil to Kew Gardens; seedlings went to Ceylon and Malaya, where plantation rubber, efficiently tapped in rows, undercut the Amazon trade after 1910. Prices collapsed by 1912, ending the boom and Manaus's golden age; smuggling prohibitions by Brazil came too late. Henry Ford's failed plantation Fordlândia (1928) symbolised attempts to revive it. A second, wartime boom followed the Japanese occupation of Southeast Asian plantations in 1942, when the Allies recruited tens of thousands of Brazilian "rubber soldiers" — but Amazon production never regained dominance after synthetic rubber and the war's end.
