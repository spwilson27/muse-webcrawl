Simple polygon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Examples of simple polygons (convex and concave).

In geometry, a simple polygon is a polygon that does not intersect itself and has no holes. The interior of a simple polygon can be partitioned into triangles in several ways, e.g. by the polygon triangulation. Simple polygons are topologically equivalent to a disk.

Definitions

A simple polygon is a flat shape consisting of straight, non-intersecting line segments that are joined pairwise to form a single closed path. The segments are called its edges or sides, and the points where two edges meet are the vertices. A polygon that is not simple is called complex or self-intersecting.

Properties

By the Jordan curve theorem, a simple polygon divides the plane into an interior region and an exterior region. The interior is an open polygonal region whose area can be computed from the vertex coordinates with the shoelace formula. The sum of the interior angles of a simple polygon with n sides is (n − 2)π radians (or (n − 2) × 180°).

A simple polygon is convex if all its interior angles are less than 180°; otherwise it is concave (non-convex) and has at least one reflex vertex. Every simple polygon has at least two "ears" (Meisters's two ears theorem) and can be triangulated into n − 2 triangles.

Computational problems

Simple polygons are fundamental in computational geometry, in problems such as point-in-polygon testing, polygon triangulation, boolean operations on polygons, and the art gallery problem.
