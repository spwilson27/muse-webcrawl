The Curia Julia (Latin: Curia Iulia) is the third named meeting house of the Roman Senate in the Roman Forum and the only one whose structure survives largely intact. Begun by Julius Caesar in 44 BC as part of his reordering of the Forum and the Comitium, it was completed and dedicated by Augustus in 29 BC. The surviving brick building is largely the restoration undertaken after a fire in AD 283, under the emperor Diocletian.

History

The Senate had previously met in the Curia Hostilia and its replacement, the Curia Cornelia. Caesar's new curia was aligned with his adjacent Forum of Caesar. Augustus dedicated the building in 29 BC, placing within it the Altar of Victory and a statue of Victory from Tarentum; the altar became a famous flashpoint in the religious conflicts of the late empire, its removal prompting Symmachus's celebrated plea for tolerance in AD 384. The building burned in the fire of AD 283 during the reign of Carinus and was rebuilt by Diocletian, whose restoration gives the structure its present form.

In the 7th century the curia was converted into the church of Sant'Adriano al Foro, which preserved the shell of the building through the Middle Ages and beyond. In the 1930s, under the fascist government of Mussolini, the church additions were stripped away and the ancient senate house was restored to roughly its Diocletianic appearance.

Description

The building is a plain, high brick-faced concrete hall, about 25.6 by 17.8 metres and 21 metres tall, with three broad, low steps along each side where the senators' seats stood — seating for roughly 300. Its marble floor, in coloured geometric opus sectile, is original to the Diocletianic restoration. The great bronze doors now at the Lateran are replicas; the originals were moved to the Basilica of St John Lateran in the 17th century. Inside are displayed two celebrated reliefs, the Plutei of Trajan, showing scenes of the emperor's public benefactions. Behind the curia stood the chalcidicum and the secretarium, an annex used for trials of senators.
