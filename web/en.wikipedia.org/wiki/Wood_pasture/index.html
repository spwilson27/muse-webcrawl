Wood pasture

Wood pasture is a historical European land management system in which livestock are grazed in open, park-like woodland. It combines scattered trees — often pollarded to keep regrowth above the reach of browsing animals — with grazing land, and is regarded as a distinct habitat of high conservation value because its ancient trees support rich communities of fungi, lichens and invertebrates.

History

Wood pasture was widespread in medieval Europe. In England, commoners often held rights of pannage, turning pigs out to eat acorns and beech mast in autumn. Surviving examples include the New Forest in Hampshire and Epping Forest near London. The Spanish dehesa and Portuguese montado are related Iberian systems based on evergreen oaks.

Ecology

Veteran trees in wood pasture are among the richest habitats in Europe for saproxylic (deadwood-dependent) species. Ecologist Frans Vera proposed that prehistoric Europe's vegetation was a wood-pasture-like mosaic maintained by large herbivores such as aurochs and wild horses, rather than unbroken closed-canopy forest.

Decline and conservation

The system declined with agricultural enclosure, intensive forestry and the loss of common grazing rights. Remaining wood pastures are now protected in many countries and are restored by reintroducing grazing and pollarding.
