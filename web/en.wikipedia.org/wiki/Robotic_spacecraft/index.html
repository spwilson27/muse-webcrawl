Robotic spacecraft

A robotic spacecraft, also called an uncrewed spacecraft or space probe when it leaves Earth orbit, is a spacecraft with no people on board, controlled remotely from the ground or operating autonomously. Robotic spacecraft perform scientific research, communications, Earth observation, navigation, and planetary exploration, and they carry out tasks too dangerous, too distant, or too long-lasting for human crews.

Types

Robotic spacecraft include artificial satellites in Earth orbit — communications satellites, weather satellites, spy satellites, and navigation constellations such as GPS — as well as deep-space probes. Deep-space missions are commonly classified by their encounter style: flyby spacecraft pass a target body (such as the Voyager probes at the outer planets), orbiters enter orbit around a planet or moon (Cassini at Saturn, Juno at Jupiter), landers descend to a surface (Viking and Phoenix on Mars), rovers move across a surface (Sojourner, Spirit, Opportunity, Curiosity, and Perseverance on Mars), and sample-return missions bring material back to Earth (Hayabusa2 from asteroid Ryugu, OSIRIS-REx from Bennu).

Design and control

Because radio signals take minutes or hours to cross interplanetary distances, deep-space probes cannot be flown in real time. They follow stored command sequences and carry fault-protection software that lets them respond to problems autonomously and enter a safe mode while awaiting instructions. Ground stations of networks such as NASA's Deep Space Network transmit commands and receive telemetry and science data, which are encoded on radio or, increasingly, optical links.

Key subsystems include power (solar arrays near the Sun, radioisotope thermoelectric generators in deep space), propulsion, attitude control using reaction wheels and thrusters, thermal control, and scientific instruments such as cameras, spectrometers, magnetometers, and radar sounders. Space telescopes such as Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope are also robotic spacecraft.

History

The first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, launched by the Soviet Union in 1957, was a robotic spacecraft. Luna 2 became the first human-made object to reach the Moon in 1959, and Luna 9 made the first soft landing in 1966. Mariner 2 flew by Venus in 1962, the first successful interplanetary mission. The Voyager spacecraft, launched in 1977, have entered interstellar space and remain the most distant human-made objects. Robotic missions have now visited all eight planets, landed on the Moon, Mars, Venus, Titan, asteroids, and a comet, and returned samples from the Moon, asteroids, and the solar wind.

Advantages and limitations

Robotic missions are far cheaper and less risky than crewed flight and can endure decades in hostile environments, but they lack human adaptability on site and depend on pre-programmed or remotely commanded responses. Advances in autonomy, miniaturisation (such as CubeSats), and electric propulsion continue to expand their capabilities.
