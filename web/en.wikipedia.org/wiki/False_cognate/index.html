False cognate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

False cognates are pairs of words in the same or different languages that seem to be cognates because of similar sounds and meanings, but actually have different etymologies. This can be a result of coincidence, or of a shared root that diverged so long ago that the resemblance is misleading, or of one language borrowing a word that happens to resemble a native word.

False cognates should be distinguished from false friends, which are words that look similar but differ significantly in meaning. Some words may be both false cognates and false friends.

Examples

Word pairActual origins
English much / Spanish muchoMuch comes from Old English myċel (great); mucho from Latin multus (many). Unrelated despite similar sound and meaning.
English dog / Mbabaram dogA famous coincidence: the Australian Aboriginal language Mbabaram word for dog is dog, by pure chance.
English bad / Persian badBoth mean "bad", but the Persian word is not the source of the English one.
English island / Spanish islaIsland is Germanic (Old English īġland); isla is from Latin insula. The s in island was added by false association.

Causes

False cognates arise through chance resemblance, convergent sound change, onomatopoeia (words for animal sounds often resemble each other independently), and nursery words such as mama and papa, which occur in many unrelated languages because of infant babbling patterns.
