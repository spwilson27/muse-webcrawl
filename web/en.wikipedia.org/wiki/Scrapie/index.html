Scrapie

Species affectedSheep, goats
CausePrion (misfolded PrP protein)
TypeTransmissible spongiform encephalopathy
First described18th century

Scrapie is a fatal, degenerative disease affecting the nervous systems of sheep and goats. It is one of several transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), and like others it is caused by a prion — an infectious agent composed of misfolded protein. Scrapie has been known since at least the 18th century and was long a significant problem for sheep farmers in Europe and elsewhere.

Signs and symptoms

The name derives from one of the clinical signs of the condition, in which affected animals compulsively scrape their fleeces against rocks, trees or fences, apparently because of intense itching (pruritus). Other signs include behavioural changes, tremors, incoordination, weight loss despite a normal appetite, and eventually an inability to stand. The incubation period is long, typically two to five years, and the disease is invariably fatal once clinical signs appear.

Transmission

Scrapie is believed to spread primarily from ewe to lamb through contact with the placenta and placental fluids at lambing, and through contaminated pasture. The prion protein is extremely resistant to heat, disinfectants and radiation, and can persist in the environment for years. Susceptibility is strongly influenced by the genetics of the prion protein gene (PRNP); selective breeding for resistant genotypes has been used in national eradication programmes.

Relation to other prion diseases

Scrapie is the prototype of the prion diseases, which include bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE) in cattle, chronic wasting disease in deer, and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease in humans. Unlike BSE, classical scrapie has not been shown to be transmissible to humans, and it is not considered a zoonosis.

Control

Control relies on culling affected flocks, movement restrictions, genotyping and breeding for genetic resistance, and fallowing of contaminated pastures. Several countries have run long-term eradication programmes, and scrapie incidence has fallen substantially where resistant genotypes predominate.
