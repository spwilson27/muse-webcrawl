History of Tunisia

The history of Tunisia spans from the prehistoric Capsian culture through the Phoenician foundation of Carthage, Roman, Vandal, Byzantine, and Arab rule, the Ottoman period, the French protectorate, and the modern republic established after independence in 1956.

Ancient period

Phoenicians founded Carthage around 814 BC, and the city grew into a maritime empire that rivalled Rome. The three Punic Wars ended with Rome's destruction of Carthage in 146 BC; the territory became the Roman province of Africa, a major grain-producing region often called the breadbasket of the empire. After the fall of Rome, Vandals ruled from 439 to 534, followed by Byzantine reconquest.

Islamic and medieval period

Arab armies conquered the region in the seventh century, founding Kairouan in 670, one of Islam's holiest cities. Successive dynasties — Aghlabids, Fatimids (who later moved their capital to Cairo), Zirids, Almohads, and Hafsids — governed Ifriqiya from Tunis. In 1574 the Ottoman Empire annexed Tunisia, ruling through deys and later the hereditary Husainid beys.

French protectorate and independence

France established a protectorate in 1881 under the Treaty of Bardo. The nationalist Neo Destour party, led by Habib Bourguiba, pressed for independence, achieved on 20 March 1956. Bourguiba became president in 1957, abolishing the monarchy and pursuing secularizing reforms, including the landmark Code of Personal Status advancing women's rights. Zine El Abidine Ben Ali seized power in 1987 and ruled until the Tunisian Revolution of 2010–2011, which ignited the Arab Spring and led to democratic elections and a new constitution in 2014.
