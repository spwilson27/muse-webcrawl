The District of Columbia Home Rule Act is a United States federal law passed by Congress in 1973 that devolved certain congressional powers over the District of Columbia to a local government administered by an elected mayor and a thirteen-member Council of the District of Columbia. Before the Act, the District had been governed directly by Congress and, from 1967, by a mayor-commissioner and council appointed by the President.

Provisions

The Act created the modern structure of D.C. government: a Mayor elected District-wide, a Council of thirteen (a chairperson and four members elected at large, and one member from each of the eight wards), and Advisory Neighborhood Commissions. The first elections under the Act were held in 1974; Walter Washington became the District's first elected mayor of the modern era. District voters approved the home rule charter in a referendum in May 1974.

Limits on home rule

Home rule is limited. The U.S. Constitution gives Congress "exclusive legislation in all cases whatsoever" over the federal district, and the Act preserves Congress's authority: all D.C. legislation is subject to a congressional review period during which Congress may disapprove it, the District's budget must be approved by Congress, and the Act forbids the Council from, among other things, taxing income earned by non-residents, altering the height-of-buildings limits, or amending the charter without congressional approval. The President also retains authority to take control of the D.C. police in emergencies, and Congress intervened fiscally in the 1990s by creating a financial control board.

Significance

The Act restored elected local self-government to Washingtonians for the first time in a century, although debates continue over D.C. voting representation in Congress and statehood — the District's residents elect a non-voting delegate to the House of Representatives but have no senators.
