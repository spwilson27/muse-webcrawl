Nucleophilic aromatic substitution

Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) is a class of substitution reactions in organic chemistry in which a nucleophile displaces a leaving group, such as a halide, on an aromatic ring. Unlike electrophilic aromatic substitution, SNAr generally requires electron-withdrawing groups (especially nitro groups) ortho or para to the leaving group to activate the ring toward nucleophilic attack.

Mechanism

The classical mechanism is an addition–elimination sequence. The nucleophile attacks the ring carbon bearing the leaving group, forming a resonance-stabilized anionic σ-complex known as a Meisenheimer complex, in which the negative charge is delocalized onto the electron-withdrawing substituents. Expulsion of the leaving group then restores aromaticity. Because bond formation to the nucleophile is usually rate-determining, fluoride is often the most reactive halogen leaving group — the reverse of the order in aliphatic SN2 reactions.

Related reactions

Other pathways to nucleophilic substitution on arenes include the benzyne mechanism (elimination–addition, as in the Dow process for phenol), the SN1-like diazonium chemistry, and vicarious nucleophilic substitution, in which the nucleophile itself carries a leaving group. SNAr is widely used in medicinal chemistry to build aryl ethers and aryl amines, including in the synthesis of many kinase inhibitors.

Examples

Classic examples include the reaction of 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene with amines or hydroxide, and the formation of picric acid derivatives from highly nitrated chloroarenes.
