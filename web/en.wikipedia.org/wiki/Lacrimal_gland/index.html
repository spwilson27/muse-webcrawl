Lacrimal gland

The lacrimal glands are paired exocrine glands, one for each eye, that secrete the aqueous layer of the tear film. In humans they are situated in the upper lateral region of each orbit, in the lacrimal fossa of the frontal bone. Each gland is roughly the size and shape of an almond and consists of a larger orbital part and a smaller palpebral part, divided by the tendon of the levator palpebrae superioris muscle.

Function

The gland secretes tears — a watery fluid containing electrolytes, proteins such as lysozyme and lactoferrin, and secretory immunoglobulin A — which wash over the cornea and conjunctiva, lubricating the eye, supplying nutrients to the cornea and protecting against infection. About a dozen small excretory ducts open into the superior conjunctival fornix, from where blinking spreads the fluid across the ocular surface; it then drains through the lacrimal puncta into the nasolacrimal duct and the nose. Secretion is driven mainly by parasympathetic fibres of the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII), which is why emotional crying floods the gland's output.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the gland is called dacryoadenitis, seen in infections and in autoimmune conditions such as Sjögren's syndrome, in which immune attack on the lacrimal (and salivary) glands produces dry eyes. Tumours of the gland include pleomorphic adenoma and adenoid cystic carcinoma. Inadequate tear production is a principal cause of dry eye disease (keratoconjunctivitis sicca).
