Estates General of 1614

Estates General of 1614

Convened: 27 October 1614, Paris
Dissolved: February–March 1615
Called by: Marie de' Medici, regent for Louis XIII
Composition: Deputies of the clergy, nobility, and Third Estate, voting by order
Significance: Last Estates General of the Ancien Régime before 1789

The Estates General of 1614 was a general assembly representing the French estates of the realm — clergy, nobility, and commoners (the Third Estate) — convened in Paris by the regent Marie de' Medici during the minority of Louis XIII. It is famous as the last meeting of the Estates General under the Ancien Régime before the fateful assembly of 1789.

Background

After the assassination of Henry IV in 1610, Marie de' Medici governed as regent for the nine-year-old Louis XIII. Her government faced restive princes of the blood, notably the Prince of Condé, who rebelled in 1614 and, by the Treaty of Sainte-Menehould (May 1614), extracted the promise that an Estates General would be called. Louis XIII was declared of age in October 1614, and the Estates opened on 27 October 1614 in the Hôtel de Bourbon in Paris, with roughly 470 deputies: some 140 for the clergy, 130 for the nobility, and nearly 190 for the Third Estate.

Proceedings

Each order drew up its book of grievances (cahier de doléances). The Estates are remembered for several episodes:

  • The Third Estate's "fundamental law" article: the Third proposed declaring it a fundamental law that no foreign power — meaning the papacy — could depose the king or absolve subjects of obedience, a Gallican article fiercely opposed by the clergy, led by Cardinal du Perron, and quietly dropped by the regent.
  • Clashes over privilege: the Third demanded the abolition of venality of office (the paulette), tax reform, and an end to noble pensions, while the nobility demanded that commoners be barred from ennobling offices and attacked the Third's presumption — at one point a noble spokesman compared the orders to three brothers, provoking the Third's famous reply that the commoners were not the family's lackeys.
  • Young Richelieu: the 29-year-old Bishop of Luçon, Armand de Richelieu, served as spokesman for the clergy at the closing session in February 1615, making his first notable appearance on the national stage.

Outcome and legacy

The regent, alarmed by the assembly's assertiveness and unwilling to enact its reforms, dissolved the Estates in early 1615 after accepting the cahiers with vague promises; almost none of their demands were implemented. No Estates General was called again for 174 years, and the institution's grievances, procedures, and conflicts — voting by order versus by head, and the Third Estate's claims — foreshadowed the opening crisis of the French Revolution when the Estates General of 1789 convened at Versailles.
