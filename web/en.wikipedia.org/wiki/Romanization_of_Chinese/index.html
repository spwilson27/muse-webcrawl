Romanization of Chinese

The romanization of Chinese is the use of the Latin alphabet to write the Chinese language, whose native script is logographic. Numerous systems have been devised since the 16th century for missionary work, scholarship, language teaching, telegraphy, indexing and international communication. The dominant system today is Hanyu Pinyin, adopted by the People's Republic of China in the 1950s and now the international standard (ISO 7098).

History

The first systematic romanizations were created by Jesuit missionaries: Matteo Ricci and Nicolas Trigault devised schemes in the early 17th century, and Ricci's dictionary marked tones. In the 19th century Thomas Wade's system, modified by Herbert Giles, became the standard Wade–Giles romanization used in English-language scholarship. Missionaries also devised romanizations for regional varieties, such as Pe̍h-ōe-jī for Southern Min in Fujian and Taiwan.

Uses and issues

Romanization serves learners, dictionaries, library cataloguing, place names, passports and computer input methods (typing pinyin to produce characters is the most common Chinese input method). Because Chinese has many homophones and romanization omits or marks tones inconsistently, romanized text is generally not used as a replacement for characters, though reformers in the 20th century periodically proposed full Latinization of the language.
