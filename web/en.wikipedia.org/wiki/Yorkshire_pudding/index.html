Yorkshire pudding

Type: Savoury pudding
Place of origin: Yorkshire, England
Main ingredients: Eggs, flour, milk or water
Traditionally served: With roast beef and gravy

A Yorkshire pudding is a baked pudding made from a batter of eggs, flour, and milk or water. A common British side dish, it is a versatile food in several ways. The typical Yorkshire pudding is distinguished from other batter puddings by its crisp, risen, hollow form, achieved by pouring batter into very hot fat in a preheated pan. It is traditionally served with roast beef as part of the British Sunday roast, though it is also eaten with other meats and, in Yorkshire, sometimes as a starter with onion gravy.

History

Batter puddings cooked under roasting meat were described in print in 1737, and the first recipe explicitly named for Yorkshire appeared in Hannah Glasse's The Art of Cookery (1747). The dish exploited the fat dripping from roast meat — originally it was cooked beneath the joint as it turned on a spit — stretching costly meat with cheap batter for poorer families. By the Victorian era the pudding had become firmly linked with roast beef as an emblematic English pairing.

Preparation and variations

The batter rests before baking and is cooked at high temperature in smoking-hot beef dripping, lard, or oil, rising around the edges into a bowl shape ideal for holding gravy. Individual puddings are baked in muffin or popover tins, while a traditional Yorkshire version may be one large pudding cut into squares. The same batter, baked in a large pan with sausages, makes toad in the hole. A 2008 ruling by the Royal Society of Chemistry held that a Yorkshire pudding is not a true Yorkshire pudding unless it is at least four inches (10 cm) tall — a criterion offered in playful response to a complaint from an expatriate cook.
