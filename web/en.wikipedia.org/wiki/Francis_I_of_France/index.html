Francis I (French: François Ier; 12 September 1494 – 31 March 1547) was King of France from 1515 until his death in 1547. He was the son of Charles, Count of Angoulême, and Louise of Savoy. He succeeded his first cousin once removed and father-in-law, Louis XII, who died without a legitimate son.

A prodigious patron of the arts, he promoted the French Renaissance, inviting Leonardo da Vinci to France and building or enlarging palaces including Chambord and Fontainebleau. His reign saw the exploration of the New World by Verrazzano and Cartier, the standardisation of French in administration, and long rivalry with the Habsburg emperor Charles V.

Wars with the Habsburgs

Francis opened his reign with victory at Marignano (1515), winning the Duchy of Milan. His candidacy for the imperial crown failed in 1519, and the ensuing Italian Wars with Charles V brought disaster at the Battle of Pavia (1525), where Francis was captured and held in Madrid. Released after signing the Treaty of Madrid and surrendering his sons as hostages, he repudiated the treaty and allied at times with the Ottoman Empire and German Protestant princes against the emperor — a scandal to many contemporaries. The celebrated meeting with Henry VIII of England at the Field of the Cloth of Gold (1520) had earlier failed to secure a durable English alliance.

Domestic rule and culture

The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) required French in legal documents and parish registers. Francis founded what became the Collège de France, built the royal library, and patronised architects and artists of the School of Fontainebleau. His later years were marked by growing religious tension and persecution of Protestants, foreshadowing the French Wars of Religion.
