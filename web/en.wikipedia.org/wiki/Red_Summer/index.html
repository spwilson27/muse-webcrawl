Red Summer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Red Summer was a period in mid-1919 during which white supremacist terrorism and racial riots occurred in more than three dozen cities across the United States, as well as in one rural county. The term "Red Summer" was coined by the civil rights activist and NAACP official James Weldon Johnson, who had been organizing peaceful protests against racial violence that year. The bloodiest events took place in Chicago, Washington, D.C., and Elaine, Arkansas.

Background

The unrest followed the First World War. The Great Migration had brought hundreds of thousands of African Americans from the rural South to northern industrial cities, where they competed with white workers, including returning servicemen and recent immigrants, for jobs and housing. Black veterans returned from the war expecting better treatment after their service, while labor unrest, fears of communism after the Russian Revolution (the First Red Scare), and a resurgent Ku Klux Klan heightened tensions.

Major events

Washington, D.C.

In July 1919, rumors of an alleged assault by a Black man on a white woman triggered days of violence in which white mobs, including servicemen, attacked Black residents. Black Washingtonians armed themselves and fought back; the violence ended after several days, with troops deployed and heavy rain helping to disperse the crowds.

Chicago

The Chicago race riot began on 27 July 1919, after a Black teenager, Eugene Williams, drifted across the unofficial racial boundary in Lake Michigan near a segregated beach and drowned after being struck by a stone thrown by a white man; a white police officer refused to arrest the suspect. Fighting spread across the city for days, leaving 38 people dead — 23 Black and 15 white — 537 injured, and roughly 1,000 Black families homeless from arson.

Elaine, Arkansas

In the autumn of 1919, Black sharecroppers in Phillips County, Arkansas, organized a union, the Progressive Farmers and Household Union of America, to demand fair settlements for their cotton crops. On 30 September, a confrontation at a church meeting in Elaine escalated into violence. White posses and federal troops killed large numbers of Black residents; estimates of Black deaths range from scores to several hundred, while five white men died. Twelve Black men were sentenced to death in hasty trials; their cases eventually reached the U.S. Supreme Court, which in Moore v. Dempsey (1923) overturned the convictions on due process grounds.

Aftermath

The Red Summer marked a shift toward organized Black resistance to racial violence, sometimes described as the emergence of the "New Negro". The NAACP grew in membership and prominence, publishing reports on the violence and campaigning for a federal anti-lynching law. Although anti-lynching legislation passed the House of Representatives in 1922, it was blocked in the Senate.
