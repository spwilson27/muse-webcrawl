Low-pressure area

A low-pressure area, low area or low is a region where the atmospheric pressure at sea level is below that of surrounding locations. Low-pressure systems form under areas of wind divergence in the upper troposphere, which cause air to rise; the rising air cools and its water vapour condenses, so lows are associated with cloudy, wet and windy weather.

Circulation

Because of the Coriolis effect, winds around a low-pressure area circulate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, spiralling slightly inward at the surface due to friction. A low-pressure system whose winds reach storm intensity is classed as a cyclone; types include extratropical cyclones along the polar front, tropical cyclones over warm oceans, subtropical and polar lows, and thermal lows over heated land in summer.

Formation and effects

Mid-latitude lows typically develop through cyclogenesis on temperature gradients, often downstream of mountain ranges (lee cyclogenesis). The lowest sea-level pressure reliably recorded was 870 hPa in the eye of Typhoon Tip (1979). Persistent semi-permanent lows, such as the Icelandic Low and Aleutian Low, anchor regional storm tracks, while the equatorial trough (ITCZ) is a near-permanent belt of low pressure around the tropics.
