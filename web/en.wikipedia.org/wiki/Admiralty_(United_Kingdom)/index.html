Admiralty (United Kingdom)

The Admiralty was a department of the Government of the United Kingdom responsible for the command of the Royal Navy until 1964. Originally the office of a single person, the Lord High Admiral, its functions were from the early 17th century executed by a board — the Lords Commissioners of the Admiralty — headed politically by the First Lord of the Admiralty. Historically, it encompassed naval operations, shipbuilding, and the civil administration of the navy, and worked alongside the separate Navy Board, which handled materiel and finance until its abolition in 1832.

History

The office of Admiral of England dates to the late medieval period; Henry VIII established the Council of the Marine (later the Navy Board) in 1546 to administer shipbuilding and supply while the Lord High Admiral retained operational command. After 1628 the office was almost permanently "in commission", exercised by a board. In 1832, Sir James Graham merged the Navy Board into the Admiralty, creating the modern department. The Admiralty's political head, the First Lord, sat in the Cabinet; its professional head was the First Sea Lord.

Organisation

By the 20th century the Board of Admiralty included the First Lord, the First Sea Lord (Chief of the Naval Staff), other Sea Lords responsible for personnel, materiel, and the fleet, and a permanent civil service staff. The department controlled naval intelligence, the Hydrographic Office, the Royal Dockyards, and world-wide naval commands. During the two world wars the Admiralty directed convoy operations and anti-submarine warfare from Whitehall and its citadel in London.

Abolition

On 1 April 1964 the Admiralty was abolished and its functions absorbed into a unified Ministry of Defence, with an Admiralty Board surviving as a sub-committee of the Defence Council chaired by the Secretary of State for Defence. The historic Admiralty buildings in Whitehall, including the Admiralty Arch at The Mall, remain landmarks associated with the department.
