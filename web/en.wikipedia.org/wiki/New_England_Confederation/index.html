The New England Confederation, formally the United Colonies of New England, was a short-lived military alliance of the English colonies of Massachusetts Bay, Plymouth, Connecticut, and New Haven, formed in May 1643. It was created primarily for mutual defence against Native American nations — the threat of the Narragansett and the influence of the Mohawk — and against the Dutch and French colonies that bordered New England, at a time when the English Civil War meant no help could be expected from the mother country.

Structure and history

Under its articles, each colony retained its own government but sent two commissioners to an annual meeting that could act on matters of war, relations with Native peoples, the return of fugitive servants and criminals, and inter-colonial disputes, with six of the eight commissioners required for a binding decision. Rhode Island and Maine were excluded, largely because of Massachusetts's religious objections to Rhode Island's toleration.

The confederation coordinated during King Philip's War (1675–76), the bloodiest conflict of the colonial era relative to population, but its authority steadily weakened: Massachusetts repeatedly defied it, refusing quotas and ignoring its rulings, and the absorption of New Haven into Connecticut in 1664 reduced it in practice. It formally lapsed when Massachusetts's charter was revoked in 1684. Although weak in practice, the United Colonies are often cited as an early precedent for American federal union, a model later echoed in the Albany Plan and the Articles of Confederation.
