Gaelic music

Gaelic music is the traditional music of the Gaelic-speaking peoples of Ireland, Scotland and the Isle of Man, together with their diaspora communities, notably in Nova Scotia, Canada. It encompasses a wide range of vocal and instrumental forms sung in Irish Gaelic, Scottish Gaelic or Manx, many of them transmitted orally over centuries.

Vocal traditions

In Ireland, the most revered form is sean-nós ("old style") singing: highly ornamented, unaccompanied solo song, usually on themes of love, loss, emigration and local events, performed with a free rhythm that follows the words. The Scottish tradition includes puirt à beul ("mouth music"), rhythmic songs used to accompany dancing when instruments were unavailable, work songs such as waulking songs sung by women fulling cloth, and the distinctive slow, unaccompanied Gaelic psalm singing of Presbyterian congregations in the Hebrides, in which a precentor lines out each phrase and the congregation responds with elaborate individual ornamentation.

Instrumental music

The classical music of the Great Highland bagpipe, ceòl mòr or pibroch, consists of elaborate theme-and-variation compositions. Harp music — the cláirseach in Ireland and clarsach in Scotland — was the art music of Gaelic courts until the decline of the clan and bardic orders in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Dance music on fiddle, pipes and accordion, organised in reels, jigs and strathspeys, forms the core of the shared Irish and Scottish traditional repertoire.

Revival

Gaelic song and music were collected and published from the eighteenth century onward, and the twentieth and twenty-first centuries have seen flourishing festivals — the Irish fleadhanna, the Scottish Royal National Mòd and the Celtic Colours festival in Cape Breton — alongside contemporary bands that fuse Gaelic material with modern styles.
