Issai Schur

Born: 10 January 1875, Mogilev, Russian Empire (now Belarus)

Died: 10 January 1941 (aged 66), Tel Aviv, Mandatory Palestine

Nationality: German

Institutions: University of Berlin; University of Bonn

Doctoral advisor: Ferdinand Georg Frobenius

Known for: Schur's lemma; Schur decomposition; Schur polynomials

Issai Schur (10 January 1875 – 10 January 1941) was a German mathematician who worked mostly in Berlin and made fundamental contributions to representation theory, group theory, number theory, combinatorics and analysis. He was a student of Ferdinand Georg Frobenius and, with him, a founder of the representation theory of finite groups.

Life

Schur was born in Mogilev in the Russian Empire into a Jewish family and grew up in Riga, where he became fluent in German. He studied in Berlin, taking his doctorate in 1901 under Frobenius. After posts in Strasbourg and Bonn, he returned to Berlin in 1916 as successor to his teacher and built one of the leading algebraic schools in Germany, attracting students such as Richard Brauer, Alfred Brauer, Heinz Hopf's collaborator Heinz Hopf (in nearby Zurich), and many others. His lectures were renowned for their clarity.

After the Nazis came to power in 1933, Schur was progressively stripped of his rights under the anti-Jewish civil service laws: forced to resign from the Prussian Academy, suspended from teaching in 1935 and dismissed from his chair in 1939. He emigrated to Mandatory Palestine in 1939, impoverished, and died in Tel Aviv on his 66th birthday in 1941.

Work

Schur's name is attached to a large body of fundamental results. Schur's lemma (1905) states that a linear map commuting with all operators of an irreducible group representation must be a scalar multiple of the identity (over an algebraically closed field), a cornerstone of representation theory. His doctoral and postdoctoral work established the characters of the symmetric group and introduced the Schur polynomials (symmetric polynomials forming a basis of symmetric functions) and the Schur–Weyl duality between representations of the general linear and symmetric groups.

In matrix analysis, the Schur decomposition shows that any complex matrix is unitarily similar to an upper triangular matrix. In combinatorics and number theory, Schur's theorem states that for any finite colouring of the positive integers there is a monochromatic solution to x + y = z — an early landmark of Ramsey theory — and he proved the Schur property in functional analysis and results on integral equations and analytic functions (the Schur algorithm and Schur-convex functions also bear his name). The Journal of Algebra's naming of the Schur multiplier in group cohomology commemorates his work on projective representations.
