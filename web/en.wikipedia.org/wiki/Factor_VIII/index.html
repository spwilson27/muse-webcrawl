Factor VIII

Coagulation factor VIII is an essential blood-clotting protein. It is encoded by the F8 gene on the X chromosome. In the circulation it travels bound to von Willebrand factor, which protects it from premature degradation.

Function

Upon injury, factor VIII is activated (factor VIIIa) by thrombin and acts as a cofactor for factor IXa in the intrinsic tenase complex, greatly accelerating the activation of factor X on phospholipid surfaces in the presence of calcium, leading to thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation.

Deficiency: haemophilia A

Deficiency or dysfunction of factor VIII causes haemophilia A, an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder characterised by spontaneous and prolonged bleeding, especially into joints and muscles. Severity correlates with residual factor activity. Treatment uses plasma-derived or recombinant factor VIII concentrates, extended half-life products, the bispecific antibody emicizumab (which mimics VIIIa's bridging function), and gene therapy (valoctocogene roxaparvovec). A serious complication is the development of inhibitory alloantibodies against infused factor VIII.

Excess

Elevated factor VIII levels are an established independent risk factor for venous thrombosis. Factor VIII is also an acute-phase reactant and rises in inflammation, pregnancy, and with age.
