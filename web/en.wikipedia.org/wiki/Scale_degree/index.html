Scale degree

Degrees of the C major scale, with names and solfège syllables

In music theory, a scale degree is the position of a particular note on a scale relative to the tonic, the first and main note of the scale from which each octave is assumed to begin. Degrees are useful for indicating the size of intervals and chords and whether an interval is major or minor.

Major and minor scales

In the diatonic scales, each degree has a traditional name:

DegreeNameSolfègeMeaning
1TonicdoKey note, point of rest
2SupertonicreAbove the tonic
3MediantmiMidway between tonic and dominant
4SubdominantfaBelow the dominant / lower dominant
5DominantsolSecond most important degree
6SubmediantlaMidway between subdominant and upper tonic
7Leading note (leading tone)ti/siA semitone below the tonic in major
8 (1)Tonic (octave)doOctave duplication of the tonic

In the natural minor scale, the seventh degree a whole tone below the tonic is called the subtonic; when raised by a semitone to create a leading tone (as in harmonic minor), it takes the leading-note name.

Notation

Scale degrees are often written with a caret (circumflex) over the numeral, or with Roman numerals when analysing chord functions built on each degree: I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii° in major keys, where uppercase denotes major chords, lowercase minor chords, and ° a diminished chord.

Altered degrees

In jazz and other idioms, degrees may be chromatically altered — for instance a flattened ninth (♭9) above a chord root. The degree system, combined with qualifiers such as "sharp" and "flat", allows musicians to describe melodies and chord extensions independent of any particular key, a flexibility exploited in the Nashville Number System used by session musicians.
