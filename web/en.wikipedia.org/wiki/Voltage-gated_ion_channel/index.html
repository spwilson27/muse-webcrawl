Voltage-gated ion channel

Voltage-gated ion channels are a class of transmembrane proteins that form ion channels activated by changes in the electrical membrane potential near the channel. The membrane potential alters the conformation of the channel proteins, opening or closing the pore through which specific ions — such as sodium, potassium, calcium or chloride — flow.

Structure

Voltage-gated channels typically contain a voltage-sensing domain, built around a transmembrane segment (S4) rich in positively charged arginine residues that move in response to the electric field, and a pore domain that lines the ion conduction pathway and contains the selectivity filter. In sodium and calcium channels a single long polypeptide contains four homologous repeats; potassium channels assemble as tetramers of four subunits.

Function

These channels are the basis of electrical excitability. Voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels generate and propagate action potentials in neurons and muscle. Voltage-gated calcium channels trigger neurotransmitter release, hormone secretion and muscle contraction, coupling excitation to cellular responses.

States

Channels cycle between closed (resting), open, and inactivated states. After opening, many sodium channels rapidly inactivate via a "ball and chain" or hinged-lid mechanism, producing the refractory period that enforces one-way propagation of action potentials.

Clinical significance

Mutations in voltage-gated channel genes cause channelopathies, including epilepsy, periodic paralysis, long QT syndrome and chronic pain syndromes. The channels are also targets of local anaesthetics, anticonvulsants, antiarrhythmic drugs and many animal toxins, such as tetrodotoxin and conotoxins.
