Impact litigation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image placeholder: Supreme Court building]

Impact litigation (also called strategic litigation or test-case litigation) is the practice of bringing carefully selected lawsuits not primarily to win compensation for an individual client, but to establish precedent and produce broad social, political or environmental change. Cases are chosen for sympathetic plaintiffs, favourable facts and jurisdictions, and are typically supported by public-interest organisations.

History

The archetype is the NAACP Legal Defense Fund's campaign, directed by Charles Hamilton Houston and Thurgood Marshall, that chipped away at school segregation through a sequence of graduate-school cases before culminating in Brown v. Board of Education (1954). Similar sustained campaigns have been waged by the ACLU, Lambda Legal and the Institute for Justice, and by conservative legal movements whose long-term strategies reshaped jurisprudence on federalism, gun rights and administrative law — the latter often associated with the Federalist Society.

Methods and criticism

Impact litigators combine case selection with amicus briefs, media strategy and follow-on enforcement suits. Critics argue that litigation is an anti-democratic route to policy change, can provoke backlash (as in debates over Roe v. Wade), and may subordinate individual clients' interests to an organisation's agenda; defenders reply that courts exist precisely to protect minorities against majoritarian politics.
