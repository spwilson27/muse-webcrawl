Entire sanctification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Entire sanctification, also known as Christian perfection, perfect love, the second blessing, or the second work of grace, is a doctrine in Methodist theology (and related holiness traditions) holding that a Christian can, in this life, be cleansed from original sin and filled with love for God and neighbor through a distinct, instantaneous work of the Holy Spirit subsequent to regeneration.

John Wesley's teaching

John Wesley, founder of Methodism, taught the doctrine extensively, notably in his treatise A Plain Account of Christian Perfection (1766). Wesley defined Christian perfection as "pure love filling the heart, and governing all the words and actions", while denying that it implied absolute perfection, freedom from mistakes, ignorance, or temptation. He held it to be normally received by faith in a moment, though preceded and followed by gradual growth in grace.

Holiness movement

In the 19th century, entire sanctification became the central teaching of the holiness movement, promoted by figures such as Phoebe Palmer, whose "altar theology" emphasized an immediate, faith-claimed experience. The doctrine spread through camp meetings and gave rise to numerous holiness denominations, and it influenced the emergence of Pentecostalism in the early 20th century.

Criticism

Other traditions, including much of Reformed and Lutheran theology, reject the doctrine, holding that sanctification is progressive throughout life and that sin remains in the believer until death. Critics have also questioned the doctrine on the basis of 1 John 1:8: "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves."
