Process music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Stylistic originsMinimalism, experimental music, systems music
Cultural origins1960s, United States
Key figuresSteve Reich, Terry Riley, La Monte Young

Process music is music that arises from a process, and in which the resulting sounds are the product of a compositional procedure that is audible to the listener. The term was coined by composer Steve Reich in his 1968 essay "Music as a Gradual Process", in which he wrote: "I am interested in perceptible processes. I want to be able to hear the process happening throughout the sounding music."

In process music, the means of producing the music and the music itself are identical: a simple rule or system, applied consistently, generates the entire work. Examples include phasing (two identical patterns played at slightly different speeds, as in Reich's Piano Phase, 1967), additive processes (patterns lengthened note by note, as in Drumming, 1971), and tape-loop systems.

Characteristics

Reich distinguished his approach from hidden compositional structures, such as serialism, in which the generating process cannot be heard. In process music, by contrast, "the process and the sounding music are one and the same". Related ideas appear in British "systems music" of the 1970s (Michael Nyman, Gavin Bryars) and in generative music, where systems devised by the composer produce the work, as in Brian Eno's ambient experiments.

Notable works

  • Terry Riley — In C (1964)
  • Philip Glass — additive-process works of the late 1960s
