The Saxon Wars were a series of campaigns and insurrections spanning more than thirty years, from 772 to 804, led by the Frankish king Charlemagne against the Saxons, a Germanic people inhabiting the region of modern north-western Germany. The wars ended with the conquest and incorporation of Saxony into the Frankish Empire and the forced conversion of the Saxons to Christianity.

Background

The Saxons were a confederation of tribes divided into groups including the Westphalians, Eastphalians, Engrians, and Nordalbingians. They practised Germanic paganism, centred on sacred sites such as the Irminsul, a great pillar venerated as a world tree. Border raids between Franks and Saxons had occurred for generations.

Course of the wars

In 772 Charlemagne invaded Saxony and destroyed the Irminsul near Eresburg. Initial submissions and baptisms followed, but Saxon resistance repeatedly revived whenever Frankish armies withdrew. In 782, after a Saxon victory over a Frankish force at the Süntel mountains, Charlemagne is said by the Royal Frankish Annals to have ordered the execution of 4,500 Saxon prisoners at Verden — the Massacre of Verden — though the figure is debated by historians.

The Saxon leader Widukind sustained resistance for years, fleeing at one point to the Danes. In 785 Widukind submitted and accepted baptism, with Charlemagne as his godfather, but revolts continued, notably in 793–804 in the north. Charlemagne responded with harsh measures including mass deportations of Saxons and their replacement by Frankish and Abodrite settlers.

Capitulary and conversion

The Capitulatio de partibus Saxoniae imposed severe penalties, including death, for refusing baptism, practising pagan rites, or burning the dead. A later, milder capitulary of 797 moderated these provisions. Missionary work, the founding of bishoprics such as Bremen, Münster, Osnabrück, and Paderborn, and the establishment of monasteries gradually Christianised the region.

Outcome

By 804 the conquest was complete. Saxony became a stem duchy within the Frankish realm, and within a century Saxon dukes — the Ottonians — would themselves become kings and emperors. Widukind was later romanticised as a Saxon national hero, while Charlemagne acquired the epithet "slaughterer of the Saxons" among critics, a legacy still debated in modern historiography.
