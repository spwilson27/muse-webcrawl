The Hopi Reservation (Hopi: Hopituskwa) is a Native American reservation for the Hopi and Arizona Tewa people, surrounded entirely by the Navajo Nation, in Navajo and Coconino counties in northeastern Arizona. The reservation occupies a high desert plateau crossed by three great mesas — First Mesa, Second Mesa, and Third Mesa — on and around which the Hopi villages stand.

Geography and communities

The reservation covers roughly 2,532 square miles (6,557 km²). Its principal communities include Kykotsmovi Village, the seat of tribal government, as well as the historic villages of Walpi, Hano, Sichomovi, Shongopovi, Mishongnovi, Shipaulovi, Hotevilla, Bacavi, Moenkopi, and Oraibi. Oraibi, founded around 1100 AD, is often described as one of the oldest continuously inhabited settlements in North America. The Moenkopi District, a small western exclave near Tuba City, is separated from the main reservation by Navajo land.

History

The reservation was established by executive order of President Chester A. Arthur in 1882. Its boundaries were a longstanding source of conflict with the surrounding Navajo Nation, a dispute known as the Navajo–Hopi land dispute. Federal legislation in 1974 partitioned the jointly used lands, and a 1996 accommodation agreement addressed the position of Navajo families remaining on Hopi partitioned land.

Culture and economy

The Hopi are renowned for dry farming of corn, beans, and squash adapted to the arid plateau, and for crafts including kachina carving, pottery, basketry, silver overlay jewelry, and textiles. The Hopi language, a Uto-Aztecan language, remains widely spoken. Tourism, tribal government, and the Hopi Cultural Center on Second Mesa form important parts of the economy.
