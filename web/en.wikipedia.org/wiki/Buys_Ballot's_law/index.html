Buys Ballot's law

In meteorology, Buys Ballot's law states that if a person stands with their back to the wind in the Northern Hemisphere, the atmospheric pressure will be low on their left hand and high on their right hand. This is because wind travels counterclockwise around a low-pressure system in the Northern Hemisphere. In the Southern Hemisphere the rule is reversed: with one's back to the wind, low pressure lies to the right.

Formulation

The law is named for the Dutch meteorologist C. H. D. Buys Ballot, who published it in 1857 (in a note to the Comptes rendus of the French Academy of Sciences), having derived it empirically from Dutch weather observations. A closely related rule had been stated earlier by the American meteorologists James Pollard Espy and William C. Redfield, and it follows theoretically from the geostrophic balance and the work of William Ferrel: air moving under a pressure gradient is deflected by the Coriolis effect, so that in the Northern Hemisphere the wind blows with low pressure on its left.

A practical mariner's version runs: "Face the wind, and low pressure is on your right" (in the Northern Hemisphere), which allows sailors to locate the centre of a storm system and choose a course away from its dangerous semicircle.

Validity

The law applies best to winds above the friction layer, a few hundred metres above the surface; near the ground, friction turns the wind somewhat across the isobars towards low pressure, so the low centre is slightly forward of the observer's left hand. It remains a standard rule of thumb in seamanship and aviation.
