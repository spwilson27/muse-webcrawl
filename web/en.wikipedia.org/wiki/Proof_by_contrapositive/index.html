Proof by contrapositive

Proof by contrapositive is a method of mathematical proof in which a conditional statement "if P, then Q" is proved by instead proving its contrapositive, "if not Q, then not P". Because an implication and its contrapositive are logically equivalent — they have identical truth tables — establishing the contrapositive establishes the original statement.

Method

To prove "if P then Q" by contraposition, one assumes ¬Q (the negation of the conclusion) and derives ¬P (the negation of the hypothesis). This is often easier when the negated statements are more concrete or easier to work with than the originals; for example, proving "if n² is even, then n is even" is awkward directly, but the contrapositive — "if n is odd, then n² is odd" — follows immediately by squaring 2k + 1.

Comparison with other methods

Proof by contrapositive is distinct from proof by contradiction, although the two are frequently confused. In a proof by contradiction of P → Q, one assumes P and ¬Q together and derives any contradiction; a proof by contrapositive specifically derives ¬P from ¬Q, and can be recast as a direct proof of the contrapositive. The contrapositive should not be confused with the converse (Q → P) or the inverse (¬P → ¬Q), neither of which is equivalent to the original statement.

Validity

The equivalence of an implication with its contrapositive is a theorem of classical logic, but fails in intuitionistic logic, where contraposition holds in only one direction; accordingly, proofs by contrapositive are not accepted in constructive mathematics without qualification.
