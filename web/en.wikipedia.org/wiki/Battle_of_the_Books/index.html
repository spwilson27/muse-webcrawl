Battle of the Books

The Battle of the Books is a short prose satire written by Jonathan Swift and published in 1704 as part of A Tale of a Tub. It is a humorous account of a literal battle fought between books in the King's Library at St James's Palace, and it gave the phrase "battle of the books" to the wider cultural debate known as the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns.

Background

The Quarrel began in France in the late 1680s, when Charles Perrault argued that modern writers had surpassed the authors of classical antiquity. In England the dispute was taken up by Sir William Temple, Swift's patron, whose essay Of Ancient and Modern Learning (1690) defended the ancients. Temple praised the Epistles of Phalaris as the finest work of antiquity, but the classical scholar Richard Bentley demonstrated that the letters were a later forgery. William Wotton's Reflections upon Ancient and Modern Learning (1694) supported the moderns, and Swift — then Temple's secretary at Moor Park — entered the fray on his patron's side.

Summary

In Swift's satire, the books in the royal library come to life at night and fall to fighting. The Moderns, led by figures such as Dryden and Descartes, challenge the Ancients, among them Homer, Virgil, Aristotle and Plato. Individual episodes parody epic combat: Dryden, wearing a helmet far too large for his head, confronts Virgil; Descartes is destroyed by Aristotle; and the modern critic Bentley is depicted as a clumsy, malignant combatant. The most famous episode is the fable of the spider and the bee: the spider, representing the Moderns, spins its web out of its own entrails, while the bee, representing the Ancients, ranges over nature and produces honey and wax — "sweetness and light," a phrase Swift's satire later made famous through Matthew Arnold.

Significance

The work is an early example of Swift's mock-heroic manner and of his lifelong suspicion of intellectual pride and modern "projectors." Although Swift sided with the Ancients, the satire is even-handed enough that its fable has been read as an ambivalent verdict on both camps. The phrase "battle of the books" is still used for any controversy over the relative value of classical and contemporary learning.
