Energy security

Energy security is the association between national security and the availability of natural resources for energy consumption. Access to easily produced energy has become essential for the efficient functioning of modern economies, and the uneven distribution of energy supplies among countries has led to significant vulnerabilities. Rapidly growing economies such as India and China, along with ongoing climate change and environmental concerns, have made energy security a central issue in international politics.

Definition and dimensions

The International Energy Agency (IEA) defines energy security as "the uninterrupted availability of energy sources at an affordable price." Energy security differs from the narrower concept of energy independence: a country need not produce all its own energy to be secure, provided its supplies are diversified and resilient to disruption. Energy security is often analysed along several dimensions: the geographic diversification of supply sources, the diversity of energy types in the national energy mix, the reliability of supply routes and infrastructure, strategic reserves, and the affordability and price stability of energy for consumers and industry.

Threats to energy security

Political instability in producing regions, manipulation of energy supplies by governments or armed groups, competition over energy sources, attacks on supply infrastructure such as pipelines and shipping lanes, and natural disasters can all threaten energy security. Accidents and terrorism have also influenced energy policy. Major historical shocks include the 1973 and 1979 oil crises, when embargoes and the Iranian Revolution sharply reduced supply and raised prices worldwide.

Measures and policies

Governments pursue energy security through diversification of supply sources and transport routes, development of domestic energy resources, maintenance of strategic petroleum reserves, international agreements and organisations such as the IEA (founded in 1974 in response to the first oil crisis), and investment in renewable energy and energy efficiency. In the long term, the transition to low-carbon energy sources such as wind, solar, hydroelectric and nuclear power is increasingly framed as a way to reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels, although it creates new dependencies on critical minerals and supply chains for batteries and other technologies.
