Cultural turn
Period1970s–1990s
FieldsHistory, sociology, geography, literary studies, anthropology
Key figuresClifford Geertz, Stuart Hall, Pierre Bourdieu, Lynn Hunt

The cultural turn is a movement in the humanities and social sciences, emerging in the 1970s and flourishing in the 1980s and 1990s, in which "culture" became a central concept of analysis. Scholars across history, sociology, geography, and literary studies turned from material and structural explanations toward meaning, language, symbols, discourse, identity, and representation.

Influences included anthropologist Clifford Geertz's interpretive approach to culture as a "web of significance", the Birmingham school of cultural studies associated with Stuart Hall, post-structuralism, and feminist and postcolonial theory. In history, the cultural turn produced new attention to ritual, memory, gender, and everyday life; Lynn Hunt's 1989 edited volume The New Cultural History is often taken as its manifesto.

Criticism

Critics charged the cultural turn with neglecting material conditions, political economy, and quantitative methods, and later "turns" — to materiality, practice, and the global — were partly framed as reactions against it.
