Challenger (politics)

In politics, a challenger is a candidate who seeks to win an elected office currently held by another person, known as the incumbent. Races between an incumbent and a challenger are typically contrasted with open seat contests, in which no incumbent is running. Challengers may first have to win a primary election against candidates of their own party before facing the incumbent in the general election.

The incumbency advantage

Challengers generally face significant disadvantages. Incumbents benefit from greater name recognition, an established record of constituent service, easier access to campaign finance, and — in many systems — the franking privilege and favourable district boundaries drawn through gerrymandering. In elections to the United States House of Representatives, incumbents are re-elected in the overwhelming majority of races, often with well over 90 per cent of incumbents who seek another term succeeding.

When challengers win

Challengers are most likely to succeed under identifiable conditions:

  • Scandal or corruption affecting the incumbent personally;
  • Economic downturns, for which voters punish the officeholder's party;
  • "Wave elections" driven by national dissatisfaction, such as the 1994, 2006, 2010 and 2018 United States midterm elections;
  • Redistricting, which can place an incumbent before many unfamiliar voters;
  • Strong challenger quality — challengers who have previously held elected office raise more money and win more often than political novices.

Because defeating incumbents is difficult, political parties concentrate recruitment and spending on a limited number of races they judge competitive, while most challengers run underfunded, symbolic campaigns. A challenger who performs unexpectedly well may nevertheless build the reputation and organisation needed to win a later contest.
