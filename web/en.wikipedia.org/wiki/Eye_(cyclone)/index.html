Eye (cyclone)

The eye is a region of mostly calm weather at the centre of a strong tropical cyclone. The eye of a storm is a roughly circular area, typically 30–65 kilometres (19–40 miles) in diameter. It is surrounded by the eyewall, a ring of towering thunderstorms where the most severe weather and highest winds of the cyclone occur. The cyclone's lowest barometric pressure occurs in the eye and can be as much as 15 percent lower than the pressure outside the storm.

In strong tropical cyclones, the eye is characterized by light winds and clear skies, surrounded on all sides by a towering, symmetric eyewall. In weaker tropical cyclones, the eye is less well defined and can be covered by the central dense overcast, an area of high, thick clouds that show up brightly on satellite imagery. Weaker or disorganized storms may also feature an eyewall that does not completely encircle the eye or have an eye that features heavy rain.

Formation and detection

Eyes form as a storm intensifies and its fastest winds draw near the centre of rotation: conservation of angular momentum and the balance between inflow at the surface and subsidence (sinking air) within the eye clear the clouds from the centre. Eyes are routinely detected by weather satellites and by reconnaissance aircraft, which also measure the storm's central pressure. A distinct "stadium effect" is often visible from above, with the eyewall sloping outward with height like the walls of a sports stadium.

Eyewall replacement cycles

In intense cyclones, a secondary outer eyewall sometimes forms and gradually replaces the inner one. During an eyewall replacement cycle the storm typically weakens temporarily and its wind field expands, before re-intensifying with a larger eye. Such cycles are common in major hurricanes such as Katrina (2005) and Irma (2017).
