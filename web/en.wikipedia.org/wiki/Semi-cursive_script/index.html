Semi-cursive script

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Semi-cursive script (Chinese: 行書; pinyin: xíngshū) — one of the five major styles of Chinese calligraphy.

The semi-cursive script, also known as running script (Chinese: 行書; pinyin: xíngshū; Japanese: ぎょうしょ, gyōsho), is one of the five major styles of Chinese calligraphy. It is a style that stands between the formal regular script (kaishu) and the highly abbreviated cursive script (caoshu): characters remain individually legible and largely separated, but strokes are written with greater speed, some strokes are joined, and components are moderately simplified.

History

The style is traditionally said to have been developed in the late Han dynasty, with the calligrapher Liu Desheng often credited as an early exponent. It reached maturity during the Jin dynasty, above all in the work of Wang Xizhi (303–361), the "Sage of Calligraphy", whose Preface to the Poems Composed at the Orchid Pavilion (353 CE) is regarded as the finest example of semi-cursive writing.

Characteristics

In semi-cursive script the brush rarely leaves the paper between strokes within a character, producing flowing connections while each character remains a discrete, readable unit. Stroke order may be altered for fluency, and radicals may be written in running forms. Because it balances legibility with speed, xingshu became the most common style for everyday handwriting, personal letters, and drafts in pre-modern China, and it remains widely taught and practised today.

Use

Beyond personal writing, semi-cursive script appears in signage, book titles, inscriptions, and much modern East Asian typeface design, where "running" fonts imitate its connected, energetic strokes. In Japan, gyōsho is one of the three basic styles taught in calligraphy education, alongside kaisho (block) and sōsho (grass).
