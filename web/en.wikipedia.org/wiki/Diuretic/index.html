Diuretic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Sites of action of diuretic drugs in the nephron

A diuretic is any substance that promotes diuresis, the increased production of urine. This includes forced diuresis. A diuretic tablet is sometimes colloquially called a water tablet. There are several categories of diuretics. All diuretics increase the excretion of water from the body, through the kidneys, though each class does so in a distinct way.

Medical uses

In medicine, diuretics are used to treat heart failure, liver cirrhosis, hypertension, influenza, water poisoning, and certain kidney diseases. Some diuretics, such as acetazolamide, help to make the urine more alkaline and are helpful in increasing excretion of substances such as aspirin in cases of overdose or poisoning.

Types

  • Loop diuretics (e.g. furosemide) act on the ascending limb of the loop of Henle and are the most potent class.
  • Thiazide diuretics (e.g. hydrochlorothiazide) act on the distal convoluted tubule and are first-line agents for hypertension.
  • Potassium-sparing diuretics (e.g. spironolactone) reduce potassium excretion.
  • Osmotic diuretics (e.g. mannitol) increase urine output by osmotic effects.
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (e.g. acetazolamide) act on the proximal tubule.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include electrolyte disturbances such as hypokalemia or hyperkalemia, dehydration, low blood pressure, and metabolic alkalosis or acidosis depending on the class.
