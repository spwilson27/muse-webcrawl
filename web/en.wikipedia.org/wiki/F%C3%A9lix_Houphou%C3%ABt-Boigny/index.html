Félix Houphouët-Boigny (18 October 1905 – 7 December 1993), affectionately called Papa Houphouët or Le Vieux ("The Old One"), was the first president of Ivory Coast, serving from independence in 1960 until his death in 1993. A tribal chief's son, trained as a medical assistant and later a wealthy cocoa planter, he became one of the most influential African statesmen of the twentieth century and coined the term Françafrique for France's close postcolonial ties with its former African colonies.

Early life and political rise

Houphouët-Boigny was born in Yamoussoukro into a family of Baoulé chiefs. He studied medicine at the École de médecine de l'AOF in Dakar and worked as a medical assistant before inheriting and expanding family cocoa plantations. In 1944 he co-founded the African Agricultural Union (SAA), which campaigned against forced labor; elected to the French constituent assembly in 1945, he authored the 1946 law abolishing forced labor in French colonies, which bears his name. He founded the Democratic Party of Ivory Coast (PDCI) in 1946 and the inter-territorial Rassemblement Démocratique Africain (RDA). He served as a minister in several French governments in the 1950s — the first African to hold ministerial office in France.

Presidency

After Ivorian independence on 7 August 1960, Houphouët-Boigny became president and pursued pro-Western, capitalist policies with close cooperation with France. Under his leadership Ivory Coast experienced two decades of rapid growth — the "Ivorian miracle" — becoming the world's largest cocoa producer, driven by agricultural exports and foreign labor. He maintained a one-party state under the PDCI and a generally stable, if authoritarian, regime. In 1983 he moved the capital from Abidjan to his hometown of Yamoussoukro, where he built the Basilica of Our Lady of Peace, consecrated in 1990 and among the largest churches in the world.

Later years and legacy

Economic decline in the 1980s, driven by falling cocoa and coffee prices, strained his model; multiparty elections were permitted in 1990, which he won. He died in office on 7 December 1993 and was succeeded by Henri Konan Bédié. The Félix Houphouët-Boigny Peace Prize, established by UNESCO in 1989, honors contributions to peace. His legacy is debated: credited with stability and prosperity relative to neighbors, he is also criticized for personal rule, vast personal wealth, and the succession struggles that followed his death and eventually led to civil conflict in the 2000s.
