Inclusivism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Inclusivism is a theological position, found in discussions of the relationship among religions, holding that while one particular religion is the fullest or final truth, aspects of truth and even the possibility of salvation can be found within other religions. It is one of three classic positions in the theology of religions, alongside exclusivism (only one religion offers salvation) and pluralism (all major religions are equally valid paths).

Christian inclusivism

In Christian theology, inclusivism holds that a person may be saved through Christ even without explicit knowledge of the Christian gospel, if they respond in faith to the revelation available to them. The idea is associated with the theologian Karl Rahner, who coined the phrase "anonymous Christian" for such a person, and with C. S. Lewis, who suggested — including in a scene in The Last Battle — that sincere worship offered to a false god might be accepted by the true God. The Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra aetate and the dogmatic constitution Lumen gentium, which teaches that those who seek God with a sincere heart may attain salvation, are widely read as inclusivist.

Other traditions

Analogous positions appear elsewhere: Hindu thought often frames other faiths as partial apprehensions of the one reality, and some strands of Buddhism and Islam likewise allow that outsiders may attain liberation or paradise. Critics in each tradition argue that inclusivism either concedes too much to other faiths or patronizingly absorbs them.
