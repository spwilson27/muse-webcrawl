A cartridge, or round, is a type of pre-assembled firearm ammunition that packages a projectile (bullet, shot or slug), the propellant (usually smokeless powder or black powder) and an ignition system (primer) within a single case, typically of brass. Cartridges made the breech-loading firearm practical, because a complete round can be loaded in one motion, and they are the standard form of ammunition for modern rifles, pistols and shotguns.

Components

The case holds the other components and seals the chamber (obturation) on firing. The primer, struck by the firing pin, ignites the propellant, whose rapidly expanding gases drive the projectile down the barrel. In centre-fire cartridges the primer is a separate cup in the centre of the case head (Boxer or Berdan designs) and cases can be reloaded; in rimfire cartridges the priming compound is spun into the hollow rim, which the firing pin crushes.

History

Paper cartridges combining ball and powder date to the 16th century, but self-contained metallic cartridges arrived in the mid-19th century: Flobert's rimfire (1845), the pinfire of Lefaucheux, and the centre-fire systems of the 1860s. Metallic cartridges enabled repeating rifles such as the Henry and Winchester lever-actions and the revolvers of Colt and Smith & Wesson, and by the 1880s, with smokeless powder and jacketed spitzer bullets, the modern bottlenecked rifle cartridge had taken shape. Shotgun ammunition, in which the projectile is a charge of shot, is usually called a shell.
