Rib vault

Rib vault
TypeArchitectural vault
StylesRomanesque (late), Gothic
Early examplesDurham Cathedral (1093–1104); Saint-Denis; Lessay Abbey
ComponentsDiagonal ribs, transverse arches, formerets, webs, bosses

A rib vault or ribbed vault is an architectural feature for covering a wide space, such as a church nave, with a vaulted ceiling in which the surface is divided and supported by a framework of arched ribs. The webs (cells) between the ribs are filled with lighter masonry. Because the loads are concentrated along the ribs and down onto piers or columns, walls between supports could be opened up with large windows—one of the enabling technologies of Gothic architecture.

Development

Ribbed vaults appeared at the end of the Romanesque period. Durham Cathedral in England (begun 1093) has among the earliest surviving high rib vaults, including rib vaults over the nave from the early twelfth century, used alongside early pointed arches. Rib vaulting also developed in Lombardy and in Norman Sicily and Lessay in Normandy; scholars continue to debate the relative priority of these regions and a possible influence from Islamic architecture, where ribbed domes (as at Córdoba) existed earlier.

Structure and construction

Ribs were built first on wooden centering; the thin masonry webs were then laid between them, sometimes without full formwork. The standard early form is the quadripartite vault, in which two diagonal ribs divide a square bay into four cells. In sexpartite vaults (as at Caen and early Notre-Dame de Paris), an additional transverse rib divides the bay into six cells. Later Gothic architects added intermediate tiercerons, short linking liernes, and purely decorative ridge ribs, producing star vaults, net vaults and, in England, the spectacular fan vaults of the Perpendicular style (e.g. King's College Chapel, Cambridge, and the Henry VII Chapel at Westminster).

Functions

Although often said to "carry" the vault, modern structural analysis indicates the ribs and webs act together as a thin masonry shell; the ribs nonetheless stiffen the vault, simplify construction, articulate the bays visually, and provide a permanent armature that masks irregularities. Combined with the pointed arch and the flying buttress, the rib vault allowed the tall, luminous interiors characteristic of the Gothic cathedral.
