Zhao Ziyang (Chinese: 赵紫阳; 17 October 1919 – 17 January 2005) was a Chinese politician. He served as the Premier of the People's Republic of China from 1980 to 1987 and as General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) from 1987 to 1989. As a senior leader, Zhao was a leading architect of China's market-oriented economic reforms during the 1980s. He was purged from power in 1989 for his sympathetic stance toward the student protesters in Tiananmen Square and spent the remaining 15 years of his life under house arrest.

Early career

Zhao was born Zhao Xiuye in Hua County, Henan, the son of a wealthy landlord. He joined the Communist Youth League in 1932 and the CCP in 1938. He worked as a local party official during the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Chinese Civil War. After 1949 he rose through the party ranks in Guangdong province, where he promoted agricultural reforms. He was purged and persecuted during the Cultural Revolution but was rehabilitated in the 1970s.

Rise to power

As party secretary of Sichuan province from 1975, Zhao implemented market-oriented agricultural reforms — including the household responsibility system — that dramatically increased food production, inspiring a popular saying: "If you want to eat, look for Ziyang." In 1980 he was appointed Premier by Deng Xiaoping, overseeing national economic reform policies. In 1987 he succeeded Hu Yaobang as General Secretary of the CCP.

1989 and downfall

During the student-led protests of spring 1989, Zhao advocated a moderate, conciliatory approach and opposed the imposition of martial law. In the early morning of 19 May 1989 he made an emotional final public appearance in Tiananmen Square, urging the students to end their hunger strike. After the military crackdown of 4 June, Zhao was removed from all posts, accused of splitting the party, and placed under house arrest, where he remained until his death in 2005.

Legacy and memoir

Zhao's secretly recorded memoir was published posthumously in 2009 as Prisoner of the State: The Secret Journal of Premier Zhao Ziyang, offering a rare insider account of the power struggles at the top of the CCP. Within mainland China, references to Zhao remain heavily censored, though he is remembered by reformists as a symbol of political liberalization that accompanied economic reform.
