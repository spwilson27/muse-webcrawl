In image processing, a Gaussian blur (also known as Gaussian smoothing) is the result of blurring an image by a Gaussian function. It is a widely used effect in graphics software, typically to reduce image noise and reduce detail. The visual effect of this blurring technique is a smooth blur resembling that of viewing the image through a translucent screen, distinctly different from the effect of an out-of-focus lens.

Principle

Mathematically, applying a Gaussian blur to an image is the same as convolving the image with a Gaussian function. Each pixel's value is replaced by a weighted average of its neighborhood, with weights given by the two-dimensional Gaussian, falling off with distance from the center pixel. The width of the blur is controlled by the standard deviation (often called the radius). Because the two-dimensional Gaussian is separable, the blur can be computed as two one-dimensional passes (horizontal then vertical), greatly reducing computation.

Properties

Gaussian blurring is a low-pass filter: it attenuates high-frequency components such as fine detail and noise while preserving broad structures. Repeated application converges toward the solution of the heat equation, and Gaussians form the basis of scale-space theory in computer vision. Unlike a box blur, the Gaussian produces no ringing artifacts and gives a natural falloff.

Applications

Gaussian blur is used as a preprocessing step in edge detection algorithms such as the Canny edge detector, in difference-of-Gaussians approximations to the Laplacian, in simulating depth of field and bokeh, in drop shadows and glow effects in user interfaces, in privacy redaction, and in anti-aliasing. It is available in virtually all image editing programs, including Photoshop and GIMP, and is implemented in hardware-accelerated graphics APIs.
