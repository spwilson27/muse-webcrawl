Stone of Remembrance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Stone of Remembrance is one of the standard architectural features of Commonwealth War Graves Commission (CWGC) cemeteries, designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens and first used in cemeteries of the First World War. It is a large, altar-like rectangular block of stone, typically about 3.5 metres long, raised on steps, and is found in the commission's larger war cemeteries, generally those with 1,000 or more burials.

Design and inscription

Lutyens deliberately designed the stone to be abstract and ecumenical, avoiding overtly Christian symbolism so as to be acceptable to people of all faiths and none. It is inscribed with the words "THEIR NAME LIVETH FOR EVERMORE", a phrase chosen by Rudyard Kipling, whose son was killed in the war, from the biblical book of Ecclesiasticus. The stone's proportions were carefully worked out using subtle entasis-like geometry that Lutyens also applied to the Cenotaph in London.

Use

Stones of Remembrance stand in CWGC cemeteries across Europe, the Middle East, Asia, and elsewhere, alongside the Cross of Sacrifice designed by Sir Reginald Blomfield. Together with uniform headstones and horticultural landscaping, they form the recognizable visual identity of Commonwealth war cemeteries and serve as focal points for ceremonies of remembrance.
