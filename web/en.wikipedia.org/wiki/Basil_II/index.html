Basil II

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Basil II in imperial dress receiving submission, from the Madrid Skylitzes manuscript]

Basil II (958 – 15 December 1025), called the Bulgar-Slayer (Boulgaroktonos), was Byzantine emperor from 976 to 1025 — the longest-reigning and, by most reckonings, greatest of the Macedonian dynasty. Crowned as a child and long overshadowed by regents and the general-emperors Nikephoros II and John I, Basil seized real power, crushed the great Anatolian military families (the Skleros and Phokas revolts) in civil wars, and then waged forty years of methodical conquest: the annihilation of Samuel's Bulgarian empire (by 1018), annexations in Armenia and Georgia, and campaigns from Italy to Syria that made Byzantium the dominant power of the Mediterranean and Near East. At his death the treasury held some 200,000 pounds of gold and the empire stood at its greatest extent since the 7th century.

The Bulgarian wars

Basil's wars against Samuel of Bulgaria began with humiliation (the ambush at Trajan's Gate, 986) and a Rus'-aided rescue of his throne — for which Prince Vladimir of Kiev received Basil's sister Anna in marriage and sent the Varangian Guard (988), the corps that guarded emperors for three centuries. Thereafter Basil ground Bulgaria down fortress by fortress until the annihilation of Samuel's army at Kleidion (29 July 1014). The epithet 'Bulgar-Slayer' derives from the atrocity story told by later sources: 14,000 or 15,000 prisoners blinded, every hundredth man left one eye to guide the rest home — Samuel dying of shock at the sight. Modern historians doubt the scale (the story appears a century later), not the victory: by 1018 Bulgaria was a Byzantine province.

The austere emperor

Basil never married, lived like a soldier on campaign, wore plain dress, held court treasuries with a miser's grip, and protected peasant soldiers' properties against the dynatoi (the powerful) with legislation — his novel of 996 annulling illegal acquisitions back to 927. He died planning a Sicilian expedition. His heirs — brother Constantine VIII and the Zoe/Theodora generation — dissipated his inheritance within a generation, a decline his partisans blame on exactly the civilian softness Basil despised.
