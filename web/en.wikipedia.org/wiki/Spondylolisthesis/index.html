Spondylolisthesis (from Greek spondylos, "vertebra", and olisthesis, "slipping") is the displacement of one spinal vertebra relative to the adjacent vertebra. It most often involves forward slippage (anterolisthesis) of L5 over the sacrum, or L4 over L5; backward slippage is termed retrolisthesis. Many cases are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging.

Classification

The Wiltse classification distinguishes dysplastic (congenital), isthmic (from a defect or stress fracture of the pars interarticularis — spondylolysis — common in adolescent athletes such as gymnasts and weightlifters), degenerative (from facet joint arthritis and disc degeneration, most common in adults over 50), traumatic, pathological and post-surgical types. Severity is graded by the Meyerding system according to the percentage of the vertebral body that has slipped: grade I is up to 25%, grade II 25–50%, grade III 50–75%, grade IV 75–100%, and grade V (spondyloptosis) complete displacement.

Signs, diagnosis and treatment

Symptoms, when present, include low back pain, stiffness, hamstring tightness and, where the slip narrows the spinal canal or foramina, leg pain and numbness from nerve compression. Diagnosis is by plain radiographs (including lateral and oblique views, which may show the "Scottie dog" sign of a pars defect), with CT or MRI for detail. Management is usually conservative — rest from aggravating activity, physiotherapy, core strengthening and analgesia. Surgery, typically decompression with spinal fusion, is reserved for progressive high-grade slips, neurological deficit or pain refractory to conservative care.
