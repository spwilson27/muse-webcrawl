Andrew the Apostle, also known as Saint Andrew, was one of the Twelve Apostles of Jesus Christ and the brother of Simon Peter. According to the New Testament he was a fisherman from Bethsaida on the Sea of Galilee, first a disciple of John the Baptist, and among the earliest followers of Jesus — in Eastern tradition he is honoured as the Protokletos, the "First-Called".

In the Gospels

The synoptic Gospels describe Andrew and Peter casting their nets in the Sea of Galilee when Jesus called them to become "fishers of men". John's Gospel gives Andrew a more prominent role: he brings Peter to Jesus, presents the boy with loaves and fishes at the feeding of the five thousand, and, with Philip, brings the Greeks who wished to see Jesus.

Mission and martyrdom

Later tradition, recorded by Eusebius and apocryphal Acts of Andrew, holds that Andrew preached around the Black Sea, in Scythia, Thrace and Greece, and founded the see of Byzantium. He is traditionally believed to have been crucified at Patras in Achaea on an X-shaped cross, now known as a saltire or Saint Andrew's Cross, which he is said to have requested as unworthy of dying on the same form of cross as Christ.

Veneration

Andrew is the patron saint of Scotland — whose flag bears his saltire — as well as of Russia, Greece, Romania, Ukraine and Barbados. His relics were taken to Constantinople and later dispersed; the cathedral town of St Andrews in Fife, Scotland, grew around a shrine said to hold his relics. His feast day, 30 November, is Scotland's national day.
