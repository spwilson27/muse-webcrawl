No miracles argument

The no miracles argument (also spelled no-miracles argument) is the central argument in favour of scientific realism. It asserts that the extraordinary empirical success of mature scientific theories — their novel, precise predictions and their role in technology — would be miraculous, a cosmic coincidence, if those theories were not approximately true and their unobservable entities real.

Overview

The argument is an inference to the best explanation. Hilary Putnam gave its canonical statement in 1975: realism "is the only philosophy that doesn't make the success of science a miracle." J. J. C. Smart had earlier used a similar contrast between real electrons and "cosmic coincidences". Defenders emphasise that realism treats theoretical success the way science treats any surprising phenomenon — as something to be explained, here by the truth of the theory.

Objections

Critics reply with Larry Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction: many once-successful theories (phlogiston, caloric, ether) proved fundamentally false, so success is a poor guide to truth. Constructive empiricists such as Bas van Fraassen hold that empirical adequacy, not truth, explains success, since unsuccessful theories are simply discarded. Others charge that the argument is circular, using inference to the best explanation to defend realism about the conclusions of such inferences.

Related arguments

The no miracles argument is closely related to the entity realism of Ian Hacking ("if you can spray them, they are real") and to "structural realism", which claims continuity of mathematical structure rather than of entities across theory change as the true explanation of success.
