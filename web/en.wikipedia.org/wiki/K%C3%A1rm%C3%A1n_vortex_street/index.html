Kármán vortex street

In fluid dynamics, a Kármán vortex street (or a von Kármán vortex street) is a repeating pattern of swirling vortices, caused by a process known as vortex shedding, which is responsible for the unsteady separation of flow of a fluid around blunt (bluff) bodies.

It is named after the engineer and fluid dynamicist Theodore von Kármán, and it is responsible for such phenomena as the "singing" of suspended telephone or power lines and the vibration of a car antenna at certain speeds.

Definition

A vortex street will form only within a certain range of flow velocities, specified by a range of Reynolds numbers (Re), typically above a limiting Re value of about 90. The (global) Reynolds number for a flow is a measure of the ratio of inertial to viscous forces in the fluid, and can be applied to many different types of flows.

When a vortex is shed, an asymmetrical flow pattern forms around the body and changes the pressure distribution. That is, if a vortex is shed on the upper side of the body, the suction on that side increases; alternating shedding from the two sides produces an oscillating side force on the body, which can cause structural vibration.

Engineering

Although a vortex street is a beautiful phenomenon, it can cause serious engineering problems. Vortex shedding can induce vibration in tall chimneys, cooling towers, bridges and periscopes, and the collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 is often popularly associated with vortex-induced resonance, although aerodynamic flutter was the more direct cause. To suppress vortex-induced vibration on chimneys and stacks, helical strakes are commonly fitted around the structure to disrupt the coherent shedding.

In meteorology, vortex streets can be seen in satellite imagery as long chains of spiral clouds in the lee of mountainous islands, such as Guadalupe Island off the coast of Baja California, where low-level winds pass around the island peak.

Formula

The frequency of vortex shedding is related to the flow velocity and the characteristic width of the body by the Strouhal number:

St = f d / V

where f is the shedding frequency, d is the characteristic length (for example the diameter of a cylinder) and V is the flow velocity. For a wide range of Reynolds numbers the Strouhal number is approximately 0.21.
