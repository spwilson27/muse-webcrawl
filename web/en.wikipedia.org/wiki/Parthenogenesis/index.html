Parthenogenesis

Parthenogenesis
TypeAsexual reproduction
MechanismDevelopment of an embryo from an unfertilised egg
Occurs inMany invertebrates, some plants, and some vertebrates (reptiles, fish, amphibians, rarely birds)
EtymologyGreek parthenos ("virgin") + genesis ("origin")

Parthenogenesis (from the Greek παρθένος, "virgin", + γένεσις, "origin") is a natural form of asexual reproduction in which growth and development of an embryo occur without fertilisation by a male. In animals, parthenogenesis involves development of an egg into an embryo without sperm; in plants, the analogous process is called apomixis. Offspring may be clones of the mother, or may differ depending on the cellular mechanism involved.

Some whiptail lizard species consist entirely of females reproducing by parthenogenesis.

Mechanisms

Parthenogenesis may be apomictic, where the egg is produced by mitosis and offspring are genetically identical to the mother, or automictic, where meiosis occurs but diploidy is restored (by fusion of meiotic products or duplication of the genome), producing partial loss of heterozygosity. Forms include:

  • Obligate parthenogenesis — species reproduce exclusively this way, e.g. many bdelloid rotifers and various all-female lizard lineages.
  • Facultative parthenogenesis — females ordinarily reproduce sexually but can produce offspring without males; documented in captive Komodo dragons, some sharks, snakes, and turkeys.
  • Cyclical parthenogenesis — generations alternate between parthenogenetic and sexual reproduction, as in aphids and water fleas (Daphnia).
  • Gynogenesis and hybridogenesis — sperm triggers development but contributes no (or only temporary) genetic material.

Occurrence

Parthenogenesis is widespread among invertebrates — rotifers, aphids, nematodes, many insects, and some crustaceans — and occurs in more than 80 vertebrate taxa, including fish, amphibians, reptiles such as the Komodo dragon and many whiptail lizards, and rarely birds. In bees and other haplodiploid insects, unfertilised eggs develop into males (arrhenotoky), as in honeybee drones. No natural cases are known in mammals, where genomic imprinting prevents embryos from developing from a single parent's genome, though parthenogenetic development can be induced experimentally.

Evolutionary significance

Parthenogenesis offers a reproductive advantage — every individual can produce offspring, and a single female can found a population — at the cost of reduced genetic diversity. Its distribution and persistence inform debates about the evolutionary advantages of sexual reproduction.
