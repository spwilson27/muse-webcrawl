Food forest

A food forest, also called a forest garden, is a low-maintenance, sustainable, plant-based food production and agroforestry system based on woodland ecosystems, incorporating fruit and nut trees, shrubs, herbs, vines and perennial vegetables which have yields directly useful to humans. These are mixed and grown together in successive layers, imitating the structure of a natural forest, to form a polyculture that is largely self-maintaining. Food forests have existed in tropical regions for thousands of years as home gardens; the concept was popularised in temperate climates by Robert Hart in Britain from the 1980s and is a central technique of permaculture.

The seven layers

Food forest design commonly identifies seven layers of vegetation:

  1. The canopy layer of large fruit and nut trees
  2. The low-tree layer of smaller fruit trees
  3. The shrub layer of fruiting bushes such as currants and berries
  4. The herbaceous layer of perennial vegetables and herbs
  5. The ground-cover layer of low, spreading plants
  6. The rhizosphere, or root layer, of root crops and fungi
  7. The vertical layer of vines and climbers

History

Forest gardening is arguably the world's oldest form of land use: prehistoric peoples tended useful trees near settlements, and elaborate multi-storey home gardens have long been common in South and Southeast Asia, Africa, and Central America. In the 1980s, Robert Hart created a model forest garden on his smallholding in Shropshire, England, and described the system in his book Forest Gardening (1996). Organisations such as Plants for a Future and practitioners including Martin Crawford of the Agroforestry Research Trust further developed temperate-climate food forestry.

Benefits and criticism

Advocates point to benefits including increased biodiversity, soil building, carbon sequestration, low input requirements once established, and resilience from crop diversity. Critics note that yields per unit area of staple crops can be lower than in conventional agriculture, that harvesting from complex polycultures can be labour-intensive, and that the system requires long-term knowledge of many species.
