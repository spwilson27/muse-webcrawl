Self-presentation

Placeholder image: managing the impression one makes on others.

Self-presentation is the process by which individuals control the impressions others form of them. The concept was developed by sociologist Erving Goffman in The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (1956), which used a dramaturgical metaphor: people are actors performing roles on a "front stage" for audiences, while relaxing "backstage." In social psychology, self-presentation is studied as a motivated activity aimed at establishing desired identities and gaining approval, status, or other outcomes.

Strategies

Researchers such as Robert Arkin, Edward E. Jones, and Barry Schlenker have catalogued self-presentational tactics, including ingratiation (flattery and agreeableness), self-promotion (highlighting competence), exemplification (displaying moral worth), intimidation, and supplication. People differ in how much they monitor and adjust their self-presentation; the personality trait of self-monitoring captures this variation. Failure of a claimed identity — embarrassment — prompts remedial work such as accounts, excuses, and apologies. Online environments and social media have made self-presentation an area of active research, since profiles allow carefully curated identities.
