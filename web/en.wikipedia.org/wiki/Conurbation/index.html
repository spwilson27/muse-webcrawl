Conurbation

A conurbation is a region comprising a number of metropolises, cities, large towns, and other urban areas which, through population growth and physical expansion, have merged to form one continuous urban or industrially developed area. The term was coined in 1915 by the Scottish planner and sociologist Patrick Geddes in his book Cities in Evolution, drawing on the Latin con- ("together") and urbs ("city").

A conurbation forms when neighbouring urban centres grow into one another.

Characteristics

A conurbation is typically polycentric: unlike a single city with suburbs, it has several identifiable centres, each historically an independent town or city with its own administration, identity, and centre. Administrative boundaries often do not match the physical extent of the built-up area, which leads to special planning and governance arrangements.

Examples

Classic examples include the West Midlands conurbation (Birmingham, Wolverhampton, Dudley, and Walsall) and Greater Manchester in England; the Ruhr area (Ruhrgebiet) in Germany, a polycentric region of more than five million people; the Randstad in the Netherlands (Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht); the Flemish Diamond in Belgium; the Keihanshin region in Japan (Kyoto, Osaka, Kobe); and in the United States, combined statistical areas such as BosWash (the Boston–Washington corridor, also described as a megalopolis) and the Dallas–Fort Worth metroplex.

Related concepts

When conurbations grow still larger and fuse at regional scale, Jean Gottmann's term megalopolis is used. Statistical definitions vary by country: the UK Office for National Statistics identifies built-up areas, while US practice distinguishes metropolitan statistical areas and combined statistical areas.
