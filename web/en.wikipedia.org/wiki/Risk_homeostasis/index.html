Risk homeostasis is a theory proposed by the Canadian psychologist Gerald J. S. Wilde which holds that every person has an accepted or "target" level of risk, and that safety interventions which reduce perceived risk will be partly or wholly offset by behavioural changes that restore that level. In Wilde's 1982 book Target Risk, individuals continuously compare the amount of risk they perceive with their target level and adjust their behaviour — for example driving faster or following more closely when a road or vehicle feels safer — so that, other things being equal, accident rates tend towards a stable equilibrium.

The theory

Wilde described behaviour as governed by a homeostatic feedback loop analogous to a thermostat: the "risk thermostat" compares perceived risk against the target level determined by the expected costs and benefits of risky and cautious behaviour. If perceived risk falls below the target, people take more risk; if it rises above, they become more careful. The theory predicts that the only durable way to reduce accidents is to lower the target level of risk itself, for instance through incentives, rather than relying solely on engineering or enforcement.

Related concepts and evidence

Risk homeostasis is the strong form of the broader phenomenon of risk compensation, and is related to the Peltzman effect identified by economist Sam Peltzman in a 1975 study of US automobile safety regulation. Studies have variously found partial offsets — for example some drivers of cars fitted with anti-lock brakes or seat belts driving less cautiously — but the existence of complete homeostasis is contested. Critics argue that large long-term declines in traffic deaths per kilometre in many countries show that engineering and regulation do work, and that the theory's predictions are difficult to falsify.

Applications

The theory has influenced approaches such as "shared space" road design, in which removing signs and signals increases perceived risk and slows traffic, and incentive schemes that reward claim-free insurance periods in order to change drivers' target risk.
