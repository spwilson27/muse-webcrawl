Fluid mosaic model

Fluid mosaic model
Proposed1972
ProposersS. Jonathan Singer and Garth L. Nicolson
SubjectStructure of the cell (plasma) membrane

The fluid mosaic model describes the structure of the plasma membrane of animal cells as a mosaic of components — including phospholipids, cholesterol, proteins, and carbohydrates — that gives the membrane a fluid character. The model was proposed by S. Jonathan Singer and Garth L. Nicolson in a landmark 1972 paper in Science, and has evolved over time but remains the standard description of membrane organisation.

Description

According to the model, the membrane is a phospholipid bilayer: two layers of phospholipid molecules whose hydrophilic heads face the aqueous environments inside and outside the cell, and whose hydrophobic fatty-acid tails face each other in the interior. Embedded within and attached to this bilayer are numerous proteins — integral proteins that span the membrane and peripheral proteins associated with one surface — distributed in a mosaic pattern. Many proteins and lipids can move laterally within the plane of the membrane, giving it a fluid, two-dimensional liquid character; carbohydrate chains attached to proteins and lipids (glycoproteins and glycolipids) form the glycocalyx on the outer surface.

Evidence

The model drew on evidence including freeze-fracture electron microscopy, which revealed particles (proteins) embedded in the bilayer, and cell-fusion experiments by Frye and Edidin (1970) showing that membrane proteins from two fused cells intermix, demonstrating lateral mobility. Later techniques such as fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) quantified the diffusion of lipids and proteins.

Refinements and limitations

Subsequent research has shown the membrane is more structured than a simple fluid mosaic: lipid rafts, cytoskeletal fences, and protein complexes restrict diffusion, and membrane composition is asymmetric between the two leaflets. The model has accordingly been updated to a more complex "fluid mosaic" with domains and dynamic interactions, but its core ideas — a fluid lipid bilayer studded with mobile proteins — remain foundational in cell biology.
