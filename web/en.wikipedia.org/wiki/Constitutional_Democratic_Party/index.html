Constitutional Democratic Party

The Constitutional Democratic Party (Russian: Конституционно-демократическая партия, Konstitutsionno-demokraticheskaya partiya), also known as the Kadets (from the initials K-D), was a liberal political party in the Russian Empire. Founded in October 1905 during the Russian Revolution of 1905, it became the leading liberal party of late imperial Russia and a major force in the early State Dumas.

History

The party grew out of the Union of Liberation and the zemstvo constitutionalist movement. Its founding congress in Moscow in October 1905 adopted a programme demanding a constitutional monarchy with a parliament elected by universal suffrage, civil liberties, an eight-hour working day, and a solution to the agrarian question that included compulsory expropriation of some private land with compensation. Its leaders included the historian Pavel Milyukov, the jurist Fyodor Kokoshkin, and former zemstvo activists; its membership drew heavily on the professional intelligentsia — professors, lawyers, doctors and teachers.

The Dumas

The Kadets won the largest share of seats in the First State Duma of 1906, holding roughly 180 of 497 seats. The Tsar dissolved the Duma after 72 days; about 200 deputies, mostly Kadets, retired to Vyborg in Finland and issued the Vyborg Manifesto calling on the population to refuse taxes and military service, for which the signatories were tried and disenfranchised. The party remained a major opposition bloc in the Second Duma, but the electoral law of June 1907 sharply reduced its representation, and it gradually declined into a patriotic-liberal opposition.

1917 and demise

After the February Revolution of 1917, Kadets joined the Provisional Government, with Milyukov as foreign minister. The party supported continuing Russia's participation in the First World War, a stance that eroded its popularity as the war dragged on. Following the October Revolution, the Bolsheviks outlawed the party, declaring its leaders "enemies of the people" in November 1917. Many Kadets later supported the White movement or emigrated, continuing party activity in exile in Paris and elsewhere.
