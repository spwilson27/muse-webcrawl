Korteweg–de Vries equation

[Image placeholder: soliton solution of the KdV equation]

The Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation is a nonlinear partial differential equation describing the propagation of waves on shallow water surfaces. In canonical form it reads

∂u/∂t + 6u ∂u/∂x + ∂³u/∂x³ = 0,

where u(x, t) represents wave height. The equation models the balance between nonlinear steepening and dispersive spreading that allows stable, localised solitary waves called solitons to propagate without changing shape.

History

The solitary wave was first reported by John Scott Russell, who in 1834 followed a single wave along a Scottish canal for miles. Joseph Boussinesq derived related equations in the 1870s, and in 1895 Diederik Korteweg and his student Gustav de Vries published the equation that now bears their names. Long considered a curiosity, the KdV equation became central to nonlinear science in the 1960s, when numerical experiments by Zabusky and Kruskal showed that its solitons survive collisions intact, and Gardner, Greene, Kruskal, and Miura developed the inverse scattering transform, allowing the equation to be solved exactly.

Applications

Beyond water waves, KdV-type equations arise in plasma physics, anharmonic crystal lattices, and optical fibres. The equation is the prototype of an integrable system and opened the modern theory of solitons, with connections to the modified KdV equation and the Miura transformation.
