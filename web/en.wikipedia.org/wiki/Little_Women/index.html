Little Women is a coming-of-age novel written by American novelist Louisa May Alcott, originally published in two volumes in 1868 and 1869. The story follows the lives of the four March sisters — Meg, Jo, Beth, and Amy — and details their passage from childhood to womanhood in Concord, Massachusetts, during and after the American Civil War. It is loosely based on the author's own life and those of her three sisters.

Plot summary

The four March sisters live with their mother, Marmee, in genteel poverty while their father serves as a chaplain in the Civil War. The eldest, Meg, is beautiful and longs for luxury; Jo is a tomboyish aspiring writer; Beth is shy, kind, and musical; Amy is artistic and vain. Their neighbour, the wealthy Mr. Laurence, and his grandson Laurie (Theodore Laurence), become close friends of the family — Laurie especially of Jo.

Over the course of the two volumes the sisters grow up: Meg marries John Brooke and has twins; Beth, who contracted scarlet fever while helping a poor family, gradually declines and dies; Amy travels to Europe, matures, and eventually marries Laurie, whom Jo had refused; Jo moves to New York, meets Professor Friedrich Bhaer, returns home, and ultimately marries him, founding a school at Plumfield, the estate she inherits from her Aunt March.

Characters

  • Margaret "Meg" March — the eldest sister.
  • Josephine "Jo" March — the second sister, a writer, widely considered Alcott's alter ego.
  • Elizabeth "Beth" March — the third sister, gentle and selfless.
  • Amy Curtis March — the youngest sister, an artist.
  • Theodore "Laurie" Laurence — the Marches' wealthy young neighbour.
  • Friedrich Bhaer — a German professor whom Jo meets in New York.

Reception and legacy

Little Women was an immediate commercial and critical success, and has never been out of print. It has been adapted numerous times for film, television, stage, and opera, including the 1933 film with Katharine Hepburn, the 1994 film with Winona Ryder, and Greta Gerwig's acclaimed 2019 adaptation. The character of Jo March in particular has become an iconic figure of independent, ambitious womanhood in American literature.
