The British gangster film is a subgenre of crime film set in the British criminal underworld, particularly that of London's East End. Distinct from its American counterpart in tone — grittier, class-conscious and laced with black humour — the genre has produced many of British cinema's most celebrated and controversial titles.

Early films

British crime films of the 1940s drew on London's real underworld. Brighton Rock (1948), adapted from Graham Greene's novel, with Richard Attenborough as the teenage killer Pinkie, and The Blue Lamp (1950) set the pattern for the post-war crime picture. The lives of the Kray twins, who dominated the East End in the 1950s and 1960s, later inspired numerous films, including The Krays (1990) and Legend (2015).

The 1970s and 1980s

Get Carter (1971), directed by Mike Hodges and starring Michael Caine as a London gangster investigating his brother's death in Newcastle, is widely regarded as the genre's masterpiece and one of the greatest British films. The Long Good Friday (1980), starring Bob Hoskins as a crime boss whose empire collapses over one weekend, updated the genre for the Thatcher era of docklands redevelopment.

Revival

Guy Ritchie's Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels (1998) and Snatch (2000) launched a wave of stylish, comic "geezer" gangster films, followed by titles such as Sexy Beast (2000), Layer Cake (2004) and The Gentlemen (2019). Critics debated the genre's glamorisation of violence, but the films' commercial success made the British gangster a durable global brand.
