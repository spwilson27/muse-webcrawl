Algebraically closed field

[Image placeholder: diagram of polynomial roots in an algebraically closed field]

In mathematics, a field F is algebraically closed if every non-constant polynomial in one variable with coefficients in F has a root in F — equivalently, if every such polynomial splits into linear factors over F, or if F has no proper algebraic extension.

Examples and counterexamples

The field of complex numbers ℂ is algebraically closed, by the fundamental theorem of algebra. Neither the rational numbers ℚ (the polynomial x² − 2 has no rational root) nor the real numbers ℝ (x² + 1 has no real root) are algebraically closed, and no finite field is algebraically closed, since the polynomial formed by multiplying (x − a) over all elements a of the field and adding 1 has no root.

Equivalent statements

For a field F, the following are equivalent: F is algebraically closed; every non-constant polynomial over F has a root in F; every non-constant polynomial over F splits into linear factors; every irreducible polynomial over F is linear; every finite extension of F is trivial; and every matrix over F has an eigenvalue in F, hence a Jordan normal form. Every field can be embedded in an algebraically closed field, its algebraic closure.

Importance

Algebraically closed fields are the natural setting for algebraic geometry: Hilbert's Nullstellensatz relates ideals in polynomial rings over such fields to geometric solution sets. In model theory, the theory of algebraically closed fields of a fixed characteristic admits quantifier elimination and is complete, making it a classic test case. Steinitz's theorem (1910) states that an algebraically closed field is determined up to isomorphism by its characteristic and its transcendence degree over its prime field.
