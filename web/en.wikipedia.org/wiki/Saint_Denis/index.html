Saint Denis (French: Denis, from Dionysius) was a 3rd-century Christian martyr and the first bishop of Paris. He is the patron saint of Paris and of France. According to tradition he was one of the seven "apostles of Gaul" sent out under Decius, and was martyred during the persecution of Christians around 250.

Legend

The Golden Legend recounts that Denis was beheaded on Montmartre — the "mount of the martyrs" — and that he miraculously picked up his head and walked some six kilometres north, preaching a sermon the whole way, before dying at the place where the Basilica of Saint-Denis now stands. He is thus the most famous of the cephalophores, saints depicted carrying their own heads.

Cult and basilica

A shrine over his tomb became one of France's great pilgrimage sites. Dagobert I richly endowed the Abbey of Saint-Denis; from the twelfth century, under Abbot Suger, the rebuilt abbey church became the cradle of Gothic architecture. Saint-Denis served as the necropolis of the kings of France, and "Saint Denis!" became the war-cry of French armies (the Montjoie Saint Denis), with the oriflamme banner kept at the abbey. His relics and cult were central to the French monarchy's sacred identity.

Identity questions

In the ninth century Denis was conflated with Dionysius the Areopagite and with the author of the mystical writings of Pseudo-Dionysius — a confusion long accepted and only dissolved by modern scholarship.
