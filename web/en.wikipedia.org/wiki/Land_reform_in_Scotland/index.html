Land reform in Scotland

Land reform in Scotland is the ongoing process of changing the legal and political framework governing land ownership in Scotland, where a strikingly concentrated pattern of private ownership — about half of privately owned rural land held by a few hundred owners — has been debated since the 19th century. Modern reform has proceeded through legislation of the Scottish Parliament since devolution.

Background

Scotland's pattern of large estates has roots in feudal tenure, the Highland Clearances, and the conversion of clan lands into private property. Crofters in the Highlands and Islands gained security of tenure under the Crofters Holdings (Scotland) Act 1886 after the agitation of the Highland Land League. Feudal tenure itself was finally abolished by the Abolition of Feudal Tenure etc. (Scotland) Act 2000. The community buyouts of the 1990s — Assynt (1993), the Isle of Eigg (1997) and Knoydart (1999) — demonstrated community ownership in practice before Parliament legislated.

Legislation

The Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003 created three pillars: a statutory right of responsible access to land and inland water (underpinning the Scottish Outdoor Access Code); a community right to buy registered land when it comes up for sale; and a crofting community right to buy, which can be exercised at any time subject to tests. The Community Empowerment (Scotland) Act 2015 extended community rights to buy to urban Scotland and added provisions for neglected or abandoned land. The Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2016 went further: it required a Land Rights and Responsibilities Statement, established the Scottish Land Commission, created a right to buy land to further sustainable development, introduced a register of persons holding a controlling interest in landowners, and reformed agricultural holdings and common good land. Later measures include the requirement that very large landholdings produce land management plans and the expansion of transparency of ownership.

Community ownership

By the 2020s, well over half a million acres were in community ownership, including the great South Uist estate bought in 2006 and the Isle of Gigha. Reformers argue land reform supports repopulation, housing, renewable energy and nature restoration; landowners' bodies argue for stability of investment and stress the management role of estates. The debate over scale of holdings — including proposed powers to break up very large estates at transfer — remains live in Scottish politics.
