The Popular Front (French: Front populaire) was an alliance of French left-wing movements, including the communist, socialist and Radical parties, that formed in 1935 in response to the rise of fascism and of France's far-right leagues after the riots of 6 February 1934. It won the legislative elections of May 1936 and formed a government under the socialist Léon Blum, the first socialist and first Jewish premier of France.

Formation and victory

Following the Stavisky affair riots of February 1934, the French left feared a fascist coup. The Communist Party, following the Comintern's new Popular Front line, joined the socialists (SFIO) and the centrist Radicals in joint demonstrations on 14 July 1935 and agreed on a common programme of "bread, peace and freedom" in January 1936. In the elections of 26 April and 3 May 1936 the alliance won a clear majority in the Chamber of Deputies; Blum became head of government, while the Communists supported the government without entering it.

The Blum government and the Matignon agreements

Victory coincided with a wave of strikes and factory occupations in May–June 1936 involving some two million workers. To settle them, the government brokered the Matignon agreements (7 June 1936) between employers and the CGT trade-union federation. Legislation followed rapidly: the 40-hour week, two weeks' paid holidays (congés payés), collective bargaining rights, and wage increases. The government also extended the school-leaving age, began nationalising the armaments industry and the railways (leading to the SNCF), reformed the Bank of France, dissolved the far-right leagues, and promoted sport and leisure under Léo Lagrange.

Difficulties and collapse

The government was weakened by economic problems — capital flight, pressure on the franc (devalued in autumn 1936), and inflation eating into wage gains. Blum's February 1937 announcement of a "pause" in reforms alienated supporters. The Popular Front also split over the Spanish Civil War: fearing a widening conflict and pressed by Britain and the Radicals, Blum adopted non-intervention despite Communist demands to aid the Spanish Republic. The Senate forced Blum's resignation in June 1937; a short-lived second Blum government fell in April 1938, and the alliance disintegrated as Édouard Daladier formed a government with the right, repealed the 40-hour week, and signed the Munich Agreement.

Legacy

The Popular Front left durable social reforms — above all paid holidays and the 40-hour week (later modified) — and a powerful place in French left-wing memory, comparable to the Resistance and May 1968. It remains a reference point in debates about uniting the French left.
