Middle Welsh (Welsh: Cymraeg Canol) is the label attached to the Welsh language of the 12th, 13th and 14th centuries, of which much more remains than for the earlier Old Welsh period. It developed from Old Welsh and is the language of nearly all surviving early Welsh manuscripts, including the prose tales of the Mabinogion and the Welsh laws traditionally attributed to Hywel Dda.

Literature

The prose of the Middle Welsh period includes the Four Branches of the Mabinogi, the tales of Culhwch and Olwen, the Welsh Triads, and translations of foreign works such as Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (rendered as Brut y Brenhinedd). Much of this literature is preserved in two great fourteenth-century manuscripts, the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest. Middle Welsh poetry includes the work of the Gogynfeirdd, the court poets of the Welsh princes.

Grammar and orthography

Middle Welsh orthography differs from the modern standard: the sound /u/ was written u (modern u), /v/ was written u or f in some positions, and the letter k was common where modern Welsh uses c. Verb morphology retained a fuller set of inflected forms than modern literary Welsh, and the language made extensive use of infixed and suffixed pronouns. Compared with Old Welsh, the language shows the loss of final syllables that reshaped Brittonic word forms.

Transition to Modern Welsh

By around 1400, written Welsh shows the changes that mark the beginning of Early Modern Welsh, including shifts in spelling conventions and the gradual fixing of forms that lead to the modern language standardised by William Morgan's 1588 translation of the Bible.
