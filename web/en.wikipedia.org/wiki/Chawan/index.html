Kanji茶碗 ("tea bowl")
UseJapanese tea ceremony
MaterialsStoneware (most common); porcelain

A chawan (Japanese: 茶碗; literally "tea bowl") is a bowl used for preparing and drinking tea in the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu). Although simple in form, the chawan is the central vessel of the tea ceremony, and connoisseurs may judge an entire gathering by the bowl chosen.

History

Tea bowls were imported from China along with tea-drinking customs from the 9th century, and Japanese potters began producing them as the ceremony developed its own aesthetic in the 15th and 16th centuries. Under the influence of the tea master Sen no Rikyū (1522–1591), the taste for rough, handmade bowls of the wabi-sabi aesthetic became canonical: irregular Raku tea bowls, pressed by hand rather than thrown on the wheel, are the archetypal chawan of the most formal tea schools (Urasenke, Omotesenke, Mushakōjisenke).

Styles

Classic regional styles include Hagi ware (with its crackled "crazing" that changes colour with use), Shino ware (white slip with underglaze), Oribe ware (with distorted forms and copper green), and Karatsu ware (humble, Korean-influenced). Chinese-style Tenmoku bowls (iron-rich glaze with a distinct foot) are also used, and porcelain bowls are preferred for drinking usucha (thin tea) in some schools.

Use and etiquette

In a ceremony the host selects the chawan for the season and occasion; guests admire the bowl before drinking, rotating it so as to drink from its "back" side as a mark of respect. The bowl's front (shōmen) typically bears the potter's decoration and is turned away during drinking. A chawan holds roughly 200–250 ml; summer bowls are wide and low to cool the tea, winter bowls tall and deep to retain warmth.

Collecting

Antique chawan — particularly early Raku, Hagi, and tea bowls from the Joseon dynasty prized as ido chawan — are among the most valuable ceramics in the Japanese art market, with important examples designated as National Treasures.
