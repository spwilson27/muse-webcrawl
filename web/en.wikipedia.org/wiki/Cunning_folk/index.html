Cunning folk, also known as cunning men and wise women, were practitioners of folk medicine, traditional healing and folk magic in Britain and Ireland from the medieval period into the early 20th century. Unlike the witches feared in early modern witch trials, cunning folk were generally valued members of their communities who sold magical services: healing people and livestock, lifting curses and identifying the witch responsible, recovering lost or stolen property, telling fortunes and providing love charms.

Services and practice

Cunning folk typically worked from their cottages for paying clients, often across considerable distances. Their methods combined herbal remedies, charms written or spoken (frequently drawn from folk-Christian prayers and psalms), astrology, and ritual objects such as witch bottles — bottles filled with pins, nails and urine buried to break a witch's power. Many kept manuscript collections of spells and recipes, and some used scrying stones or mirrors to locate thieves and stolen goods. In Cornwall, practitioners were called pellars, said to derive from their power to "expel" evil spirits.

Attitudes and the law

Attitudes were ambivalent. The church condemned all magic, and although cunning folk insisted their power came from God, they were occasionally prosecuted — under statutes including the Witchcraft Acts — or themselves accused of witchcraft. Nevertheless, most villagers distinguished sharply between harmful "black" witchcraft and the beneficent "white" magic of the cunning person, and even clergy and gentry sometimes consulted them in secret.

Decline

The trade declined through the 19th century under pressure from professional medicine, policing, compulsory education and acts such as the Vagrancy Act 1824, under which fortune-tellers could be prosecuted. Historians such as Owen Davies have documented how a handful of practitioners nonetheless continued into the 1900s, and how the image of the cunning person fed into modern revivals of folk magic and witchcraft.
