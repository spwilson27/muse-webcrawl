Silla (Korean: 신라, 57 BCE – 935 CE) was a Korean kingdom located on the southern and central parts of the Korean Peninsula. One of the Three Kingdoms alongside Goguryeo and Baekje, Silla began as a small chiefdom in the southeast and, allied with Tang China, conquered its rivals in 660–668, ruling most of the peninsula as "Unified Silla" for nearly three centuries. With a history spanning almost a millennium, it is one of the longest-lived dynastic states in world history.

History

Foundation and growth

Tradition dates Silla's founding to 57 BCE by Bak Hyeokgeose in the Gyeongju basin. For centuries it was the weakest of the Three Kingdoms, governed by rotating kingship among the Bak, Seok and Kim clans. Kingship became hereditary in the Kim line, and under kings such as Beopheung (514–540) Silla adopted Buddhism as the state religion, codified laws, and under Jinheung (540–576) expanded aggressively up the Han River valley and along the east coast.

Unification

In alliance with Tang China, Silla under King Muyeol and his son Munmu destroyed Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668. When Tang sought to govern the peninsula itself, Silla fought and expelled Tang forces by 676, uniting Korea south of the Taedong River for the first time. The unified kingdom prospered: the capital Gyeongju grew to perhaps a million inhabitants, and monuments such as Bulguksa temple and the Seokguram grotto exemplify its Buddhist art.

Decline

Unified Silla was rigidly stratified by the bone-rank (golpum) system, which reserved office and even marriage by hereditary rank. From the late eighth century, succession disputes, aristocratic factionalism and peasant revolts weakened the centre; in the Later Three Kingdoms period (c. 892–936) the warlords Gung Ye and Gyeon Hwon revived Taebong and Later Baekje. In 935 the last Silla king, Gyeongsun, surrendered to Wang Geon of Goryeo, ending the kingdom peacefully.

Culture

Silla is famed for its gold crowns and jewellery from the great tumuli of Gyeongju, for the Cheomseongdae astronomical observatory (built under Queen Seondeok, r. 632–647), and for the hwarang — elite youth corps central to Silla's military and cultural life. Its language is an ancestor of modern Korean.
