Proleptic Gregorian calendar

The proleptic Gregorian calendar is the Gregorian calendar extended backwards to dates before it was officially introduced in October 1582. "Proleptic" refers to applying a dating system to a period before that system existed. In this calendar, the Gregorian leap-year rules are projected into the past without interruption, producing dates that differ from the historically used Julian calendar by an offset that grows the further back one goes.

Rules and year numbering

The proleptic Gregorian calendar follows the same rules as the ordinary Gregorian calendar: years are divided into 12 months of the familiar lengths; a year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4, except that century years are leap years only if divisible by 400. For expressing dates before the year 1, the system is normally combined with astronomical year numbering, in which the year before AD 1 is year 0, the year before that is −1, and so on. This convention is mandated by the ISO 8601 standard, which uses the proleptic Gregorian calendar for dates before 1582 by mutual agreement between the parties exchanging data; ISO 8601 supports years from 0000 to 9999 and, with additional digits, beyond.

Difference from the Julian calendar

The Julian calendar inserts a leap day every four years without exception, so it runs progressively ahead of the proleptic Gregorian calendar. The difference was 10 days by 1582 (when Catholic countries skipped 10 days on adopting the Gregorian calendar) and increases by one day in each Julian century year not divisible by 400 — 1700, 1800, 1900, and so on going forward, while it decreases for earlier centuries. Between 1 March AD 200 and 28 February AD 300, Julian and proleptic Gregorian dates coincide. Historians generally do not use proleptic Gregorian dates for events originally recorded in the Julian calendar, preferring to quote the date as recorded; the proleptic calendar is mainly used in astronomy, computing and standardised data interchange.

Usage

Astronomers use the proleptic Gregorian (or Julian, for earlier epochs) framework in computing Julian day numbers. Many software libraries — for example Java's java.time API — adopt the ISO 8601 proleptic Gregorian calendar as their default dating system, even for dates long before 1582.
