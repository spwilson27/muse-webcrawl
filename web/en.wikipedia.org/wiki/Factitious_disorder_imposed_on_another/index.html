Factitious disorder imposed on another

Factitious disorder imposed on another (FDIA), formerly called Munchausen syndrome by proxy, is a mental disorder and form of abuse in which a caregiver fabricates, exaggerates, or induces illness or injury in a person under their care — most often a parent harming their own child — in order to gain attention, sympathy, or other psychological gratification.

Signs and diagnosis

Warning signs include a child whose reported symptoms do not match test results, symptoms that occur only in the caregiver's presence, a caregiver who is medically knowledgeable and unusually eager for invasive procedures, and illness that improves when the child is separated from the caregiver. Diagnosis is difficult and rests on careful review of records and exclusion of genuine disease; confirmed fabrication may require child-protection intervention.

Naming and recognition

The term "Munchausen syndrome by proxy" was introduced by paediatrician Roy Meadow in 1977. The DSM-5 renamed the condition factitious disorder imposed on another, classifying the diagnosis with the perpetrator rather than the victim. The behaviour is considered child abuse (medical child abuse), and, in severe cases, can be fatal; separation and legal intervention may be required to protect the victim.

Notable cases

High-profile cases have included those of Dee Dee Blanchard, whose daughter Gypsy-Rose killed her in 2015 after years of imposed illness, and serial cases in healthcare settings that have raised controversy about expert evidence in prosecutions.
