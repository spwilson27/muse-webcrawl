Table of Ranks

The Table of Ranks (Russian: Табель о рангах, Tabel' o rangakh) was a formal list of positions and ranks in the military, government and court of the Russian Empire, introduced by Peter the Great by decree of 24 January (4 February) 1722. It remained, with amendments, in force until the Russian Revolution of 1917.

Structure

The table divided service into three parallel ladders — military (army and navy separately), civil, and court — each with fourteen classes, class 1 being the highest. Every official position was assigned a class, and promotion depended in principle on seniority and merit rather than birth alone. Ranks conferred personal nobility at lower thresholds and hereditary nobility at higher ones: in the civil service hereditary nobility originally came at class 8, later raised to class 5, while in the military it came at class 14, the lowest commissioned rank.

Purpose and effects

Peter intended to break the power of the hereditary boyar aristocracy and the old precedence system (mestnichestvo, abolished in 1682) and to create a service nobility loyal to the state. Critics, including later historians, have noted that it also entrenched bureaucracy and a preoccupation with rank — the chin — satirised in works such as Gogol's The Government Inspector and Chekhov's stories. Holders of rank were addressed by elaborate forms such as "Your Excellency" graded by class.

Later history

Successive emperors modified thresholds for hereditary nobility in 1845 and 1856 to slow the growth of the noble estate. The Provisional Government and then the Bolsheviks abolished ranks and estates in late 1917. The concept influenced rank systems in other states and, indirectly, the reintroduction of formal ranks in the Soviet Union in the 1930s–40s.
