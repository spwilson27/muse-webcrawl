Ex-voto

Ex-voto
Full termEx voto suscepto ("from the vow made")
FormsPaintings, plaques, models, anatomical votives, crutches, candles
TraditionsCatholic and Orthodox Christianity; ancient Greek and Roman religion

An ex-voto is a votive offering to a saint or to a divinity, given in fulfilment of a vow (whence the Latin term, short for ex voto suscepto, "from the vow made") or in gratitude or devotion for a favour received, such as recovery from illness or survival of an accident. Ex-votos are displayed in churches, chapels, and shrines, especially those associated with miraculous healings and deliverance from danger.

Forms

Ex-votos take many forms. In the ancient world, worshippers offered models of healed body parts at healing sanctuaries such as those of Asclepius; anatomical votives in terracotta, bronze, or silver (milagros in Latin American tradition) remain common. In Catholic Europe and Latin America, painted ex-votos — small narrative panels depicting the peril and the saint's intervention, often with a written account — became a major folk art form, especially in Mexico from the 18th century onward. Other offerings include silver plaques, ship models hung in sailors' churches, crutches and medical devices left at healing shrines, wedding dresses, sports trophies, and inscribed marble tablets abbreviated "P.G.R." (per grazia ricevuta, "for grace received").

History

The practice continues a tradition going back to ancient Egypt, Greece, and Rome, where votive deposits accumulated in temples. In Christianity it flourished from the Middle Ages at pilgrimage shrines such as Lourdes, Fátima, and Santiago de Compostela, and it remains widespread in Mediterranean and Latin American Catholicism today. Major art collections include large numbers of ex-voto paintings, which historians value as vivid documentary sources for the everyday dangers, clothing, and devotions of ordinary people.
