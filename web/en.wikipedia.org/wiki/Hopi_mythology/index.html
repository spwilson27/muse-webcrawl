Hopi mythology


Kachina figures in Hopi tradition

The Hopi mythology is part of the oral traditions and religious life of the Hopi people, a Native American tribe in northeastern Arizona in the southwestern United States. Hopi religion is complex and is not generally discussed with outsiders; the accounts available in published sources describe emergence narratives, deities, and ceremonial figures such as the kachinas.

Emergence narrative

According to Hopi tradition, humans emerged into the present world—the Fourth World—from worlds below, climbing up through a reed or opening called the sipapu, which is symbolically represented in Hopi ceremonial rooms (kivas). Earlier worlds were destroyed because their inhabitants fell into corruption and forgot the Creator's plan of life. The deity Taiowa, the Creator, is associated with the sun, and his nephew Sotuknang carried out the creation of the worlds. Spider Grandmother (Kokyangwuti) is a major figure who helped create life and guided the people.

Deities and spirits

Important figures include Masau'u (Masaw), the guardian of the Fourth World and a god of death, fire, and the earth, who met the emerging people and permitted them to settle in exchange for following the Hopi way of life. The kachinas (katsinam) are spirit messengers and ancestors associated with rain, clouds, and natural forces; they are represented in ceremonies by masked dancers and in carved figures.

Ceremonial life

Hopi ceremonialism follows a calendar tied to the solstices and the agricultural cycle of corn growing in a dry land. Ceremonies such as Soyal (winter solstice), Niman (the home dance, when the kachinas depart), and the Snake Dance are organised by clans and religious societies responsible for particular rituals and traditions.
