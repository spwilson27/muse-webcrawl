Overview

Triangulation is a method of determining positions by building a network of triangles: if one side of a triangle (a baseline) is measured directly and the angles at its ends are observed, the whole triangle — and chains of triangles sharing its sides — can be computed by trigonometry. It turned surveying from a craft of chained distances into an exact science.

The Dutch mathematician Gemma Frisius described the method in 1533, and his countryman Snellius first executed a large arc measurement with it in 1615. The great national surveys of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — in France, India and Britain — were triangulation networks of hilltop stations, and high-order geodesy still uses the same geometry, now observed by satellites rather than theodolites.

The same principle, run in reverse, fixes a position from angles to known landmarks — the resection and intersection problems of everyday surveying and coastal navigation.
