Eastern State Penitentiary

Eastern State Penitentiary is a former American prison in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. Designed by John Haviland and opened on 25 October 1829, it was the first prison in the world built to implement the "separate system" of solitary confinement and became the model for hundreds of prisons internationally. It operated until 1971 and is now a museum and U.S. National Historic Landmark.

Design and philosophy

The prison was conceived by reformers of the Philadelphia Society for Alleviating the Miseries of Public Prisons, who believed criminals would become penitent — hence "penitentiary" — through solitude, labour and reflection. Haviland's radial plan had seven single-storey cell blocks radiating from a central rotunda, each cell with its own small exercise yard, running water and central heating — amenities the White House did not yet have. The Gothic Revival exterior was intended to intimidate.

History

The separate system drew visitors and imitators from around the world, including Charles Dickens, who described its psychological effects in American Notes (1842). Overcrowding undermined solitary confinement, and congregate workshops were introduced; the separate system was formally abandoned in 1913. The prison later held notorious inmates including bank robber Willie Sutton and gangster Al Capone, whose furnished cell survives. It closed in 1971.

Museum

After years of abandonment, the site was stabilised and opened for tours in 1994. It is known for its annual Halloween attraction, "Terror Behind the Walls," and for art installations and exhibits on mass incarceration.
