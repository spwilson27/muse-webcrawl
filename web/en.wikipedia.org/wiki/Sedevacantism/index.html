Sedevacantism

Sedevacantism is a traditionalist Catholic position which holds that the present occupier of the Holy See is not a valid pope because of the alleged heresies of the purported pontiffs, and that, for lack of a valid pope, the See of Rome is vacant. The term derives from the Latin sede vacante, meaning "the see being vacant". Sedevacantists generally reject the legitimacy of the popes since the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), typically beginning with John XXIII or Paul VI.

History

The position arose from traditionalist opposition to the reforms of Vatican II, especially the revised liturgy, religious liberty and ecumenism. One of its earliest proponents was the Mexican Jesuit Joaquín Sáenz y Arriaga, whose 1971 book La nueva iglesia montiniana argued that Paul VI was not a true pope. In the United States, Francis Schuckardt founded the Congregation of Mary Immaculate Queen (CMRI) in the late 1960s. Some sedevacantist groups maintain their own bishops, consecrated through lines deriving from Archbishop Ngô Đình Thục or from bishops consecrated by sedevacantist prelates.

Positions and divisions

Sedevacantists differ among themselves: "totalists" hold that all Vatican II popes are invalid and the hierarchy devoid of ordinary jurisdiction, while sedeprivationists (followers of Michel-Louis Guérard des Lauriers's Cassiciacum thesis) hold that the popes are popes materially but not formally. A small minority, the conclavists, have elected their own popes. The movement has always been numerically small, though its chapels and religious orders exist in many countries.
