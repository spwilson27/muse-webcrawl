The Spanish Prisoner is a classic confidence trick that originated in the 19th century. In its original form, the con artist tells the victim that he is in contact with a wealthy man secretly imprisoned in Spain under a false identity, and seeks money to bribe the guards and free him; in exchange, the victim is promised a share of the prisoner's fortune or the hand of his beautiful daughter. Once the victim pays, further complications require more money, until the fraudster disappears.

History

The scam was described in newspaper reports and crime literature from the mid-1800s and was conducted by letter, often with elaborate forged documents. Eugène François Vidocq mentioned a version in his memoirs. The plot requires secrecy — the victim must not alert authorities — which also prevents them from reporting the fraud once they realise they have been cheated.

Modern forms

The Spanish Prisoner is the direct ancestor of modern advance-fee fraud, in which a stranger promises a large sum in return for a smaller up-front payment. Variants include the "Nigerian prince" or 419 scam (named after a section of Nigeria's criminal code), lottery frauds, and romance scams; all preserve the original structure of a large promised reward, escalating fees, and enforced secrecy.

In popular culture

The scam lends its name to David Mamet's 1997 film The Spanish Prisoner, a neo-noir about an elaborate con game.
