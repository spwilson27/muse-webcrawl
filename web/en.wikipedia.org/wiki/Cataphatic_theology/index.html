Cataphatic theology

Cataphatic theology (from Greek κατάφασις kataphasis, "affirmation") is theology that uses positive terms to describe God — speaking of what God is, rather than what God is not. It affirms, for example, that God is good, wise, loving, powerful, and a Trinity of persons, drawing on Scripture, liturgy, and religious experience as genuine, if analogical, sources of knowledge about the divine nature. It stands in contrast and complement to apophatic theology, the "negative way", which stresses that God ultimately transcends all human concepts.

Approach

Cataphatic theology underlies most doctrinal affirmation, preaching, prayer, and hymnody in Christianity and other theistic traditions: the creeds, for instance, are thoroughly cataphatic in confessing God as "Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth". Theologians such as Thomas Aquinas held that positive terms apply to God not univocally (in exactly the same sense as to creatures) nor merely equivocally, but analogically — they truly describe God, though in an infinitely higher mode. The 5th–6th-century writer Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, whose works founded the apophatic tradition, nonetheless wrote extensively in the cataphatic mode in The Divine Names.

Balance with the apophatic way

Most classical traditions combine both approaches: affirmations about God are made, and then qualified by the recognition that God exceeds them. In Eastern Orthodox theology, cataphatic statements are considered a first step suited to beginners, while apophatic negation leads more deeply into the mystery of God; in the West, scholastic theology integrated the two in the doctrine of analogy. Cataphatic language also shapes devotional practice, since images, icons, and metaphors — shepherd, king, rock, light — give believers concrete forms with which to approach God in prayer.
