The Marburg Colloquy was a meeting at Marburg Castle, Hesse, from 1 to 4 October 1529, convened by Philip the Magnanimous, Landgrave of Hesse, to forge a political and theological alliance among the Protestant reformers against the Habsburg emperor Charles V. It brought together Martin Luther and Philipp Melanchthon from Wittenberg, and Huldrych Zwingli and Johannes Oecolampadius from the Swiss and Upper German reformations, among others.

The dispute over the Eucharist

The parties agreed on fourteen of the fifteen Marburg Articles — covering the Trinity, the person of Christ, original sin, faith, baptism, confession, and civil government. They broke on the fifteenth: the mode of Christ's presence in the Lord's Supper. Luther, famously chalking hoc est corpus meum ("this is my body") on the table, insisted on a real, oral presence of Christ's body in the bread; Zwingli held the elements to be signs commemorating an absent, ascended Christ and "is" to mean "signifies." Neither would yield, and Luther refused Zwingli's extended hand of fellowship, reportedly saying "You have a different spirit from ours."

Consequences

The failure at Marburg prevented a united Protestant front at the 1530 Diet of Augsburg and entrenched the lasting division between Lutheran and Reformed (Zwinglian/Calvinist) traditions over sacramental theology — a divide that later agreements such as the Wittenberg Concord (1536) only partly bridged, and that the Leuenberg Agreement of 1973 finally sought to close. The colloquy remains the emblematic moment when the Reformation's two great streams failed to unite.
