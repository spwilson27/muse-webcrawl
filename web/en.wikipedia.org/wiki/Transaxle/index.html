Transaxle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transaxle is a single mechanical device that combines the functions of an automobile's transmission, axle, and differential into one integrated assembly. The name is a portmanteau of transmission and axle. Transaxles are used wherever the driven axle is at the same end of the vehicle as the engine — most commonly in front-engine, front-wheel-drive cars, but also in rear- and mid-engine layouts.

Front-engine, front-wheel drive

In the dominant modern configuration, the engine is mounted transversely (sideways) at the front, and the transaxle sits beside or beneath it, driving the front wheels directly through short half-shafts. Combining the gearbox and final drive in one casing saves space, weight, and cost, and eliminates the long propeller shaft of a front-engine, rear-drive car. Both manual and automatic transaxles are produced in very large numbers; automatic versions integrate the torque converter and planetary gearsets into the same housing.

Rear-engine and mid-engine layouts

Rear-engined cars such as the original Volkswagen Beetle and the Porsche 911 use a transaxle behind the rear axle line. In another layout, found on some high-performance front-engine cars (notably many Corvettes, Ferraris and the Porsche 924/944/968), the gearbox is moved to the rear axle and combined with the differential as a transaxle, with the engine at the front connected by a torque tube. This distributes mass more evenly between the axles, improving handling balance, at the cost of complexity.

Design considerations

Because the transmission and differential share a casing, they usually share a lubricant, so transaxle fluids must suit both gear synchronisers and the final-drive gears. In front-wheel-drive cars the transaxle's output shafts are often of unequal length, which can cause torque steer under hard acceleration; manufacturers counter this with equal-length shafts, intermediate bearings, or electronic controls.

Electric vehicles

Battery-electric vehicles revive the concept in simplified form: most use a single-speed reduction gearbox integrated with the electric motor and differential in a unit often called an e-axle, functionally analogous to a transaxle.
