Othello

Othello, the Moor of Venice
Written: c. 1603
Author: William Shakespeare
First performed: 1 November 1604, Whitehall Palace, London
Published: 1622 (first quarto); 1623 (First Folio)
Genre: Tragedy

Othello, the Moor of Venice (full title: The Tragedy of Othello, the Moor of Venice) is a tragedy written by William Shakespeare, probably in 1603. It is based on the story "Un Capitano Moro" by Cinthio (Giovanni Battista Giraldi), a disciple of Boccaccio, first published in 1565. The play revolves around two central characters: Othello, a Moorish general in the service of the Venetian state, and his treacherous ensign, Iago.

Plot

Othello, a respected general, secretly marries Desdemona, the daughter of the Venetian senator Brabantio. Passed over for promotion in favour of Michael Cassio, Iago plots revenge. When Othello is sent to Cyprus to repel a Turkish invasion, Iago insinuates that Desdemona is unfaithful with Cassio, contriving "proof" in the form of her handkerchief, planted in Cassio's lodgings. Consumed by jealousy, Othello smothers Desdemona in her bed. Iago's wife Emilia then reveals the deception; Othello, realising his error, wounds Iago and kills himself. Iago is arrested, and Cassio is left in command.

Themes

The play explores jealousy, love, betrayal, racism and the outsider's place in Venetian society. Othello's identity as a Moor — a black African in the service of a white state — has made the play a central text in discussions of race in early modern literature. Iago, whose motives have been debated for centuries (Coleridge famously called his evil "motiveless malignity"), is among Shakespeare's most compelling villains.

Performance history

The play was first performed by the King's Men at Whitehall Palace on 1 November 1604. For most of its stage history Othello was played by white actors in blackface; notable interpreters include Edmund Kean, Edwin Booth, Laurence Olivier and, in the 20th century, Paul Robeson, whose 1930 London and 1943 Broadway performances were landmarks. Verdi's opera Otello (1887), with a libretto by Arrigo Boito, is among the greatest operatic adaptations of Shakespeare.

Sources and text

Shakespeare took his story from Cinthio's Gli Hecatommithi, altering it substantially — compressing the action, inventing characters such as Roderigo and Brabantio, and giving the heroine (called Disdemona by Cinthio) a far more substantial role. Two early texts survive: the first quarto of 1622 and the First Folio of 1623, which differ by about 160 lines and many readings; modern editions generally combine them.
