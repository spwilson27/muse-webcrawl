Woodrow Wilson

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thomas Woodrow Wilson (28 December 1856 – 3 February 1924) was the 28th president of the United States (1913–1921), a political scientist and former president of Princeton University and governor of New Jersey. As president he enacted the “New Freedom” programme (tariff reduction, the Federal Reserve System, antitrust legislation and the federal income tax after the Sixteenth Amendment), then led the United States into the First World War (1917) and staked his presidency on a liberal peace.

His Fourteen Points (January 1918) proclaimed open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, self-determination and a “general association of nations”; at Paris in 1919 he secured the League of Nations Covenant within the Treaty of Versailles, compromising on reparations and colonial mandates to do so. The Senate refused ratification, partly over reservations Wilson instructed his supporters to reject; a national speaking tour to force the issue ended with his collapse and a severe stroke (October 1919) that left him incapacitated for much of his remaining term. He received the Nobel Peace Prize for 1919.

Reassessment

Wilson's reputation has been substantially revised downward in recent scholarship and public debate for his racial policies: his administration segregated the federal civil service, he screened The Birth of a Nation at the White House, and his “self-determination” was not extended to colonised peoples (Ho Chi Minh's petition at Versailles was ignored). Institutions including Princeton (2020) have removed his name from buildings, while historians continue to credit his role in creating the modern presidency and the liberal international order.
