Port and starboard

Port and starboard are nautical terms of orientation that refer, respectively, to the left and right sides of a vessel (ship or aircraft), when facing forward (toward the bow). Because they are fixed to the vessel rather than to the observer, they are unambiguous regardless of which way a person aboard is facing — unlike "left" and "right" — which makes them essential for safe communication at sea.

Etymology

Starboard derives from the Old English steorbord, the side of the ship on which the steering oar (steerboard) was mounted before the invention of the centreline rudder. Most sailors being right-handed, the steering oar was worked over the right side of the stern. The opposite side was originally called larboard, from a loading-side term; because "larboard" and "starboard" sounded dangerously alike in noise and wind, the Royal Navy ordered the use of port instead in 1844 — the left side being the side laid against the quay (port) when in harbour, since the steering oar side had to be kept clear of the wharf. The United States Navy adopted the term shortly afterwards.

Usage

The terms describe both directions and locations aboard: the port side, port bow, port quarter, the port engine, and so on. Under the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea, vessels display navigation lights on these sides: a red light on the port side and a green light on the starboard side, with a white masthead light — an arrangement extended to aircraft as well, which use the same red (left/port) and green (right/starboard) wingtip lights. Aviation also applies port and starboard to seating and engine numbering. In rowing and other nautical traditions the same fixed-side convention prevents confusion in shouted orders.

Related terms

The corresponding directions toward the ends of the vessel are forward (toward the bow) and aft (toward the stern); athwartships describes the sideways axis, and amidships the middle. A memory aid popular among sailors is that "port" and "left" both have four letters, and that the (short) word port matches the red of port wine left at the table's passing side in the old mnemonic "the port is left at the table."
