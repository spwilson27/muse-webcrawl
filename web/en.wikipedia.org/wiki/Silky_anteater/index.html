The silky anteater, or pygmy anteater (Cyclopes didactylus), is the smallest of the anteaters and the only living member of the family Cyclopedidae. A nocturnal, strictly arboreal mammal of Central and South American rainforests, it spends the day curled asleep in the canopy—often in silk cotton trees, whose seed-pod fibres match its golden fur—and feeds at night almost entirely on ants. Its scientific name refers to its forefeet, which bear two large claws ("didactylus", two-toed).

Description

Weighing only 175–400 grams, the silky anteater has dense, soft, golden-yellow to grey fur, a prehensile tail as long as its body, and strongly curved claws on the forefeet used for tearing into ant nests and defending itself. Like other anteaters it has no teeth and a long sticky tongue; its snout is relatively short compared with the giant anteater's. It rarely descends to the ground, where it moves awkwardly.

Behaviour and ecology

Silky anteaters are solitary. An individual may eat thousands of ants (estimates run to several thousand per night) from dozens of trees, avoiding soldier-ant colonies and returning to nests in rotation, which makes them important predators regulating arboreal ant populations; they do not destroy the colonies they feed from. Females give birth to a single young, carried on the mother's back or left in a leaf-lined hollow. Predators include harpy eagles, owls and ocelots. Defensive posture—rearing up and lashing with the foreclaws—has been compared to a miniature boxer.

Taxonomy

Cyclopes didactylus was described by Linnaeus in 1758 as Myrmecophaga didactyla. A 2017 taxonomic review of museum specimens and DNA proposed splitting the silky anteater into as many as seven species (including C. catellus, C. dorsalis, C. ida, C. rufus, C. thomasi and C. xinguensis), a rearrangement still being evaluated by mammalogists.
