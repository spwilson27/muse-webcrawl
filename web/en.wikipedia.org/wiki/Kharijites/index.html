Kharijites

The Kharijites (Arabic: الخوارج, al-Khawārij, singular Khārijī, "those who went out") were an early Islamic sect that emerged during the First Fitna (656–661), the first civil war in the Muslim community. They broke with the fourth caliph, Ali ibn Abi Talib, over his acceptance of arbitration to settle his conflict with Muawiyah I, the governor of Syria, at the Battle of Siffin in 657.

Origins

At Siffin, when Ali agreed to negotiate, a group of his supporters seceded, declaring lā ḥukma illā li-llāh — "judgment belongs to God alone" — and holding that submitting God's cause to human arbitration was apostasy. Defeated by Ali at the Battle of Nahrawan in 658, the Kharijites survived in scattered communities, and in 661 a Kharijite, Abd al-Rahman ibn Muljam, assassinated Ali in Kufa.

Doctrine

The Kharijites held that any Muslim, regardless of lineage or race, could become caliph if morally upright, and that a caliph who sinned must be deposed or killed. They considered grave sinners to be unbelievers (takfir), a doctrine that led the more radical branches, such as the Azariqa, to sanction violence against other Muslims. More moderate groups, notably the Ibadis, rejected such violence; Ibadi Islam survives today principally in Oman and parts of North Africa, although Ibadis generally do not identify themselves as Kharijites.

History

Kharijite revolts troubled the Umayyad and early Abbasid caliphates for two centuries, and Kharijite-leaning communities established states in North Africa (the Rustamid imamate of Tahert) and in Arabia and Persia. Modern scholars have drawn comparisons between Kharijite doctrines, especially takfir, and those of some contemporary militant movements, although such comparisons are contested.
