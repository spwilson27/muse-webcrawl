Crush syndrome (also termed Bywaters syndrome) is a medical condition characterized by major shock and kidney failure after a crushing injury to skeletal muscle. It arises when prolonged pressure on a limb or other body part is released, allowing breakdown products of damaged muscle cells — myoglobin, potassium, phosphate and creatine kinase — to flood the systemic circulation. The condition is a form of traumatic rhabdomyolysis and is a leading cause of death and disability among survivors rescued from collapsed buildings after earthquakes and bombings.

History

Although isolated descriptions appeared earlier — the condition was noted after the 1908 Messina earthquake and in German medical literature during the First World War — crush syndrome was defined by the British physician Eric Bywaters and his colleague Desmond Beall, who described casualties of the London Blitz in 1941. They linked prolonged limb compression to subsequent renal failure and identified myoglobin in the urine of victims. The syndrome was subsequently recognized as a major problem in the Korean and Vietnam wars and in later disasters, including the 1988 Armenian earthquake and the 1995 Kobe earthquake.

Pathophysiology

Sustained compression impairs blood flow to muscle, causing ischemic necrosis. While the pressure is maintained, necrotic products are largely sequestered in the limb, a phenomenon sometimes called the "smiling death": a casualty may appear stable while trapped, then deteriorate rapidly after extrication as reperfusion washes potassium, myoglobin, phosphate and organic acids into the circulation. Consequences include hypovolemic shock (fluid shifts into injured muscle), hyperkalemia with fatal cardiac arrhythmias, metabolic acidosis, and acute kidney injury as myoglobin precipitates in renal tubules — a risk greatly increased by dehydration and acidic urine. Local injury may also produce compartment syndrome, requiring fasciotomy.

Management

Treatment ideally begins before or during extrication. Large-volume intravenous crystalloid — commonly isotonic saline started at rates around 1–1.5 litres per hour in adults — is the cornerstone, diluting myoglobin, maintaining renal perfusion and limiting hyperkalemia. Forced alkaline diuresis with sodium bicarbonate and osmotic diuretics such as mannitol have historically been used, though evidence for their superiority over fluids alone is limited. Hyperkalemia is treated with calcium, insulin and glucose, and other measures; severe or refractory cases require hemodialysis. Early field treatment, sometimes including amputation of a non-viable limb in extremis, and rapid transport are critical to outcome.

Epidemiology

Crush syndrome complicates a significant fraction of earthquake hospitalizations; reported incidence among injured survivors ranges from roughly 2% to 15%, and a large proportion of those affected develop acute kidney injury, of whom many require dialysis — making crush-related renal failure a defining logistical challenge of disaster medicine.
