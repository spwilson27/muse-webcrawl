Clear Script

The Clear Script (Mongolian: Тодо бичиг, todo bichig, literally "clear script"; Manchu: ᡨᠣᠯᡤᠣ ᡥᡝᡵᡤᡝᠨ, tolgo hergen) is an alphabet created in 1648 by the Oirat Mongol Buddhist monk Zaya Pandita for the Oirat language. It was developed on the basis of the traditional Mongolian script with the goal of distinguishing every sound in the spoken language and of easing the transcription of Tibetan and Sanskrit.

History

Zaya Pandita, educated in Tibet and a translator of Buddhist texts, found that the classical Mongolian script left many vowels and consonant distinctions ambiguous: the same graphemes served for several different sounds, which made texts difficult to read aloud accurately. Working in 1648, he reformed the script by modifying letter shapes, adding diacritics and new letters, and fixing each letter to a single sound value wherever possible. The result was called "clear" (todo) because of its greater precision.

Use

The script was adopted by the Oirat Mongols of Dzungaria and the Volga region, and was used for religious literature, chronicles, letters, and translations from Tibetan. The Kalmyks of Russia continued to use the Clear Script in print into the Soviet era, until it was replaced by a Cyrillic-based alphabet in the 1920s–1940s. Among the Oirats of Xinjiang in China, the script — often called simply the Oirat script — remains in limited use alongside the classical Mongolian script.

Clear Script letters share the vertical orientation and cursive joining of the parent Mongolian script, but many vowels that are indistinguishable in the classical script (such as o/u and ö/ü distinctions in certain positions) are written with distinct forms.
