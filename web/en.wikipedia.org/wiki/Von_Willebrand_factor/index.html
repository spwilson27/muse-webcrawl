Von Willebrand factor

Von Willebrand factor (VWF) is a blood glycoprotein that plays a central role in hemostasis (blood clotting). It is a large multimeric protein produced by endothelial cells and megakaryocytes and stored in Weibel–Palade bodies and platelet α-granules. VWF is named after the Finnish physician Erik Adolf von Willebrand, who described the associated bleeding disorder in 1926.

Function

VWF performs two main functions. First, it mediates platelet adhesion to sites of vascular injury: under high shear stress, VWF unfolds and binds exposed subendothelial collagen and the platelet receptor glycoprotein Ib, tethering platelets to the wound, and it also supports platelet aggregation via glycoprotein IIb/IIIa. Second, it binds and stabilizes coagulation factor VIII in the circulation, protecting it from premature clearance and proteolysis. The enzyme ADAMTS13 regulates VWF size by cleaving its ultra-large multimers.

Clinical significance

Deficiency or dysfunction of VWF causes von Willebrand disease, the most common inherited bleeding disorder, affecting up to 1% of the population, characterized by mucocutaneous bleeding, easy bruising, nosebleeds, and heavy menstrual bleeding. It is classified into type 1 (partial quantitative deficiency), type 2 (qualitative defects, with subtypes), and type 3 (near-complete deficiency). Conversely, failure to cleave VWF due to ADAMTS13 deficiency causes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), in which platelet-rich microthrombi form in small vessels. Elevated VWF levels are also a marker of endothelial activation and are associated with increased cardiovascular risk.

Treatment

Mild von Willebrand disease is often treated with desmopressin (DDAVP), which releases stored VWF from endothelium; more severe cases use plasma-derived or recombinant VWF concentrates.
