Underwater diving

Underwater diving, as a human activity, is the practice of descending below the water's surface to interact with the environment. It may be done for recreation, scientific research, commercial work, public safety or military purposes. Divers are supplied with breathing gas either by scuba (self-contained underwater breathing apparatus), by surface-supplied equipment connected to a hose, or not at all in the case of freediving, where the diver relies on a single breath.

Modes of diving

Scuba diving uses a portable gas supply carried by the diver and is the dominant mode for recreation, science and much military work. Surface-supplied diving, using an umbilical from the surface, predominates in commercial offshore and construction diving, and includes saturation diving, in which divers live under pressure in a habitat for days or weeks. Atmospheric diving suits keep the occupant at surface pressure inside an articulated armoured suit. Breath-hold freediving is practised both recreationally and competitively.

History

Humans have dived for food, pearls and sponges since prehistory, and diving bells were used from the sixteenth century. The standard diving dress with a copper helmet, developed in the nineteenth century by Augustus Siebe, enabled industrial salvage and construction. Demand regulators and the Aqua-Lung of Jacques Cousteau and Émile Gagnan (1943) freed the diver from the hose and created modern scuba. Mixed-gas, rebreather and saturation techniques extended depth capability through the twentieth century.

Physiology and hazards

Pressure increases by about one bar every ten metres of depth, compressing gas spaces and driving inert gas into solution in tissues. The chief hazards include barotrauma of the ears, sinuses and lungs; decompression sickness from ascending with excess dissolved gas; nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity at depth; drowning; and hypothermia. Training, dive planning, decompression tables and dive computers manage these risks. The human body is well adapted to short immersion but poorly suited to prolonged exposure, so diving remains a fundamentally equipment-dependent activity.
