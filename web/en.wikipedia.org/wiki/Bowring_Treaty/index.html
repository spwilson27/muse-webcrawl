Signed18 April 1855, Bangkok
PartiesUnited Kingdom and the Kingdom of Siam

TypeTreaty of friendship and commerce

The Bowring Treaty was a treaty signed between the United Kingdom and the Kingdom of Siam on 18 April 1855. Negotiated by Sir John Bowring, governor of Hong Kong, with King Mongkut, it liberalised foreign trade in Siam and is often taken as the starting point of the modern Thai economy — opening the country to world markets while imposing the unequal terms characteristic of 19th-century treaty diplomacy.

Terms

The treaty abolished the royal trade monopolies and the system of measurement duties, replacing them with a low fixed import duty of 3 per cent and fixed export duties. British subjects gained the right to buy and sell freely in all Siamese ports, to reside in Bangkok, to acquire land outside the city walls, and to be tried in British consular courts — extraterritoriality. The opium trade was regulated through a government farm, and British ships could import salt, fish and other previously restricted goods.

Consequences

Rice exports expanded dramatically in the decades that followed, transforming the Chao Phraya delta into one of the world's great rice bowls and integrating Siam into the global economy. Similar treaties were soon signed with other Western powers and Japan. The extraterritoriality and tariff limits of the "unequal treaty" regime endured into the 20th century, and Mongkut and his successor Chulalongkorn used diplomatic engagement with the treaty powers as part of the strategy that preserved Siam's independence between British Burma and French Indochina.
