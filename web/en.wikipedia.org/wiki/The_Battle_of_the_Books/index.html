The Battle of the Books

The Battle of the Books is a short prose satire by Jonathan Swift, written in 1697 and published in 1704 together with A Tale of a Tub. Its full title is A Full and True Account of the Battel Fought last Friday, Between the Antient and the Modern Books in St. James's Library. The work is Swift's contribution to the so-called Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns — the debate, imported from France, over whether contemporary learning and literature had surpassed the achievements of classical antiquity.

Background

In England the quarrel centred on Swift's patron, Sir William Temple, whose essay Upon Ancient and Modern Learning (1690) praised the ancients and, disastrously, the spurious Letters of Phalaris as the finest classical prose. The scholars William Wotton and Richard Bentley answered for the moderns, and Bentley's dissertation proved the letters were a forgery, humiliating Temple's side. Swift, then Temple's secretary, defended his patron in the Battle, describing a night on which the books in the royal library come to life and fight a mock-heroic war, with authors hurling volumes and ink at one another in the manner of Homeric heroes.

The fable of the spider and the bee

The most famous episode interrupts the battle: a spider, boasting of the mathematics of its self-spun web, is answered by a bee, who ranges freely over nature's fields and produces honey and wax — sweetness and light. Aesop, judging the dispute, pronounces for the bee and hence for the ancients, whose works draw on universal nature rather than on self-generated dirt and poison. The phrase "sweetness and light", which Matthew Arnold later adopted in Culture and Anarchy, derives from this passage. True to the satire's form, the manuscript "breaks off" mid-battle, leaving the outcome comically unresolved.
