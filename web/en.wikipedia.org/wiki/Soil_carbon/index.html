Soil carbon

Soil carbon is the carbon held within the soil, globally in the form of soil organic matter and inorganic carbonates. Soil carbon plays an important role in the global carbon cycle, with soils storing more carbon than the atmosphere and all living plants combined — roughly 2,300 gigatonnes in the top two metres, of which organic carbon accounts for about 1,500 gigatonnes.

Dark topsoil rich in organic carbon overlying a paler subsoil

Forms

Soil organic carbon (SOC) derives from plant and animal residues at various stages of decomposition: living roots and microbes, fresh litter, labile compounds, and stabilised humus that can persist for centuries to millennia, protected by aggregation and binding to minerals. Soil inorganic carbon consists mainly of carbonate minerals, dominant in arid soils.

Dynamics

Carbon enters soil chiefly through plant photosynthesis, root exudates and litterfall, and leaves through microbial respiration, erosion and leaching. Turnover times range from days for sugars to thousands of years for mineral-associated organic matter. Permafrost soils hold a vast, climate-sensitive store; thawing exposes previously frozen carbon to decomposition.

Management and climate

Land use change — deforestation, drainage of peatlands, tillage — has depleted soil carbon stocks. Practices that rebuild it include cover cropping, reduced tillage, agroforestry and peatland restoration, promoted by initiatives such as the "4 per 1000" programme. Because losses are easier than gains, soil carbon is both a mitigation opportunity and a risk in climate change. Soil carbon also underpins soil quality, governing water retention, structure and nutrient cycling.

Measurement

Stocks are estimated from soil surveys, spectroscopy, eddy-covariance flux towers and process models. Vertical distribution matters: most assessments sample the top 30 cm, matching the layers defined by soil horizons.
