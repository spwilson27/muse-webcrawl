Derailleur

TypeBicycle gearing mechanism
ComponentsFront and rear derailleurs, shifters, chain, cassette, chainrings
First practical designsEarly 20th century

A derailleur is a variable-ratio bicycle gearing system consisting of a chain, multiple sprockets, and a mechanism to move the chain from one sprocket to another. Although some derailleur systems were designed in the late nineteenth century, derailleurs became the dominant gear-shifting system on bicycles in the later twentieth century. The word is from the French dérailleur, originally a railway term for a device that derails a train.

How it works

The rear derailleur consists of a cage holding two small toothed wheels (jockey wheels) through which the chain passes. A spring-loaded parallelogram body, moved laterally by a cable or electronic signal from the shifter, pushes the chain sideways so it climbs or drops between the sprockets (cogs) of the cassette on the rear wheel, while the cage takes up chain slack. The front derailleur is simpler: a metal cage above the chainrings pushes the chain between the two or three chainrings at the cranks.

Modern systems use indexed shifting, in which each click of the shifter moves the derailleur by a precise increment matched to the sprocket spacing. Electronic systems, introduced by Shimano, SRAM, and Campagnolo in the 2000s and 2010s, replace the cable with electric motors and wired or wireless signals.

History

Early derailleur-like devices appeared in the 1890s, and French riders such as Paul de Vivie championed variable gears for touring. The derailleur was long banned from the Tour de France by Henri Desgrange, who prized single-speed toughness; it was finally permitted in 1937. Tullio Campagnolo's parallelogram rear derailleur of the early 1950s set the pattern still used today, and Japanese manufacturers Shimano and SunTour popularized reliable indexed systems in the 1980s.

Alternatives

Derailleurs compete with hub gears, which enclose the mechanism in the rear hub, and with single-speed and fixed-gear drivetrains. Derailleurs offer the widest gear range and lowest weight but expose the mechanism to dirt and damage, and shifting under heavy load is limited because the chain must be moving to change sprockets.
