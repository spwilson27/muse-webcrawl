Serapeum of Alexandria

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Serapeum of Alexandria was a great temple to the Greco-Egyptian god Serapis in Alexandria, Egypt. Built under Ptolemy III Euergetes (reigned 246–222 BC) on a rocky plateau in the Egyptian quarter of the city (Rhakotis), it was described in antiquity as the most magnificent temple in the western world after the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus. A "daughter library" of the Great Library of Alexandria was associated with the temple.

History

Serapis combined aspects of Osiris and Apis with Zeus, Hades and Dionysus, and was promoted by Ptolemy I Soter as a unifying cult for Greeks and Egyptians. The Ptolemaic Serapeum replaced an earlier sanctuary of the royal necropolis area. Excavations revealed foundation plaques of Ptolemy III naming the temple and a Harpocrates shrine. The complex included courts, a nilometer, and rooms connected with the library collection stored there; ancient sources suggest tens of thousands of scrolls may have been kept in the Serapeum after the main library's shrinkage.

Destruction

In AD 391, after the Emperor Theodosius I decreed the suppression of pagan cults, a Christian mob led by Patriarch Theophilus stormed and destroyed the Serapeum, smashing the cult statue of Serapis. The destruction is narrated by several late antique authors, including Rufinus, Socrates Scholasticus and Eunapius, and became a famous symbol of the end of paganism in the Roman world. The column known as Pompey's Pillar still stands on the site.
