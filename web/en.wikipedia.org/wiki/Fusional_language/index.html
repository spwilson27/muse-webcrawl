Fusional language

Fusional language
TypeMorphological typology
ExamplesLatin, Greek, Sanskrit, Russian, German, Spanish
Contrasts withAgglutinative, isolating languages

A fusional language is a type of synthetic language, distinguished from agglutinative languages by its tendency to use single inflectional morphemes to denote multiple grammatical, syntactic, or semantic features. For example, the Spanish verb form habló ("he/she spoke") encodes tense, aspect, person and number in a single ending that cannot be segmented into separate pieces with one meaning each.

Fusional languages often show extensive morphophonemic alternation (such as vowel changes and stem modifications) at morpheme boundaries, making morphemes hard to segment. Most Indo-European languages are fusional to some degree, including Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, the Slavic languages, German and the Romance languages; English retains only traces of fusion (e.g. sang, geese).

Declension and conjugation

In fusional languages, a word's grammatical category membership (its declension class for nouns, conjugation class for verbs) largely determines the inflections it takes. A single ending typically "fuses" several categories at once: a Latin noun ending simultaneously signals case, number and declension class, but not gender, which is inherent to the noun. This contrasts with agglutinative languages such as Turkish or Finnish, where each feature tends to be expressed by a separate, easily segmented suffix.
