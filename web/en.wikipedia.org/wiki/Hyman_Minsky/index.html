Hyman Minsky

Hyman Philip Minsky (September 23, 1919 – October 24, 1996) was an American economist at Washington University in St. Louis and a scholar at the Levy Economics Institute of Bard College. His research analyzed the causes of financial crises and the possibility that financial markets generate endogenous instability. Minsky is best known for the financial instability hypothesis and for the phrase, widely quoted after 2008, that "stability is destabilizing." The onset of the 2007–2008 financial crisis was popularly dubbed a "Minsky moment."

Financial instability hypothesis

Minsky argued that within a period of economic prosperity, the financial system shifts progressively through three states of borrowing. In the hedge phase, borrowers can repay both principal and interest from cash flow. In the speculative phase, borrowers can service interest but must roll over principal. In the Ponzi phase, borrowers can cover neither and depend on rising asset prices to refinance. As successful risk taking is rewarded, leverage grows and balance sheets become fragile, so a modest shock — a rise in interest rates or falling asset prices — can trigger defaults, forced selling, and a debt deflation of the kind described by Irving Fisher.

Career and influence

Minsky was educated at the University of Chicago, where his parents' socialist circles exposed him to figures such as Oskar Lange, and received his PhD from Harvard in 1954, studying with Joseph Schumpeter and Wassily Leontief. He taught at Brown University, the University of California, Berkeley, and, from 1965 until his retirement in 1990, at Washington University in St. Louis. His major works include John Maynard Keynes (1975), Stabilizing an Unstable Economy (1986), and The Financial Instability Hypothesis (1992). Long associated with post-Keynesian economics and advocacy of the employer of last resort, he received renewed attention after the financial crisis of 2008, when his framework was widely cited in commentary on the subprime mortgage collapse.
