Oceanic languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Oceanic languages are a well-established branch of the Austronesian language family, comprising some 450 languages spoken by about two million people in the islands of the Pacific Ocean — in Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia — as well as parts of coastal New Guinea. They descend from Proto-Oceanic, spoken by the Lapita culture in the Bismarck Archipelago roughly 3,500–3,000 years ago.

Classification

Oceanic is generally divided into major subgroups including the Admiralties languages, the St Matthias group, Western Oceanic (including the North New Guinea and Papuan Tip clusters), Southeast Solomonic, Southern Oceanic (Vanuatu and New Caledonia), and Central Pacific (including Rotuman, Fijian and the Polynesian languages), with a number of Micronesian languages. The diversity is greatest in Melanesia, especially in Papua New Guinea and Vanuatu, which has the highest density of languages per capita in the world.

Features

Proto-Oceanic is reconstructed with a modest consonant inventory and five vowels; many daughter languages have very small phoneme inventories — Rotuman and Hawaiian are famous examples. Common grammatical features include possessive classifiers distinguishing food, drink and general possessions; inclusive/exclusive distinctions in first-person pronouns; and serial verb constructions. Many Oceanic languages are endangered, though some, such as Fijian, Samoan, Tongan, Māori and Hawaiian, have hundreds of thousands of speakers and official status.

History of study

The comparative study of Oceanic began with missionary grammars and was put on a rigorous footing by Otto Dempwolff in the early twentieth century; the modern subgrouping is associated especially with the work of Robert Blust, Malcolm Ross, Andrew Pawley and John Lynch. The Linguistic Society's reconstructions of Proto-Oceanic vocabulary — covering canoes, sails, fish and garden plants — have been used alongside archaeology to reconstruct the settlement of the Pacific.
