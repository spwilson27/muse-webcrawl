Beta (finance)

In finance, beta (β) is a measure of the volatility — or systematic risk — of a security or portfolio compared with the market as a whole. It is used in the capital asset pricing model (CAPM), which describes the relationship between systematic risk and expected return. A beta of 1 indicates that the security's price tends to move with the market; a beta greater than 1 means it is more volatile than the market; a beta less than 1 means it is less volatile; and a negative beta implies movement opposite to the market.

Calculation

Beta is calculated by regressing the asset's returns on the market's returns; it equals the covariance of the asset and market returns divided by the variance of the market returns:

β = Cov(Ra, Rm) / Var(Rm)

In CAPM, expected return is given by E(R) = Rf + β × (E(Rm) − Rf), where Rf is the risk-free rate. Betas are typically estimated from several years of monthly returns against a benchmark index such as the S&P 500. Levered beta reflects a company's debt; the Hamada equation relates levered and unlevered beta.

Interpretation and criticism

Defensive sectors such as utilities often have betas below 1, while technology stocks frequently exceed 1. Gold and some hedge-fund strategies may show near-zero or negative betas. Critics note that beta is backward-looking, unstable over time, and captures only market-related risk — empirical work (notably Fama and French) found beta alone poorly explains long-run return differences, motivating multi-factor models. Nevertheless beta remains a standard, widely quoted risk statistic.
