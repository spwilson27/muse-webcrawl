The Soviet–Afghan War was a protracted armed conflict fought in Afghanistan from 1979 to 1989. The Soviet Union deployed troops to support the communist Democratic Republic of Afghanistan against the Afghan mujahideen, who were backed by the United States, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and other countries. The war is often described as the Soviet Union's "Vietnam War" and contributed to the USSR's collapse.

Background

The Saur Revolution of 1978 brought the People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan to power; its radical reforms and repression provoked rebellions across the country. In December 1979, Soviet special forces killed president Hafizullah Amin in Operation Storm-333, installed Babrak Karmal, and began a large-scale intervention.

Course of the war

Soviet forces, peaking at over 100,000 troops, controlled cities and main roads but could not pacify the countryside. The mujahideen, supplied through Pakistan and increasingly armed with US-made Stinger anti-aircraft missiles from 1986, waged guerrilla warfare from mountain strongholds. The war devastated Afghanistan: millions became refugees in Pakistan and Iran, and landmines and bombing destroyed villages and irrigation systems.

Withdrawal and aftermath

Under Mikhail Gorbachev, the USSR signed the Geneva Accords in 1988 and completed its withdrawal on 15 February 1989. The government of Mohammad Najibullah survived until 1992, after which Afghanistan descended into civil war, eventually bringing the Taliban to power in 1996. Foreign fighters who had joined the jihad, including Osama bin Laden, formed networks that later became al-Qaeda.
