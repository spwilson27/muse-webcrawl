The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (ANILCA) is a United States federal law passed by the Congress and signed by President Jimmy Carter on December 2, 1980. The act protected over 157 million acres (64 million hectares) of federal lands in Alaska, more than doubling the size of the country's national park and wildlife refuge system and tripling the area designated as wilderness.

Background

The Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971 had left unresolved the question of which federal lands in Alaska would be set aside for conservation. Section 17(d)(2) of that act directed the Secretary of the Interior to withdraw up to 80 million acres for study as national parks, wildlife refuges, forests, and wild and scenic rivers. Prolonged negotiations between conservationists, the state of Alaska, Native corporations, and development interests shaped the legislation, which passed in the final weeks of the Carter administration.

Provisions

ANILCA designated new and expanded units of the National Park System, including Denali, Wrangell–St. Elias, Gates of the Arctic, and Glacier Bay. It created or enlarged national wildlife refuges, designated wilderness areas, and protected segments of wild and scenic rivers. The act also guaranteed subsistence hunting and fishing priority for rural Alaska residents on federal lands and protected access rights across conservation units.

Legacy

ANILCA remains one of the most significant conservation laws in American history. Its provisions continue to govern land management disputes in Alaska, including debates over oil development in the Arctic National Wildlife Refuge and road access through protected areas.
