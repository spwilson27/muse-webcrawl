Neoshamanism

Neoshamanism refers to modern forms of shamanism — new religious movements and spiritual practices that draw, selectively and often eclectically, on the traditions of indigenous peoples. It emerged mainly in the Western world from the 1960s onward, influenced by anthropology, the counterculture, and the New Age movement. Unlike the shamanisms of particular cultures, neoshamanic practice is typically individualised and decontextualised, taught through books, workshops, and retreats rather than inherited within a community.

Origins

Key influences include Mircea Eliade's comparative study Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy (1951), which portrayed shamanism as a universal archaic religion, and the controversial works of Carlos Castaneda on the Yaqui sorcerer Don Juan. Anthropologist Michael Harner synthesised what he presented as the cross-cultural essentials into "core shamanism", teaching techniques such as rhythmic drumming to enter a "shamanic state of consciousness", power animals, and soul retrieval through his Foundation for Shamanic Studies from 1979. The uptake of Amazonian ayahuasca practices and sweat-lodge ceremonies by Western seekers is part of the same current.

Practices

Common neoshamanic practices include drumming and rattling journeys to "non-ordinary reality", vision quests, working with spirit guides and power animals, healing rituals, and — in some circles — the ceremonial use of entheogens such as ayahuasca, peyote, or psilocybin mushrooms. Many practitioners frame the work in terms of personal healing, self-development, and ecological spirituality.

Criticism

Indigenous activists and scholars have criticised neoshamanism as cultural appropriation: the extraction of ceremonies from their communal, linguistic, and ethical contexts, sometimes commercialised by self-appointed teachers with no accountability — critics use terms such as "plastic shaman" for fraudulent practitioners. Lakota and other Native American groups have issued declarations against the sale of their ceremonies. The 2009 deaths of three participants in a commercial sweat lodge in Sedona, Arizona, run by self-help author James Arthur Ray, sharpened debate about safety and legitimacy. Academics also question whether Eliade's universal "shamanism" itself flattens very different traditions.
