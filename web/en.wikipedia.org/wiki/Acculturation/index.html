Acculturation

Acculturation
Key theorist: John W. Berry — integration, assimilation, separation, marginalisation

Acculturation is a process of social, psychological and cultural change arising from the balancing of two cultures, occurring when groups of individuals from different cultures come into continuous first-hand contact, with subsequent changes in the original cultural patterns of either or both groups (Redfield, Linton and Herskovits, 1936).

Berry's model

Psychologist John Berry proposed four strategies based on maintaining heritage culture versus engaging the host culture: integration (both), assimilation (host only), separation (heritage only), and marginalisation (neither). Integration is generally associated with the best psychological outcomes, depending on the host society's receptivity.

Levels and effects

Acculturation operates at group level (changes in customs, economy, political organisation) and individual level (acculturative stress, identity, language shift). Outcomes vary with voluntariness, similarity between cultures, and discrimination.

Related concepts

It differs from enculturation (learning one's own culture), assimilation (one-way absorption), and transculturation. Historians apply it to colonisation, missionisation, and Indigenous responses in the Americas and elsewhere.
