SourceSeeds of the flax plant (Linum usitatissimum)
TypeDrying oil

Linseed oil, also known as flaxseed oil, is a colourless to yellowish oil obtained from the dried, ripened seeds of the flax plant (Linum usitatissimum). The oil is obtained by pressing, sometimes followed by solvent extraction. Because it polymerises on exposure to air, linseed oil is a drying oil and has been valued for centuries as a binder and wood preservative.

Properties

Linseed oil is rich in the omega-3 fatty acid α-linolenic acid (typically around half or more of its fatty acids), which gives it its strong drying ability: the polyunsaturated chains react with oxygen and cross-link into a tough, flexible film ("linoxyn"). Raw oil dries slowly; "boiled" linseed oil is processed (historically by heating with metallic drying agents) so that finishes cure faster, while stand oil is heat-polymerised for paint making. A well-known hazard is spontaneous combustion: rags soaked in linseed oil can self-heat as the oil oxidises and ignite, so they must be stored in sealed metal containers or dried flat.

Uses

Traditional oil paints use linseed oil as the principal binder, and it remains standard in artists' and many industrial paints, varnishes, stains and wood finishes, where it protects timber (for example on cricket bats and furniture) while enhancing the grain. It is a key ingredient of linoleum flooring — whose name derives from linum (flax) and oleum (oil) — of glazing putty, oilcloth, and some printing inks. Food-grade flaxseed oil is marketed as a nutritional supplement for its omega-3 content, and the seed's press cake is fed to livestock; whole and ground flaxseed are eaten directly.
