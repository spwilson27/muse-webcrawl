Möbius strip

The Möbius strip or Möbius band is a surface with only one side and only one boundary component. It can be constructed in three-dimensional Euclidean space by taking a strip of paper, giving it a half-twist, and joining the ends. The Möbius strip is a non-orientable surface: an object traversing it returns to its starting point mirror-reversed. It was discovered independently by the German mathematicians Johann Benedict Listing and August Ferdinand Möbius in 1858.

Properties

The Möbius strip has several curious properties. A line drawn starting from the seam down the middle meets back at the seam on the "other side"; continued, it passes through both apparent sides and returns to the start, so the strip has a single surface. Cutting a Möbius strip lengthwise along its centre line yields, surprisingly, a single longer strip with two full twists rather than two separate strips. Cutting one third of the way across the width yields two interlocked strips: one a thinner Möbius strip and one a doubly twisted loop.

Mathematically, the Möbius strip can be parametrised as a ruled surface and serves as the total space of the non-trivial line bundle over the circle. It is a standard counterexample to orientability and appears throughout topology: the Klein bottle can be formed by gluing two Möbius strips along their edges, and the real projective plane by gluing a Möbius strip to a disc.

Applications and culture

The Möbius strip has been used in conveyor belts (so that wear is distributed over the whole surface), in continuous-loop recording tapes, and in chemistry, where "Möbius aromaticity" describes molecules whose π-system follows a twisted topology; Möbius–Klein bottle resonators appear in electronics. In culture, the recycling symbol echoes a Möbius-like form, and the strip inspired M. C. Escher's lithograph Möbius Strip II (1963), depicting ants traversing its single surface. A giant Möbius sculpture by Max Bill stands outside the Pompidou Centre in Paris.
