Shared space

Shared space is an urban design approach that minimises the segregation between modes of road user. It is achieved by removing features such as traffic signs, road surface markings, traffic lights, and kerbs, so that drivers, cyclists, and pedestrians share the street and negotiate movement through eye contact and social interaction rather than rules alone. The concept is associated above all with the Dutch traffic engineer Hans Monderman, and with related work by Ben Hamilton-Baillie in the United Kingdom.

Philosophy

The philosophy rests on the idea that excessive regulation makes road users less attentive, whereas a degree of ambiguity and uncertainty encourages slower speeds and greater awareness — an application of risk compensation ideas to street design. Monderman's schemes in Frisian towns such as Drachten famously replaced signal-controlled junctions with spaces where traffic flows freely at low speed, with reported reductions in accidents.

Examples

Implementations include Exhibition Road in London (reopened 2011), New Road in Brighton, Seven Dials in London, schemes in Ashford and Poynton (England), Bohmte (Germany), and the woonerf tradition in the Netherlands, from which shared space partly descends.

Criticism

Shared space schemes have been criticised by organisations representing blind and partially sighted people, who rely on kerbs and controlled crossings to navigate safely; a 2015 UK review (the Holmes report, Accidents by Design) called for a moratorium on shared-space schemes that remove kerbs and crossings. Proponents respond that well-designed schemes include delineation and comfort features for visually impaired users, and dispute some of the evidence.
