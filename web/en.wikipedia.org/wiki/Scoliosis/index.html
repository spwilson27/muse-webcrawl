Scoliosis (from Greek: σκολιός, romanized: skoliós, lit. 'bending') is a condition in which a person's spine has a sideways curve. The curve is usually "S"- or "C"-shaped over three dimensions. In some, the degree of curve is stable, while in others, it increases over time. Mild scoliosis does not typically cause problems, but more severe cases can affect breathing and movement. Pain is usually present in adults, and can worsen with age.

The cause of most cases is unknown, but it is believed to involve a combination of genetic and environmental factors. Risk factors include other affected family members. It can also occur due to another condition such as muscle spasms, cerebral palsy, Marfan syndrome, and tumors such as neurofibromatosis. Diagnosis is confirmed with X-rays. Scoliosis is typically classified as either structural in which the curve is fixed, or functional in which the underlying spine is normal.

Treatment depends on the degree of curve, location, and cause. Minor curves may simply be watched periodically. Treatments may include bracing, specific exercises, posture checking, and surgery. The brace must be fitted to the person and used daily until growing stops. Evidence for specific exercises is limited, though they may help reduce the risk of progression. Surgery is usually recommended for severe curves, and may involve spinal fusion.

Signs and symptoms

People who have reached skeletal maturity are less likely to have a worsening case. Signs of scoliosis can include uneven musculature on one side of the spine, a rib prominence or a prominent shoulder blade caused by rotation of the rib cage, uneven hips, arms or leg lengths, and slow nerve action. In severe cases, diminished lung capacity, back pain and physical deformity may occur.

Causes

About 65% of scoliosis cases are idiopathic, about 15% are congenital, and about 10% are secondary to a primary neuromuscular condition. Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis has no clear causal agent and is generally believed to be multifactorial. It affects 2–4% of the population between 10 and 16 years of age. Congenital scoliosis can be attributed to a malformation of the spine during weeks three to six of gestation.

Diagnosis

People suspected of having scoliosis should be evaluated initially by conducting a physical examination. The standard method for assessing the curvature quantitatively is measurement of the Cobb angle, which is the angle between two lines drawn perpendicular to the endplates of the vertebrae involved in the curve, measured from a standard AP radiograph. Scoliosis is defined as a Cobb angle of 10° or more.

Treatment

The traditional medical management of scoliosis is complex and is determined by the severity of the curvature and skeletal maturity. Options include observation for mild curves, bracing for moderate curves in growing children, and surgery for curves exceeding about 45–50 degrees. Scientific studies of the benefits of chiropractic manipulation, electrical stimulation, and dietary supplements have not proven them effective in correcting or preventing progression of idiopathic scoliosis.

Epidemiology

Scoliosis affects approximately 2–3% of adolescents worldwide. Females are affected more severely than males, with curves in girls being more likely to progress. The condition has been described since antiquity; Hippocrates used the terms kyphosis and scoliosis for spinal curvatures, and Galen later defined the terms more precisely.
