A mount of piety (Italian: monte di pietà; Spanish: monte de piedad) is a charitable institution that lends money at low interest against pledged goods, originally established in late medieval Italy to protect the poor from usurious moneylending.

History

The first monte di pietà was founded in Perugia in 1462, promoted by Franciscan friars including Barnaba Manassei and Fortunato Coppoli. The movement spread rapidly across Italy under the preaching of friars such as Bernardino da Feltre, who established montes in many cities in the 1480s and 1490s. Funds were raised from charitable donations, and borrowers pledged clothing, tools, or household goods as security for small loans.

The charging of modest interest to cover operating costs was initially controversial, since lending at any interest was widely condemned as usury. The Fifth Lateran Council in 1515, under Pope Leo X, formally approved the montes and declared their moderate charges legitimate. From Italy the model spread to Spain, France, the Low Countries, and the Spanish colonies — Mexico City's Nacional Monte de Piedad, founded in 1775, still operates today.

Legacy

The monti di pietà are regarded as forerunners of modern microcredit and public pawnbroking. Some evolved into savings banks; numerous Italian banks trace their origins to a local monte, and the word monte survives in several Italian bank names.
