Kim Jong-nam

Born: 10 May 1971, Pyongyang, North Korea

Died: 13 February 2017 (aged 45), Kuala Lumpur International Airport, Malaysia

Father: Kim Jong-il

Mother: Song Hye-rim

Kim Jong-nam (Korean: 김정남; 10 May 1971 – 13 February 2017) was the eldest son of North Korean leader Kim Jong-il and the half-brother of Kim Jong Un. From roughly 1994 to 2001, he was widely considered the heir apparent to his father, but he fell out of favour after embarrassing the regime in May 2001, when he was caught travelling to Japan on a forged passport, reportedly to visit Tokyo Disneyland.

Early life

Kim Jong-nam was born in Pyongyang to Song Hye-rim, an actress. Because Kim Jong-il wished to keep his relationship with Song secret from his own father, Kim Il-sung, the boy was largely kept out of public view and educated at home and abroad, including periods in Switzerland and Russia. He later worked in the North Korean computer industry, chairing the country's Computer Committee.

Fall from favour

In May 2001, Kim was arrested at Narita International Airport in Tokyo while travelling on a forged Dominican Republic passport under a Chinese alias. He was deported to China. The incident was a public humiliation for Pyongyang and is generally credited with ending his prospects of succession; his younger half-brother Kim Jong Un was eventually groomed as heir instead.

Exile

After 2003, Kim lived mainly outside North Korea, principally in Macau, and occasionally spoke to foreign journalists. He was reported to be critical of the dynastic succession, remarking that he doubted a third-generation transfer of power would suit the country, while insisting he had no political ambitions of his own.

Assassination

On 13 February 2017, Kim was attacked at Kuala Lumpur International Airport in Malaysia by two women who smeared his face with a substance later identified as the nerve agent VX. He died shortly afterwards while being taken to hospital. The killing caused a diplomatic crisis between Malaysia and North Korea; South Korean and United States officials attributed the assassination to the North Korean regime, which denied involvement. The two women — an Indonesian and a Vietnamese national — were charged with murder but were later released after the charges were dropped or reduced; four North Korean suspects fled Malaysia on the day of the attack.
