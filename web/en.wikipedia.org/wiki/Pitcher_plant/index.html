OriginConvergent evolution in at least five plant families

Pitcher plants are several different carnivorous plants that have modified leaves known as pitfall traps — a prey-trapping mechanism featuring a deep cavity filled with digestive liquid. The traps of what are considered to be "true" pitcher plants are formed by specialized leaves. The plants attract and drown their prey with nectar.

Types of pitcher plants

The pitcher trap has evolved independently in at least five families. The most familiar are the Old World tropical pitcher plants of the genus Nepenthes (family Nepenthaceae), whose pitchers hang from tendrils at the ends of leaves and range from small cups to the great flask-shaped traps of Nepenthes rajah of Borneo, which can hold over three litres of fluid and occasionally drown small mammals. The New World trumpet pitchers, Sarracenia (family Sarraceniaceae), grow upright, hooded tubes in North American bogs; the same family includes the cobra lily, Darlingtonia californica, and the sun pitchers, Heliamphora, of South American tepuis. The unrelated Australian pitcher plant, Cephalotus follicularis, is the sole member of its family.

Trapping mechanism

Prey, usually insects and other small arthropods, are lured by nectar secreted on the trap rim (peristome) and by bright colouration and scent. The rim becomes extremely slippery when wet — in many Nepenthes through aquaplaning on a water film — so visitors fall into the fluid below. Waxy scales or downward-pointing hairs hinder escape, and the prey drowns and is digested by enzymes and symbiotic microorganisms, supplying the plant with nitrogen and phosphorus lacking in its nutrient-poor soils.

Ecology

Pitcher plants typically grow in wet, sunny, nutrient-poor habitats such as bogs, wet savannas, and tropical montane forests. Many species host communities of inquiline animals — mosquito larvae, midges, and even small frogs and crabs — that live in the pitcher fluid. Some Nepenthes obtain nutrients less grimly: N. lowii and relatives are fed on the droppings of tree shrews that lick nectar from the lid while perched on the pitcher.
