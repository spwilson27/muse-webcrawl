Synchronization gear

A synchronization gear (also called a gun synchronizer or interrupter gear) is a device enabling a single-engine tractor-configuration aircraft to fire through the arc of its own propeller without striking the blades. It was introduced in 1915 during the First World War and became standard equipment on fighter aircraft, remaining in use until the end of the Second World War era of propeller-driven fighters.

History

The French pilot Roland Garros fitted steel deflector plates to his propeller in early 1915, allowing some bullets to pass and deflecting others, but the definitive solution was a true synchronizer. Anthony Fokker's team in Germany developed a cam-and-pushrod mechanism fitted to the Fokker Eindecker, which gave Germany a period of air superiority known as the "Fokker Scourge". The Allies soon produced equivalent systems, including the Vickers-Challenger gear, the Scarff-Dibovski gear and the hydraulic Constantinesco gear, which transmitted impulses through a column of liquid and was widely used on British aircraft.

Operation

Most systems were driven by a cam connected to the propeller shaft. Each revolution, the cam timed an impulse to the gun's trigger so that firing occurred only when no blade was in front of the muzzle. Because reliability was never perfect, synchronized guns fired at a reduced effective rate, and occasional rounds still struck propellers even in well-maintained systems.

Decline

As speeds increased, guns moved into the wings outside the propeller arc or fired through the hollow propeller shaft, and jet aircraft eliminated the problem entirely. Synchronization gear thus became obsolete after 1945.
