The Columbia District was a fur-trading district in the Pacific Northwest of North America, roughly corresponding to the Columbia River drainage and adjacent coastal regions. It was administered successively by the Pacific Fur Company, the North West Company and, from 1821 to 1858, the Hudson's Bay Company (HBC). Under the Treaty of 1818 the region was jointly occupied by Britain and the United States; the Oregon Treaty of 1846 divided it along the 49th parallel, and the HBC's remaining territory north of the line became the Colony of British Columbia in 1858.

History

The district's trade began with John Jacob Astor's Pacific Fur Company, which founded Fort Astoria at the mouth of the Columbia in 1811. During the War of 1812 the post was sold to the North West Company and renamed Fort George. After the 1821 merger of the rivals, the HBC under Governor George Simpson reorganised the district, moving headquarters in 1825 to Fort Vancouver on the north bank of the river, administered for two decades by Chief Factor John McLoughlin, sometimes called the "Father of Oregon".

From Fort Vancouver the company ran a network of posts — Fort Nez Percés, Fort Okanagan, Fort Colville, Fort Langley and later Fort Victoria (founded 1843 in anticipation of the boundary settlement) — and annual fur brigades to California and the Snake River country. The company also farmed, milled lumber and traded with Russian America and Hawai'i, making the district a largely self-sufficient commercial empire.

Division and aftermath

American settlers arriving over the Oregon Trail shifted the balance of the joint occupation. The 1846 treaty set the boundary at the 49th parallel (with Vancouver Island left British), and the HBC progressively shifted its operations north, moving its western depot to Fort Victoria. In 1858, during the Fraser Canyon Gold Rush, the mainland north of the line was proclaimed the Crown Colony of British Columbia, ending the fur district era.
