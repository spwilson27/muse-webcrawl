Self-coup

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A self-coup, also known as an autocoup or autogolpe, is a form of coup d'état in which a nation's leader, having come to power through legal means, dissolves or renders powerless the legislature and other constitutional checks, and assumes extraordinary powers not granted under normal circumstances. The classic modern instance is Peruvian President Alberto Fujimori's autogolpe of 5 April 1992: with military backing he dissolved Congress, suspended the constitution, purged the judiciary and ruled by decree until a new, compliant constitution was written (1993).

Historical examples span Louis-Napoléon's coup of December 1851 (an elected president becoming Napoleon III), Hitler's Enabling Act route (1933 — legal forms used to abolish legality from the presidency/chancellorship), and a series of Latin American and post-Soviet cases. Political science distinguishes the self-coup from the ordinary coup (seizure by outsiders) and from slower “executive aggrandisement” or Democratic backsliding (Bermeo's “promissory coups” and elected autocrats hollowing institutions incrementally, as in Venezuela and Hungary debates): the self-coup is backsliding at a sprint — a bright-line event that regional democracy charters (the OAS's Democratic Charter, applied after Fujimori) treat as an unconstitutional interruption triggering sanctions.
