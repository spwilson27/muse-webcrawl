Drug delivery

Drug delivery refers to approaches, formulations, manufacturing techniques, storage systems, and technologies involved in transporting a pharmaceutical compound in the body so as to safely achieve its desired therapeutic effect. It may involve scientific site-targeting within the body, or it might involve facilitating systemic pharmacokinetics; in any case, it is typically concerned with both quantity and duration of drug presence. Drug delivery is often approached via a drug's chemical formulation, but it may also involve medical devices or drug-device combination products. Drug delivery is a concept heavily integrated with dosage form and route of administration, the latter sometimes being considered part of the definition.

Routes of administration

Drugs may be administered by many routes, including oral (by mouth), parenteral (by injection), topical (on the skin), inhalation, rectal, sublingual, and transdermal routes. The oral route is the most common because of its convenience, low cost, and ease of self-administration, but it subjects drugs to first-pass metabolism in the liver and to degradation by stomach acid and digestive enzymes. Parenteral routes, such as intravenous, intramuscular, and subcutaneous injection, bypass first-pass metabolism and can provide rapid onset of action.

Dosage forms

Common dosage forms include tablets, capsules, syrups, creams, ointments, suppositories, inhalers, and injectable solutions. Modified-release formulations use coatings or matrices that dissolve at controlled rates to extend the interval between doses. Osmotic pump tablets, for example, release drug at a nearly constant (zero-order) rate.

Targeted delivery

Modern drug delivery research focuses on targeting drugs to specific tissues to increase efficacy and reduce side effects. Strategies include liposomes and lipid nanoparticles, polymeric nanoparticles, monoclonal antibody–drug conjugates, and implantable devices. Lipid nanoparticles became widely known through their use in mRNA vaccines against COVID-19. The enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect is exploited to accumulate nanoparticles in tumour tissue, whose vasculature is leakier than that of healthy tissue.

Controlled release

Controlled-release technologies aim to maintain drug concentrations within a therapeutic window over an extended period. Examples include transdermal patches (such as nicotine and fentanyl patches), depot injections, drug-eluting stents, and intrauterine devices that release hormones over several years.
