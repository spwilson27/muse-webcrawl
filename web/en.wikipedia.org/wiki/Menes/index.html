Menes

Menes
Reignc. 3200–3000 BC (traditionally)
RoleFirst pharaoh; unifier of Upper and Lower Egypt
DynastyFirst Dynasty

Menes (Greek: Μήνης) was a pharaoh of ancient Egypt, credited in later Egyptian and classical tradition with uniting Upper and Lower Egypt and founding the First Dynasty, around 3100 BC. The name Menes appears in king lists of the New Kingdom and in the works of the Egyptian priest Manetho and the Greek historian Herodotus, but no contemporary inscription bears it, and Menes's identity remains one of the classic problems of Egyptology.

Identity

Scholars have most often identified Menes with one of the earliest attested kings: Narmer, whose famous palette from Hierakonpolis shows the king wearing both the White Crown of Upper Egypt and the Red Crown of Lower Egypt, or his successor Hor-Aha, whose name has been found alongside the nebti-name "Men" on an ivory label from Naqada. Others treat Menes as a legendary conflation of several early rulers.

Traditional accounts

Manetho credited Menes with a reign of some 60 years and with founding the capital of Memphis at the apex of the Nile Delta, allegedly diverting the river to build on reclaimed land. Herodotus likewise names "Min" as the first king of Egypt and builder of Memphis and its temple of Ptah. Later legend added that Menes introduced writing, religious cult and luxury to Egypt, and that he was killed by a hippopotamus.

Historical assessment

Archaeology shows the unification of Egypt as a process unfolding over generations in the late Predynastic (Naqada III) period rather than a single act. Whether or not "Menes" was one historical man, the figure stands at the head of the pharaonic king lists and marks the traditional beginning of Egypt's three-thousand-year dynastic history.
