Irene of Athens (c. 752 – 803) was a Byzantine empress consort, then regent for her son Constantine VI, and finally empress regnant in her own right from 797 to 802 — the first woman to rule the Byzantine Empire as sole sovereign, on occasion styling herself basileus ("emperor").

Early life and regency

An orphan from Athens, Irene was brought to Constantinople and married the heir Leo IV in 769. When Leo died in 780, Irene became regent for their nine-year-old son Constantine VI. A committed iconophile, she moved cautiously against the iconoclast policies of the previous emperors, dismissing iconoclast officials and eventually convening the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, which restored the veneration of icons and ended the first period of Byzantine Iconoclasm.

Sole rule

As Constantine came of age, mother and son clashed over power. In 790 Irene was briefly displaced; restored, she eventually had Constantine blinded in 797 — he died of his wounds — and assumed sole rule. Her accession, as a woman, was treated in the Latin West as leaving the imperial throne vacant, one pretext for Charlemagne's coronation as emperor in Rome in 800. Irene pursued diplomacy with Charlemagne and even, according to chroniclers, contemplated marriage with him. Her favouritism toward the eunuch Aetios, lavish tax remissions and court intrigues eroded support; in 802 she was deposed in a palace coup by her finance minister, Nikephoros I, and exiled to Lesbos, where she died the following year.

Legacy

Irene is venerated as a saint in the Eastern Orthodox Church for restoring icon veneration. Her reign illustrates both the possibilities and the limits of female rule in Byzantium, and her blinding of her own son shocked contemporaries.
