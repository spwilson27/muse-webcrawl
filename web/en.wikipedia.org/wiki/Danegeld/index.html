Danegeld (Old English: Danegield, "Dane payment") was a tax levied in England, originally to pay tribute to Viking raiders — literally "buying off" the Danes — and later transformed into a regular annual land tax. Huge quantities of English silver pennies found in Scandinavian hoards testify to the scale of the payments.

Origins

The first recorded payment was in 991, when, after the Danish victory at the Battle of Maldon, the government of Æthelred the Unready paid £10,000 of silver to secure peace. Escalating payments followed — £16,000 in 994, £36,000 in 1007, and £48,000 in 1012 — yet the raids continued, and in 991–1013 England was effectively conquered by Sweyn Forkbeard, whose son Cnut became king. Under Cnut a final huge payment of £82,500 was made in 1018 to pay off the invasion army.

From tribute to tax

Under the later Anglo-Saxon kings the levy (also called the heregeld, "army tax", used to maintain a standing force) became a regular national land tax assessed on hides. The Norman kings continued it: William the Conqueror levied a famous six-shilling geld in 1083–84, and the Domesday survey recorded geld liabilities. The tax was levied intermittently until 1162, when it disappears from the Pipe Rolls, replaced by other forms of revenue.

Legacy

The Danegeld became a byword for the folly of appeasing blackmail — captured in Rudyard Kipling's 1911 poem "Dane-Geld", whose refrain warns that "once you have paid him the Dane-geld / You never get rid of the Dane". Historians nevertheless note that the geld also demonstrated the remarkable sophistication of the late Anglo-Saxon fiscal state.
