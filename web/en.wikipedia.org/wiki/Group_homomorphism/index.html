Group homomorphism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A homomorphism maps one group structure into another while preserving the operation

In mathematics, given two groups, (G, ∗) and (H, ·), a group homomorphism from (G, ∗) to (H, ·) is a function h : G → H such that for all u and v in G it holds that

h(u ∗ v) = h(u) · h(v)

where the group operation on the left side of the equation is that of G and on the right side that of H.

From this property, one can deduce that h maps the identity element eG of G to the identity element eH of H, and it also maps inverses to inverses in the sense that h(u−1) = h(u)−1.

Image and kernel

The image of a homomorphism is the set of images of elements of G, a subgroup of H. The kernel is the set of elements of G mapped to the identity of H; it is always a normal subgroup of G. These two constructions lead to the first isomorphism theorem: the quotient group G/ker(h) is isomorphic to the image of h.

Special types

  • A bijective homomorphism is a group isomorphism.
  • A homomorphism from a group to itself is an endomorphism; a bijective endomorphism is an automorphism.
  • An injective homomorphism is a monomorphism; a surjective one is an epimorphism.

Examples

The exponential function gives a homomorphism from the additive group of real numbers to the multiplicative group of positive real numbers. The determinant gives a homomorphism from the general linear group GL(n) to the multiplicative group of non-zero scalars, whose kernel is the special linear group. The map sending an integer to its parity is a homomorphism from (ℤ, +) to (ℤ/2ℤ, +).
