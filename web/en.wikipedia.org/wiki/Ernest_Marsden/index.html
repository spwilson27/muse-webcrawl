Sir Ernest Marsden (19 February 1889 – 15 December 1970) was an English-New Zealand physicist. He is recognised internationally for his contributions to science, and for the Geiger–Marsden experiment carried out in Ernest Rutherford's laboratory.

The gold foil experiment

As an undergraduate at the University of Manchester in 1909, Marsden worked with Hans Geiger under Rutherford's direction, firing alpha particles at thin gold foil. Their unexpected observation that a small fraction of particles were scattered through large angles led Rutherford to propose the nuclear model of the atom in 1911, one of the foundational results of modern physics. Marsden later worked on the detection of hydrogen nuclei (protons) produced in collisions.

Career in New Zealand

After service in the First World War, Marsden moved to New Zealand in 1922 as professor of physics at Victoria University College, Wellington. He became the founding secretary of the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research (DSIR) in 1926 and a key architect of New Zealand's scientific establishment, promoting research in agriculture, radio and radar during and after the Second World War. He was knighted in 1958.
