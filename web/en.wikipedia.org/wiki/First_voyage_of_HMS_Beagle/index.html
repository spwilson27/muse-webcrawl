First voyage of HMS Beagle

The first voyage of HMS Beagle was a hydrographic surveying expedition of the British Admiralty, lasting from 1826 to 1830, in which the sloop HMS Beagle accompanied the larger HMS Adventure on a survey of the southern coasts of South America, including Patagonia, Tierra del Fuego, and the Strait of Magellan. It preceded the ship's famous second voyage of 1831–1836, on which Charles Darwin sailed as naturalist.

Background and command

The expedition was commanded overall by the Australian-born Captain Phillip Parker King aboard Adventure. Beagle was initially commanded by Captain Pringle Stokes. The cramped, stressful survey work in the desolate channels of Tierra del Fuego took a toll on Stokes, who fell into depression and shot himself in August 1828 at Port Famine; he died days later. Lieutenant William George Skyring briefly took command before the young aristocratic officer Robert FitzRoy was appointed captain — the same FitzRoy who would command the second voyage.

The Fuegian captives

After a whaleboat was stolen by Fuegian inhabitants, FitzRoy took hostages, and eventually decided to take four young Fuegians to England to be educated and Christianised, intending to return them as missionaries. They were given the names York Minster, Boat Memory, Fuegia Basket, and Jemmy Button (the last said to have been "purchased" for a pearl button). Boat Memory died of smallpox after vaccination in England; the other three were presented at court to King William IV and Queen Adelaide, and were returned to Tierra del Fuego on the second voyage, with Darwin aboard.

Survey work

The expedition charted extensive stretches of previously poorly mapped coastline, surveyed the Strait of Magellan and the channels around Tierra del Fuego, identified the Beagle Channel (named after the ship), and carried out observations of tides, magnetism, and natural history. The ships returned to England in October 1830.
