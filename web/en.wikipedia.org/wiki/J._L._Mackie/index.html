J. L. Mackie

John Leslie Mackie
Born: 25 August 1917, Sydney, Australia
Died: 12 December 1981, Oxford, England
Era: 20th-century philosophy
Main interests: Metaethics, philosophy of religion, metaphysics

J. L. Mackie defended moral scepticism and an "error theory" of moral judgement.

John Leslie Mackie (25 August 1917 – 12 December 1981) was an Australian philosopher. He made significant contributions to ethics, the philosophy of religion, metaphysics, and the philosophy of history. Mackie is best known for his defence of metaethical moral scepticism — in particular his "error theory" — and for his sophisticated defence of atheism, notably in The Miracle of Theism (1982).

Life

Mackie was educated at the University of Sydney, where he came under the influence of John Anderson, and later at Oriel College, Oxford. After war service, he lectured at the University of Otago and held the chair of philosophy at the University of Sydney before moving to the University of York and then to University College, Oxford, where he was a fellow from 1967 until his death.

Ethics and error theory

In Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977), Mackie argued that ordinary moral judgements purport to state objective, categorically prescriptive facts, but that there are no such objective values; hence moral discourse is systematically in error. His two best-known arguments are the argument from relativity, based on the variation of moral codes across cultures, and the argument from queerness, which holds that objective values would be metaphysically and epistemologically strange entities unlike anything else in the universe. His alternative was captured in the book's famous opening line: "There are no objective values."

Philosophy of religion

Mackie wrote extensively on the problem of evil, arguing in an influential 1955 paper that the existence of an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God is logically incompatible with the existence of evil, and pressing objections against free-will defences. The Miracle of Theism, published posthumously, assessed arguments for and against God's existence and defended atheism.

Other work

Mackie also contributed to metaphysics and causation, notably in The Cement of the Universe (1974), where he developed the notion of an INUS condition — an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition — as an analysis of causal claims. He wrote on Locke, Hume, and the philosophy of history as well.
