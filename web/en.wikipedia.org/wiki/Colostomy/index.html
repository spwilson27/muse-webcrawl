A colostomy is an opening (stoma) in the large intestine (colon), or the surgical procedure that creates one. The opening is formed by drawing the healthy end of the colon through an incision in the anterior abdominal wall and suturing it into place. This opening, often in conjunction with an attached ostomy pouching system, provides an alternative channel for the contents of the colon to leave the body. Colostomies may be temporary or permanent, and are a form of ostomy.

Indications

Colostomy is performed for diseases and injuries of the lower bowel, including colorectal cancer, complicated diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease, Hirschsprung disease, intestinal obstruction, perforation and abdominal trauma. It may divert the fecal stream to protect a distal anastomosis, relieve obstruction, or serve as the definitive end point of the bowel after removal of the rectum and anus (abdominoperineal resection).

Types

An end colostomy brings the cut end of the colon out as a single stoma; in a Hartmann's procedure the rectal stump is closed inside the pelvis. A loop colostomy brings a loop of colon to the surface and opens it, creating two adjacent openings, and is usually temporary and easier to reverse. A double-barrel colostomy separates the two ends completely. The consistency of output depends on the level: ascending colostomies produce liquid stool, sigmoid colostomies near-normal formed stool.

Care and complications

Patients learn to manage a pouching system and the skin around the stoma. Complications include parastomal hernia, prolapse, retraction, stenosis, skin irritation and, after reversal, the usual risks of bowel surgery. With modern appliances and stoma nursing, most people with colostomies resume normal activities, work and exercise.
