The glottalic theory is a proposal in Indo-European linguistics that the stop consonants of Proto-Indo-European (PIE) traditionally reconstructed as a three-way contrast of voiced, voiceless, and voiced aspirated stops should instead be reconstructed with a series of ejective (glottalized) consonants. The theory was developed independently in the early 1970s by Thomas V. Gamkrelidze and Vyacheslav Ivanov, and by Paul J. Hopper.

Overview

In the traditional reconstruction, PIE had three series of stops:

TraditionalGlottalic
*p *t *k (voiceless)*pʰ *tʰ *kʰ (voiceless, optionally aspirated)
*b *d *g (voiced)*pʼ *tʼ *kʼ (ejectives)
*bʰ *dʰ *gʰ (voiced aspirated)*b *d *g (voiced, optionally aspirated)

The proposal rests on typological grounds: a system with voiced aspirates but no voiceless aspirates is rare among the world's languages, whereas systems containing ejectives are common in the Caucasus, where Gamkrelidze and Ivanov situated the Indo-European homeland. The near-absence of *b in the traditional reconstruction is also explained naturally if that series was ejective: /pʼ/ is cross-linguistically the most likely ejective to be missing from a system.

Supporting arguments

Supporters note that the glottalic model elegantly explains certain constraints in PIE root structure — for example, the impossibility of roots containing two traditional voiced stops — and the behaviour of stops in daughter languages such as Armenian and Germanic, where Grimm's law outcomes can be reanalysed. Winter's law in Balto-Slavic, under which vowels lengthened before traditional voiced stops but not aspirates, has also been cited: lengthening is a known effect before glottalized consonants in other languages.

Criticism and reception

Critics argue that the typological argument is overstated, that ejectives in daughter branches (such as in some Armenian dialects) may be secondary developments, and that no daughter language preserves ejectives in the systematic way the theory might predict. The glottalic theory remains a minority but influential position; many Indo-Europeanists adopt elements of it, while others retain the traditional system or prefer alternative revisions.
