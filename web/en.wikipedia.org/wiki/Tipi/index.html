Tipi

A tipi (also teepee or tepee) is a conical tent, historically made of animal hides supported by a frame of long wooden poles, used by the Indigenous peoples of the Great Plains and Canadian Prairies of North America. The word comes from the Lakota thípi, meaning "a dwelling". Tipis are distinguished from other conical tents by the smoke flaps at the top, which let occupants control ventilation for an interior fire.

Construction

A typical family tipi used three or four foundation poles lashed together near the top, with a dozen or more additional poles leaned against them to form a slightly tilted cone. A cover of sewn-together buffalo hides — later, canvas — was wrapped around the frame and pinned with wooden sticks, leaving an opening at the apex flanked by two adjustable "ears" or smoke flaps, each worked by an external pole. An inner liner (ozan) hung around the lower wall created an insulating air gap and stopped downdraughts. A large dwelling might be 5–6 metres in diameter and require fourteen or more hides.

Use

The design was superbly adapted to nomadic bison hunting: warm in winter, coolable in summer, rain-shedding, wind-stable, and capable of being struck, packed, and re-pitched within an hour. Before horses, dogs pulled the poles as a travois; the arrival of horses in the eighteenth century allowed much larger tipis. Covers and liners were often painted with visionary or record-keeping imagery. Tipi rings of stones that once anchored covers still mark the plains today. Tipis remain in use for ceremonial and cultural purposes — powwows, sun dances, and educational programmes — and canvas versions are raised by many Plains families each summer.
