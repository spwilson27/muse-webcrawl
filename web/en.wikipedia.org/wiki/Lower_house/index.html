Lower house

A lower house is one of two chambers of a bicameral legislature, the other chamber being the upper house. Despite its official position "below" the upper house, in many legislatures worldwide the lower house has come to wield more power, or at least to be the chamber to which the executive is responsible. Members of a lower house are typically elected directly by the people, serve shorter terms, and represent smaller, population-based constituencies.

Characteristics

Lower houses commonly share the following features:

  • Direct election — members are almost always elected directly, unlike upper houses which may be appointed, indirectly elected, or hereditary.
  • Financial primacy — money bills typically originate in the lower house, and the upper house's power over them is limited or absent.
  • Executive accountability — in parliamentary systems the government must retain the confidence of the lower house; loss of confidence forces resignation or an election.
  • Larger membership and shorter terms — lower houses are usually larger than upper houses and are dissolved more frequently.

Origins of the terms

The terms "upper" and "lower" arose from historical precedence rather than power: in England, the House of Lords — the chamber of the aristocracy and clergy — ranked above the House of Commons, which represented commoners. As democracy spread, the elected commons in many countries became the dominant chamber, inverting the practical hierarchy while the names remained.

Powers compared with upper houses

In the United Kingdom, the House of Commons can ultimately override the House of Lords under the Parliament Acts — a subordination at the heart of debates over House of Lords reform. In presidential systems the division is more equal: the United States House of Representatives shares legislative power with the Senate, though revenue bills must originate in the House. In India, the Lok Sabha dominates joint sittings by weight of numbers and alone controls supply. Australia's House of Representatives is the chamber of government formation, though the Senate is unusually powerful for an upper house.

Names of lower houses

Lower houses are variously called the House of Commons (United Kingdom, Canada), House of Representatives (United States, Australia, Japan), National Assembly (France's Assemblée nationale, South Korea), Chamber of Deputies (Italy, Brazil, Mexico), Bundestag (Germany), Lok Sabha (India), and Sejm (Poland), among many others.
