Paracas Candelabra

LocationParacas Peninsula, Pisco Bay, Peru
TypeGeoglyph
Length~181 m (595 ft)
Periodc. 200 BCE (attributed)

The Paracas Candelabra, also called the Candelabra of the Andes, is a large prehistoric geoglyph carved into the northern face of the Paracas Peninsula in Pisco Bay, Peru. Roughly 181 metres (595 feet) long, the figure resembles a stylized candelabrum or trident and is cut into the sandy hillside to a depth of about 60 centimetres, with stones placed along its edges. It is large enough to be visible from many kilometres out at sea.

Origin and purpose

The geoglyph's age and purpose are unknown. Pottery found near the site has been radiocarbon dated to around 200 BCE, the era of the Paracas culture, and the figure is often attributed to that culture, though it cannot be proven to be contemporary. The nearby Nazca Lines are younger and belong to the Nazca culture.

Various purposes have been proposed: a navigational marker for sailors; a religious symbol — the central motif has been compared to the staff of the creator god Viracocha, and to a trident or a stylized cactus plant used in ritual; or a marker associated with the offshore guano islands, valuable as a source of fertilizer. Local legend in later centuries connected the figure with the heroes of Peruvian independence, but the design predates them by many centuries.

Preservation

The geoglyph lies within the Paracas National Reserve. Despite wind and occasional human disturbance, the design survives because the desert slope is sheltered and the incised lines collect darker stones. It was declared a cultural heritage site of Peru and is a regular stop on boat tours to the Ballestas Islands.
