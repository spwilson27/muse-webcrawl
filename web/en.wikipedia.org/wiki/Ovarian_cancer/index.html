Ovarian cancer

Ovarian cancer is a cancer that forms in or on an ovary. It results in abnormal cells that have the ability to invade or spread to other parts of the body. When this process begins, there may be no or only vague symptoms; as the cancer progresses, symptoms such as bloating, pelvic pain, abdominal swelling and loss of appetite become more noticeable.

Types

Most ovarian cancers are epithelial carcinomas arising from the surface of the ovary or, as later research suggested, from the fallopian tube. Other types include germ cell tumours and sex cord–stromal tumours. High-grade serous carcinoma is the most common and most lethal subtype.

Risk factors and genetics

Risk increases with age, nulliparity and a family history of the disease. Inherited mutations in BRCA1 and BRCA2 substantially raise risk, as does Lynch syndrome. Use of oral contraceptives and tubal ligation reduce risk.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis typically involves pelvic examination, transvaginal ultrasound and the CA-125 blood test, confirmed by surgery and pathology. Treatment usually combines cytoreductive surgery with platinum-based chemotherapy; targeted agents such as PARP inhibitors are used in selected patients. Because early-stage disease is often silent, ovarian cancer is frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage, which accounts for its relatively poor prognosis.
