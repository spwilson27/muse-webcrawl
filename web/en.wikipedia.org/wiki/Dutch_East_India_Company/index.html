The Dutch East India Company, officially the United East India Company (Dutch: Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie, VOC), was a chartered company established in 1602 by the States General of the Netherlands, merging competing Dutch trading ventures to monopolize the spice trade with Asia. It is regarded as the world's first publicly traded company and the prototype of the multinational corporation: it issued transferable shares and bonds on the Amsterdam Stock Exchange, paid dividends for nearly two centuries, and at its height employed tens of thousands and maintained its own armies, fleets, and colonies.

Powers and trade

The VOC's charter granted quasi-sovereign powers: to wage war, conclude treaties, coin money, administer justice, and establish colonies between the Cape of Good Hope and the Strait of Magellan. It built Batavia (present-day Jakarta) in 1619 under Governor-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen as its Asian headquarters, seized the Banda Islands in 1621 — in a campaign of mass killing against the Bandanese to monopolize nutmeg — and controlled the clove and cinnamon trades through posts in the Moluccas and Ceylon. It founded the Cape Colony (1652) as a refreshment station, traded at Deshima in Nagasaki as the only Europeans permitted in Japan, and dominated European imports of tea, textiles, and porcelain. Its intra-Asian trade network was as important as its Europe-bound shipping. Abel Tasman's voyages (1642–44) charted Tasmania and New Zealand for the company.

Decline and legacy

By the 18th century the VOC was burdened by corruption, overhead, shifting trade toward tea and textiles (where the English East India Company competed fiercely), and the costs of territorial rule. The Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–84) crippled its shipping. The Dutch state took over its debts and possessions in the 1790s; the charter lapsed at the end of 1799. Adjusted for inflation, some modern commentators have valued the VOC at sums exceeding the largest companies of today, though such comparisons are contested. Its legacy includes the foundations of modern corporate finance — and, in Indonesia, South Africa, and elsewhere, the origins of Dutch colonial rule and its lasting harms.
