High-speed steel

High-speed steel (HSS or HS) is a subset of tool steels valued for its ability to retain hardness at the high temperatures generated when cutting metal at speed — a property called red hardness. It is used for drill bits, end mills, lathe tools, saw blades, taps, and broaches.

History

HSS was developed around 1900 by Frederick Winslow Taylor and Maunsel White at Bethlehem Steel. Their tungsten-chromium steels, heat-treated at unusually high temperatures, roughly doubled or tripled cutting speeds over carbon tool steel and were dramatically demonstrated at the Paris Exhibition of 1900, where tools cut while glowing dull red without losing their edge.

Composition and grades

Classic grades are built on tungsten (the T series, e.g. T1 with about 18% W) or molybdenum (the M series, e.g. M2, the most common general-purpose grade, and cobalt-bearing M35 and M42 for higher hot hardness). Alloying elements include chromium (~4%), vanadium, molybdenum, and carbon; the hard carbides they form give wear resistance, and hardening followed by multiple tempering cycles develops the final properties.

Use

Although cemented carbide has displaced HSS in much high-volume machining, HSS remains preferred where toughness matters, in hand tools, and in complex form tools, and is often improved with surface coatings such as titanium nitride (TiN).
