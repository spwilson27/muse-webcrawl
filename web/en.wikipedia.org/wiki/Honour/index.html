Honour

Honour (Commonwealth English) or honor (American English) is a concept of perceived quality of worthiness and respectability that affects the social standing and self-evaluation of a person or group. Historically, honour was closely tied to reputation in the eyes of one's community: a person of honour kept their word, fulfilled obligations to family and rank, and defended their name against insult, while dishonour brought shame and social exclusion.

Historical codes

Many societies developed explicit honour codes. In feudal Europe, chivalry bound knights to courage, loyalty, and courtesy; in early modern Europe, gentlemen defended honour in duels, a practice that persisted into the nineteenth century despite legal bans. In Japan, the samurai code of bushidō prized loyalty unto death, and ritual suicide (seppuku) could restore a disgraced warrior's honour. Honour concepts also structured Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and South Asian social life, where family honour was often linked to the conduct—especially the perceived sexual conduct—of women, a linkage that has underpinned so-called "honour killings," now widely condemned and prosecuted as murder.

Honour in modern societies

Anthropologists and social psychologists distinguish "honour cultures"—where reputation must be actively defended, insults answered, and self-reliance prized—from "dignity cultures," in which worth is treated as inherent and inalienable, and disputes are referred to law. Research in the American South, for example, links Scots-Irish herding heritage to a persistent culture of honour. Vestiges of honour survive in professional codes ("your honour" for judges, military decorations, academic honours), in notions of "honour among thieves," and in the everyday expectation that honest people keep their promises.

Philosophical treatment

Philosophers have debated whether honour is merely external reputation or an internal standard of integrity. Modern ethics generally values the inner quality—honesty, courage, fidelity to principle—over the audience-dependent approval that classical honour required, though critics note that public esteem remains a powerful motivator of good and bad behaviour alike.
