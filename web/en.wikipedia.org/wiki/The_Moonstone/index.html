The Moonstone

The Moonstone: A Romance (1868) by Wilkie Collins is a 19th-century British epistolary novel. It is an early modern example of the detective novel, and established many of the ground rules of the genre.

Plot

The Moonstone is a huge yellow diamond, stolen from a sacred Hindu statue during the Siege of Seringapatam by Colonel John Herncastle. On her eighteenth birthday, Rachel Verinder inherits the gem — and it is stolen from her room that same night, under the eyes of three Indian jugglers who have been watching the house. The narrative, assembled by the family steward Gabriel Betteredge from the accounts of multiple witnesses — including the devout and detestable Miss Clack, and with the famous Sergeant Cuff investigating — gradually reveals that Rachel's suitor Franklin Blake took the diamond himself while under the influence of opium secretly administered to him. The diamond is eventually recovered by the Indians and returned to its shrine in India.

Significance

T. S. Eliot called The Moonstone "the first, the longest, and the best of modern English detective novels." It introduced devices now standard in the genre: the country-house setting, the professional detective, the inside-job revelation, red herrings, and reconstruction of the crime. The novel also reflects Victorian anxieties about British imperialism in India. It was serialised in Charles Dickens's magazine All the Year Round in 1868 and has been adapted many times for stage, film, radio and television.
