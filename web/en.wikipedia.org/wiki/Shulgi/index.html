Shulgi (Sumerian: 𒀭𒂄𒄀, dŠulgi), formerly read as Dungi, was the second king of the Third Dynasty of Ur, reigning for 48 years in the late 22nd and early 21st centuries BCE. Succeeding his father Ur-Nammu, the dynasty's founder, he transformed Ur's kingdom into a centralised empire and is regarded as one of the most accomplished rulers of ancient Mesopotamia.

Reign

The first half of Shulgi's reign was devoted to administration, cult and building; the second to sustained military expansion, with campaigns against the highland peoples of the Zagros — Simurrum, Lullubi and Karakhar — and the annexation or subjugation of territories from Susa to the upper Tigris region. He reorganised the state into a famously bureaucratic empire: provinces governed by appointed officials (ensi), a standing army under generals, a uniform taxation system (bala) levying goods from each province in rotation, and vast administrative centres such as Puzrish-Dagan, which processed tens of thousands of livestock. Standardised weights, measures, a reformed calendar, scribal schools and a royal courier road system with rest stations bound the empire together.

Self-deification and hymns

Unusually, Shulgi was deified during his own lifetime — his name written with the divine determinative and temples built to his cult — following the precedent of Naram-Sin of Akkad. An extensive cycle of royal hymns, composed for the scribal schools, celebrates his learning, music, athletic feats and hunting; in them Shulgi boasts of mastering the scribal art and of running between the shrines of Nippur and Ur, a feat commemorated in his early regnal years. These hymns are among the finest works of Sumerian literature.

Legacy

Shulgi's administrative machine outlived him briefly under Amar-Sin and Shu-Sin, but within decades the Ur III state collapsed under Amorite pressure, Elamite invasion and internal strain. His reign nonetheless stands as the high point of Sumerian civilisation and bureaucracy, attested by the hundreds of thousands of cuneiform administrative tablets surviving from his era.
