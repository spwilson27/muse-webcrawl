The Willys MB, and its Ford-built counterpart the Ford GPW, are four-wheel drive light military utility vehicles manufactured during World War II. Together they are the standardised "jeep" of the Allied forces: some 650,000 were built between 1941 and 1945, making the vehicle an enduring symbol of the American war effort. The U.S. Army formally designated it the "Truck, 1/4-ton, 4×4, Command Reconnaissance".

Development

In 1940 the U.S. Army sought a lightweight reconnaissance vehicle and invited bids on an extremely tight schedule. The American Bantam Car Company produced the first prototype, the Bantam BRC, but lacked production capacity. Willys-Overland's design — chiefly engineered by Karl Probst's Bantam work adapted by Willys, with Barney Roos developing the "Go-Devil" engine — won the contract as the Willys MA and, after standardisation improvements, the MB. Ford was brought in as a second manufacturer, building the nearly identical GPW to the same interchangeable specification. Distinctive features included the slatted grille (replaced on the MB by the stamped nine-slot grille later associated with the Jeep brand), flat fenders, folding windshield, and blackout lighting.

Service

The jeep served virtually every Allied army in every theatre, as a staff car, weapons carrier, ambulance, artillery tractor, and radio vehicle. Generals praised its versatility; Ernie Pyle called it, with the Douglas C-47, among the war's most important non-combat weapons, and Dwight D. Eisenhower credited it as one of the tools that won the war. Examples were supplied to the Soviet Union and other allies under Lend-Lease.

Legacy

After the war Willys adapted the design into the civilian CJ ("Civilian Jeep") series, and the vehicle ultimately gave its name and design language to the Jeep marque. The origin of the word "jeep" is disputed — commonly linked to the slurred initials "GP" (general purpose) or to Eugene the Jeep, a character in the Popeye comics. Original MB and GPW vehicles are highly prized by military-vehicle collectors.
