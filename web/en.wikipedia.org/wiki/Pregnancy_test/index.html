Pregnancy test

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pregnancy test is used to determine whether a person is pregnant. Modern tests detect the hormone human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which is produced by the developing placenta shortly after implantation of a fertilized egg and appears in blood and urine. The two main types are urine tests — including home pregnancy tests — and quantitative blood tests performed in clinical settings.

Accuracy and timing

Home urine tests are about 99 percent accurate when used correctly from the day of a missed period; testing earlier increases the chance of a false negative because hCG levels may still be low. Blood tests can detect hCG earlier and measure its concentration, which can help assess very early pregnancy and detect problems such as ectopic pregnancy. False positives are uncommon but can result from certain medications, recent pregnancy loss, or rare medical conditions.

History

Ancient Egyptian texts describe a test in which a woman urinated on wheat and barley seeds — sprouting was taken as a sign of pregnancy. In the 1920s the Aschheim–Zondek test injected urine into immature mice and examined ovarian changes; the later "rabbit test" and frog-based Hogben test followed the same bioassay principle. Immunological tests replaced animals in the 1960s, and Margaret Crane's design work led to the first home test kits in the 1970s; lateral-flow "dipstick" tests followed in the late 1980s.
