Gastroenteritis, also known as infectious diarrhea or stomach flu, is inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract — the stomach and intestines — causing diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and sometimes fever. It is most often caused by viral infection, especially norovirus in adults and rotavirus in children, but bacteria such as Campylobacter, Salmonella, and toxin-producing E. coli, and parasites such as Giardia, also cause it.

Signs and transmission

The condition spreads through contaminated food and water or person-to-person contact via the fecal–oral route. Symptoms typically begin 12–72 hours after exposure and last a few days. The main danger is dehydration, particularly in infants, the elderly, and the immunocompromised.

Treatment and prevention

Most cases resolve without specific treatment. Oral rehydration solution — water with balanced salts and glucose — is the cornerstone of therapy and has greatly reduced child mortality worldwide. Antibiotics are reserved for certain bacterial causes, and antimotility drugs are generally discouraged in children. Prevention centers on hand hygiene, safe water, food safety, and rotavirus vaccination, which is now part of routine childhood immunization in many countries.

Epidemiology

Gastroenteritis causes roughly 1.5–2 billion cases annually and remains a leading cause of death in young children in low-income settings, though mortality has fallen sharply with rehydration therapy and sanitation improvements.
