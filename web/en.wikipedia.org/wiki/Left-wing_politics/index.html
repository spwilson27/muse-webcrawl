Left-wing politics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Left-wing politics describes the range of political ideologies that support and seek to achieve social equality and egalitarianism, often in opposition to social hierarchy as a whole or to particular hierarchies. The terms derive from the French Revolution, when anti-royalist deputies sat on the left of the president in the National Assembly. Left-wing politics typically involves a concern for those in society whom its adherents perceive as disadvantaged relative to others, as well as a belief that unjustified inequalities should be reduced or abolished through political and social reforms or socially owned means of production.

Spectrum and currents

The mainstream left — the centre-left — includes social democrats and social liberals who accept markets with strong welfare states, public services and regulation; further left stand democratic socialists, communists, and anarchists and other far-left currents rejecting capitalism or the state itself. Historically the left championed labour rights, suffrage extension, secularism, decolonisation and civil rights; contemporary left politics also encompasses environmentalism, feminism and multiculturalism, sometimes in tension with older class-based programmes.

Criticism and usage

Right-wing critics associate the left with excessive state power and economic levelling; internal debates cover markets vs. planning, identity vs. class and reform vs. revolution. The left–right scale is context-dependent — positions counted as centre-left in Europe may be framed differently in the United States — and scholars often supplement it with a second, libertarian–authoritarian axis.
