Joseph Chamberlain (8 July 1836 – 2 July 1914) was a British statesman who was first a radical Liberal, then a Liberal Unionist after opposing home rule for Ireland, and eventually served as Colonial Secretary in a Conservative government. He was a dominant figure in British politics for decades without ever becoming prime minister, and he is famous for splitting both major parties: the Liberal Party in 1886 and the Conservative Party in 1903.

Business and Birmingham

Born in London, Chamberlain moved to Birmingham at eighteen to join the family screw-manufacturing business, in which he made a fortune. As mayor of Birmingham (1873–76), he became the leading exponent of "municipal socialism" — sometimes called "gas and water socialism" — bringing utilities under civic ownership, clearing slums, and transforming the city with new libraries, baths, and public buildings. His "civic gospel" made Birmingham a model of progressive municipal government.

Radical Liberal

Elected to Parliament in 1876, Chamberlain built a formidable political machine through the Birmingham caucus and the National Liberal Federation. As President of the Board of Trade in Gladstone's second government (1880–85), he pressed radical causes including free education. In 1885 he issued the "Unauthorized Programme," demanding land reform and graduated taxation under the slogan of "Three acres and a cow."

Home Rule split and Colonial Secretary

In 1886 Chamberlain broke with Gladstone over Irish Home Rule, founding the Liberal Unionists and dooming the bill. Allied with Lord Salisbury's Conservatives, he became Secretary of State for the Colonies in 1895, the office he made famous. He promoted imperial development, favored imperial federation, and bore heavy responsibility for the diplomacy preceding the Second Boer War (1899–1902), which he defended vigorously though he was implicated in controversy over the Jameson Raid.

Tariff Reform and final years

In 1903 Chamberlain resigned from the cabinet to campaign for tariff reform — imperial preference in trade — splitting the Unionist alliance and contributing to its landslide defeat in 1906. He suffered a stroke that year and withdrew from active politics, dying in Birmingham on 2 July 1914, days before the outbreak of the First World War. His sons Austen and Neville both became leading statesmen, Neville serving as prime minister from 1937 to 1940. The University of Birmingham, which he helped found and served as first chancellor, commemorates him with the Joseph Chamberlain Memorial Clock Tower ("Old Joe").
