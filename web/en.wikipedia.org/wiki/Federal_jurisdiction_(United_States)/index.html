Federal jurisdiction (United States)

Federal jurisdiction is the legal authority of United States federal courts to hear a civil case or criminal charge. Because the United States is a federal system, jurisdiction is divided between federal and state courts.

Federal jurisdiction

Source: Article III, Section 2, U.S. Constitution; Title 28 U.S. Code

Types

The two principal bases of civil subject-matter jurisdiction are federal question jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. §1331) — cases arising under the Constitution, federal laws, or treaties — and diversity jurisdiction (§1332), where parties are citizens of different states and the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000.

Supplemental and removal jurisdiction

Under §1367, federal courts may exercise supplemental jurisdiction over related state-law claims. Defendants may remove eligible cases from state to federal court under §1441. Federal courts lack jurisdiction where sovereign immunity, the Rooker-Feldman doctrine, or abstention doctrines apply.

Criminal jurisdiction

Federal criminal jurisdiction covers offenses defined by federal statutes, crimes on federal enclaves, and conduct affecting interstate commerce. The Erie doctrine governs application of state substantive law in diversity cases.
