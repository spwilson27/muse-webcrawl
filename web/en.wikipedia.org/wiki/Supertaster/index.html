Supertaster

A supertaster is a person whose sense of taste is substantially more intense than average. The term was coined by experimental psychologist Linda Bartoshuk and colleagues at Yale University, who studied individual variation in taste perception using the bitter compound propylthiouracil (PROP).

Biology

People can be divided roughly into non-tasters, medium tasters and supertasters according to their sensitivity to the bitter taste of PROP and the related compound phenylthiocarbamide (PTC). Sensitivity is strongly influenced by variants of the bitter taste receptor gene TAS2R38. Supertasters also tend to have a higher density of fungiform papillae — the structures housing taste buds — on the tongue, which magnifies not only taste but also oral sensations of texture, fat and the burn of chilli or alcohol.

Prevalence

Roughly 25 percent of people in studied populations are classified as supertasters, around 50 percent as medium tasters and 25 percent as non-tasters, though proportions vary between ethnic groups, and women are more likely than men to be supertasters.

Implications

Supertasters often find bitter vegetables such as Brussels sprouts and kale, grapefruit, coffee and dark chocolate intensely unpleasant, and may avoid certain healthy foods; research has linked supertasting to differences in vegetable intake, alcohol consumption and some measures of body weight and disease risk, though findings are mixed. The heightened perception can also be an advantage in food professions, where sensitive palates are valued.
