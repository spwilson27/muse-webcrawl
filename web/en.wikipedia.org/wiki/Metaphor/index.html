Metaphor

A visual metaphor — the light bulb as an idea

A metaphor is a figure of speech that, for rhetorical effect, directly refers to one thing by mentioning another. It may provide clarity or identify hidden similarities between two different ideas. Unlike a simile, which makes an explicit comparison with "like" or "as", a metaphor states the comparison directly: "All the world's a stage" (Shakespeare).

Structure

Analyses since Aristotle describe a metaphor in terms of a tenor (subject) and a vehicle (the image), terms coined by I. A. Richards. In cognitive linguistics, George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's Metaphors We Live By (1980) argued that metaphor is not merely decorative language but structures thought itself — e.g. ARGUMENT IS WAR ("he demolished my point") and TIME IS MONEY ("spend time").

Types

Related forms include extended metaphor, mixed metaphor, dead metaphor (one so conventional its origin is forgotten, such as "the leg of a table"), allegory, and metonymy, in which an associated attribute stands for the thing.
