Freemium

Business modelFree basic tier + paid premium tier
Term coinedPopularised 2006 (from free + premium)

Freemium, a portmanteau of the words "free" and "premium", is a pricing strategy by which a basic product or service is provided free of charge, but money is charged for additional features, services or virtual goods. The model has been used by businesses since the 1980s, especially in software, and became dominant on the web and in mobile applications in the 2010s.

Origins

The practice predates the term: software companies long distributed free "lite" versions to attract users to paid full versions. The word itself was suggested in 2006 in response to a blog post by venture capitalist Fred Wilson describing the model, with the coinage credited to Jarid Lukin. In his book Free: The Future of a Radical Price (2009), Chris Anderson popularised the economics of zero-price digital goods.

How it works

A freemium service lowers the barrier to adoption by making the core product free, then converts a minority of users — often only 2–5 per cent — into paying customers for premium tiers that remove limits or advertising or add storage, collaboration or advanced features. Because the marginal cost of serving additional free users is low for digital products, the free tier functions as marketing. Conversion strategies include free trials, feature gating, usage caps and team plans sold to employers.

Examples and criticism

Prominent freemium services include Spotify (ad-supported music versus Premium), Dropbox (limited free storage), LinkedIn, Slack and Zoom. Critics note that free tiers may be subsidised by advertising and data collection, that conversion rates vary widely and many startups fail because free users never convert, and that aggressive upselling or deliberately crippled free tiers can alienate users. The model differs from free-to-play games mainly in emphasis: freemium usually refers to productivity and media software sold as subscriptions.
