A truth commission, or truth and reconciliation commission, is an official body charged with discovering and revealing past wrongdoing by a government (or, depending on the circumstances, non-state actors), in the hope of resolving conflict left over from the past. Truth commissions are, under various names, an element of transitional justice. They are usually established by states emerging from periods of internal conflict, civil war, or authoritarian rule.

Functions

Truth commissions typically hold public hearings at which victims testify, gather statements and documents, and issue a final report with findings and recommendations, which may include reparations, institutional reform, and — in some designs, such as South Africa's — amnesty for perpetrators who make full disclosure. Unlike trials, commissions focus on establishing a broad historical record and acknowledging victims rather than punishing individual offenders.

Examples

Notable commissions include Argentina's CONADEP (1983), whose report Nunca Más documented disappearances under the military junta; Chile's Rettig Commission (1990); El Salvador's UN-backed Commission on the Truth (1992); South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1995), chaired by Desmond Tutu; Peru's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2001); Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission on residential schools (2008); and Colombia's Commission for the Clarification of Truth (2018).

Criticism

Scholars debate whether truth commissions promote reconciliation or instead trade justice for stability, and whether amnesty provisions undermine deterrence and victims' rights. Empirical studies have found mixed effects, with some suggesting commissions work best when combined with trials and reparations programmes.
