Dordogne (French pronunciation: [dɔʁdɔɲ]; Occitan: Dordonha) is a large rural department in southwestern France, with its prefecture in Périgueux. Located in the region of Nouvelle-Aquitaine between the Loire Valley and the Pyrenees, it is named after the river Dordogne that runs through it. It roughly corresponds to the ancient county of Périgord.

Geography

The department is part of the Aquitaine Basin and is characterised by rolling hills, oak and chestnut forests, and limestone plateaus cut by the Dordogne, Vézère and Isle rivers. It is divided informally into four colour-coded areas: Périgord Vert (green, north), Périgord Blanc (white, centre), Périgord Pourpre (purple, southwest, around Bergerac) and Périgord Noir (black, southeast, around Sarlat-la-Canéda).

History and tourism

The Vézère valley is famous for its prehistoric sites, including the Lascaux cave paintings and the Cro-Magnon rock shelters, recognised as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Dordogne is also noted for its many medieval castles — traditionally said to number around a thousand — its bastide towns such as Monpazier, and its gastronomy, including foie gras, truffles, walnuts and Bergerac wines. It is a popular destination for British holidaymakers and second-home owners.
