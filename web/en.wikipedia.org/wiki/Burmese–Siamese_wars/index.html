The Burmese–Siamese wars were a series of conflicts fought between the kingdoms of Burma and Siam from the mid-16th to the mid-19th century. Control of the Tenasserim coast, the trans-peninsula trade routes, and the manpower of the Chao Phraya and Irrawaddy valleys made the two states the principal rivals of mainland Southeast Asia for three centuries.

Background

War between Burmese and Siamese states was not primarily about land but about people: in the sparsely populated region, capturing and deporting skilled populations was the surest way to increase a kingdom's wealth. Control of ports on the Andaman Sea and the Gulf of Siam also gave access to lucrative Indian Ocean and South China Sea trade.

Principal wars

Sixteenth century

The Toungoo dynasty of Burma invaded Ayutthaya in 1547–49 and again in 1563–64, when King Bayinnaung made Siam a vassal state. Siamese independence was restored by King Naresuan, whose victories in the 1580s and 1590s, including the famous elephant duel of 1593, ended Burmese overlordship and pushed Siamese power north into Lan Na.

Fall of Ayutthaya (1765–1767)

The most destructive campaign came under the Konbaung dynasty. Burmese armies converged on Ayutthaya in 1765 and, after a fourteen-month siege, stormed and destroyed the city in April 1767. Thousands of Siamese artisans, scholars and nobles were deported to Burma. Within a few years, however, the general Taksin expelled the Burmese garrisons and reunified Siam from his new capital at Thonburi.

Later conflicts

Burmese invasion attempts continued under the Chakri dynasty, notably the Nine Armies' War of 1785–86 and campaigns of 1797, 1802 and 1809, all repelled. The last Burmese–Siamese fighting took place in the 1850s over Kengtung. By then British annexation of Burma was shifting the regional balance entirely.

Legacy

The wars remain central to the national histories of both Myanmar and Thailand, celebrated in Thai royal chronicles, literature and film, and commemorated in the careers of figures such as Naresuan, Taksin and Rama I.
