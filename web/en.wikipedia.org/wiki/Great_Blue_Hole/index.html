Great Blue Hole

The Great Blue Hole, seen from the air.

The Great Blue Hole is a giant marine sinkhole off the coast of Belize. It lies near the centre of Lighthouse Reef, a small atoll 70 kilometres (43 miles) from the mainland and Belize City. The hole is circular in shape, 318 metres (1,043 feet) across and 124 metres (407 feet) deep, making it one of the largest sea caves or sinkholes in the world. Its striking deep-blue colour contrasts with the turquoise shallows of the surrounding reef.

Formation

The Great Blue Hole formed during several phases of Quaternary glaciation, when sea levels were far lower than today. Limestone caves dissolved in what was then dry land were submerged as the ocean rose after the last ice age, and the collapse of their roofs created the sinkhole. Divers have found submerged stalactites tilted from the vertical, evidence that the underlying rock shifted after the caves formed.

Exploration and diving

The site was made famous by Jacques Cousteau, who investigated it with the Calypso in 1971 and declared it one of the world's top scuba diving sites. Divers descend past a sandy slope into a cavern system with massive stalactites, meeting Caribbean reef sharks and other pelagic species; below about 90 metres the water is anoxic and devoid of life. In 2018, a sonar-mapping expedition in the submersible Roatán produced a complete three-dimensional survey of the hole. The Great Blue Hole forms part of the Belize Barrier Reef Reserve System, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
