Pachomius the Great

Saint Pachomius the Great (c. 292 – 9 May 348) was an Egyptian monk generally regarded as the founder of Christian cenobitic monasticism—monastic life lived in community under a rule, as distinct from the solitary life of hermits. His federation of monasteries in Upper Egypt, organized under a written rule, became the model for later communal monasticism in both East and West.

Life

Pachomius was born in the Thebaid in Upper Egypt to pagan parents. Conscripted into the Roman army around 312, he was impressed by the charity of Christians who ministered to imprisoned recruits and converted to Christianity after his discharge. After baptism he became a disciple of the hermit Palaemon, living as an anchorite near the Nile.

According to tradition, a vision at the deserted village of Tabennisi instructed him to build a monastery for the many who would come to him. Around 320 he founded his first community there, organizing monks into houses by craft, with common prayer, meals, and work under obedience to a superior.

The Pachomian federation

By Pachomius's death, his federation—the Koinonia—comprised nine monasteries for men and two for women, housing several thousand monks. His sister Mary led the women's communities. Pachomius drew up a Rule, the earliest known Christian monastic rule, prescribing the hours of prayer, manual labour, dress, and discipline. His successor Horsiesius and Theodore of Tabennisi continued the federation.

Influence

The Pachomian model spread through Egypt and beyond. Basil of Caesarea drew on it for his own ascetic rules, and through translations by Jerome and the influence on John Cassian and Benedict of Nursia, Pachomian communal organization shaped Western monasticism. Pachomius is venerated as a saint in the Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, and Roman Catholic churches; his feast day is 9 May in the West and 15 May in the East.
