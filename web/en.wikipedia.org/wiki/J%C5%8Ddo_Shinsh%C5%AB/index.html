Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗, "The True Essence of the Pure Land Teaching"), also known as Shin Buddhism or True Pure Land Buddhism, is a school of Pure Land Buddhism founded by the Japanese religious reformer Shinran (1173–1263), a former Tendai monk and disciple of Hōnen. It is the most widely practised branch of Buddhism in Japan.

Doctrine

Shinran taught that, in the age of mappō (the latter days of the Dharma), human beings cannot attain enlightenment through their own self-power (jiriki) practices. Instead, liberation comes entirely through other-power (tariki): entrusting oneself to Amida Buddha's Primal Vow, which promises birth in the Pure Land to all who sincerely entrust themselves to Amida. The central practice is the nembutsu — reciting "Namu Amida Butsu" ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha") — understood not as a meritorious act but as an expression of gratitude for salvation already assured through shinjin (entrusting faith).

History

After Shinran's death, his daughter Kakushinni helped preserve his teachings, and the Hongan-ji temple in Kyoto became the institutional centre of the movement. Under the eighth head priest Rennyo (1415–1499), Jōdo Shinshū expanded dramatically among peasants and provincial warriors. In the Edo period the Hongan-ji split into the Nishi (Western) and Higashi (Eastern) branches, which remain the two largest Shin organizations. Shin priests, unlike most Buddhist clergy in Japan, may marry and eat meat — a practice begun by Shinran himself.
