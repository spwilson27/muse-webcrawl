World literature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Canonical works from world literature

World literature is used to refer to the total of the world's national literatures, but usually it refers to the circulation of works into the wider world beyond their country of origin. Often used in the past primarily for masterpieces of Western European literature, world literature today is increasingly seen in a global context. Readers today have access to an unprecedented range of works from around the world in translation, and scholarly interest in the notion of world literature has grown with globalisation.

The concept

The term derives from the German Weltliteratur, coined by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe in 1827 in conversations recorded by his secretary Johann Peter Eckermann. Goethe declared that "national literature is now a rather unmeaning term; the epoch of world literature is at hand". The concept was later taken up by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels in The Communist Manifesto (1848), which linked the rise of a world literature to the global expansion of the bourgeoisie.

Modern scholarship

Contemporary theorists disagree about what makes a work "world literature". David Damrosch, in What Is World Literature? (2003), proposed a circulation-based definition: world literature is writing that gains in translation and circulates beyond its culture of origin. Pascale Casanova's The World Republic of Letters (1999) examined the unequal international literary system, while Franco Moretti advocated "distant reading" of large literary corpora. Critics have raised concerns about the dominance of English-language publishing and the flattening of cultural difference in global markets.

Canon

Works commonly treated as world literature range from the Epic of Gilgamesh, Homer, and the Mahabharata through Dante, Shakespeare, Cervantes, Murasaki Shikibu's The Tale of Genji, and Journey to the West, to modern authors such as García Márquez, Achebe, Tagore and Murakami.
