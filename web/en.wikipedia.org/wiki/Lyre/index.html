Lyre

Lyre
Classification: Yoke lute / chordophone
Hornbostel–Sachs: 321.2
Origins: Sumer, 3rd millennium BC

The lyre is a stringed musical instrument in the yoke lute family. It consists of a soundbox with two arms joined by a crossbar, with strings running from the soundbox up to the crossbar. Lyres are played by strumming or plucking, often with a plectrum, and are among the oldest known string instruments.

History

The earliest lyres come from Sumer in Mesopotamia, dating to around 2600 BC, including the ornate Lyres of Ur decorated with gold and lapis bulls' heads. The instrument spread across the ancient Near East and Mediterranean. In ancient Greece the lyre was central to music education and poetry; lyric poetry takes its name from verse sung to the lyre. The kithara was its larger, professional wooden counterpart, associated with Apollo.

Types

Varieties include the Greek chelys (tortoiseshell lyre), the phorminx, the Welsh crwth, the Germanic lyres excavated at Sutton Hoo and Trossingen, and the East African lyres such as the Ethiopian krar and Kenyan nyatiti, which remain in use today.

In culture

The lyre is the attribute of Apollo, Orpheus, and the Muses, and a widespread symbol of music and poetry. Its shape is echoed in the lyrebird's tail and in constellation Lyra, named for Orpheus's instrument.
