Grand Tour

The Grand Tour was the traditional trip through Europe, principally Italy and France, undertaken by upper-class young European — especially British — men of means from about 1660 until the advent of mass rail travel in the 1840s. It was considered a rite of passage completing a gentleman's education.

Periodc. 1660 – 1840s
ParticipantsAristocratic young men, artists, scholars
Key destinationsParis, Turin, Florence, Rome, Naples, Venice

Itinerary

Travellers crossed the Channel to Calais, visited Paris, then crossed the Alps to Turin, Milan, Florence — to study Renaissance art — Rome for classical antiquities and the Vatican, Naples for Herculaneum and Pompeii (excavated from 1738/1748), and returned via Venice, Germany and the Low Countries. Journeys lasted months to years, accompanied by a tutor called a bear-leader.

Cultural impact

Tourists brought home classical art, books and tastes that fuelled Neoclassicism, Palladian architecture and collecting. Writers from Goethe (Italian Journey) to Byron memorialised it. Women, artists like Angelica Kauffmann, and later Americans also undertook tours. See Rome and Neoclassicism.
