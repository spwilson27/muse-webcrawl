Republic of Texas

The Republic of Texas (Spanish: República de Tejas) was a sovereign state in North America between the United States and Mexico that existed from 2 March 1836 to 19 February 1846. Bordered by Mexico to the west and southwest, the Gulf of Mexico to the southeast, the two US states of Louisiana and Arkansas to the east and northeast, and the US unorganized territory encompassing present-day states like Oklahoma, Kansas, Colorado, Wyoming, and New Mexico to the north and northwest. The Anglo residents of the area and the Mexican government of Antonio López de Santa Anna both considered it part of Coahuila y Tejas.

History

Texas declared independence from Mexico during the Texas Revolution of 1835–1836. After the Texian victory at the Battle of San Jacinto on 21 April 1836, the Republic was de facto independent, though Mexico never recognised its sovereignty and continued to claim it as a rebellious territory. The United States recognised Texas in 1837, followed by France, Britain, the Netherlands and Belgium, but Mexico rejected recognition.

The Republic claimed borders based on the Treaties of Velasco — the Rio Grande to the south and west — but never controlled much of the claimed western territory, including Santa Fe, which remained under Mexican control. Comanche and other Native American nations contested Texian expansion throughout the republic's existence.

Sam Houston, the first elected president, favoured annexation by the United States and peace with Native Americans and Mexico. His successor, Mirabeau B. Lamar, opposed annexation, pursued an aggressive policy toward Native peoples, moved the capital to Austin, and expanded education. Mounting public debt and continuing border conflict pushed the republic toward annexation; under President Anson Jones, Texas was admitted to the Union as the 28th state on 29 December 1845, an act that helped precipitate the Mexican–American War of 1846–1848.

Government and politics

The Constitution of 1836 modelled the republic closely on that of the United States, with a president, a bicameral Congress, and a judiciary, though it also protected slavery and barred free Black people from citizenship. The president served a single three-year term and could not succeed himself immediately, a provision that led to the alternation of Houston and Lamar in office.

International relations

The republic maintained legations in Washington, London and Paris and concluded treaties of commerce and amity. Britain and France, interested in checking American expansion and securing cotton and trade, cultivated relations with Texas, which in turn used the prospect of European alignment as leverage to press the United States for annexation.
