Western blot

The western blot (sometimes called the protein immunoblot) is a widely used analytical technique in molecular biology and immunogenetics to detect specific proteins in a sample of tissue homogenate or extract. It uses gel electrophoresis to separate denatured proteins by length or by three-dimensional structure, transfers them to a membrane, and probes the membrane with antibodies specific to the target protein.

ClassificationAnalytical technique; immunoblot
AnalytesProteins
Related techniquesSouthern blot, northern blot, ELISA

History

The technique was described in 1979 by Harry Towbin and colleagues, building on earlier blotting methods, and was given the name "western blot" by W. Neal Burnette in 1981 as a play on the Southern blot (named after Edwin Southern) and the northern blot for RNA.

Steps

Sample preparation and electrophoresis

Protein samples are denatured, usually with the detergent SDS, and separated by size using SDS-PAGE (polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis). Smaller proteins migrate faster through the gel.

Transfer

The separated proteins are transferred ("blotted") from the gel onto a membrane, typically nitrocellulose or PVDF, using an electric field, so that they are accessible to antibodies.

Blocking and antibody probing

The membrane is blocked to prevent non-specific binding, then incubated with a primary antibody specific to the protein of interest, followed by a labelled secondary antibody. The label — commonly the enzyme horseradish peroxidase — generates a detectable signal by chemiluminescence, colorimetry or fluorescence.

Applications

Western blotting is used in research to measure protein expression and in medical diagnostics, historically including confirmatory testing for HIV antibodies and for BSE-associated prion protein. Its specificity derives from the combination of size separation and antibody recognition, though antibody quality is critical and results are generally semi-quantitative.
