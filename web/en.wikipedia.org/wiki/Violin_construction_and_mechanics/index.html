Violin construction and mechanics

The principal components of a violin

The construction of a violin combines traditional craftsmanship with the physics of stringed instruments. A modern violin consists of a hollow resonating body, a neck with fingerboard, four strings tuned in fifths (G3, D4, A4, E5), and accessories including a bridge, tailpiece, chinrest and fine tuners. The body is assembled from carved plates of spruce (top) and maple (back, ribs and neck), joined with hide glue so that repairs remain possible.

The body

The top plate, or soundboard, is carved from quarter-sawn Norway spruce and graduated to thicknesses of roughly 2.4–3.5 mm. Its two f-holes flank the bridge and influence both flexibility and air resonance. Inside, a bass bar of spruce is glued beneath the top along the G-string side, while a soundpost of spruce is wedged between top and back near the treble foot of the bridge, transmitting vibration and supporting the arching. The back is usually carved from flamed maple in one or two pieces. Purfling inlaid around the edge protects against cracks and stiffens the plate.

Acoustics and mechanics

The strings pass over the bridge at a height that produces a down-bearing force of roughly 80 newtons. The bridge acts as an impedance matcher and filter between the strings and the body; its "bridge hill" resonance lies near 2–3 kHz and contributes to brilliance. The lowest main body resonance, the Helmholtz-like air resonance through the f-holes, is near 275 Hz, while prominent corpus modes (often labelled A0, B1− and B1+) structure the low and mid frequency response. Bowing mechanics follow the Helmholtz motion of the string, in which a single corner circulates around an envelope bounded by the bow and the finger.

Neck, fingerboard and fittings

The neck, scroll and pegbox are carved from maple. The fingerboard, traditionally ebony, projects over the body at an angle that sets the bridge height. Four friction pegs (or geared pegs) tension the strings in the pegbox. Modern players almost universally use a steel E string and synthetic-core or gut strings for the lower courses, with fine tuners integrated into the tailpiece. The chinrest, patented in the early nineteenth century, clamps over the tailpiece end and protects the top plate.

History

The violin's form was stabilised in Cremona, Italy, in the sixteenth century by the Amati family and later makers such as Antonio Stradivari and Giuseppe Guarneri. Nineteenth-century demands for greater projection led to a longer neck, higher string angle, heavier bass bar and the modern Tourte-model bow.
