Localism in Hong Kong

Localism in Hong Kong is a political movement that emerged in the early 2010s, centred on the preservation of Hong Kong's distinct cultural identity, autonomy and way of life under the "one country, two systems" framework, and resistance to the perceived political, economic and cultural influence of mainland China. Localist thought ranges from defending the Basic Law's promised high degree of autonomy, through demands for greater self-determination, to outright advocacy of Hong Kong independence.

Origins

The movement grew out of heritage-preservation and community campaigns of the 2000s — the defence of the Star Ferry and Queen's Piers, the Anti-Express Rail Link movement — and from grievances over parallel trading, mainland "birth tourism", individual-visit scheme tourism, and the erosion of Cantonese culture and Hong Kong's local identity. Scholar Chin Wan's 2011 book On the Hong Kong City-State argued for treating Hong Kong as a distinct polity and helped supply the movement's intellectual language. Online forums and social media were key organising spaces.

Development

After the 2014 Umbrella Movement failed to win universal suffrage, younger activists turned toward localism; groups such as Hong Kong Indigenous (founded 2015) and Youngspiration promoted localist candidates and rhetoric. In the 2016 Legislative Council elections several localists won seats, but a controversy over oath-taking — in which two Youngspiration legislators used the oath ceremony to advocate independence — led to their disqualification following an interpretation of the Basic Law by Beijing, and further candidates were barred from standing. Localist sentiment fed into the atmosphere preceding the 2019 anti-extradition bill protests, though the movement's organised groups largely declined or were dissolved after the imposition of the Hong Kong national security law in 2020, which criminalised secession and led to the disbandment of many political organisations.

Ideas and criticism

Localism emphasises Hong Kong identity, Cantonese, local priority in housing and welfare, and democratic self-government. Critics within the traditional pro-democracy camp and among commentators have accused some strands of localism of nativism and xenophobia toward mainlanders, while supporters argue it is a civic movement defending a distinct community and its institutions.
