Dievturība is a modern Pagan religious tradition in Latvia that seeks to reconstruct and revive the pre-Christian religion of the Latvian people. Its name derives from the Latvian words Dievs ("God") and turēt ("to hold, to maintain"), so its adherents, called Dievturi, are "those who hold to God". The movement draws primarily on Latvian folklore, especially the vast corpus of folk songs known as dainas, as well as folk customs, mythology and the seasonal calendar.

History

The movement was founded in the 1920s during Latvia's first period of independence, with the first Dievturi congregation established in 1925–1926. A central figure was the artist and historian Ernests Brastiņš (1892–1942), who systematised the tradition's theology and ethics from folk-song material. Suppressed during the Soviet occupation of Latvia — Brastiņš was deported and died in Soviet captivity — the movement survived among the Latvian diaspora, particularly in the United States and Canada, and revived openly after Latvia regained independence in 1991.

Beliefs and practice

Dievturība is monistic in orientation: Dievs is understood as the supreme, universal source of reality, with other divine figures — such as Laima (fate and fortune), Māra (associated with the earth and birth) and Pērkons (thunder) — representing aspects or manifestations of the divine order rather than wholly separate gods. Ethics centre on the virtues celebrated in the dainas: diligence, generosity, wisdom, and harmony with nature and community. Practices include seasonal festivals tied to solstices and equinoxes (such as Jāņi at midsummer), name-giving, wedding and funeral ceremonies conducted in a Latvian folk idiom, and the singing of dainas.

Recognition

Dievturi communities exist in Latvia and in the diaspora. In 2025, Latvia's parliament passed legislation granting the Dievturi organisation a legal status comparable to that of traditionally recognised religious denominations, including rights concerning marriage ceremonies.
