Healthcare in the United States

Healthcare in the United States is provided by many distinct organisations, made up of insurance companies, healthcare providers, hospital systems, and independent providers. Health care facilities are largely owned and operated by private-sector businesses. The United States spends more on health care per person than any other country — roughly 18 per cent of GDP — yet has lower life expectancy and higher rates of uninsurance than peer nations.

Coverage

Most Americans obtain health insurance through their employers. Government programmes provide coverage for specific groups: Medicare for those aged 65 and over and some people with disabilities, Medicaid for low-income people (expanded in most states under the Affordable Care Act of 2010), the Children's Health Insurance Program, and military health systems such as TRICARE and the Veterans Health Administration. Despite these programmes, several per cent of the population — tens of millions of people — remain uninsured, and many more are considered underinsured because of deductibles and out-of-pocket costs.

Costs and outcomes

US health care prices for services, drugs, and administration are substantially higher than in other developed countries. Medical bills are a leading cause of personal bankruptcy. At the same time, the US is a leader in medical research, specialist care, and rapid access to elective procedures for the well-insured. Reform of the system — including proposals for universal coverage and a possible "public option" — has been a central issue in American politics for decades.
