Act of God

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, an act of God is a natural event not caused by any human agency — such as a flood, earthquake, volcanic eruption or lightning strike — that could not have been foreseen or prevented by the exercise of reasonable care. The term is used chiefly in the law of contract, tort and insurance in common-law jurisdictions.

Contract law

In contract law, an act of God may excuse a party from performing obligations, under doctrines such as impossibility, frustration or a force majeure clause. Carriers and bailees were traditionally not liable for goods destroyed by acts of God, in contrast to losses caused by human action, although statute has modified this position in many areas.

Insurance

Insurance policies often distinguish damage caused by acts of God (for example, storm or flood damage, which may be covered or expressly excluded depending on the policy) from damage caused by negligence. The phrase is not a technical term of art in insurance law, and courts interpret it according to the policy wording.

Distinctions

An event ceases to qualify as an act of God if human negligence contributed to the damage — for example, where a flood causes harm only because a dam was poorly maintained. The concept is related to, but narrower than, force majeure, which may also include war, terrorism and other extraordinary human events. In Scots law the equivalent term is damnum fatale.
