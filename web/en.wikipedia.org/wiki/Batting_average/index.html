Batting average

In baseball, a player's batting average is the ratio of hits to at bats, conventionally expressed as a three-decimal figure (for example, .300). It is one of the oldest and most familiar statistics in the sport, used to measure a batter's success at reaching base by hitting safely, though modern analysis considers it incomplete because it ignores walks and extra-base power.

History

The statistic was developed in the 19th century, adapted from cricket by pioneers such as Henry Chadwick. It long served as the headline measure of hitting: the league leader each season is the "batting champion". Ty Cobb holds the highest career average in Major League Baseball at .366, and no player has hit .400 in a season since Ted Williams batted .406 in 1941.

Interpretation

An average of .300 marks an excellent season; the league average typically fluctuates between about .240 and .280 across eras. Because batting average treats all hits equally and excludes bases on balls, sabermetricians supplement or replace it with on-base percentage and slugging percentage (combined as OPS). Nevertheless it remains prominent in records, awards, and everyday discussion.

Cricket

In cricket, batting average is runs scored divided by times out, so a strong Test average is around 50; Don Bradman's career average of 99.94 is the sport's most famous statistic.
