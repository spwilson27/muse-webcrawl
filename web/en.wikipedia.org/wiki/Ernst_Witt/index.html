Ernst Witt

Ernst Witt
Born: 26 June 1911, Als, German Empire (now Denmark)
Died: 3 July 1991 (aged 80), Hamburg, Germany
Alma mater: University of Göttingen
Fields: Algebra, quadratic forms, Lie algebras
Known for: Witt vectors; Witt group; Witt's theorem

Ernst Witt (26 June 1911 – 3 July 1991) was a German mathematician, one of the leading algebraists of his time. His name is attached to several fundamental constructions, notably the Witt vectors, the Witt ring and Witt group of quadratic forms, and Witt's theorem on the extension of isometries of quadratic spaces.

Life

Witt was born on the island of Als, then part of the German Empire, and grew up partly in China, where his father was a missionary. He studied at the University of Freiburg and the University of Göttingen, receiving his doctorate in 1934 under Gustav Herglotz (with Emmy Noether's algebraic influence evident in his work). He habilitated at Hamburg in 1937, joined the Hamburg faculty, and — after military service in the Second World War, during which he worked in a cryptographic unit — became professor at the University of Hamburg, where he remained until his retirement in 1979.

Mathematics

In his 1937 paper "Theorie der quadratischen Formen in beliebigen Körpern", Witt founded the algebraic theory of quadratic forms, introducing the Witt decomposition of a quadratic space into a hyperbolic part and an anisotropic kernel, and the Witt group — ideas that link algebra, topology and number theory. His Witt vectors (1936–37) provide a ring of "universal" p-adic-type integers built from any perfect field of characteristic p, a construction of lasting importance in arithmetic geometry. Other eponymous results include the Bourbaki–Witt fixed-point theorem, the Witt algebra (the complexification of the algebra of vector fields on a circle, central to conformal field theory), work on the Leech lattice and the Mathieu groups — Witt's 1938 work on Steiner systems anticipated the "Witt design", the Steiner system underlying the Mathieu group M24 — and a short, later-famous rediscovery proof related to the Cayley–Dickson construction.
