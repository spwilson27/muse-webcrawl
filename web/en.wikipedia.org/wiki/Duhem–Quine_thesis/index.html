Duhem–Quine thesis

The Duhem–Quine thesis (also called the Duhem–Quine problem) is a claim in the philosophy of science: a scientific hypothesis cannot be tested in isolation, because an empirical test always presupposes a body of auxiliary assumptions — about instruments, background theories, and initial conditions. When a prediction fails, logic alone cannot tell us whether to blame the hypothesis or one of the auxiliaries. The thesis is a form of confirmational holism.

Origins

The idea was developed by the French physicist and historian Pierre Duhem in The Aim and Structure of Physical Theory (1906), who argued that a physical experiment never condemns an isolated hypothesis but only a whole theoretical group. W. V. O. Quine generalised the point in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), portraying knowledge as a "web of belief" in which any statement can be held true, come what may, if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere — and conversely, no statement, not even a law of logic, is in principle immune to revision.

Implications

The thesis underlies the problem of underdetermination of theory by evidence and complicates falsificationism: there can be no perfectly "crucial experiment" that decisively refutes a single theory, because defenders can always redirect the modus tollens at an auxiliary assumption — as when astronomers saved Newtonian mechanics from Uranus's anomalous orbit by positing Neptune, but failed when a similar move postulated Vulcan for Mercury. Philosophers disagree over the thesis's scope: critics such as Donald Gillies argue that in practice auxiliaries can often be tested independently, weakening the holism to a practical, rather than logical, point.
