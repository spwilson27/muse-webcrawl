Cropmark

Cropmarks or crop marks are a means through which sub-surface archaeological, natural and recent features may be visible from the air or a vantage point on higher ground or a temporary platform. Along with soil marks and frost marks, they can reveal buried archaeological sites not visible from the ground.

Formation

Crops growing over a buried ditch or pit, where the soil is deeper and moister, grow taller and ripen later, producing a positive cropmark that appears darker and greener than the surrounding field. Conversely, crops over a buried wall or road, where the soil is thin and dry, are stunted and ripen early, producing a negative cropmark. The effect is strongest in cereals such as wheat and barley and during dry summers, when moisture differences are most pronounced.

Use in archaeology

Cropmarks are recorded mainly by aerial photography and, increasingly, by drone surveys. In Britain, the drought summer of 1976 and the hot summers of 2018 produced exceptional numbers of new discoveries, revealing Neolithic enclosures, Roman villas, medieval settlements and Second World War defences. Pioneers of the technique included O. G. S. Crawford, and the Cambridge University Committee for Aerial Photography assembled vast archives of cropmark sites. Cropmarks show only the plan of a feature; excavation or geophysical survey is needed to date and characterise what lies below.
