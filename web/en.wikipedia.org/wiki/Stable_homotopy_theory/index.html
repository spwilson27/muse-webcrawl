Stable homotopy theory

In mathematics, stable homotopy theory is the part of homotopy theory (and thus of algebraic topology) concerned with structures and phenomena that persist after applying the suspension functor enough times. Its fundamental objects are spectra, and its fundamental invariants are the stable homotopy groups of spheres.

Background

By the Freudenthal suspension theorem, the suspension homomorphism between homotopy groups of spheres becomes an isomorphism in a range of dimensions that grows with the dimension of the sphere; the resulting "stable" groups πnS no longer depend on the particular sphere chosen. Computing them is famously difficult: the first groups are π0S ≅ ℤ, π1S ≅ ℤ/2, π2S ≅ ℤ/2 and π3S ≅ ℤ/24, and beyond the first few stems the groups are known only through extensive computation, much of it driven by the Adams spectral sequence.

Spectra and generalised cohomology

A spectrum is a sequence of spaces (or simplicial sets) linked by suspension maps; categories of spectra provide the setting in which generalised (extraordinary) cohomology theories are represented. Examples include ordinary cohomology (Eilenberg–Mac Lane spectra), topological K-theory, and cobordism spectra such as MU. The chromatic approach organises the field by "height", relating stable homotopy to formal group laws and producing the celebrated periodicity phenomena of John Adams and others.

Significance

Stable homotopy theory underlies much of modern algebraic topology and has deep connections with algebraic geometry (motivic homotopy theory), number theory (through the image of the J-homomorphism and Bernoulli numbers), and mathematical physics.
