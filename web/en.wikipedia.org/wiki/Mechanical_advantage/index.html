Mechanical advantage

Mechanical advantage is a measure of the force amplification achieved by using a tool, mechanical device or machine system. The device trades off input forces against movement to obtain a desired amplification in the output force. The model for this is the law of the lever. Machine components designed to manage forces and movement in this way are called mechanisms.

Definition

The (actual) mechanical advantage is the ratio of the output force delivered by a machine to the input force applied to it: MA = Fout / Fin. For an ideal, frictionless machine, energy conservation implies that the same ratio equals the distance (or velocity) ratio in reverse: the force gained is paid for by moving the input through proportionally more distance, so that work in equals work out. Real machines fall short of the ideal mechanical advantage because of friction; the ratio of actual to ideal advantage gives the machine's efficiency.

Examples

For a lever, MA equals the ratio of the effort arm to the load arm — Archimedes' "give me a place to stand" boast. For a block and tackle, the ideal MA equals the number of rope segments supporting the moving block. For a screw, it depends on the circumference traced by the handle divided by the thread pitch; for gears and chain drives, on the ratio of teeth on the driven and driving wheels; for a hydraulic press, on the ratio of piston areas, an application of Pascal's principle.

Velocity ratio

Closely related is the velocity ratio: the ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load in the same time. In an ideal machine the velocity ratio equals the mechanical advantage; in real machines it exceeds it, the difference measuring frictional loss.
