Structural equation modeling (SEM) is a diverse set of methods used by scientists in both experimental and observational research across the sciences, business and other fields. It is used most in the social sciences and marketing. SEM involves the construction of a model representing how different aspects of some phenomenon are causally related to one another, and the comparison of that model's predictions with observed data.

SEM is formally defined by two sets of linear equations: the inner model, which specifies the causal relationships between latent variables, and the outer model or measurement model, which specifies the relationships between latent variables and their observed indicators. A latent variable cannot be measured directly but is inferred from several observed variables, allowing the analyst to separate measurement error from the relationships of substantive interest.

History

The roots of SEM lie in Sewall Wright's development of path analysis in the 1920s, Charles Spearman's factor analysis, and econometric simultaneous-equation models. The modern synthesis came in the 1970s with Karl Jöreskog's LISREL software, which combined factor analysis and path analysis in a covariance-based framework. Partial least squares SEM (PLS-SEM), an alternative variance-based approach developed from the work of Herman Wold, became popular in management and marketing research.

Steps in SEM

  • Model specification – defining the hypothesised relationships, usually drawn as a path diagram.
  • Model identification – ensuring the model has enough information (degrees of freedom) to estimate all parameters.
  • Parameter estimation – typically by maximum likelihood, minimising the discrepancy between the observed and model-implied covariance matrices.
  • Model evaluation – assessing fit using indices such as the chi-square test, CFI, TLI, RMSEA and SRMR.
  • Model modification – respecifying the model, cautiously, where theory and diagnostics suggest.

Software

Widely used SEM software includes LISREL, AMOS, Mplus, EQS, the R packages lavaan, sem and OpenMx, and Stata's sem command.

Criticism

SEM is criticised when researchers equate model fit with proof of causation, when modification indices are used to chase fit at the expense of theory, and when large samples make the chi-square test reject nearly every model. Critics emphasise that SEM tests the consistency of a causal story with data; it cannot by itself establish that the story is true.
