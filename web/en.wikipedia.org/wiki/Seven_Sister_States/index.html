Seven Sister States

The Seven Sister States are a region in northeastern India comprising the seven contiguous states of Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Meghalaya, Manipur, Mizoram, Nagaland and Tripura. The term was coined by the journalist Jyoti Prasad Saikia in 1972 during a radio talk show held to mark the inauguration of the new states. The region covers an area of about 255,511 square kilometres, roughly 7 percent of India's total area, and is connected to the rest of the country by the narrow Siliguri Corridor in West Bengal.

History

During British rule, most of the region was administered as part of the province of Assam. After Indian independence in 1947, the hill areas were progressively reorganised: Nagaland became a state in 1963; Meghalaya, Manipur and Tripura in 1972; and Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh in 1987. Sikkim, which joined India in 1975, is sometimes informally called the "brother" state of the seven sisters.

Geography and people

The region is predominantly hilly, drained by the Brahmaputra and Barak river systems, and receives some of the heaviest rainfall in the world, notably around Mawsynram and Cherrapunji in Meghalaya. It is home to a great diversity of ethnic groups — more than 200 distinct tribes are often cited — speaking languages of the Tibeto-Burman, Austroasiatic and Indo-Aryan families. The region shares long international borders with China, Myanmar, Bangladesh, Nepal and Bhutan.

Development

The Ministry for Development of North Eastern Region (DoNER), established in 2001, and the North Eastern Council coordinate development efforts across the states. Connectivity projects under India's "Act East" policy aim to link the region more closely with Southeast Asia.
