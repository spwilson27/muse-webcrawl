A mamluk (Arabic: مملوك, romanised: mamlūk, literally "owned") was a soldier of slave origin who was converted to Islam, trained in an elite military school, and manumitted on graduation. Though technically freed, mamluks remained bound by lifelong loyalty to their patron. From the 9th century onward, Muslim rulers across the Middle East relied on mamluk troops, and in several regions the mamluks seized power themselves — most famously in Egypt and Syria, where the Mamluk Sultanate ruled from 1250 to 1517.

Origins

The institution developed under the Abbasid caliphs of Baghdad, who recruited Turkic slave-soldiers from the Central Asian steppe as a loyal counterweight to tribal and factional forces. Boys were purchased young, converted, educated in Arabic, Islam, and the military arts (furusiyya — horsemanship, archery, lance, and sword), and then freed into the service of their master. Freed mamluks could rise to the highest commands; their sons, however, were generally excluded from the mamluk corps, which replenished itself by fresh imports of slaves — Kipchak Turks and later Circassians from the Caucasus.

The Mamluk Sultanate

In Egypt the Ayyubid sultan's mamluks took power in 1250. The Bahri dynasty (1250–1382), drawn from the Kipchak regiments quartered on the Nile, and the Burji dynasty (1382–1517) of Circassian mamluks ruled Egypt, Syria, and the Hejaz. Mamluk armies defeated the Mongols at Ain Jalut (1260) — a turning point in world history — and extinguished the last Crusader states, retaking Acre in 1291. Cairo under the Mamluks became one of the great cities of the Islamic world, adorned with mosques, madrasas, and mausolea renowned for their carved stone domes.

Decline and later history

The Ottoman Empire conquered the sultanate in 1516–17, but mamluk households persisted as Egypt's military aristocracy under Ottoman suzerainty until Muhammad Ali massacred their leaders in the Cairo Citadel massacre of 1811. Mamluk regimes also ruled elsewhere: the Mamluk (Slave) dynasty founded the Delhi Sultanate in India (1206–1290), and mamluk beys governed Iraq in the 18th century. Napoleon fought the Egyptian mamluks at the Battle of the Pyramids in 1798, and his own Imperial Guard included a Mamluk squadron.
