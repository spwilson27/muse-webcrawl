Tranche

A securitised pool sliced into tranches of different seniority and risk.

In structured finance, a tranche is one of a number of related securities offered as part of the same transaction. In the financial sense of the word, each bond is a different slice of the deal's risk. Transaction documentation (see prospectus) usually defines the tranches as different "classes" of notes, each identified by letter (Class A, Class B, Class C securities) with different bond credit ratings.

The term tranche means a division or portion of a pool or whole and is derived from the French for "slice", "section", "series", or "portion", and is also a cognate of the English "trench" ("to dig").

How tranches work

All the tranches together make up what is referred to as the deal's capital structure or liability structure. They are generally paid sequentially from the most senior to the most subordinate (subordinated), although certain tranches with the same security may be paid pro rata (equally). The more senior rated tranches generally have higher bond credit ratings (ratings) than the lower-rated tranches. For example, senior tranches may be rated AAA, AA or A, while a junior or unrated tranche may be rated BBB or not rated at all.

The deal's equity tranche is the riskiest, and is usually the first to absorb losses when defaults occur in the underlying pool of assets. In return, it typically offers the highest potential yield. This cascading payment structure is often described as a "waterfall".

Examples

Tranches are a defining feature of collateralised debt obligations (CDOs), collateralised mortgage obligations (CMOs) and other asset-backed securities. The practice of tranching played a central role in the financial crisis of 2007–2008, because losses in subprime mortgage pools propagated through the capital structures of mortgage-backed securities in ways that many investors had underestimated.
