Dying-and-rising deity

A dying-and-rising deity, or dying god, is a proposed motif in comparative mythology in which a god dies and is resurrected. The concept was popularised by Sir James George Frazer in The Golden Bough (1890), who associated such gods with vegetation cycles, the seasons, and fertility rituals in the ancient Near East and the Mediterranean.

Proposed examples

Deities frequently cited in Frazer's scheme include the Mesopotamian Dumuzi (Tammuz), the Egyptian Osiris, the Phrygian Attis, the Levantine Adonis and Baal, and the Greek Dionysus and Persephone. In Frazer's interpretation, the god's death and return mirrored the annual death and rebirth of crops, and ritual enactments of the myth were intended to ensure fertility.

Scholarly assessment

Modern scholarship has largely rejected the motif as a single universal category. Critics such as Jonathan Z. Smith argued that the "dying and rising god" is a scholarly construction: many of the proposed examples involve gods who die and do not return, who disappear and reappear without dying, or whose "resurrection" is a much later reinterpretation. A 1987 survey in the Encyclopedia of Religion concluded that clear cases are rare, with Baal and the Ugaritic narratives often given as the strongest example. The concept remains influential in popular literature and in debates about the relationship between pagan mythology and Christian accounts of the resurrection of Jesus.
