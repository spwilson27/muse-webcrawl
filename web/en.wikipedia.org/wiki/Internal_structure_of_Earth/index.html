Internal structure of Earth

Earth's layers
Crust: 5–70 km
Mantle: to 2,900 km depth
Outer core: 2,900–5,150 km
Inner core: 5,150–6,371 km

The internal structure of Earth is divided into layers by their chemical and physical properties. Working inwards, these are the crust, the mantle, the liquid outer core and the solid inner core. Knowledge of the interior comes almost entirely from seismology: changes in the speed and behaviour of seismic waves at boundaries reveal the composition and state of each layer.

Crust and mantle

The crust is Earth's thin outer skin, ranging from about 5 km beneath the oceans to 70 km under mountain ranges. Beneath it lies the mantle, composed of silicate rocks rich in iron and magnesium, extending to a depth of about 2,900 km. Although solid, mantle rock flows slowly over geological time, driving plate tectonics. The boundary between crust and mantle is the Mohorovičić discontinuity, or Moho.

Core

The core is an iron–nickel alloy. The outer core is liquid, and convection within it generates Earth's magnetic field through the geodynamo. The inner core, with a radius of about 1,220 km, is solid despite temperatures comparable to the surface of the Sun, because of immense pressure. Seismologist Inge Lehmann discovered the inner core in 1936 from reflections of seismic waves.
