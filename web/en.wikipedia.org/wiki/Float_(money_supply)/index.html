Float (money supply)

In economics, float is duplicate money present in the banking system during the time between a deposit being credited to the recipient's account and the amount being debited from the sender's account. It is most commonly associated with paper checks: a check deposited is provisionally credited, and until the check clears (is presented to and paid by the drawer's bank) the same money exists in both accounts and is counted, in effect, in the money supply twice.

Types of float

Banking distinguishes several components: mail float (checks in the mail), processing float (checks waiting to be handled), and clearing (or availability) float (checks in the clearing process). Businesses historically managed cash by exploiting float — "playing the float" — timing disbursements to maximise delay, a practice largely curtailed by expedited funds-availability rules such as the US Expedited Funds Availability Act (1987) and Regulation CC, and by electronic clearing.

Federal Reserve float

In the United States, "Federal Reserve float" arises when the Federal Reserve credits a depositing bank's reserve account before debiting the bank on which the check was drawn. Because reserves are part of the monetary base, large swings in float — for example from weather delaying check transport — complicate monetary policy implementation. The Monetary Control Act of 1980 required the Fed to price its services and reduce float. The decline of paper checks and the adoption of electronic image exchange under the Check 21 Act (effective 2004) have reduced float to a fraction of its former importance.
