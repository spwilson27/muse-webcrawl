Opera seria (plural: opere serie; usually translated as "serious opera") is an Italian musical term which refers to the noble and "serious" style of Italian opera that predominated in Europe from the 1710s to about 1770. The term itself is rarely used by contemporaries; it became common only in the nineteenth century, when the genre had fallen from favour, to distinguish it from opera buffa, the comic-opera genre of the same period.

Structure and conventions

Opera seria was built on the elevated, classicising librettos of Pietro Metastasio, whose dramas — drawn from ancient history and mythology and typically ending in magnanimous reconciliation (lieto fine) — were set dozens of times by different composers. A typical work has three acts alternating secco recitative, which advances the plot, with da capo arias, in which a character expresses a single emotion (an "aria of affect") and then exits. Star singers, especially castrati such as Farinelli and Senesino, commanded enormous fees and expected arias tailored to display their virtuosity.

Reform and decline

From mid-century, composers such as Christoph Willibald Gluck sought to reform opera seria's conventions, subordinating vocal display to drama — a programme announced in the preface to Alceste (1767) and realised also in Mozart's Idomeneo (1781). As tastes changed and the castrato voice disappeared, opera seria gave way to the mixed genres of the early nineteenth century, though works by Handel, Hasse, and Mozart were revived to acclaim in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.
