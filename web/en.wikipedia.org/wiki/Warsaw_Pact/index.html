Warsaw Pact

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Warsaw Pact (Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance) was a collective defence treaty established by the Soviet Union and seven other Eastern Bloc socialist republics of Central and Eastern Europe in Warsaw, Poland, on 14 May 1955. It was the military counterpart to NATO in the Cold War, created in response to West Germany's integration into NATO, and it formalised Soviet military dominance over its allies.

History

Members were the USSR, Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and (until 1968) Albania. The Pact's joint command — always headed by a Soviet officer — coordinated the bloc's forces, but its only major deployments were against its own members: Hungary in 1956 (with the earlier Soviet intervention) and the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia that crushed the Prague Spring; Romania refused to participate in 1968 and pursued an autonomous line. As communist regimes collapsed in 1989, the Pact lost its purpose; its military structures were dissolved in March 1991 and the treaty formally terminated on 1 July 1991 in Prague. Most former members subsequently joined NATO — the central grievance in later Russian security rhetoric.
