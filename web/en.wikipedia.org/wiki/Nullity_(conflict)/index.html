Nullity (conflict)

In law, nullity is the state of being null and void — that is, a juridical act, contract, marriage or legal proceeding that is treated as if it had never existed. The topic arises in conflicts between legal systems: where a marriage, judgment or transaction is valid under one jurisdiction's law but void under another's, courts must decide which act counts as a nullity. This article concerns nullity in the conflict-of-laws and legal sense, not the mathematical concept of nullity (mathematics).

Absolute and relative nullity

Civil law systems traditionally distinguish absolute nullity, which may be invoked by any interested party (and often by the court itself) because the act offends a rule of public order — for example, a contract with an illegal object — from relative nullity, which protects a particular party, such as a minor or a victim of fraud or duress, and can be invoked only by that party. An absolutely null act generally cannot be confirmed or cured; a relatively null act may be ratified once the defect ceases.

Nullity of marriage

The best-known application is the annulment of marriage: a decree of nullity declares that a marriage was void from the start (for example on grounds of bigamy, prohibited degrees of kinship, or lack of consent) or voidable. In the conflict of laws, this creates the problem of the "limping marriage" — annulled in one country but subsisting in another. In Roman Catholic canon law, marriage nullity is adjudicated by ecclesiastical tribunals on grounds such as defect of consent.

Other applications

Nullity also appears in constitutional and administrative law, where a statute or official act passed without competence is void ab initio; in company law, where a defectively formed company may be declared null; and in litigation, where procedural nullity can invalidate proceedings. Questions of nullity are also central to conflicts between domestic courts and arbitral tribunals, and between national law and European Union law, whose doctrines of supremacy can render national provisions inapplicable.
