Terms of trade

The terms of trade (TOT) represent the ratio between the prices of a country's exports and the prices of its imports, and thus the quantity of imports a country can purchase for a given quantity of exports. They are usually expressed as an index:

Terms of trade = (Export price index / Import price index) × 100

An increase in the index — an improvement in the terms of trade — means export prices have risen relative to import prices, so the country can buy more imports with the same volume of exports, raising its real income. A decrease — a deterioration — has the opposite effect. The concept was developed in the work of classical and early neoclassical economists including Robert Torrens, John Stuart Mill, and Alfred Marshall.

Types

Economists distinguish several variants. The net barter terms of trade are the simple price ratio above. The income terms of trade multiply the price ratio by the volume of exports, measuring the total import-purchasing capacity of export earnings. The single factoral terms of trade adjust for productivity changes in the export sector, and the double factoral terms of trade adjust for productivity in both the home export sector and the foreign sector producing imports.

Influences and significance

Terms of trade are driven by exchange-rate movements, changes in world commodity prices, shifts in global supply and demand, and changes in productivity and trade policy. For commodity-exporting countries, swings in the terms of trade can dominate macroeconomic fortunes: a resource boom improves export prices and national income, while a price collapse forces adjustment. Large countries may be able to improve their terms of trade with an "optimal tariff", at the expense of trading partners.

A famous debate concerns the long-run trend for primary-commodity exporters. The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis, advanced around 1950 by Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer, held that the terms of trade of commodity exporters deteriorate secularly relative to manufactured goods, a claim used to justify import-substitution industrialisation in developing countries and still debated in the empirical literature.
