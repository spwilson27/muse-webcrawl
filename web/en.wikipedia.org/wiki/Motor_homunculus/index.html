Motor homunculus

The motor homunculus is a pictorial representation of the anatomical divisions of the primary motor cortex and the relative amount of cortical area devoted to controlling each part of the body. It was developed from the work of neurosurgeon Wilder Penfield and his colleagues in the 1930s–1950s, who mapped the cortex by electrically stimulating the brains of conscious patients during surgery.

Drawn as a distorted human figure draped over the precentral gyrus, the homunculus has grossly enlarged hands, lips and tongue and a comparatively small trunk and legs, reflecting the fine motor control and large cortical representation of the face and hands. The body parts are arranged somatotopically: the leg and foot are represented medially (in the paracentral lobule), while the face and hand lie laterally along the convexity of the hemisphere.

Alongside its sensory counterpart, the sensory (somatosensory) homunculus of the postcentral gyrus, the motor homunculus is a classic teaching image in neuroscience. Modern imaging studies have refined Penfield's map, showing more overlapping and distributed representations, but the basic somatotopic organisation remains valid.
