The Battle of Karnal (24 February 1739), fought between the invading Persian army of Nader Shah and the forces of the Mughal Empire under Emperor Muhammad Shah, was one of the most one-sided great battles of the 18th century. In about three hours, Nader Shah's smaller, modern army destroyed the enormous Mughal host, opening the road to Delhi and exposing the hollowness of Mughal power.

Nader Shah's mobile forces at Karnal, 1739.

The battle

Nader Shah drew the Mughal vanguard under Sa'adat Khan out of its entrenched camp with a feigned retreat, then annihilated it with disciplined musketry, camel-mounted swivel guns (zamburaks) and cavalry. When the Mughal main body advanced piecemeal to rescue it, it too was shattered. Mughal losses are estimated at 20,000–30,000 killed, against only a few thousand Persian casualties. Muhammad Shah, who had watched from the rear with the bulk of the army barely engaged, surrendered days later.

Aftermath

Nader entered Delhi as the emperor's "guest" in March 1739. After a rumour of Nader's death sparked riots that killed Persian soldiers, he ordered a general massacre (the qatl-e-aam) in which perhaps 20,000–30,000 Delhi citizens died in a single day. Persia carried off a treasure of legendary size — including the Peacock Throne, the Koh-i-Noor and Darya-i-Noor diamonds — worth hundreds of millions of rupees, enabling Nader to remit Persian taxes for three years. The catastrophe accelerated the Mughal Empire's terminal decline and the rise of successor states and, ultimately, the British East India Company.
