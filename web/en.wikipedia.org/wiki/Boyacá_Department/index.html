Boyacá (Spanish pronunciation: [boʝaˈka]) is a department of Colombia, located in the central part of the country on the Eastern Cordillera of the Andes. Its capital is Tunja. Boyacá is one of the original nine states of the former United States of Colombia and is celebrated as the cradle of Colombian independence: the decisive Battle of Boyacá was fought here in 1819. The department is known for its cold highland climate, colonial towns, emerald mines, and strong agricultural tradition.

History

Before the Spanish conquest, the region was inhabited by the Muisca people, whose confederation controlled rich salt and emerald deposits; the zaque of Hunza (modern Tunja) was one of their principal rulers. Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada entered the territory in 1537. Tunja was founded in 1539 and became an important colonial city, notable for its churches and colonial art.

Boyacá occupies a central place in Colombia's national story. After the patriot victory at the Pantano de Vargas in July 1819, Simón Bolívar's army defeated the Spanish royalists at the Battle of Boyacá on 7 August 1819, securing the independence of New Granada. The small bridge where the battle was decided, the Puente de Boyacá, is a national monument, and 7 August is a public holiday in Colombia.

Geography

The department covers about 23,189 square kilometres of the Eastern Cordillera, ranging from the hot Magdalena River valley in the west to the high páramos and the snow-capped Sierra Nevada del Cocuy in the north-east, whose peaks exceed 5,300 metres. The Altiplano Boyacense, a high plateau shared with Cundinamarca, holds most of the population and the main cities: Tunja, Duitama, Sogamoso, and Chiquinquirá, a major Catholic pilgrimage centre. Lake Tota, Colombia's largest natural lake, lies in the east of the department. Boyacá borders Venezuela (Apure) to the north-east and the departments of Arauca, Casanare, Cundinamarca, Santander, Antioquia, and Caldas.

Economy

Agriculture dominates the economy: potatoes, maize, wheat, onions, and dairy farming on the high plateau, with sugarcane, coffee, and fruit in warmer valleys. The western province around Muzo and Chivor produces some of the world's most prized emeralds, for which Colombia is famous. Steel production at Sogamoso and Tunja, cement, and beverages add an industrial base, and tourism is growing around colonial towns such as Villa de Leyva and Monguí, the battlefield monuments, and the Cocuy national park.

Culture

Boyacá is noted for its rich folk traditions, including the ruana (a woollen poncho worn against the highland cold), carranga music, and festivals such as Tunja's Aguinaldo Boyacense. The department's cuisine includes cocido boyacense, mazamorra, and the cheeses of Paipa. Cycling is a passion in the region, which has produced celebrated riders who trained on its mountain roads.
