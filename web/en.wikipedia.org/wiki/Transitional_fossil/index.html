Transitional fossil

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transitional fossil is any fossilised remains of a life form that exhibits traits common to both an ancestral group and its derived descendant group. This is especially important where the descendant group is sharply differentiated by gross anatomy and mode of living from the ancestral group. Because the fossil record is incomplete, transitional forms typically appear as mosaics of features rather than as literal "halfway" organisms.

Examples

Archaeopteryx, discovered in the Solnhofen limestone of Germany in 1861, two years after Darwin published On the Origin of Species, combines feathers and wings with teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail, documenting the transition from non-avian dinosaurs to birds. Tiktaalik roseae, a Devonian lobe-finned fish described in 2006, has fish-like fins and scales together with a mobile neck and wrist-like fin bones, bridging fish and tetrapods. The Eocene whale Ambulocetus ("walking whale") and the series of hominin fossils exemplified by Australopithecus document the land-to-sea transition of cetaceans and the evolution of bipedalism in humans respectively.

"Missing link"

The popular term "missing link" reflects an outdated, linear view of evolution (the "great chain of being"). Modern evolutionary theory describes branching trees: transitional fossils need not be direct ancestors of living groups, but relatives that reveal the sequence in which characteristic features evolved. Gaps are expected because fossilisation is rare and sedimentary deposits are discontinuous.
