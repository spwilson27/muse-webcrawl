Molybdenite

Formula: MoS2
Crystal system: Hexagonal
Color: Lead-grey, silvery
Mohs hardness: 1–1.5
Luster: Metallic
Streak: Bluish grey

Molybdenite is a mineral of molybdenum disulfide, MoS2. Similar in appearance and feel to graphite, molybdenite has a lubricating effect arising from its layered structure: sheets of molybdenum atoms sandwiched between sulfur atoms are held together only by weak van der Waals forces, so the layers slide over one another easily. It is the principal ore of molybdenum, an important alloying element in steel. The name derives from the Greek molybdos, meaning lead, reflecting the historic confusion of the mineral with lead ores such as galena and with graphite.

Occurrence

Molybdenite occurs in high-temperature hydrothermal ore deposits, especially in porphyry molybdenum and porphyry copper deposits, as well as in quartz veins, pegmatites, and contact metamorphic deposits. Important producing districts include the Climax and Henderson mines in Colorado, Questa in New Mexico, and major porphyry copper mines in Chile and the United States where molybdenite is recovered as a by-product. Well-formed hexagonal crystals are uncommon; the mineral usually appears as soft, flexible flakes and foliated masses that mark paper with a bluish-grey streak.

Structure and polytypes

The hexagonal polytype (2H) is the common form; a rhombohedral polytype (3R) also occurs. Monolayers of molybdenite are semiconductors with a direct band gap, unlike the bulk indirect-gap material, and have attracted research interest in two-dimensional electronics and optoelectronics alongside graphene and other transition metal dichalcogenides.

Uses

Beyond its role as the main source of molybdenum metal, molybdenite itself is used as a dry lubricant (molybdenum disulfide) in greases, coatings, and high-temperature and vacuum applications where oils would fail. Powdered MoS2 is also used in catalysts for hydrodesulfurization of fuels.
