Bouillon (broth)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A clear beef bouillon (image placeholder)

Bouillon (French: [bujɔ̃], from bouillir, "to boil") is a seasoned broth made by simmering meat, bones, seafood or vegetables in water. In French usage it is a clear, strained liquid; in English the word is often used interchangeably with broth and for the dehydrated stock cube dissolved in hot water.

Preparation

A classic French bouillon begins with meat and bones covered in cold water, brought slowly to a simmer and skimmed, then flavoured with a bouquet garni, onion (often studded with cloves or charred for colour), carrot, celery and leek. Long, gentle simmering extracts flavour and gelatin; the liquid is strained for use as a soup base or poaching medium. Clarified with egg whites and lean meat it becomes consommé.

Bouillon cubes and granules

Dehydrated bouillon — as cubes, powder or paste — was commercialised in the late 19th and early 20th centuries by companies such as Maggi, OXO and Knorr. Made from salt, hydrolysed vegetable protein, fat, yeast extract and flavourings, cubes became a staple convenience seasoning worldwide, foundational to countless home and institutional dishes, though often criticised for high sodium content.

Related preparations

Bouillon is distinguished from stock, which is typically unseasoned and used as an ingredient rather than served, and from broth, which in English may contain pieces of the simmered ingredients and can be eaten as a light soup in itself. The Haitian soup bouillon is a hearty dish of meat, tubers and vegetables in a seasoned broth.
