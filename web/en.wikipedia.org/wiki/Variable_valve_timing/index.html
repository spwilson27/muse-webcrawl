Variable valve timing

In internal combustion engines, variable valve timing (VVT) is the process of altering the timing of a valve lift event, and is often used to improve performance, fuel economy or emissions. It is increasingly used in combination with variable valve lift systems. The simplest form, cam phasing, rotates the camshaft slightly relative to the crankshaft to advance or retard the opening and closing of the intake and/or exhaust valves.

A cam phaser adjusts camshaft timing in a VVT system.

Principles

Fixed valve timing is a compromise: timing optimal for high engine speeds hurts low-speed torque, idle quality and emissions. Advancing intake closing at low speed raises effective compression and torque, while retarding at high speed improves volumetric efficiency. VVT systems vary timing continuously (or in steps) under electronic control, typically using oil-pressure-driven phasers on the camshafts, switched or sliding cam profiles, or — in more advanced systems — fully variable lift and duration. Notable implementations include Honda's VTEC (timing and lift), Toyota's VVT-i and Valvematic, BMW's VANOS and Valvetronic, Fiat's MultiAir electro-hydraulic system, and Nissan's VVEL.

History and adoption

Although experiments date to the steam age and early automobile patents, the first production automotive VVT system is generally credited to Alfa Romeo in 1980, followed by Honda's VTEC in 1989, which popularised the technology. By the 2010s some form of VVT had become standard on the great majority of new petrol car engines, and it is also applied to exhaust cams for internal exhaust gas recirculation, reducing pumping losses and NOx emissions.
