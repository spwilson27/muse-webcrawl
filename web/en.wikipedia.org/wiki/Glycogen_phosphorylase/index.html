Glycogen phosphorylase

Glycogen phosphorylase is an enzyme that catalyses the rate-limiting step in glycogenolysis: the phosphorolytic cleavage of glucose units from the non-reducing ends of glycogen, yielding glucose-1-phosphate. It was the first phosphorylase enzyme to be discovered and the first enzyme shown to be regulated by reversible phosphorylation.

Mechanism and regulation

The enzyme requires pyridoxal phosphate as a cofactor and stops four residues from a branch point, where the debranching enzyme takes over. Glycogen phosphorylase exists in liver and muscle forms; it is activated by phosphorylation via phosphorylase kinase (in turn activated by glucagon, adrenaline, and calcium) and by AMP, and inhibited by glucose-6-phosphate, ATP, and caffeine. Its discovery by Carl and Gerty Cori, and the phosphorylation cascade work of Krebs and Fischer, won Nobel Prizes.

Clinical significance

Defects in the liver form cause Hers disease (glycogen storage disease type VI); defects in the muscle form cause McArdle disease (type V), characterised by exercise intolerance and cramps.
