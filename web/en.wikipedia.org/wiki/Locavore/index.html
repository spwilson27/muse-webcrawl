Locavore

A locavore is a person whose diet consists only or principally of locally grown or produced food. The local food movement, of which locavorism is a part, is a social movement concerned with the sustainability and eco-consciousness of food sources, and locavores are often motivated by environmental concerns, support for local economies, and the perceived freshness and flavour of local produce.

Farmers markets are a principal source of food for locavores.

Origin of the term

The word "locavore" was coined in 2005 by Jessica Prentice of the San Francisco Bay Area, during World Environment Day, for a group promoting eating only food grown within a 100-mile radius. It combines the Latin locus (place) with vorare (to devour). In 2007, "locavore" was named the Oxford American Dictionary's Word of the Year.

Definitions and practice

The radius defining "local" varies among practitioners — 50, 100, 150 or 400 miles are all used; "within a day's drive" is another common criterion. Locavores typically obtain food through farmers markets, community-supported agriculture (CSA) schemes, farm shops, gardening, hunting and foraging. Many make exceptions for items that cannot be produced locally, such as coffee, tea, chocolate, salt and certain spices — the so-called "Marco Polo rules".

Criticism

Critics argue that "food miles" are a poor proxy for environmental impact, since transport typically accounts for a small share of food's total carbon footprint compared with production methods; that local production in heated greenhouses can be worse than importing; and that strict localism could harm farmers in developing countries who depend on export markets. Proponents respond that the movement is as much about community, taste, and food security as about carbon.
