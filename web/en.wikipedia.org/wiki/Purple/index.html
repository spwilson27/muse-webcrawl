Purple

Purple is a color similar in appearance to violet light. In the RGB color model used in computer and television displays, purple is produced by combining red and blue light, and it occupies the range between red and blue on the spectrum of visible light. Unlike violet, which is a spectral color with its own wavelength (approximately 380–435 nm), purple is a non-spectral color perceived when red and blue are stimulated together.

History and symbolism

In antiquity, purple dye — Tyrian purple, extracted laboriously from the glands of Murex sea snails around the eastern Mediterranean — was so costly that purple became the color of emperors, kings, and high clergy. Roman emperors wore purple togas, Byzantine emperors were described as "born in the purple," and Roman Catholic bishops traditionally wear purple vestments. The association with royalty, luxury, and power persists, as does its use for penitence in the liturgical seasons of Advent and Lent.

In 1856 the young chemist William Henry Perkin accidentally synthesized the first synthetic purple dye, mauveine, while attempting to make quinine; it launched the synthetic dye industry and a fashion craze for mauve. Purple has also been adopted by political and social movements, including the women's suffrage movement in Britain and the United States, where it stood for dignity.

Shades and usage

Shades include lavender, lilac, mauve, orchid, plum, magenta, and indigo. Purple is a secondary color in pigment (red + blue) and relatively rare in nature, appearing in lavender, violets, eggplants, and some minerals such as amethyst. In heraldry the tincture is called purpure. The Purple Heart is a United States military decoration awarded for wounds received in action.
