Calcitriol is the active form of vitamin D, a secosteroid hormone. Chemically it is 1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol, formed from calcifediol (25-hydroxyvitamin D) by a second hydroxylation step in the kidney, catalysed by the enzyme 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1).

Physiological role

Calcitriol circulates in the blood and binds the vitamin D receptor (VDR), a nuclear receptor that regulates gene expression. Its best-known role is in mineral homeostasis: it increases absorption of calcium and phosphate from the intestine, promotes reabsorption in the kidney, and, together with parathyroid hormone, regulates calcium release from bone. It also modulates immune function and cell differentiation.

Production and regulation

Production in the proximal tubules of the kidney is stimulated by parathyroid hormone and by low calcium or phosphate levels, and is inhibited by fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) and by calcitriol itself. Extra-renal production occurs in macrophages and other tissues, important in granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, where overproduction can cause hypercalcaemia.

Medical use

As a drug, calcitriol is used to treat hypocalcaemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism in chronic kidney disease, hypoparathyroidism, and vitamin D–resistant rickets. Topical analogues such as calcipotriol are used in psoriasis. Excess calcitriol causes hypercalcaemia, so calcium levels are monitored during therapy.
