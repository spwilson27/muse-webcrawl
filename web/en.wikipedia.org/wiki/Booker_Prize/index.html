Booker Prize

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Booker Prize trophy and a stack of shortlisted novels, the winner's cheque and the promise of transformed sales on the table]

The Booker Prize, known between 2002 and 2019 as the Man Booker Prize, is a literary award conferred each year for the best novel written in English and published in the United Kingdom or Ireland. Founded in 1969 (as the Booker-McConnell Prize), it is the most prestigious award in British and Commonwealth letters; winning transforms a novel's sales and its author's career (the 'Booker effect'), and the televised announcement is British publishing's biggest night.

Initially limited to Commonwealth, Irish and Zimbabwean citizens, the prize was opened in 2014 to any English-language novel published in the UK — a controversial expansion that admitted American writers and changed the shortlist's geography permanently. The prize money is £50,000.

Famous winners

Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children (1981) — twice voted 'Booker of Bookers' as the prize's finest winner — set the template of the postcolonial novel sweeping London. The roll runs through Kingsley Amis, Kazuo Ishiguro (The Remains of the Day), Arundhati Roy (The God of Small Things), Hilary Mantel (twice, for the Cromwell novels), Margaret Atwood and Bernardine Evaristo (the shared 2019 prize that broke the rules and the colour line together — Evaristo the first Black woman winner).

The machinery

Publishers submit titles under strict quotas; a panel of five judges reads over a hundred novels; the longlist ('Booker dozen', actually thirteen) and shortlist of six generate the autumn's literary conversation. A sister prize, the International Booker (since 2016 in its present form), honours translated fiction, splitting its £50,000 between author and translator.
