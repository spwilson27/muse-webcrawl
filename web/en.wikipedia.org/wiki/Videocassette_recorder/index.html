A videocassette recorder (VCR) is an electromechanical device that records analog audio and analog video from broadcast television or another source onto removable magnetic tape videocassettes and can play the recordings back. VCRs transformed home entertainment by letting viewers time-shift broadcasts and watch rented or purchased films at home.

A typical front-loading VHS VCR of the 1980s–90s.

History

Professional videotape recording began with Ampex machines in the 1950s, but home use awaited cassette formats. Sony introduced Betamax in 1975 and JVC launched the competing VHS format in 1976. The ensuing "format war" ended with VHS dominant by the late 1980s, owing to longer recording times and broader licensing to other manufacturers. By the 1990s the VCR was present in the great majority of television households in developed countries.

Technology

VCRs use helical scanning: a spinning drum with video heads writes diagonal tracks across the tape as it moves, allowing high effective writing speeds. Most home machines were front-loading, with timers for unattended recording, on-screen menus, and later features such as hi-fi stereo audio and automatic clock setting. The blinking "12:00" of unset VCR clocks became a cultural joke about user-unfriendly design.

Legal and cultural impact

In Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios (1984), the U.S. Supreme Court held that home time-shifting constituted fair use, securing the technology's legality. Video rental stores such as Blockbuster built an enormous industry around cassette rentals. The VCR faded in the 2000s as DVDs, DVRs, and streaming superseded it; Funai, the last manufacturer, ended production in July 2016.
