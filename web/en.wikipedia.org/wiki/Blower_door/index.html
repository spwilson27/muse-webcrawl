Blower door

[Hero image: a blower door fan sealed into an exterior doorway with its red nylon panel and pressure gauges]

A blower door is a machine used to test the airtightness of buildings. It consists of a powerful, calibrated fan temporarily sealed into an exterior doorway, which pressurises or depressurises the building while instruments measure the resulting air flow and pressure difference. The test quantifies the overall air leakage of the building envelope and helps locate individual leaks.

Equipment and procedure

The system has three main components: a variable-speed fan capable of moving several thousand cubic metres of air per hour; a door panel and frame that seal the fan into a doorway; and pressure gauges (manometers) measuring the indoor–outdoor pressure difference and the fan flow. After closing exterior openings, dampers and ash doors, the tester typically depressurises the building to −50 pascals relative to outside and records the flow needed to hold that pressure.

Metrics

Results are reported as air flow at 50 Pa (Q50), as air changes per hour at 50 Pa (ACH50 or n50 = Q50 / building volume), or as specific leakage area. Typical existing houses may measure 7–15 ACH50 or worse, code-built new homes around 3–5 ACH50, and Passivhaus buildings must achieve no more than 0.6 ACH50. Test standards include ISO 9972, ASTM E779 and EN 13829.

Finding leaks

While the building is depressurised, testers can find leaks by feel, with smoke pencils, or with infrared thermography; common leakage sites include attic hatches, plumbing and wiring penetrations, recessed lights, and rim joists. Combined with a duct tester ("duct blaster"), the method also measures leakage in forced-air ductwork.

History

Early airtightness tests were performed in Sweden in the 1970s; the door-mounted fan system was developed in the United States by Harold Orr and others around 1977–1980, and commercialised by companies including The Energy Conservatory (Minneapolis Blower Door) and Retrotec. Blower door testing is now required by many building codes, including the International Energy Conservation Code, for new residential construction.
