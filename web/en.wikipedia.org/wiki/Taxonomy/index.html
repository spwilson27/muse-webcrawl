Taxonomy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: a branching phylogenetic tree diagram showing the three domains of life radiating from a common root]
The tree of life: taxonomy aims to map every species onto the branching history of descent.

Taxonomy is the science of naming, defining and classifying groups of biological organisms based on shared characteristics — in modern practice, on evolutionary descent. The hierarchy of ranks runs domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus and species. Taxonomy supplies the shared naming system without which biologists could not communicate unambiguously about the millions of known forms of life.

The discipline was founded in its modern form by Carl Linnaeus in the 18th century (Systema Naturae, 1735) with binomial nomenclature. Since Darwin, classification has aimed to reflect phylogeny — the tree of descent — and since the late 20th century DNA sequence comparisons and cladistics have repeatedly redrawn the tree, most famously in Woese's three-domain system (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya).

Methods

Taxonomists combine morphology, anatomy, behaviour, ecology and molecular sequences. Cladistics groups organisms by shared derived characters into clades — branches of the tree containing an ancestor and all its descendants. Groups found not to be clades (such as the old 'reptiles' excluding birds) are revised or abandoned.

Institutions

Type specimens anchor names and are held in museums and herbaria worldwide. International codes (ICZN for animals, ICN for plants and fungi, and codes for bacteria and viruses) govern naming, priority and dispute resolution.
