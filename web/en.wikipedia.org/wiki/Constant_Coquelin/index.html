Constant Coquelin

Benoît-Constant Coquelin (23 January 1841 – 27 January 1909), known as Coquelin aîné ("Coquelin the elder"), was a French actor, one of the most celebrated performers of the nineteenth-century stage. He created the title role in Edmond Rostand's Cyrano de Bergerac in 1897, a performance that became the defining triumph of his career.

Career

Born in Boulogne-sur-Mer, Coquelin studied at the Paris Conservatoire and joined the Comédie-Française in 1860, becoming a sociétaire in 1864. He was famed for the precision and technical polish of his acting, expressed in his theoretical work L'Art et le comédien (1880), in which he argued that the actor should consciously construct a performance rather than rely on inspiration. After disputes with the Comédie-Française he resigned in 1886 and toured internationally, including in England and the United States, before taking over the Théâtre de la Porte Saint-Martin in Paris, where he premiered Cyrano de Bergerac.

Legacy

Coquelin's younger brother Ernest, known as Coquelin cadet, was also a well-known actor. Constant Coquelin is remembered as the archetype of the virtuoso French classical actor, and his Cyrano — with its famous prosthetic nose — set the standard for the role.
