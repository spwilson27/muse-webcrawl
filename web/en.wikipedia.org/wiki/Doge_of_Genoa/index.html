The Doge of Genoa was the chief magistrate and head of state of the Republic of Genoa. The office was modelled in part on that of the Doge of Venice, Genoa's great maritime rival, though the Genoese dogeship evolved differently over five centuries.

History

The first doge, Simone Boccanegra, was acclaimed in 1339 amid popular reaction against the nobility; he is remembered as the hero of Verdi's opera Simon Boccanegra. Early doges ruled for life and the office often alternated between popular and noble factions, with interruptions in which Genoa submitted to foreign rulers such as France and Milan. The constitutional reform of Andrea Doria in 1528 stabilised the republic as an aristocratic state: thereafter doges were elected by the Grand Council for two-year terms from among the great families organised in alberghi, and wore the insignia of a king of Corsica.

End of the office

The last doge, Giacomo Maria Brignole, abdicated in 1797 when Napoleon transformed the republic into the client Ligurian Republic; the office briefly survived in altered form until annexation to the French Empire in 1805.
