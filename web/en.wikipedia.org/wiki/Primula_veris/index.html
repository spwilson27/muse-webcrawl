Primula veris, the cowslip, cowslip primrose, or true cowslip, is a herbaceous perennial flowering plant in the primrose family Primulaceae. The species is native throughout most of temperate Europe and western Asia, growing in dry grassland, meadows, woodland edges, and dunes. It is one of the classic spring flowers of the European countryside, flowering from April to May.

Description

The plant forms a rosette of wrinkled, oblong leaves from a short rhizome. From the rosette rise leafless stalks (scapes) 5–20 cm tall, each bearing a one-sided umbel of 10–30 nodding, bell-shaped flowers. Each flower is deep yellow with orange spots at the base of the petals, sweetly scented, and enclosed in an inflated green calyx. Like its relative the primrose (Primula vulgaris), the cowslip is heterostylous: individual plants bear either "pin" flowers (long style) or "thrum" flowers (short style), an arrangement promoting cross-pollination that was studied by Charles Darwin. Seeds are dispersed when the upright capsules open in summer.

Ecology and conservation

The cowslip is an important early nectar source for bees and the larval food plant of the Duke of Burgundy butterfly. It declined sharply in Britain and parts of Europe during the twentieth century as permanent pastures were ploughed or fertilized, but has recovered in places thanks to conservation grazing, road-verge management, and its use in wildflower seed mixes. It hybridizes with the primrose to produce the false oxlip (Primula × polyantha).

Uses and folklore

Cowslip flowers were traditionally made into cowslip wine and used in herbal medicine as a mild sedative and expectorant. The common name is thought to derive from Old English cūsloppe, "cow slop" (dung), reflecting its association with pastures. Cowslips feature in Shakespeare — the "cowslips tall" of Ariel's song in The Tempest — and in county-flower lists, being the county flower of Essex, Northamptonshire, Surrey, and Worcestershire.
