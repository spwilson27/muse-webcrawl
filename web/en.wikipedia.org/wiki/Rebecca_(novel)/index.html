Rebecca (novel)

Rebecca is a 1938 Gothic novel by the English author Daphne du Maurier. The novel depicts an unnamed young woman who impetuously marries a wealthy widower, only to discover that he and his household are still haunted by the memory of his late first wife, the title character.

A bestseller that has never gone out of print, Rebecca sold 380,000 copies between 1938 and 1965 in the United Kingdom alone. The novel is remembered for the character of Mrs Danvers, the fictional estate Manderley, and its famous opening line: "Last night I dreamt I went to Manderley again." In 1938 it won the National Book Award in the United States.

Plot summary

While working as the companion to a rich American woman holidaying in Monte Carlo, the unnamed narrator — a naïve young woman in her early twenties — becomes acquainted with the wealthy Englishman Maxim de Winter, a widower aged 42. After a brief whirlwind courtship, she agrees to marry him and accompany him to his mansion, the beautiful estate Manderley in Cornwall.

At Manderley, the second Mrs de Winter finds life difficult. She is intimidated by the housekeeper, Mrs Danvers, who remains devoted to Maxim's first wife, Rebecca, who drowned in a sailing accident about a year before Maxim and the narrator met. Mrs Danvers continually attempts to undermine the new marriage, even encouraging the narrator to throw herself from a window. Matters come to a head at the Manderley costume ball, where — at Mrs Danvers' suggestion — the narrator wears a dress that Rebecca had worn, provoking Maxim's fury.

The next day, Maxim confesses that he never loved Rebecca: she was cruel, selfish and unfaithful, and on the night of her death he shot her in a rage, then sank her body aboard her boat. The boat is recovered with the body inside, and an inquest initially returns a verdict of suicide; but Rebecca's cousin and lover, Jack Favell, produces evidence leading to suspicion of murder. A London doctor then reveals that Rebecca had visited him on the day she died: she was terminally ill with cancer and unable to bear children, and had taunted Maxim that she was pregnant by another man. The death is again ruled suicide, and Maxim and his wife are free — but as they drive home, they see Manderley ablaze in the distance, set on fire by Mrs Danvers.

Background and themes

Du Maurier's novel has been read as a Gothic romance in the tradition of Jane Eyre, with Manderley standing in for Thornfield Hall and Rebecca as a darker counterpart to Bertha Mason. Themes include the power of the past and of memory, jealousy, identity (the heroine's namelessness is often noted), and contested masculinity and femininity. The author's own feelings about Menabilly, the Cornish estate she later leased, fed the depiction of Manderley.

Adaptations

The novel was adapted by Orson Welles for radio in 1938 and most famously filmed by Alfred Hitchcock as Rebecca (1940), winner of the Academy Award for Best Picture — the only Hitchcock film so honoured. A Broadway stage adaptation opened in 1945; the German-language musical Rebecca premiered in Vienna in 2006. A Netflix film adaptation directed by Ben Wheatley and starring Lily James and Armie Hammer was released in 2020. The story continues the unnamed narrator's tale in Sally Beauman's authorised sequel Rebecca's Tale (2001).

Legacy

In 2003 the novel was listed at number 14 on the BBC's The Big Read poll of the nation's best-loved novels. "Mrs Danvers" has entered critical vocabulary as an archetype of the sinister housekeeper, and the novel's first line is among the most quoted openings in English fiction.
