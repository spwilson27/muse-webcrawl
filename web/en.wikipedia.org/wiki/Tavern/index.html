Tavern

A tavern is a business establishment where alcoholic drinks are sold to be consumed on the premises, often together with food, and historically also providing lodging. The word derives from the Latin taberna, a shop or hut. Taverns were central social institutions in Europe and colonial America from the Middle Ages through the nineteenth century, serving as places of news, business, politics and entertainment as well as drinking.

History

Roman tabernae and popinae sold wine and hot food in every town of the empire; Pompeii preserves dozens. Medieval taverns sold wine (ale being the province of the alehouse in England) and were regulated by licensing and assizes of price and measure. In early modern London, taverns and coffeehouses were hubs of commerce and political discussion; Lloyd's insurance market began in a coffee house. In colonial British America the tavern was often the largest building in a town after the meeting house: courts, assemblies, militia musters and elections were held there, and revolutionary politics was organised in establishments such as Boston's Green Dragon Tavern and Williamsburg's Raleigh Tavern. Fraunces Tavern in New York hosted Washington's farewell to his officers in 1783.

Tavern versus inn and pub

Usage varies. In England the terms tavern, inn and alehouse were once legally distinct — inns lodged travellers, taverns sold wine, alehouses beer — before "public house" (pub) became the general term. In the United States "tavern" is used in liquor licensing law in several states as a class of on-premises licence, and survives in countless bar and restaurant names. In Greece a taverna is a small restaurant serving traditional food and wine, a different institution from the drinking tavern.

Culture

Taverns occupy a stock place in literature and folklore, from Chaucer's Tabard Inn in The Canterbury Tales to the Prancing Pony in Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings, as the threshold where journeys begin and strangers meet.
