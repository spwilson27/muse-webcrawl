Andean civilizations

The Andean civilizations were the complex societies that arose in the Andes mountains and the Pacific coastal desert of western South America — in present-day Peru, Bolivia, Ecuador, and parts of Chile, Argentina, and Colombia. Over some four millennia they produced monumental architecture, fine textiles and ceramics, sophisticated agriculture on terraced and irrigated land, and, eventually, the Inca Empire, the largest empire in the pre-Columbian Americas.

Origins: Norte Chico and the preceramic

Around 3500–1800 BCE the Norte Chico (Caral–Supe) civilisation of the north-central Peruvian coast built some of the earliest cities in the Americas, with platform mounds and sunken circular plazas at sites such as Caral, sustained by maritime resources and irrigated cotton and food crops — remarkably, before ceramics or maize became widespread. Late preceramic centres on the coast, such as Huaca Prieta, show early monumental building and complex textiles.

Chavín and the Early Horizon

The highland temple centre of Chavín de Huántar (c. 900–200 BCE) spread a unifying religious art style — jaguars, caymans, and raptorial birds carved in stone and rendered in textiles and pottery — across much of Peru, giving archaeologists their first "horizon" style. Contemporary coastal cultures such as Paracas developed celebrated textile traditions.

Early Intermediate Period

After Chavín's decline, regional states flourished: the Moche (Mochica) of the north coast, famed for portrait-head vessels, monumental adobe huacas, and the rich royal tombs of Sipán; the Nazca of the south coast, makers of the Nazca Lines and polychrome pottery; and highland cultures such as Recuay and Cajamarca.

Wari and Tiwanaku

In the Middle Horizon (c. 600–1000 CE) two highland empires expanded widely: Wari (Huari), which built administrative centres and road networks across Peru, and Tiwanaku (Tiahuanaco) on the shores of Lake Titicaca, known for monumental stone architecture such as the Akapana and the Gateway of the Sun and for raised-bed (chinampa-like) agriculture on the altiplano.

Late Intermediate Period and the Inca

Following their collapse, regional kingdoms such as the coastal Chimú empire, centred on the great adobe city of Chan Chan, dominated. From the 15th century the Inca of Cusco conquered and integrated nearly the whole Andean world into Tawantinsuyu, an empire of perhaps ten million people administered through roads, storehouses, quipu knotted-string records, and labour taxation — without writing, wheeled transport, or iron. The Spanish invasion under Francisco Pizarro, beginning in 1532, and the capture of the emperor Atahualpa at Cajamarca brought the independent Andean civilizations to an end, though Andean peoples, languages (notably Quechua and Aymara), and traditions endure.

Shared characteristics

Recurring features include the domestication of the potato, quinoa, llama, and alpaca; vertical exploitation of stacked ecological zones ("vertical archipelagos"); reciprocity and redistribution as organising principles; ancestor veneration and mummification; and fine metallurgy in gold, silver, and bronze.
