A bishop is an ordained member of the clergy who is entrusted with a position of authority and oversight in a religious institution. In many Christian traditions, bishops are considered successors to the apostles of Jesus and constitute the highest of the three orders of ordained ministry — bishop, priest (presbyter), and deacon. The word derives from the Greek epískopos (ἐπίσκοπος), meaning "overseer" or "guardian", used in the New Testament for church leaders.

Role and ministry

A bishop typically governs a diocese — a defined territory comprising many parishes — from an episcopal see centred on a cathedral. Episcopal duties include ordaining priests and deacons, confirming the faithful, consecrating churches and chrism, teaching and guarding the faith, and providing pastoral oversight of clergy and laity. In the Roman Catholic Church, bishops are appointed by the pope and teach in communion with him; the pope himself is the bishop of Rome. In Eastern Orthodoxy, bishops are usually chosen from among celibate monastic clergy and govern in conciliar fashion under patriarchs and synods. Catholic and Orthodox theology holds that bishops stand in an unbroken line of apostolic succession through the laying on of hands.

Other traditions

Anglican, Lutheran (especially Scandinavian), and some Methodist churches retain bishops, though their authority and theology of orders vary; United Methodist bishops, for example, are elected and serve as superintendents without lifetime tenure in one see. Many other Protestant churches reject the episcopal office or use the title functionally. Historically bishops have also wielded political power — prince-bishops of the Holy Roman Empire ruled territories — and remain culturally prominent figures. Traditional episcopal insignia include the mitre, crosier (pastoral staff), pectoral cross, and episcopal ring.
