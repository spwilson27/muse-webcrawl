Trichome

Trichomes are small hair-like or scale-like outgrowths of the epidermis of plants, algae, and some other organisms. The word derives from the Greek tríchōma, "growth of hair". Trichomes vary enormously in size, shape, density, and function: they may be unicellular or multicellular, branched or unbranched, glandular or non-glandular.

Functions in plants

Non-glandular trichomes protect plants by deterring herbivores, reflecting ultraviolet radiation, reducing water loss, and insulating leaves against frost and heat; the dense silvery hairs of many alpine and desert plants are examples. Glandular trichomes secrete substances including essential oils (as in mint and lavender), sticky resins that trap insects, and the stinging fluid of nettles, whose hairs act as tiny hypodermic needles. In carnivorous plants, trichomes may help capture or digest prey, while root hairs — also trichomes — absorb water and minerals. In cannabis, glandular trichomes produce the cannabinoids and terpenes for which the plant is cultivated, and their cloudy appearance is used to judge harvest timing.

Other uses of the term

The term also appears in mycology and zoology for hair-like structures on fungi and animals, and in medicine "trichome" is rarely used in preference to more specific terms. Trichome morphology is a standard character in plant taxonomy and identification keys.
