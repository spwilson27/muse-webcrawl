Negative pressure ventilation

Negative pressure ventilation is a form of mechanical ventilation in which sub-atmospheric (negative) pressure is applied around the chest or the whole body, causing air to be drawn into the lungs through the upper airway. It contrasts with positive pressure ventilation, in which air is pushed into the lungs through a mask or endotracheal tube.

History

The best-known negative pressure ventilator is the iron lung, developed in the late 1920s by Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw at Harvard. Iron lungs became iconic during the polio epidemics of the mid-20th century, sustaining patients whose respiratory muscles were paralysed by poliomyelitis. Large wards of iron lungs were a defining image of the polio era.

Devices

Modern negative pressure devices include cuirass ventilators (a rigid shell fitted over the chest and abdomen) and wrap-style systems. Because the patient's airway remains unobstructed, negative pressure ventilation can be used in some patients with chronic neuromuscular disease or chest-wall deformity who wish to avoid tracheostomy.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages include preservation of speech, swallowing and airway defence mechanisms, and avoidance of ventilator-associated pneumonia associated with invasive tubes. Limitations include poor access to the patient for nursing care, possible upper-airway obstruction during sleep, and unsuitability for many forms of acute respiratory failure, where positive pressure ventilation is now standard.
