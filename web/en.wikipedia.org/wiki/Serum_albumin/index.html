Human serum albumin is the most abundant protein in blood plasma.

Serum albumin, often simply called albumin, is the most abundant protein in human blood plasma, constituting about 50% of total plasma protein. It is produced in the liver, encoded by the ALB gene, and secreted as a single polypeptide chain of 585 amino acids with a heart-shaped, predominantly α-helical structure stabilised by 17 disulfide bonds.

Functions

Albumin's main roles are:

  • Oncotic pressure: it maintains the colloid osmotic pressure of blood, keeping fluid within the circulation and preventing oedema.
  • Transport: it binds and carries a wide range of substances, including fatty acids, bilirubin, hormones (such as thyroxine), metal ions (calcium, copper, zinc), and many drugs (e.g. warfarin, sulfonamides), influencing their pharmacokinetics.
  • Buffering and antioxidant activity: albumin contributes to plasma pH buffering and scavenges free radicals, notably through its free thiol group at Cys34.
  • Nutrition: it serves as a reserve of amino acids that tissues can draw upon.

Clinical significance

Low serum albumin (hypoalbuminemia) occurs in liver disease (reduced synthesis), nephrotic syndrome (urinary loss), malnutrition, chronic inflammation, and protein-losing enteropathy, and is a strong general marker of illness severity and mortality risk. High albumin (hyperalbuminemia) is usually due to dehydration. Albumin measured in urine (microalbuminuria/albuminuria) is an early marker of kidney damage, especially in diabetes. Purified human albumin is administered therapeutically as a plasma volume expander in conditions such as cirrhosis with spontaneous bacterial peritonitis and large-volume paracentesis. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is a standard reagent in laboratories, used as a protein concentration standard and blocking agent.
