Crowd collapses and crowd crushes are catastrophic incidents that can occur when body-to-body contact and density in a crowd reach a critical point. In a crowd collapse, someone falls, creating a hole in the crowd into which surrounding people are pushed by the pressure of those behind, causing a pile-up. In a crowd crush, people are compressed so tightly that their chests cannot expand to breathe, causing compressive asphyxia — often while victims remain standing.

Contrary to the popular term "stampede," victims in most such disasters are not trampled by a panicking mob; investigations consistently find that the crowd behaves largely rationally within a physical system that has become lethal. The forces involved can exceed 4,500 newtons — enough to bend steel barriers — generated purely by pushing and pressure transmission through densely packed bodies.

Mechanics

Crowd researchers such as John Fruin and Dirk Helbing describe a progression with density. Below about 4 people per square metre, individuals can move reasonably freely. At 6–7 per square metre, bodies are in constant contact and shock waves begin to propagate through the crowd; people lose voluntary control of movement. When someone falls in these conditions, a void opens and others topple onto them — a "progressive crowd collapse" — while nearby victims may suffocate standing up because pressure on the rib cage prevents inhalation. Most deaths in crushes are from compressive asphyxia, not trampling.

Terminology

Safety experts avoid "stampede" and "panic," which misleadingly blame victims and suggest animal irrationality. Modern reporting guidelines following the 1989 Hillsborough disaster and later events increasingly use "crush," "surge," or "collapse." Journalists' use of "panic" has been criticized by inquiry reports because it distorts causation and hinders learning.

Causes and risk factors

Recurring factors include overcrowding beyond a venue's design capacity; bottlenecks, dead ends, and contraflows (two streams of people meeting); hard barriers that stop movement; a focal attraction (a celebrity, entrance, or view) that concentrates density; poor visibility and communication; and management failure to measure density and halt inflow. Religious gatherings, festivals, sales events, transport termini, and nightlife districts all feature in the incident record.

Major incidents

  • Bethnal Green, London (1943) — 173 people died on the stairs of a Tube station used as an air-raid shelter.
  • Ibrox Stadium, Glasgow (1971) — 66 deaths in a stairway crush after a football match.
  • Hillsborough, Sheffield (1989) — 97 Liverpool supporters died in fenced pens at an FA Cup semi-final; decades of campaigning overturned the initial narrative, and a 2016 inquest found the victims unlawfully killed.
  • Hajj incidents — repeated mass-fatality crushes during the pilgrimage, including 1,426 deaths in a tunnel in 1990 and the 2015 Mina disaster with deaths estimated from 769 to over 2,400.
  • Love Parade, Duisburg (2010) — 21 deaths at a ramp bottleneck into the festival site.
  • Astroworld Festival, Houston (2021) — 10 deaths from compressive asphyxiation during the headline set.
  • Itaewon, Seoul (2022) — 159 deaths in a narrow sloped alley during Halloween festivities.
  • Phnom Penh (2010) — at least 347 deaths on a bridge during the Water Festival.

Prevention

Modern crowd management emphasizes real-time density monitoring (via CCTV analytics and trained spotters), separating inflow and outflow, removing bottlenecks and dead ends, presetting emergency lanes, staged entry and metering, and clear stop authority empowered to pause shows or close entries. The UK's "Green Guide" for sports grounds and similar codes codify capacity calculation based on entry, holding, and exit rates rather than area alone.
