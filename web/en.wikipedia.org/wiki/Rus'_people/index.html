The Rus' people (Old East Slavic: Роусь, romanised Rus'; Old Norse: Garðar) were an ethnos in early medieval Eastern Europe. The scholarly consensus holds that they originally consisted of Norse peoples — Varangians — originating from what is now Sweden, who settled along the river routes between the Baltic Sea and the Black and Caspian Seas from the late 8th century. They founded the polity that became known as Kievan Rus', and gave their name to the lands and, eventually, to the East Slavic peoples who populated them — the ancestors of modern Russians, Ukrainians, and Belarusians.

Origins

Arab, Byzantine, and Frankish sources from the 9th and 10th centuries describe the Rus' as traders and raiders distinct from the Slavs among whom they settled. The Annals of St. Bertin record a Rus' embassy to the Frankish court in 839 whose members were identified as Swedes. The Russian Primary Chronicle, compiled in the early 12th century, recounts the "calling of the Varangians" in 862, when the Slavic and Finnic tribes invited the Rus' prince Rurik to rule over them; Rurik's successors, notably Oleg of Novgorod, seized Kiev and united the northern and southern Rus' lands.

The name Rus' is generally derived from the Finnic name for Sweden (Ruotsi), ultimately from an Old Norse word related to "rowing" (roðr). As the Norse elite intermarried with and adopted the language and customs of their Slavic subjects, the term expanded: by the 11th–12th centuries "Rus'" designated the whole population and territory of the Kievan state.

Trade and warfare

The Rus' controlled the great river trade routes — above all the route "from the Varangians to the Greeks" down the Dnieper to Constantinople — trading furs, wax, honey, and slaves for Byzantine silk, wine, and silver, including dirhams from the Islamic world via the Volga route. Rus' fleets raided Constantinople in 860, 907, and 941, and Rus' merchants and mercenaries served the Byzantine emperors; the Varangian Guard, famed as an imperial bodyguard, drew heavily on Rus' and Scandinavian recruits.

Christianisation and assimilation

Contacts with Byzantium brought gradual Christianisation, culminating in the baptism of Vladimir I of Kiev in 988 and the mass baptism of Kiev's inhabitants. Church Slavonic literacy, Byzantine law, and dynastic marriages with European courts integrated the Rus' into medieval Christendom, while the Norse element's distinct identity faded; by the mid-11th century the Rus' were an East Slavic, Orthodox people.

Historiography

The degree of Norse involvement in founding the Rus' state has been debated for centuries under the labels "Normanist" and "anti-Normanist." The mainstream scholarly view accepts a Scandinavian origin for the Rus' elite while emphasising rapid Slavicisation and the Slavic character of the resulting civilisation.
