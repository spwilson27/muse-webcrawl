Genetic programming

In artificial intelligence, genetic programming (GP) is a technique of evolving programs, starting from a population of unfit (usually random) programs, fit for a particular task by applying operations analogous to natural genetic processes — selection, crossover and mutation. It is essentially a heuristic search technique, often described as a type of genetic algorithm where the individuals being evolved are computer programs, commonly represented as tree structures.

Method

A population of candidate programs is generated at random from a set of primitive functions and terminals. Each program is executed and assigned a fitness score measuring how well it solves the target problem. Fitter individuals are selected to produce offspring: crossover swaps subtrees between two parent programs, while mutation randomly alters a subtree. The process repeats for many generations until a satisfactory program emerges or a generation limit is reached.

History

The idea of evolving programs by natural selection was suggested by Alan Turing in 1950 and explored computationally by several researchers in the following decades. The modern field was established by John R. Koza, whose 1992 book Genetic Programming popularised tree-based GP in LISP. Later variants include linear GP, Cartesian GP, gene expression programming and stack-based systems such as PushGP.

Applications

GP has been applied to symbolic regression, classification, control, electronic circuit design — including the reinvention of patented circuits — antenna design, and automated program improvement. Koza termed evolved solutions that match or exceed human designs "human-competitive". A persistent challenge in GP is bloat: the tendency of programs to grow in size without corresponding improvements in fitness, countered by parsimony pressure and size limits.
