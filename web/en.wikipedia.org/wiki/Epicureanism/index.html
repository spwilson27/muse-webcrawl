Epicureanism is a system of philosophy founded around 307 BCE by Epicurus of Samos. Taught in his Athenian school, the Garden — which unusually admitted women and enslaved people as full members — it held that pleasure, understood as the absence of pain in the body (aponia) and trouble in the mind (Ataraxia), is the beginning and end of the blessed life.

Teachings

Epicurus taught that death is nothing to us, since when we exist death is not, and when death exists we are not; that the gods exist but do not intervene in human affairs, removing fear of divine punishment; and that desires should be classified as natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, or vain, with only the first class pursued without limit. Justice is a mutual agreement neither to harm nor be harmed. The physics underlying the system is the atomism of Democritus, modified by the doctrine of the atomic "swerve" (clinamen) to preserve free will.

Texts and influence

Most of Epicurus's many writings are lost; his doctrines survive in three letters and collections of sayings preserved by Diogenes Laertius, in the poem De rerum natura by the Roman Epicurean Lucretius, and in papyri from the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum. Long caricatured as crude sensualism — "eat, drink and be merry" — Epicureanism was revived in the 17th century by Pierre Gassendi and influenced early modern science and Enlightenment thought. The modern everyday word "epicurean", suggesting luxury dining, misrepresents the school's famously simple diet of bread, water and a little cheese.
