William Ford Gibson (born 17 March 1948) is an American-Canadian speculative fiction writer and essayist widely credited with pioneering the science fiction subgenre known as cyberpunk. He coined the term "cyberspace" in his 1982 short story "Burning Chrome," and his debut novel Neuromancer (1984) popularized a vision of a global computer network and virtual reality years before the World Wide Web — earning him the description, by critic Fredric Jameson, of the "Noir prophet" of science fiction.

Life

Gibson was born in South Carolina and grew up in Virginia, where his father, a construction contractor, died when Gibson was eight. A shy, bookish teenager, he left boarding school in Arizona for Canada in 1967 — in his own account, partly to avoid the Vietnam draft — and has lived in Canada ever since, settling in Vancouver in 1977. He took a degree in English literature from the University of British Columbia in 1977 while writing his first stories, among them "Fragments of a Hologram Rose" and "Johnny Mnemonic" (1981), the latter filmed in 1995 with a Gibson screenplay.

Career

Neuromancer won the Hugo, Nebula, and Philip K. Dick awards — science fiction's "triple crown" — upon publication in 1984. It anchored the Sprawl trilogy, completed by Count Zero (1986) and Mona Lisa Overdrive (1988), set in a near future of multinational corporate power, console cowboys jacking into the matrix, and street-level high technology. With Bruce Sterling, Gibson wrote The Difference Engine (1990), a foundational steampunk novel imagining a Victorian age of Babbage computers. The Bridge trilogy (1993–99) and the near-contemporary Blue Ant books, beginning with Pattern Recognition (2003), moved his setting steadily into the present, which Gibson treats as already science-fictional. Later novels include The Peripheral (2014), a split-time narrative adapted for television in 2022, and its sequel Agency (2020).

Style and influence

Gibson's prose — dense, cool, saturated with brand names, fashion, and borrowed slang — treats technology as something used, hacked, and lived in rather than explained; his famous aphorism holds that "the street finds its own uses for things." His imagery shaped hackers' self-understanding, influenced films from The Matrix onward, anime including Ghost in the Shell, and the design vocabulary of virtual reality. He has frequently remarked that he wrote Neuromancer on a manual typewriter with almost no computer experience.
