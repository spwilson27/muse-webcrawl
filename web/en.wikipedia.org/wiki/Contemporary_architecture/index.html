Contemporary architecture

Contemporary architecture is the architecture of the 21st century. No single style is dominant; contemporary architects work in several different styles, from postmodernism, high-tech architecture and new interpretations of traditional architecture to highly conceptual forms and designs, resembling sculpture on an enormous scale. The different styles and approaches have in common the use of very advanced technology and new building materials, such as tube structures which allow construction of buildings that are taller, lighter and stronger than those in the 20th century.

Characteristics

Contemporary buildings frequently employ computer-aided design, curved and irregular forms made possible by parametric modelling, and new materials such as titanium, glass composites and high-performance concrete. Sustainability has become a central concern, with green roofs, passive ventilation, and energy-efficient envelopes common features of major projects. Many landmark commissions are cultural buildings — museums, concert halls and libraries — designed as civic icons, a phenomenon sometimes called the "Bilbao effect" after the success of Frank Gehry's Guggenheim Museum Bilbao.

Notable architects and works

  • Frank Gehry — Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (1997), Walt Disney Concert Hall (2003)
  • Zaha Hadid — Heydar Aliyev Center, Baku (2012); Guangzhou Opera House (2010)
  • Norman Foster — 30 St Mary Axe ("the Gherkin"), London (2003); Apple Park (2017)
  • Rem Koolhaas / OMA — CCTV Headquarters, Beijing (2012); Seattle Central Library (2004)
  • Renzo Piano — The Shard, London (2012); Centre Pompidou (with Richard Rogers, 1977)
  • Bjarke Ingels — 8 House, Copenhagen (2010); VIA 57 West, New York (2016)
  • Jean Nouvel — Louvre Abu Dhabi (2017)

Trends

Recent currents include deconstructivism, blobitecture, sustainable and biophilic design, adaptive reuse of industrial structures, and the use of mass timber in high-rise construction. Critics debate whether the pursuit of iconic form serves users and cities well, and movements such as New Urbanism advocate a return to traditional urban patterns.
