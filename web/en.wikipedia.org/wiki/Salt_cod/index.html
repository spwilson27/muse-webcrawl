Salt cod

Salt cod
TypePreserved fish
Main ingredientsCod (or related white fish), salt
NamesBacalhau (Portuguese), bacalao (Spanish), baccalà (Italian), klippfisk (Norwegian), morue salée (French)

Salt cod is cod (Gadus morhua and related species) that has been preserved by drying after salting. Produced for over 500 years in the North Atlantic fisheries of Newfoundland, Iceland, Norway, and the Faroe Islands, salted and dried fish formed a durable, lightweight staple that could be stored for months and transported worldwide, shaping trade routes and cuisines on four continents.

Production

Traditional production involves gutting and splitting the fish, stacking it in salt for several weeks ("wet salting" or pickling), and then drying it on wooden racks called flakes, or in modern plants in drying tunnels. Norwegian klippfisk ("cliff fish") was originally dried on coastal rocks. Before cooking, salt cod must be rehydrated and desalted by soaking in cold water for one to three days with frequent changes of water.

Culinary use

Salt cod is central to many cuisines. In Portugal, bacalhau is said to be preparable in 365 ways — one for every day of the year — including bacalhau à Brás and bacalhau com natas. In Spain it appears as bacalao al pil-pil; in Italy as baccalà alla vicentina; in France it is whipped with olive oil and garlic into brandade. In the Caribbean it features in Jamaican ackee and saltfish, the national dish, and in fritters across the islands. In West Africa it is known as stockfish when dried without salt, and in New England it was historically served in chowders and codfish balls.

History

Basque fishers were salting cod on distant North Atlantic grounds by the early 16th century, and the Grand Banks fishery made salt cod a bulk commodity of the triangular trade: fish went to Europe and the Caribbean, where low-grade "West India cure" cod fed enslaved plantation workers. Portugal and Brazil remained the largest import markets. The collapse of northern cod stocks in the late 20th century led producers to substitute other white fish such as pollock, ling, and tusk for some markets.
