Angular defect

FieldDifferential geometry
Related conceptsGaussian curvature, polyhedra, Descartes' theorem

In geometry, the angular defect or simply defect of a vertex of a polyhedron is the amount by which the sum of the face angles at that vertex falls short of a full angle around a point. For a convex polyhedron, the defect is always positive; it measures, in a sense, how much the surface "curves" at that vertex.

Definition

If the angles of the faces meeting at a vertex are θ1, θ2, …, θn, then the defect at that vertex is defined as

δ = 2π − (θ1 + θ2 + … + θn).

For example, at each vertex of a cube three squares meet, each contributing an angle of 90°, so the defect is 360° − 270° = 90°. For a regular tetrahedron, three equilateral triangles meet at each vertex with angles of 60°, giving a defect of 360° − 180° = 180°.

Descartes' theorem

René Descartes proved a theorem, equivalent to the polyhedral form of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, stating that the sum of the defects at all vertices of a convex polyhedron is exactly 4π radians (720°). For the cube, the eight vertices each have a defect of 90°, totalling 720°, as the theorem requires.

Generalizations

The notion of angular defect extends to higher dimensions and to non-Euclidean geometry. In spherical geometry, the angular defect of a spherical polygon is related to its area, and in hyperbolic geometry a similar quantity, sometimes called the defect, is defined for triangles. The defect also plays a role in Regge calculus, a formulation of general relativity that approximates smooth spacetime by flat simplices joined along their edges.
