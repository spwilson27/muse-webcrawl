In nuclear engineering, critical mass is the smallest amount of fissile material needed for a sustained nuclear chain reaction. The critical mass of a fissionable material depends upon its nuclear properties (the fission cross-section), density, purity, temperature, shape, and the surrounding neutron-reflecting material.

Explanation

A fission event releases neutrons; if enough of them strike other fissile nuclei before escaping, the reaction sustains itself. In a subcritical mass more neutrons are lost (through escape or non-fission absorption) than are produced, and the reaction dies out. In a supercritical mass the neutron population grows exponentially. Because neutrons escape through the surface while production scales with volume, a larger or denser mass is more efficient; a sphere minimises surface area per unit volume and therefore gives the lowest critical mass for a given material and density.

The effective neutron multiplication factor k describes the state: k < 1 subcritical, k = 1 critical, k > 1 supercritical. Compression (as in an implosion-type weapon) increases density and can make an initially subcritical mass supercritical. Criticality accidents — such as the "demon core" incidents at Los Alamos in 1945 and 1946 — occur when fissile material accidentally reaches a critical configuration.

Critical size and reflectors

The corresponding concept of critical size is the minimum size of a reactor core or assembly of given composition that can sustain a chain reaction. A neutron reflector (tamper) of beryllium, tungsten carbide or natural uranium around the mass can reduce the required critical mass substantially, as can moderation in reactor systems using low-enriched fuel.

Other uses

The phrase "critical mass" is used metaphorically in sociology, economics and technology for the threshold at which a process becomes self-sustaining — for example, the user base at which a social network becomes viable.
