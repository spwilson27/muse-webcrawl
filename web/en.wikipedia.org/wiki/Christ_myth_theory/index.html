The Christ myth theory, also called mythicism, is the hypothesis that Jesus of Nazareth never existed as a historical figure, and that the origins of Christianity lie in a mythical, possibly celestial, saviour figure whose story was later historicized in the Gospels. The theory is held by a small minority of writers and is rejected by nearly all specialists in early Christianity and ancient history, who maintain that a historical Jesus was baptized by John the Baptist and crucified under Pontius Pilate.

History of the theory

Speculation about a mythical origin for Jesus appeared in the Enlightenment with Charles-François Dupuis's Origine de tous les cultes (1795) and Constantin Volney's The Ruins (1791), which interpreted religions as solar myths. In the 19th century, the German scholar Bruno Bauer argued that Jesus was a literary invention of the Gospel writers. Arthur Drews popularized the thesis in The Christ Myth (1909), and in the early 20th century writers such as J. M. Robertson and George Albert Wells developed the case in English.

Arguments

Mythicist arguments typically include: the absence of reliably dated non-Christian references to Jesus from his own lifetime; parallels between Gospel narratives and earlier dying-and-rising gods and mystery cults; the late and legendary character of the Gospels; and, in Earl Doherty's version, a reading of Paul's letters as referring to a purely spiritual Christ who was crucified in a heavenly realm. Richard Carrier has defended the theory using Bayesian reasoning about background probabilities.

Scholarly response

The mainstream position holds that the evidence — including Paul's references to Jesus's brother James and to a recent crucifixion, the references in Josephus and Tacitus (whatever their textual complications), and the embarrassment of details such as a crucified messiah and baptism by John — is best explained by a historical Jesus. Critics of mythicism also argue that the alleged pagan parallels are overstated, and that ancient historians routinely accept the existence of figures attested by comparable or thinner evidence. Scholars across confessional lines, including Bart Ehrman in Did Jesus Exist? (2012), have written rebuttals of the theory.
