John le Carré

John le Carré
Born: David John Moore Cornwell, 19 October 1931, Poole, England
Died: 12 December 2020 (aged 89), Truro, England
Occupation: Novelist
Genre: Spy fiction

John le Carré (pen name of David John Moore Cornwell; 19 October 1931 – 12 December 2020) was a British-Irish author, best known for his espionage novels. Drawing on his own service in MI5 and MI6 during the 1950s and 1960s, he reshaped the spy genre with morally complex, realistic depictions of intelligence work, centred on his recurring character George Smiley.

Life

Cornwell was born in Poole, Dorset. His father was a con man whose frauds and absences overshadowed his childhood, an experience he later explored in the autobiographical novel A Perfect Spy and the memoir The Pigeon Tunnel. He studied at Sherborne School, read modern languages at the University of Bern and Lincoln College, Oxford, taught at Eton, and then worked for the British intelligence services, first MI5 and then the Secret Intelligence Service (MI6), serving in Germany. The exposure of Kim Philby and other scandals ended his intelligence career when his cover was compromised, and he turned to writing full-time.

Works

His third novel, The Spy Who Came In from the Cold (1963), was an international bestseller whose bleak, anti-heroic view of Cold War espionage contrasted sharply with the glamour of James Bond. His reputation peaked with the "Karla trilogy" — Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy (1974), The Honourable Schoolboy (1977), and Smiley's People (1979) — following Smiley's pursuit of his Soviet counterpart Karla. After the Cold War he turned to new subjects: arms dealing in The Night Manager (1993), pharmaceutical corruption in The Constant Gardener (2001), and the war on terror in later novels. Many of his books were adapted for acclaimed film and television productions.

Style and legacy

Le Carré is widely regarded as one of the finest novelists of the post-war period in English, credited with bringing literary seriousness to the thriller and coining or popularising much of the now-standard vocabulary of espionage, including "mole" and "honey trap".
