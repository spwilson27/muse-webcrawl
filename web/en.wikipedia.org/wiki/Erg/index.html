Erg

The erg is a unit of energy in the centimetre–gram–second (CGS) system of units. It is defined as the work done by a force of one dyne exerted over a distance of one centimetre, and is equal to 10−7 joules, the SI unit of energy.

Definition and conversions

One erg equals one gram centimetre squared per second squared (g·cm²/s²). Equivalently, 1 erg = 10−7 J = 100 nanojoules. About 1.34 × 10−3 ergs correspond to one foot-pound in energy conversions more familiar in engineering, and the electronvolt is about 1.602 × 10−12 erg.

History

The erg was proposed in 1873 by the British Association for the Advancement of Science's committee on electrical standards, as part of the CGS system put forward by Clerk Maxwell and William Thomson. The name derives from the Greek word ἔργον (ergon), meaning "work" or "task". Although the erg has been largely displaced by the joule in most contexts, it remains in common use in astrophysics and other fields where CGS units persist, for example in expressing the energy of supernovae (often in units of 1051 erg, sometimes called a "foe").
