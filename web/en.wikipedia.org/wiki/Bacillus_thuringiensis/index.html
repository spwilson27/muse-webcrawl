Bacillus thuringiensis


Scientific classification
Domain: Bacteria
Phylum: Bacillota
Class: Bacilli
Order: Bacillales
Family: Bacillaceae
Genus: Bacillus
Species: B. thuringiensis

Bacillus thuringiensis (or Bt) is a gram-positive, soil-dwelling bacterium, one of the most commonly used biological pesticides worldwide. During sporulation, many Bt strains produce crystal proteins, called δ-endotoxins or Cry proteins, that have insecticidal action. This has led to their use as insecticides, and more recently to genetically modified crops using Bt genes, such as Bt corn.

Discovery and naming

The bacterium was discovered in 1901 by Japanese biologist Shigetane Ishiwatari, who isolated it as the cause of the soto disease that was killing large populations of silkworms. He named it Bacillus sotto. In 1911 Ernst Berliner rediscovered it when he isolated it as the cause of a disease called Schlaffsucht in flour moth caterpillars in Thuringia, Germany, and named it thuringiensis after the region.

Mechanism of insecticidal action

Bt toxins are highly specific to their target insect species. The crystal proteins are activated by the alkaline conditions of the insect gut and by gut proteases. The activated toxins bind to receptors on midgut epithelial cells, form pores, and cause cell lysis, gut paralysis, and death of the insect. Because the activation conditions differ from those of the human gut, Bt preparations are regarded as safe for humans and most non-target organisms.

Use in pest control

Sprays and liquid formulations of Bt are used on a wide range of crops, and Bt genes have been introduced into crops such as corn, cotton, potato and soybean (Bt cotton and Bt corn) so the plants themselves produce the toxin. Concerns include the evolution of insect resistance, managed through refuges of non-Bt crops, and effects on non-target Lepidoptera such as monarch butterfly larvae exposed to Bt corn pollen.
