Radiocontrast agent

A radiocontrast agent, also called contrast medium, is a substance used to improve the visibility of internal structures in medical imaging such as X-ray, computed tomography (CT) and angiography. Contrast agents absorb or alter the external energy used in imaging, making blood vessels, the urinary tract or the gastrointestinal tract stand out against surrounding tissues.

Types

Iodinated contrast media are the most widely used agents for CT and angiography. Based on iodine-containing organic compounds, they may be ionic or non-ionic, with modern non-ionic low-osmolar agents having fewer adverse effects. Barium sulfate suspensions are swallowed or given rectally to image the gastrointestinal tract and are not absorbed by the body. For magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), gadolinium-based agents are used instead; microbubble preparations serve as contrast for ultrasound.

Adverse effects

Reactions range from mild nausea, warmth and itching to, rarely, severe anaphylactoid reactions. Iodinated contrast has been associated with contrast-induced acute kidney injury, particularly in patients with pre-existing renal impairment, and with thyroid dysfunction due to its iodine load. Barium must not be used when perforation of the gut is suspected, as leakage into the abdomen can cause peritonitis.

Uses

Contrast agents are essential for CT pulmonary angiography, coronary imaging, intravenous urography, myelography in selected cases, and interventional radiology procedures.
