Kumulipo

The Kumulipo is a Hawaiian oral genealogy chant and cosmogonic narrative, comprising more than 2,000 lines in the Hawaiian language. It was composed in honour of the high chief Kalaninuiʻiamamao (Lonoikamākahua line) and his son, and was preserved by the priests of the Lono and Kū orders. Its title means "beginning-in-deep-darkness": ku (to stand), muli (beneath), pō (night, darkness).

Structure

The chant is divided into sixteen wā (eras or epochs). The first seven wā take place in Pō, the age of spirit and darkness, tracing the emergence of life from a slime-like origin through coral polyps, sea creatures, insects, plants and increasingly complex organisms — a sequence often noted for its resemblance to evolutionary development. The remaining nine wā take place in Ao, the age of light and of human beings, and consist chiefly of genealogies linking the chiefly lines back to the gods, including Wakea and Papa, and the first humans Kumu-honua and his wife.

Preservation and translation

The Kumulipo was transmitted orally for generations. King Kalākaua had the text transcribed, and it was first published in Hawaiian in 1889. His sister, Queen Liliʻuokalani, translated it into English during her imprisonment in 1895, publishing the translation in 1897. A definitive edition with commentary was produced by Martha Warren Beckwith in 1951.

Cultural significance

The Kumulipo is regarded as one of the most important documents of Hawaiian literature and religion, embodying the native concept of the genealogical unity of all life — the idea that humans, plants, animals and gods share a single ancestry.
