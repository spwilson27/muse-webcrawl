Structural linguistics is an approach to linguistics originating from the work of Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, which treats language as a self-contained system of signs whose elements derive their value from their relations to one another within the system. It became the dominant paradigm in linguistics for much of the twentieth century and the seed of the broader structuralist movement in the humanities.

Saussure's foundations

Saussure's posthumously published Course in General Linguistics (1916) introduced the distinction between langue (the shared system of a language) and parole (individual acts of speech), and between synchronic study (language at a point in time) and diachronic study (language through history). The linguistic sign was analyzed as an arbitrary bond between a signifier (sound-image) and a signified (concept).

Schools

The Prague Linguistic Circle (Trubetzkoy, Jakobson) developed structural phonology around the phoneme and functional analysis. Copenhagen's glossematics (Hjelmslev) pushed abstraction further. In the United States, Leonard Bloomfield and his successors fused structural method with behaviorist psychology. The Geneva school kept closest to Saussure.

Central concepts

  • Two axes of relation: syntagmatic (combination in sequence) and paradigmatic (substitution).
  • Opposition and markedness: features are meaningful by contrast within the system.
  • Arbitrariness of the sign: no natural bond links sound and meaning.

Criticism and legacy

From the 1950s, Noam Chomsky's generative grammar displaced American structuralism, criticizing its taxonomic procedures and neglect of creativity and syntax. Nonetheless structural concepts — phoneme, distinctive feature, opposition — remain standard tools, and structuralism migrated into anthropology (Lévi-Strauss), literary theory, and semiotics.
