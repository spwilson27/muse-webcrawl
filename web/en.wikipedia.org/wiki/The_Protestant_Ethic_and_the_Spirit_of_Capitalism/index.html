The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism

Author: Max Weber
Published: 1904–1905 (essays); 1920 (revised book)

The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (German: Die protestantische Ethik und der Geist des Kapitalismus) is a work by German sociologist Max Weber, first published as essays in 1904 and 1905 and revised in 1920. It is one of the founding texts of economic sociology and remains among the most debated works in the social sciences.

Argument

Weber argued that the religious ideas of certain Protestant groups — especially Calvinism — unintentionally helped create the value system of modern capitalism. The Calvinist doctrine of predestination generated anxiety about salvation, which believers eased by interpreting worldly success in a disciplined calling (Beruf) as a sign of election. Combined with "this-worldly asceticism" — methodical work, frugality, and the reinvestment of profit rather than its consumption — this produced a "spirit of capitalism" exemplified in the writings of Benjamin Franklin.

The iron cage

Weber concluded that once established, capitalism no longer needed its religious foundation, and that individuals became trapped in an stahlhartes Gehäuse — famously translated by Talcott Parsons as the "iron cage" of rationalized modern life.

Reception

The thesis has been criticized from many angles: economic historians have questioned the correlation between Protestantism and capitalist development, and Weber himself presented the work as one side of a causal story, not a complete explanation.
