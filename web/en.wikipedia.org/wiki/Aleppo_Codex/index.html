The Aleppo Codex (Hebrew: כֶּתֶר אֲרָם צוֹבָא, Keter Aram Tzova) is a medieval bound manuscript of the Hebrew Bible, written in Tiberias around AD 920. Regarded for a thousand years as the most authoritative copy of the Masoretic Text, it was the manuscript consulted by Maimonides when he set out the exact rules for writing Torah scrolls, and it remains a foundational source for the text of the Hebrew Bible.

Creation

The codex was written by the scribe Shlomo ben Buya'a, and its vocalisation, cantillation marks and Masoretic notes were supplied by Aaron ben Asher, the most celebrated scholar of the ben Asher family of Masoretes, whose system of pointing became the standard for the Hebrew Bible. Originally it contained the entire Bible in some 490 leaves.

History

After periods in Jerusalem and Cairo, the codex came to rest in the Central Synagogue of Aleppo, Syria, where the Jewish community guarded it in an iron chest for some six centuries, believing it protected the community. During the anti-Jewish riots that followed the UN partition vote in December 1947, the synagogue was burned and the codex damaged; about 40 percent of its leaves — including most of the Torah section — were lost. What remained was smuggled to Israel in 1958 by Murad Faham and presented to President Yitzhak Ben-Zvi.

Modern significance

The codex is preserved in the Shrine of the Book at the Israel Museum in Jerusalem. It has served as the primary source text for modern scholarly editions of the Hebrew Bible, including the Hebrew University Bible Project and the Keter Yerushalayim (Jerusalem Crown) edition of 2000. In 2015 UNESCO inscribed the Aleppo Codex on its Memory of the World Register. The story of its loss and partial recovery was popularised by Matti Friedman's book The Aleppo Codex (2012), which raised questions about the fate of the missing pages.
