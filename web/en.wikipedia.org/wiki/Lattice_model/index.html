Lattice model

A lattice model is a simplified physical model defined on a discrete lattice, rather than in continuous space. Lattice models arise in statistical mechanics, condensed matter physics, and materials science, where they capture the essential physics of interacting degrees of freedom placed on the sites of a regular grid, while discarding details that are irrelevant at large scales.

A square lattice, the setting for many lattice models.

History

The study of lattice models began in earnest with the Ising model, proposed by Wilhelm Lenz in 1920 and solved in one dimension by his student Ernst Ising in 1925. Lars Onsager's exact solution of the two-dimensional Ising model in 1944 was a landmark of statistical mechanics, demonstrating that a simple lattice model could exhibit a genuine phase transition with non-classical critical exponents.

Examples

Well-known lattice models include the Ising model of ferromagnetism, the Potts model, the XY model, the Heisenberg model, percolation models, and lattice gases such as the lattice Boltzmann methods used in fluid dynamics. In each case, variables (spins, particles, or occupation numbers) live on lattice sites and interact through short-ranged rules.

Methods

Lattice models are studied by exact solution where possible, by series expansions, by renormalization group methods, and extensively by Monte Carlo simulation. Because the models are defined on a finite or countable grid, they are particularly well suited to computation.
