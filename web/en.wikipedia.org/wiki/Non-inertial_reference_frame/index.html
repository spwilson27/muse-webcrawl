Non-inertial reference frame

A non-inertial reference frame (also non-inertial frame of reference) is a frame of reference that undergoes acceleration with respect to an inertial frame. An accelerometer at rest in a non-inertial frame will, in general, detect a non-zero acceleration. While the laws of motion are the same in all inertial frames, in non-inertial frames they vary from frame to frame depending on the acceleration.

Fictitious forces

To apply Newton's laws of motion within a non-inertial frame, one introduces fictitious forces (also called pseudo-forces or inertial forces) that have no physical source and do not obey Newton's third law. Common examples include:

  • Centrifugal force — experienced in a rotating frame, directed away from the axis of rotation.
  • Coriolis force — acting on objects moving within a rotating frame, responsible (in the Earth frame) for the deflection of winds and ocean currents.
  • Euler force — appearing when the rate of rotation itself changes.
  • Translational inertial force — the apparent force pressing passengers back in an accelerating car, or felt in a braking train and a lifting elevator.

Examples

The surface of the Earth is a non-inertial (rotating) frame, which is why precise mechanics on Earth must include Coriolis and centrifugal terms — visible in the precession of a Foucault pendulum. Elevators, accelerating vehicles, and centrifuges are everyday non-inertial frames. In general relativity, gravity itself can be interpreted as an inertial effect: a freely falling frame is locally inertial, a view formalised by the equivalence principle.
