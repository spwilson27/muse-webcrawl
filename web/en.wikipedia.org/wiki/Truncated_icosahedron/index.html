In geometry, the truncated icosahedron is an Archimedean solid with 12 regular pentagonal faces, 20 regular hexagonal faces, 60 vertices and 90 edges. It is produced by truncating (cutting off) the twelve vertices of a regular icosahedron to one-third of the edge length.

Recognition

The shape is famous as the pattern of the classic association football (soccer) ball, with black pentagons surrounded by white hexagons, and as the structure of the buckminsterfullerene molecule (C60, the "buckyball"), whose sixty carbon atoms sit at the vertices of a truncated icosahedron — a discovery recognised by the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It is also the shape of the lens configuration used in some geodesic domes inspired by Buckminster Fuller.

Construction and properties

Truncating an icosahedron so that each cut passes one-third of the way along every edge converts its 20 triangular faces into hexagons and creates 12 new pentagonal faces where the vertices were. All edges have the same length and every vertex is identical: one pentagon and two hexagons meet at each vertex (vertex configuration 5.6.6). Dual to it is the pentakis dodecahedron.

For edge length a, the surface area is approximately 72.6 a² and the volume approximately 125.9 a³. Of all Archimedean solids, the truncated icosahedron is one of the closest to a sphere, which is why it works so well as a ball: when inflated, the leather panels bulge into a near-spherical shape.

In culture

Beyond football and chemistry, the form appears in architecture, in the design of some loudspeaker enclosures and children's climbing frames, and as the d20-like shape used for the "spherical" dice in some games. The truncated icosahedron was already known to Leonardo da Vinci, who drew it for Luca Pacioli's De Divina Proportione (1509).
