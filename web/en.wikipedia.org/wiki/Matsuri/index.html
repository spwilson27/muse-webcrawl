Matsuri

Matsuri
Japanese祭り / 祭
TypeTraditional Japanese festival
Religious rootsShinto (also some Buddhist festivals)
Typical elementsMikoshi processions, floats, dance, food stalls, fireworks
Famous examplesGion Matsuri (Kyoto); Awa Odori (Tokushima); Nebuta Matsuri (Aomori); Sanja Matsuri (Tokyo)

Matsuri (祭り) is the Japanese word for a festival or holiday. In Japan, festivals are usually sponsored by a local Shinto shrine or temple, though some, like the Sapporo Snow Festival, are secular. There are countless local matsuri across the country, most held annually to pray for good harvests, honour the shrine's kami, or mark the seasons; many feature processions in which the local kami is carried through the streets in a portable shrine called a mikoshi, accompanied by music, dancing, floats and street food stalls.

Elements

Common elements include the mikoshi procession, in which bearers in festival dress (happi coats) carry the shrine while chanting; dashi or yatai floats, sometimes elaborate multi-storey structures as at the Takayama Matsuri; traditional music with taiko drums, flutes and bells; communal dances such as Bon Odori in summer; and rows of yatai stalls selling food like takoyaki and yakisoba. Participants often wear yukata or happi.

Famous festivals

Kyoto's Gion Matsuri, held throughout July since the 9th century, is among Japan's most famous festivals, noted for its parade of enormous floats. Others include Tokyo's Sanja Matsuri at Asakusa Shrine, Osaka's Tenjin Matsuri with its river procession and fireworks, Aomori's Nebuta Matsuri with illuminated paper floats, Tokushima's Awa Odori dance festival, and the snow and ice sculptures of the Sapporo Snow Festival.

Origins

Most matsuri derive from Shinto agricultural rites — spring planting and autumn harvest thanksgiving — overlaid with Buddhist observances such as Obon, when ancestral spirits are honoured. Local festivals remain a focus of community identity and neighbourhood association (chōnaikai) organisation.
