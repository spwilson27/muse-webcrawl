47 Tucanae (NGC 104) or 47 Tuc is a globular cluster located in the constellation Tucana. It is about 4.0 ± 0.35 kpc (13,000 light-years) away from Earth, and 120 light-years across. 47 Tuc can be seen with the naked eye, with an apparent magnitude of 4.1. It appears about the same size as the full moon in the sky, under ideal conditions, and is the second-brightest globular cluster in the sky after Omega Centauri.

Characteristics

47 Tucanae is one of the most massive globular clusters in the Milky Way, containing millions of stars within a volume of only about 120 light-years in diameter. It has a very dense and bright core and was the subject of a major survey for planets in 2000. The cluster is about 13 billion years old and contains at least two distinct stellar populations with differing proportions of light elements.

The cluster contains a large number of millisecond pulsars — at least 29 have been identified — as well as numerous blue straggler stars and cataclysmic variables. In February 2017, indirect evidence was reported for an intermediate-mass black hole of roughly 2,300 solar masses at the center of the cluster, based on simulations of stellar motions and pulsar accelerations, although no direct accretion signatures have been detected.

Observation history

The cluster was discovered by Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille in 1751–52, during his expedition to the Cape of Good Hope. He initially catalogued it as a nebula, believing it to be the nucleus of a comet. James Dunlop observed it repeatedly from 1826, and John Herschel described it in detail during his stay at the Cape in the 1830s. Because it had been mistakenly identified with a star, it carries the Bayer-style designation "47 Tucanae," assigned by Johann Elert Bode.
