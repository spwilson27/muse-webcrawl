Contract for difference

In finance, a contract for difference (CFD) is a financial agreement between two parties, commonly described as the "buyer" and the "seller", stipulating that the buyer will pay the seller the difference between the current value of an asset and its value at contract time. If the difference is negative, the seller pays the buyer instead. A CFD is an agreement to exchange the difference in value of an asset from the time the contract is opened until it is closed.

History

CFDs were originally developed in the early 1990s in London as a type of equity swap that was traded on margin. Their invention is generally credited to Brian Keelan and Jon Wood, both of UBS Warburg, on their Trafalgar House deal in the early 1990s. Initially, hedge funds and institutional traders used CFDs to gain cost-effective exposure to stocks on the London Stock Exchange, partly because they required only a small margin and partly because no tax was payable on shares traded in this way.

Trading

CFDs allow traders and investors to profit from price movements without owning the underlying assets. The value of a CFD contract does not consider the asset's underlying value, only the change in price between the trade entry and exit. This is accomplished through a contract between a client and a broker, and does not use any stock, forex, commodity, or futures exchange. Trading CFDs offers several major advantages, including access to the underlying asset at a lower cost than buying the asset outright, ease of execution, and the ability to go long or short.

Trading on margin, known as leverage, means that only a small percentage of the full value of the position needs to be deposited. This magnifies both profits and losses, and regulators in many jurisdictions require prominent risk warnings, since a large proportion of retail CFD accounts lose money. CFDs are banned for retail investors in the United States, where the Securities and Exchange Commission and Commodity Futures Trading Commission restrict such over-the-counter leveraged products.

Risks

The main risks of CFD trading include market risk amplified by leverage, counterparty risk (the risk that the provider defaults), and the costs of holding positions overnight. Critics argue that many CFD providers create a conflict of interest by acting as the counterparty to their clients' trades.
