Treason Act 1695

Citation: 7 & 8 Will. 3. c. 3

Enacted by: Parliament of England

Royal assent: 1696

Status: Repealed in part; procedural provisions largely superseded

The Treason Act 1695 (7 & 8 Will. 3. c. 3) is an Act of the Parliament of England which laid down the rules of evidence and procedure in trials for high treason. Passed in the aftermath of the Glorious Revolution and a series of controversial Jacobite treason trials, it introduced significant safeguards for the accused and is regarded as one of the most liberal pieces of criminal legislation of its era.

Provisions

The Act required that a person indicted for treason be furnished with a copy of the indictment five days before trial, and with a list of the witnesses and the jury panel two days before. The accused was entitled to the assistance of counsel — a notable exception to the general rule of the time, under which those charged with felony could not have counsel speak for them. It also provided that no evidence could be admitted except that of two lawful witnesses, either both testifying to the same overt act of treason or one to one overt act and another to a second overt act of the same species of treason, and imposed a three-year time limit for prosecutions (with an exception for attempts on the sovereign's life).

Influence

The two-witness rule and other protections of the Act strongly influenced the framing of the Treason Clause of the United States Constitution, which requires "the Testimony of two Witnesses to the same overt Act". Scottish treason law was assimilated to the English model by the Treason Act 1708.
