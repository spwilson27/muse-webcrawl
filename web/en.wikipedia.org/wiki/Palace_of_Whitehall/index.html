Palace of Whitehall

The Palace of Whitehall was the main residence of the English monarchs from 1530 until 1698, when most of its structures, which had sprawled over about 23 acres, were destroyed by fire. Located in the City of Westminster, London, the palace gave its name to Whitehall, the street and government district that still form the centre of the British government.

History

The site began as York Place, London residence of the Archbishops of York; Cardinal Wolsey enlarged it into one of the grandest houses in England. After Wolsey's fall, Henry VIII seized it in 1529–30 and made it his principal London palace, adding a tiltyard, tennis courts, and a privy garden. Successive monarchs extended the rambling complex along the Thames.

In 1622 James I added the Banqueting House by Inigo Jones, the first fully classical building in London, its ceiling later painted by Rubens. In 1649 Charles I was executed on a scaffold outside it. The palace housed the court of Charles II and James II, and William III, who disliked its damp riverside air, moved the court to Kensington.

Destruction

Fires in 1691 and, catastrophically, on 4 January 1698 destroyed nearly the whole palace; only the Banqueting House and a few fragments survived. The court never returned, and the site was redeveloped with government offices and private houses, leaving Whitehall as the metonym for British government.
