Geometric measure theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Geometric measure theory (GMT) is the study of geometric properties of sets (typically in Euclidean space) through measure theory. It allows mathematicians to extend tools from differential geometry to a much larger class of surfaces that are not necessarily smooth, such as surfaces with corners, cusps, or fractal-like structure.

The field arose in the first half of the twentieth century, when researchers sought rigorous solutions to variational problems such as Plateau's problem — the problem of finding a surface of least area spanning a given boundary curve. Key early figures include A. S. Besicovitch, Herbert Federer, and Wendell Fleming. Federer's 1969 monograph Geometric Measure Theory gave the subject its name and much of its modern framework.

Key concepts

Hausdorff measure

The Hausdorff measure generalises the notions of length, area, and volume to sets of arbitrary, possibly non-integer, dimension. The Hausdorff dimension of a set, defined via these measures, is a central tool for describing fractals and irregular sets.

Rectifiability

A set is called rectifiable if it can be covered, up to a set of measure zero, by countably many Lipschitz images of Euclidean space. Rectifiable sets possess approximate tangent planes at almost every point, which makes it possible to do differential geometry on them. The complementary notion, purely unrectifiable sets, was studied extensively by Besicovitch in connection with projections.

Currents

Currents, introduced by Federer and Fleming, generalise the concept of an oriented submanifold and allow one to formulate and solve Plateau-type problems in great generality. The compactness theorem for integral currents guarantees the existence of area-minimising surfaces in many settings.

Important theorems

  • The Besicovitch–Federer projection theorem, characterising purely unrectifiable sets by their projections.
  • Federer–Fleming compactness theorem for integral currents.
  • De Giorgi's regularity theorem for minimal surfaces, later extended by Almgren and others.
  • Preiss's theorem (1987), which solved the Besicovitch 1/2 problem's generalisation by showing that the existence of a positive, finite density almost everywhere implies rectifiability.

Applications

Geometric measure theory underlies the modern theory of minimal surfaces, harmonic maps, free-boundary problems, and the calculus of variations. It also provides tools used in materials science, image processing, and the study of phase transitions via the theory of sets of finite perimeter (Caccioppoli sets).
