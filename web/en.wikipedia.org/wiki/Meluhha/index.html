Meluhha or Melukhkha (Sumerian: Me-luh-ha) was the Sumerian name for a prominent trading partner of Sumer in the Middle Bronze Age, usually identified with the Indus Valley civilisation of ancient India and Pakistan. Mesopotamian texts of the Akkadian, Ur III, and Isin-Larsa periods mention ships of Meluhha arriving at the ports of southern Mesopotamia alongside those of Dilmun (Bahrain) and Magan (Oman).

Trade

Cuneiform texts describe Meluhha as a source of gold, carnelian, ivory, timber, lapis and fine woods, exotic birds (probably peacocks), and monkeys. Archaeology supports the connection: etched carnelian beads, Indus stamp seals, cubical weights, and shell objects of Indus manufacture have been found at Mesopotamian sites such as Ur and Kish, and a cylinder seal belonged to an interpreter of the Meluhhan language, indicating a settled trading presence and resident merchants.

Identification

The identification of Meluhha with the Indus region rests on the congruence of the textual lists of imports with Indus products, the geographical sequence Dilmun–Magan–Meluhha along the Gulf and Arabian Sea coasts, and the archaeological distribution of Indus artefacts. The name may derive from a local word; some scholars compare Sanskrit mleccha ("foreigner, barbarian").

Decline

Mentions of Meluhha fade after about 1900–1700 BCE, coinciding with the decline of the Indus cities and the reorientation of Mesopotamian trade. The name later survives in learned and geographical lists, sometimes transferred to other regions.
