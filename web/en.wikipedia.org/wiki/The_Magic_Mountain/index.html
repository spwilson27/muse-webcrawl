The Magic Mountain (German: Der Zauberberg) is a novel by Thomas Mann, first published in 1924. It follows Hans Castorp, a young engineer from Hamburg who travels to Davos in the Swiss Alps to visit his consumptive cousin at the Berghof sanatorium for a planned three weeks — and remains for seven years, until the outbreak of the First World War calls him back to the "flatland" below.

Plot and ideas

Immersed in the hermetic, time-dissolving world of the sanatorium, Castorp is drawn into intellectual duels between the humanist Lodovico Settembrini, who champions reason and progress, and the Jesuit-trained Leo Naphta, apologist for terror and absolutism — debates that stage the ideological conflicts of pre-war Europe. His infatuation with the Russian patient Clavdia Chauchat, the meditations on time and illness, and the visionary "Snow" chapter, in which Castorp nearly freezes to death in a blizzard, are among the novel's best-known passages. The narrative ends as Castorp marches off to the Western Front, his fate unknown.

Origins and reception

Mann drew on his wife Katia's stay at a Davos sanatorium in 1912, initially conceiving a comic novella as a counterpart to Death in Venice; the work grew through the war years into a vast novel of ideas. The Magic Mountain is considered one of the most influential works of twentieth-century German literature, and contributed to Mann's receipt of the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929. The standard English translations are by H. T. Lowe-Porter (1927) and John E. Woods (1995).
