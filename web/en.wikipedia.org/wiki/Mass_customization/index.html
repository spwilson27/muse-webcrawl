Mass customization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Mass customization is a marketing and manufacturing technique that combines the flexibility and personalisation of custom-made products with the low unit costs of mass production. It allows customers to design certain features of a product — colour, size, components, configuration — while keeping manufacturing costs close to those of mass-produced goods.

History of the term

The concept was anticipated by Alvin Toffler in Future Shock (1970) and named by Stan Davis in his 1987 book Future Perfect. B. Joseph Pine II popularised the idea in Mass Customization: The New Frontier in Business Competition (1993), arguing that businesses increasingly faced markets fragmenting into niches and that information technology and flexible manufacturing made it possible to serve them at scale.

Methods

Pine distinguished four approaches: collaborative customizers engage the customer in dialogue to design the product; adaptive customizers offer a standard product the user can alter in use; cosmetic customizers present a standard product differently to different customers; and transparent customizers provide individually tailored goods without the customer's explicit involvement. Common enablers include modular product architectures, postponement (delaying final differentiation until an order is received), build-to-order production, and online "configurator" or "choiceboard" software. Examples include customised computers from Dell, personalised trainers from Nike's Nike By You service, made-to-measure suits, and printed books produced on demand.

Challenges

The strategy demands flexible processes, robust supply-chain coordination and careful management of complexity: too much choice can overwhelm customers ("choice overload"), and unit costs rise if variety is not built on shared modules and platforms.
