Christogram

A Christogram (Latin: Monogramma Christi) is a monogram or combination of letters that forms an abbreviation for the name of Jesus Christ, traditionally used as a religious symbol within the Christian Church. These monograms range from simple letter combinations to elaborate designs used on coins, manuscripts, altarpieces, and vestments.

Common Christograms

  • Chi Rho (☧) — formed by superimposing the Greek letters chi (Χ) and rho (Ρ), the first two letters of Christos (ΧΡΙΣΤΟΣ). According to tradition, the emperor Constantine the Great adopted the symbol after a vision before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge in 312 CE, placing it on his soldiers' shields and on the labarum standard.
  • IHS — from the first three letters of Jesus' name in Greek capitals, ΙΗΣΟΥΣ (Iota-Eta-Sigma); also given the Latin backronym Iesus Hominum Salvator ("Jesus, Saviour of Men"). It is especially associated with the Society of Jesus (Jesuits).
  • IC XC — a Greek abbreviation for "Jesus Christ" common in Eastern Orthodox iconography.
  • Chi Rho variants and the Alpha and Omega (ΑΩ), referencing Christ's statement "I am the Alpha and the Omega" in the Book of Revelation.
  • INRI — from the Latin Iesus Nazarenus, Rex Iudaeorum ("Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews"), the inscription placed above the cross at the Crucifixion.

History

Christograms appear on early Christian sarcophagi, coins of the late Roman Empire, and illuminated manuscripts. They remain in widespread use in church architecture, heraldry, and denominational emblems.
