The Black Hills land claim is an ongoing land dispute between Native American tribes of the Sioux and the United States government. The Sioux claim the United States illegally seized the Black Hills of South Dakota and Wyoming after gold was discovered there in the 1870s, in violation of the Treaty of Fort Laramie of 1868, which had guaranteed the Sioux exclusive use of the region as part of the Great Sioux Reservation.

Background

The Black Hills, known to the Lakota as Pahá Sápa, are sacred to the Sioux peoples. The 1868 treaty ended Red Cloud's War and set aside the western half of present-day South Dakota, including the Black Hills, for the "absolute and undisturbed use and occupation" of the Sioux. It provided that no part of the reservation could be ceded without the consent of three-fourths of adult male tribal members.

The gold rush and seizure

In 1874, an expedition led by Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer confirmed the presence of gold in the Black Hills, triggering a rush of miners into the region. After attempts to purchase the land failed, the United States demanded that all Sioux bands report to agencies by January 1876, setting the stage for the Great Sioux War. In 1877, Congress passed an act annexing the Black Hills, obtained with far fewer signatures than the treaty required.

Litigation

The Sioux pursued compensation through the Indian Claims Commission and the courts for decades. In 1980, in United States v. Sioux Nation of Indians, the Supreme Court ruled 8–1 that the taking of the Black Hills had been illegal and awarded the tribes just compensation plus interest, then amounting to about $105 million. Justice Harry Blackmun wrote that "a more ripe and rank case of dishonorable dealings will never, in all probability, be found in our history."

Refusal of the award

The Sioux have refused to accept the monetary award, insisting that the Black Hills themselves be returned. The funds, held in trust, have grown with interest to well over $1 billion. Tribal members remain divided between those who want the land returned and those who favor accepting the settlement. Proposals such as the Bradley bill of the 1980s, which would have returned some federal land in the Hills, failed in Congress.
