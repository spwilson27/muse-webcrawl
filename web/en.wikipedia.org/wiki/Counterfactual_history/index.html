Counterfactual history

Counterfactual history (also virtual history) is a form of historiography that attempts to answer "what if" questions by extrapolating a timeline in which a key historical event did not happen, or had a different outcome. It is related to, but distinct from, the alternate-history genre in fiction.

Practice

Popular subjects include a Confederate victory in the American Civil War, an Axis victory in the Second World War, or a world in which the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand failed. Niall Ferguson's edited volume Virtual History (1997) defended the method, arguing that contemporaries themselves weighed alternatives, so plausible counterfactuals — constrained by evidence of what actors considered — illuminate the role of contingency versus inevitability in causation.

Criticism

Historians such as E. H. Carr dismissed counterfactuals as a "parlour game," arguing they lack evidence and can drift into fantasy. Supporters reply that all causal claims implicitly contain counterfactuals ("but for X, Y would not have occurred"), so disciplined, minimal rewrites of the record are a legitimate tool of historical explanation.
