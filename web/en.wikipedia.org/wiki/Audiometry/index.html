Audiometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pure tone audiometry testing in a sound-treated booth

Audiometry is a branch of audiology and the science of measuring hearing acuity for variations in sound intensity and pitch and for tonal purity, involving thresholds and differing frequencies. Typically, audiometric tests determine a subject's hearing levels with the help of an audiometer, but may also measure ability to discriminate between different sound intensities, recognize pitch, or distinguish speech from background noise.

Types of audiometry

Pure tone audiometry

Pure tone audiometry is the standard subjective hearing test. Pure tones at frequencies typically from 250 Hz to 8000 Hz are presented through headphones (air conduction) or a bone vibrator (bone conduction), and the quietest level the subject can detect at each frequency is recorded as their threshold. Results are plotted on an audiogram, with frequency on the horizontal axis and hearing level in decibels (dB HL) on the vertical axis. Comparing air and bone conduction thresholds distinguishes conductive from sensorineural hearing loss.

Speech audiometry

Speech audiometry measures how well a person understands spoken words. The speech reception threshold is the lowest level at which the subject can correctly repeat spondee words, while word recognition scores measure the percentage of single-syllable words repeated correctly at a comfortable loudness level.

Objective tests

Objective measures do not require a behavioural response. Tympanometry assesses middle ear function by measuring eardrum mobility; otoacoustic emissions record sounds generated by the outer hair cells of the cochlea; and auditory brainstem response testing records electrical activity from the auditory nerve and brainstem, commonly used for newborn hearing screening.

Clinical use

Audiometry is used to diagnose and characterize hearing loss, to fit and verify hearing aids, to monitor ototoxic medication effects, and in occupational health programs that screen workers exposed to hazardous noise. Hearing thresholds of 0–20 dB HL are considered normal for adults; losses are classed as mild, moderate, severe, or profound with increasing threshold elevation.
