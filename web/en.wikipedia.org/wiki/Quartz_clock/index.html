Quartz clock

A quartz clock is a clock that uses an electronic oscillator regulated by a quartz crystal to keep time. This crystal oscillator creates a signal with a very precise frequency, so quartz clocks and watches are at least an order of magnitude more accurate than mechanical clocks. Generally, some form of digital logic counts the cycles of this signal and provides a numerical time display, usually in units of hours, minutes, and seconds.

History

The piezoelectric properties of quartz were discovered by Jacques and Pierre Curie in 1880. The first quartz crystal oscillator was built by Walter G. Cady in 1921. In 1927, the first quartz clock was built by Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Canada. The following decades saw the development of quartz clocks as precision time measurement devices in laboratory settings; the bulky and delicate counting electronics, built with vacuum tubes, limited their use elsewhere.

In 1969, Seiko produced the world's first quartz wristwatch, the Astron 35SQ, which revolutionized watchmaking. The greater accuracy and lower cost of production eventually resulted in the "quartz crisis" of the 1970s, during which the Swiss watch industry declined sharply.

Mechanism

Modern quartz timepieces use a low-frequency bar or tuning-fork-shaped crystal, often 32,768 Hz, chosen because it is a power of two (215), allowing a simple binary counter to divide it down to one pulse per second. The crystal is driven by an oscillator circuit that amplifies the piezoelectric signal. Temperature changes affect the resonant frequency, but typical crystals are cut so that this effect is minimized over the expected operating range.

Accuracy

Ordinary quartz clocks are accurate to within about 15 seconds per month. Precision quartz movements in laboratory or observatory use can be far more accurate. The quartz clock remained the most accurate timekeeping standard until it was superseded by atomic clocks.
