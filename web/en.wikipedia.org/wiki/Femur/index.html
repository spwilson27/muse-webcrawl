Femur

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Femur
ArticulationsHip: acetabulum of pelvis superiorly; knee: tibia and patella inferiorly
IdentifiersLatin: os femoris, os longissimum

The femur (plural femurs or femora), or thigh bone, is the only bone in the thigh. It is the longest and, by most measures, strongest bone in the human body, averaging about 48 cm (19 in) in adult men — roughly a quarter of a person's total height. The femur forms part of the hip joint at its upper end and part of the knee joint at its lower end, transmitting the entire weight of the upper body to the tibia.

Structure

Upper part

The proximal femur consists of a rounded head, which fits into the acetabulum of the pelvis to form the hip joint; a constricted neck, angled at roughly 125 degrees to the shaft; and two prominent processes for muscle attachment, the greater trochanter laterally and the lesser trochanter posteromedially, joined by the intertrochanteric line and crest.

Shaft

The shaft (body) is roughly cylindrical, bowed slightly forward, and marked on its posterior surface by the linea aspera, a roughened ridge giving attachment to the adductor muscles and the intermuscular septa. At its upper end the linea aspera divides into the gluteal tuberosity and pectineal line.

Lower part

The distal femur broadens into two rounded condyles — medial and lateral — separated behind by the intercondylar fossa, which houses the cruciate ligaments. In front, the condyles merge into the patellar surface, a smooth groove over which the patella (kneecap) glides.

Ossification

The femur is the first long bone to begin ossification, around the seventh week of embryonic life. It ossifies from five centres: one for the shaft, one for each end, and one for each trochanter. The lower epiphysis — the only epiphysis whose centre appears before birth — is used in forensic medicine as an indicator of full-term gestation.

Muscle attachments

Twenty-three muscles originate from or insert on the femur, including the gluteus maximus, the quadriceps femoris group, the hamstrings, and the adductors of the thigh. The femur's neck-shaft angle and trabecular architecture adapt to these forces, a relationship formalized in Wolff's law.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the femoral neck are among the most common and consequential injuries in elderly people, usually following a fall in the setting of osteoporosis; because the blood supply to the femoral head runs along the neck, such fractures risk avascular necrosis. Fractures of the femoral shaft require major force — typically road traffic collisions — and can cause significant blood loss into the thigh. The femur's length is also used in forensic anthropology to estimate the stature of skeletal remains.

In other animals

A femur is present in all tetrapods. In quadrupeds it is angled so the knee points forward; in birds and non-avian dinosaurs the femur is held nearly vertically beneath the body, a posture associated with bipedalism and efficient locomotion. In snakes and some lizards, vestigial femurs persist as pelvic spurs.
