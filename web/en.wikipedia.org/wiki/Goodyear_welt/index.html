Goodyear welt

[Image: Goodyear welt]

A Goodyear welt is a strip of leather, rubber, or synthetic material that runs along the perimeter of a shoe outsole. The machinery used for the process was invented in 1869 by Charles Goodyear Jr., the son of Charles Goodyear, and is a hallmark of high-quality shoemaking because it allows the shoe to be resoled repeatedly.

Construction

In Goodyear welt construction, the upper and insole are stitched to the welt, and the welt is then stitched to the outsole. Because the outsole is attached to the welt rather than directly to the upper, worn soles can be removed and replaced without damaging the rest of the shoe. The process creates a small cavity between the insole and outsole that is filled with cork, which moulds to the wearer's foot over time.

Advantages and disadvantages

Goodyear-welted shoes are prized for durability, water resistance, and repairability. Their disadvantages include higher cost, greater weight, and a longer break-in period compared with cemented footwear. The method is used by makers such as Crockett & Jones, Allen Edmonds, and many Northamptonshire shoemakers.
