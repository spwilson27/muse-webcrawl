Pandiagonal magic square
Also calledNasik square; panmagic square
PropertyAll broken diagonals also sum to the magic constant
Smallest order4 (excluding trivial order 1)

A pandiagonal magic square or panmagic square is a magic square with the additional property that the broken diagonals — diagonals that wrap around the edges of the square — also sum to the magic constant. Equivalently, if the square is tiled in a repeating plane pattern, every diagonal line in the tiling is magic. Pandiagonal squares were historically called Nasik squares, after the Indian city where the mathematician D. N. Lehmer's predecessors and the pandiagonal tradition associated with Narayana's work were studied.

Properties

In a pandiagonal square, not only the rows, columns, and two main diagonals but all 2n diagonal lines of the toroidal square share the same sum. Consequently, any n×n sub-square of the infinite tiling that aligns with the pattern behaves like a magic square, and shifting a row from top to bottom (or a column from side to side) preserves magicity. No pandiagonal square of order 3 exists, and none exists for any singly even order (n ≡ 2 mod 4).

Construction

Pandiagonal squares of order 4, 5, 7, 8, and all orders divisible by 4 or coprime constructions via knight's-move and linear (algebraic) methods are known; linear methods using two orthogonal "step" vectors generate most examples for orders coprime to 6.
