Analysis of variance (ANOVA) is a family of statistical models and their associated estimation procedures (such as the "variation" among or between groups) used to analyse the differences among group means in a sample. ANOVA was developed by the statistician Ronald Fisher. It is based on partitioning the total variation in the data into components attributable to different sources, and testing whether the variation between group means is larger than would be expected from variation within groups.

How it works

In its simplest form, one-way ANOVA tests the null hypothesis that several groups are drawn from populations with the same mean. The ratio of between-group to within-group mean squares gives an F-statistic, compared against the F-distribution to obtain a p-value. ANOVA generalises the two-sample t-test to more than two groups while controlling the error rate of the overall test; significant results are typically followed by post-hoc comparisons such as Tukey's HSD to identify which groups differ.

Assumptions and extensions

Classical ANOVA assumes independent observations, normally distributed residuals, and homogeneity of variances across groups. Extensions include factorial designs that estimate interaction effects between factors, repeated-measures designs for matched observations, and MANOVA for multiple response variables. ANOVA is a special case of linear regression and can be framed within the general linear model.
