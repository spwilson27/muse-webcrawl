Draco (lawgiver)

Draco
Activec. 622/621 BC
CityAthens, Ancient Greece
Known forFirst written law code of Athens; proverbial severity ("draconian")

Draco (Greek: Δράκων, Drákōn; fl. c. 7th century BC) was the first recorded legislator of Athens in Ancient Greece. Around 622/621 BC he replaced the prevailing system of oral law, administered and interpreted by aristocratic officials, with a written code that could be applied by a court of law — the first written constitution of Athens. Tradition holds that he was appointed to draw up the code in response to popular anger at arbitrary aristocratic justice.

The Draconian code

Draco's laws were notorious for their harshness: Aristotle and Plutarch report that the death penalty was prescribed even for minor offences such as stealing a cabbage or idleness, and a later Athenian orator said the laws were written "not in ink, but in blood". This severity gave rise to the modern adjective "draconian", meaning excessively harsh or severe. Whether literally true or exaggerated, the tradition reflects how the code was remembered. One distinctive feature was its homicide law, which distinguished premeditated from involuntary killing.

Survival and replacement

Within a generation, Solon (594 BC) repealed most of Draco's laws as part of his reforms, retaining only the homicide statutes, which remained in force for centuries; the text of the homicide law was republished on a stone stele in 409/408 BC, from which part of it survives today. Almost nothing is known of Draco's life; later legend claimed he died of suffocation under a pile of hats and cloaks thrown onto him by an appreciative audience in the theatre at Aegina.
