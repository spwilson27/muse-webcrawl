Katabatic wind

A katabatic wind (from the Greek katabasis, "descending") is a local wind that carries high-density air from a higher elevation down a slope under the force of gravity. Such winds are sometimes called fall winds; the spelling catabatic is also used. Katabatic winds can rush down elevated slopes at hurricane speeds, but most are not as intense as that, and many are on the order of 10 knots (18 km/h) or less.

Mechanism

A katabatic wind originates through the cooling of air on a high plateau, mountain or glacier. Since the density of air increases with lower temperature, the air will flow downwards, warming approximately adiabatically as it descends. The temperature at arrival depends on the region: in Antarctica the air arrives still extremely cold, while in foehn-like cases it may arrive quite warm.

Distinct from katabatic flow is the drainage of cold air that pools in valleys on calm nights; the purest katabatic winds occur where a large reservoir of cold air sits on a dome or plateau and spills outward under gravity, the descending air being continually replaced by sinking air above.

Examples

The strongest and most persistent katabatic winds on Earth blow off the Antarctic plateau, converging into valleys and reaching the coast at places such as Cape Denison in Commonwealth Bay, which is often described as the windiest place on Earth; Sir Douglas Mawson's expedition endured mean wind speeds there approaching 80 km/h. Greenland produces similar flows. Regional fall winds include the bora of the Adriatic, the mistral of southern France, the Santa Ana winds of California (in part), the oroshi of Japan and the piteraq of Greenland.

Effects

Katabatic outflow from ice shelves helps create coastal polynyas and drive sea-ice production, influencing ocean circulation. In wine-growing and agricultural regions, draining cold air determines frost pockets. Wind farms and polar research stations must be engineered for katabatic gusts, and the winds complicate aviation at Antarctic coastal stations.
