Charles Maurice de Talleyrand-Périgord (1754–1838), known simply as Talleyrand, was a French statesman and diplomat who worked at the highest level of French government through regime after regime — the monarchy, the Revolution, Napoleon, and the Bourbon and July monarchies — earning a reputation as the supreme political survivor and one of the most skilful (and unscrupulous) diplomats in European history.

Career

A club foot barred him from a military career, so the church was chosen for him: he became Bishop of Autun, then, in the Revolution, championed the nationalisation of church lands and was excommunicated. As foreign minister under the Directory (1797–99) he negotiated widely (and took bribes — the XYZ Affair with the United States arose from his agents' demands). Under Napoleon he served as foreign minister until 1807, quietly opposing the emperor's endless wars, taking money from Austria and Russia, and urging moderation; Napoleon called him (in a famous tirade) "silk stocking filled with dung". At the Congress of Vienna, representing defeated France, Talleyrand brilliantly exploited the victors' quarrels over Poland and Saxony, invoked the principle of "legitimacy", and signed a secret alliance with Austria and Britain against Russia and Prussia — so that France emerged with its 1792 borders and a seat at the top table. His aphorisms ("Above all, not too much zeal"; "Treason is a matter of dates") remain proverbial, and Metternich counted him both partner and rival.
