Stylometry

Stylometry is the statistical analysis of literary style, used to investigate authorship, chronological development, and genre in texts. It rests on the assumption that authors have distinctive, quantifiable habits in their use of language — such as the frequencies of common function words, sentence lengths, and vocabulary richness — that persist across their works and can be measured computationally.

History

The field traces back to 19th-century scholars such as Augustus de Morgan and T. C. Mendenhall. A landmark application was the 1964 study by Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace, who used Bayesian analysis of function-word frequencies to attribute disputed essays of The Federalist Papers to James Madison. Stylometric methods have since been applied to the works of Shakespeare, the letters of Paul, and anonymous or pseudonymous modern texts.

Methods

Common techniques include analysis of function-word frequencies, character and word n-grams, principal component analysis, cluster analysis, and Burrows's Delta, a measure of stylistic distance between texts. Modern approaches increasingly employ machine learning classifiers trained on corpora of known authorship.
