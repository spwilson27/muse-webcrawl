Great American Interchange

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great American Interchange (also called the Great American Biotic Interchange) was an important palaeozoological event in which land and freshwater faunas migrated between North and South America after the volcanic Isthmus of Panama rose from the sea to bridge the two continents. The main pulse of migration occurred around 2.7 million years ago, during the Piacenzian age of the Pliocene, although earlier "herald" species crossed by island-hopping and rafting tens of millions of years before the isthmus closed completely.

Background

South America had been an island continent since the breakup of Gondwana, and evolved an endemic fauna including marsupial predators (sparassodonts), xenarthrans (armadillos, sloths, anteaters), native ungulates (notoungulates and litopterns), and giant flightless terror birds (phorusrhacids). North America's fauna, connected intermittently to Eurasia, was dominated by placental carnivorans, horses, camels, deer, and proboscideans.

Migrations

Moving south were dogs, cats (including the sabre-tooth Smilodon), bears, raccoons, weasels, horses, tapirs, deer, peccaries, rabbits, and gomphotheres. Moving north were opossums, armadillos (including the giant glyptodonts), ground sloths, porcupines, capybaras, anteaters, terror birds such as Titanis, and the marsupial-derived fauna of Central America.

Outcome

The interchange was asymmetric: a far greater proportion of South America's native mammal fauna was displaced or driven extinct than the reverse. Roughly half of modern South American mammal genera descend from North American immigrants, whereas only about a fifth of North American genera have South American origins. Explanations proposed include superior competition and predation by northern placental carnivores, climate change favouring savanna-adapted northern forms, and disease. The Virginia opossum remains one of the most successful northward colonists; the armadillo's ongoing range expansion in the United States is sometimes described as the interchange still in progress.
