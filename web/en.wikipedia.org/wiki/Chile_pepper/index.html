Chile pepper

A variety of chile peppers

The chile pepper (also chili pepper or chilli pepper) is the berry-fruit of plants from the genus Capsicum, which are members of the nightshade family Solanaceae. Chile peppers are widely used in many cuisines as a spice to add pungency (spicy heat) and flavor, usually in the form of powders and sauces. The substances giving chile peppers their pungency are capsaicin and related compounds known as capsaicinoids.

Chile peppers originated in Central or South America and were first cultivated in Mexico. Following the Columbian Exchange, many cultivars of chile pepper spread across the world, used for both food and traditional medicine. This led to a wide variety of cultivars, including the Ancho, Jalapeño, New Mexico chiltepin, and Cayenne.

All varieties are descendants of five domesticated species: Capsicum annuum (bell peppers, jalapeños), Capsicum frutescens (tabasco, Thai peppers), Capsicum chinense (habanero, Scotch bonnet, Carolina Reaper), Capsicum pubescens (rocoto), and Capsicum baccatum (ají peppers).

History

Capsicum plants are native to modern-day Bolivia and have been a part of human diets since about 7,500 BC, making them one of the oldest cultivated crops in the Americas. Chile peppers were domesticated independently in several regions. After the Columbian Exchange, chile peppers spread rapidly through Asia via Portuguese and Spanish trade routes, becoming integral to cuisines in India, Thailand, Korea, and China.

Pungency

The pungency (spiciness) of chile peppers is measured on the Scoville scale, in Scoville Heat Units (SHU). Bell peppers rate 0 SHU, jalapeños roughly 2,500–10,000 SHU, habaneros 100,000–350,000 SHU, and the hottest known peppers, such as Pepper X and the Carolina Reaper, exceed 1.6 million SHU. Pure capsaicin rates 16 million SHU.

Culinary use

Chile peppers are used fresh, dried, smoked, or ground into spice blends. They are essential to Mexican, Indian, Thai, Korean, Sichuan, and many other cuisines. Dried smoked jalapeños are known as chipotles; dried poblanos are anchos. Capsaicin is also used in pepper spray and in topical pain-relief preparations.
