Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.

Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Decided: January 17, 1984
Citation: 464 U.S. 417
Decision: 5–4

Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc., 464 U.S. 417 (1984), known as the "Betamax case", is a decision by the Supreme Court of the United States that established that the making of individual copies of complete television shows for the purpose of time shifting does not constitute copyright infringement, but is fair use. The Court also ruled that manufacturers of home video recording devices, such as Betamax or other VCRs, cannot be liable for contributory copyright infringement for the potential uses of their products.

Background

In 1976, Universal City Studios and Walt Disney Productions sued Sony, alleging that consumers using its Betamax video tape recorders to record copyrighted broadcasts were infringing copyright and that Sony was contributorily liable. The District Court ruled for Sony in 1979, but the Ninth Circuit reversed in 1981, holding Sony liable and suggesting an injunction or royalty scheme. The case became a landmark contest between the motion picture industry — whose spokesman, MPAA president Jack Valenti, famously compared the VCR to the "Boston Strangler" — and consumer electronics manufacturers.

Decision

Writing for a 5–4 majority, Justice John Paul Stevens held that private, noncommercial time-shifting (recording a broadcast to watch once later) is fair use under the Copyright Act, emphasising that copyright holders had failed to show any meaningful likelihood of harm to the potential market for their works. The Court imported from patent law the "staple article of commerce" doctrine: selling an article capable of substantial noninfringing uses does not make the seller a contributory infringer. Justice Blackmun's dissent would have found time-shifting infringing and emphasised protection of copyright owners' exclusive rights.

Impact

The ruling secured the legal foundation of the home video industry, which soon became a larger revenue source for studios than theatrical exhibition. Its "substantial noninfringing use" standard has shaped later disputes over peer-to-peer file sharing (distinguished in MGM Studios v. Grokster, 2005), digital video recorders, and cloud storage services such as those at issue in Cartoon Network v. CSC Holdings (the Cablevision case).
