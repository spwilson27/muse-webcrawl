Revisionist Zionism is a nationalist faction within the Zionist movement, founded and led by Ze'ev Jabotinsky. It advocated a "revision" of the mainstream Zionist Organization's gradualist, settlement-first approach, demanding instead an explicit public commitment to a Jewish state on both banks of the Jordan River — encompassing the whole of Mandatory Palestine including Transjordan — and mass Jewish immigration backed, if necessary, by a Jewish military force.

History

Jabotinsky, a Odessa-born journalist who had raised the Jewish Legion in the First World War, founded the Union of Zionist Revisionists (Hatzohar) in Paris in 1925 after resigning from the Zionist Executive. The movement's youth wing, Betar, founded in Riga in 1923, emphasized military training, discipline and national honour. The Revisionists seceded from the World Zionist Organization in 1935 to form the New Zionist Organization after failing to win endorsement of their programme.

Ideology

Revisionism stressed political pressure on Britain, large-scale immigration, Hebrew labour and armed self-defence. Jabotinsky's 1923 essay "The Iron Wall" argued that Arab opposition could only be overcome by overwhelming Jewish strength, after which negotiation would become possible. Economically the movement was more liberal and middle-class in orientation than the dominant Labour Zionism, opposing the Histadrut's socialist monopoly.

Legacy

In Mandatory Palestine the Revisionist underground, the Irgun (Etzel), led from 1943 by Menachem Begin, fought the British authorities. After Israeli independence in 1948, Begin founded the Herut party, the political heir of Revisionism; Herut later merged into the Likud bloc, which first won power in 1977. Revisionist ideas about territory, security and free markets have remained influential in Israeli politics.
