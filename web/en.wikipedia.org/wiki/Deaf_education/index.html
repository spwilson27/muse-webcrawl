Deaf education

Deaf education is the education of students with any degree of hearing loss or deafness. It spans early intervention, preschool, primary, secondary, and post-secondary schooling, and has historically been shaped by a long-running debate over communication methods: instruction through sign language (manualism) versus instruction centered on speech and lip-reading (oralism).

Most deaf children are born to hearing parents, so language access in the first years of life is a central policy concern. Institutions range from dedicated schools for the deaf, such as the American School for the Deaf (founded 1817), to mainstream classrooms with interpreters and assistive technology.

History

Systematic deaf education began in the 16th–18th centuries with teachers such as Pedro Ponce de León in Spain and the Abbé Charles-Michel de l'Épée, who founded the first public school for the deaf in Paris around 1760 using methodical signs. Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet brought French methods to the United States with Laurent Clerc, founding the Hartford school in 1817. In Germany, Samuel Heinicke championed the oral method.

Methods and controversies

The 1880 Milan Conference declared oral education superior and discouraged sign language in schools, a decision the Deaf community long resisted and which the international deaf-education community formally repudiated in 2010. Approaches since include total communication, bilingual–bicultural (Bi-Bi) programs that treat a national sign language as the first language, auditory-verbal therapy, and cued speech.

Language acquisition

Research shows that deaf children exposed early to an accessible language — signed or, with successful technology, spoken — develop language on a typical timetable, while delayed access risks lasting language deprivation. Early identification through newborn hearing screening and intervention before six months of age significantly improve outcomes.

Technology

Hearing aids, cochlear implants, FM/Roger systems, captioning, and video relay services have transformed classroom access. Cochlear implantation in infancy is increasingly common in many countries, though its role remains debated within parts of the Deaf community, which regards deafness as a cultural identity as well as an audiological condition.

Higher education

Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C. (chartered 1864) is the world's only university designed for deaf and hard-of-hearing students; the National Technical Institute for the Deaf at Rochester Institute of Technology is another major center.
