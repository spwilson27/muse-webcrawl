Alkali Act 1863

The Alkali Act 1863 was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, among the earliest modern pieces of environmental legislation. It was passed to control air pollution from alkali works that produced soda ash by the Leblanc process, which released large quantities of hydrogen chloride gas, devastating vegetation and property around factories in places such as Widnes and St Helens.

Provisions

The Act required alkali manufacturers to condense at least 95 per cent of the hydrochloric acid gas produced in their works. To enforce this, it created the Alkali Inspectorate — often described as the world's first national pollution inspectorate — headed by the chemist Robert Angus Smith, a pioneer in the study of acid rain.

Later development

The Act was initially temporary but was made permanent and extended by later legislation, including the Alkali, &c. Works Regulation Act 1906, which broadened the range of industries and emissions covered. The inspectorate's approach of regulating the "best practicable means" of preventing pollution became a durable feature of British environmental law and a forerunner of modern agencies.
