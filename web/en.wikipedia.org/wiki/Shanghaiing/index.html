Shanghaiing

Shanghaiing, or crimping, is the practice of kidnapping people to serve as sailors by coercive techniques such as trickery, intimidation or violence. Those engaged in the practice were known as crimps. The term derives from the port of Shanghai, a common destination of ships with abducted crews.

Practice

In 19th-century ports on the American West Coast, such as San Francisco and Portland, and in British ports, crimps supplied crews to ships desperate for labour. Victims might be drugged, beaten or plied with drink until they signed ship's articles while incapacitated; boarding-house keepers were paid a fee, "blood money", for each man delivered. Portland's underground "Shanghai tunnels" became famous in local lore about the practice.

End of the practice

Legislative reforms such as the United States Shipping Commissioners Act of 1872 and the La Follette Seamen's Act of 1915, together with the decline of commercial sail, progressively eliminated shanghaiing by the early 20th century.
