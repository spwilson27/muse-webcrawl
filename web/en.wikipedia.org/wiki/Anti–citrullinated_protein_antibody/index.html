Anti–citrullinated protein antibody

Anti–citrullinated protein antibodies (ACPA) are autoantibodies directed against proteins in which the amino acid arginine has been converted to citrulline by the enzyme peptidylarginine deiminase, a process called citrullination. They are a hallmark of rheumatoid arthritis and are measured clinically as anti-CCP (anti–cyclic citrullinated peptide) antibodies.

Clinical use

The anti-CCP test has a sensitivity of roughly 60–70% and a specificity of about 95% or higher for rheumatoid arthritis, making it more specific than rheumatoid factor. ACPA can appear years before symptoms begin, and their presence predicts a more erosive, severe disease course. ACPA are included in the 2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for rheumatoid arthritis.

Pathogenesis

Citrullination occurs naturally during inflammation and cell death. In genetically susceptible individuals — particularly carriers of the HLA-DRB1 "shared epitope" — environmental triggers such as smoking promote citrullination of proteins in the lungs and loss of immune tolerance, leading to ACPA production. ACPA-containing immune complexes are thought to drive synovial inflammation and bone erosion in the joints.
