First-past-the-post voting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

First-past-the-post (FPTP) is a plurality voting method used in single-member districts: the candidate with the most votes in each district wins, whether or not they obtain a majority. It is the system used for the UK House of Commons, the US House of Representatives, Canada's House of Commons and India's Lok Sabha, and in a number of other states influenced by British practice.

Effects

FPTP typically magnifies the seat share of the largest party (the "winner's bonus"), frequently manufacturing single-party parliamentary majorities from vote pluralities of 35–45%, and penalises geographically dispersed smaller parties while rewarding regionally concentrated ones. Consequences include tactical voting, many "wasted votes" in safe seats, and the possibility that the party winning most votes nationwide does not win most seats (as in the UK in 1951 and February 1974, and in the US Electoral College analogues). Duverger's law links the system to two-party competition at district level.

Debate

Defenders prize FPTP's simplicity, strong local representation, decisive outcomes and accountability of a single governing party; reformers criticise disproportionality and argue for proportional or preferential alternatives. Referendums on changing the system were lost in the UK (2011, on the alternative vote) and in several Canadian provinces, illustrating status-quo advantages enjoyed by the system's own beneficiaries.
