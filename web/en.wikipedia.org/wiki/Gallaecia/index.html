Gallaecia, also spelled Callaecia or Galicia in Latin sources, was a Roman province in the north-west of Hispania, roughly corresponding to modern Galicia, northern Portugal, Asturias and León. It took its name from the Gallaeci (Callaeci), the Celtic tribal group occupying the region's south-west when Rome arrived.

Roman conquest and administration

The region was subdued during the Cantabrian Wars of Augustus (29–19 BC), the last phase of Rome's two-century conquest of Iberia. Initially administered as part of Hispania Tarraconensis, Gallaecia was divided into three judicial districts (conventus) centred on the new cities of Bracara Augusta (Braga), Lucus Augusti (Lugo) and Asturica Augusta (Astorga). Caracalla briefly created a separate province of Hispania Nova Citerior Antoniniana, and under Diocletian's reforms an enlarged Gallaecia became a province of the Diocese of Hispania, extending over the whole north-west. Its gold mines, notably Las Médulas, made it strategically and economically important to the empire.

Late antiquity

In 409 the Suebi crossed into Iberia and by 411 had established themselves in Gallaecia, where their kingdom — the first barbarian kingdom to be founded within the Roman Empire and to mint its own coins — lasted until the Visigothic king Leovigild annexed it in 585. The province's Roman cities, bishoprics and road network, and its distinctive late Roman Christianity associated with figures such as Priscillian and Martin of Braga, shaped the early medieval kingdom of Galicia that inherited its name.
