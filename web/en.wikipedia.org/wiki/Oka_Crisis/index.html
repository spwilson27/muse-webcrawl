The Oka Crisis (French: Crise d'Oka), also known as the Kanesatake Resistance, was a land dispute between a group of Mohawk people, the town of Oka, Quebec, and the Canadian government in the summer of 1990. It became a defining event in relations between First Nations and the Canadian state, and images of armed confrontation — particularly a photograph of a Canadian soldier standing face-to-face with a masked Mohawk warrior — were broadcast around the world.

Background

The Mohawk of Kanesatake had contested the status of their lands since the early 18th century, when the Sulpician order received a seigneury at Lac des Deux Montagnes. In 1989 the town of Oka announced plans to expand a nine-hole golf course and build luxury condominiums on land claimed by the Mohawk, including a pine forest and a burial ground. After legal challenges failed, Mohawk protesters erected a barricade on a dirt road leading to the disputed pines in early 1990.

The standoff

On 11 July 1990 the Sûreté du Québec launched a raid on the barricade; in the ensuing gunfire Corporal Marcel Lemay was killed. The crisis escalated: warriors from Kahnawake blockaded the Mercier Bridge into Montreal in solidarity, thousands of Canadian Armed Forces personnel were deployed under Operation Salon, and angry crowds in Châteauguay stoned cars evacuating Mohawk elders and children. The last holdouts at the treatment centre near Kanesatake surrendered on 26 September 1990.

Aftermath

The federal government purchased the disputed land and halted the development; the golf course was never expanded. The crisis spurred the creation of the Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples and reforms to federal land-claims policy, and it remains a landmark in the modern Indigenous rights movement in Canada.
