Tubulin in molecular biology can refer either to the tubulin protein superfamily of globular proteins, or one of the member proteins of that superfamily. α- and β-tubulin polymerize into dynamic microtubules, a major component of the eukaryotic cytoskeleton. Microtubules function in many essential cellular processes, including mitosis, intracellular transport, and the structure of cilia and flagella.

Structure and dynamics

The functional unit of the microtubule is a heterodimer of one α- and one β-tubulin subunit, each binding GTP. Dimers assemble head-to-tail into protofilaments, typically 13 of which form the hollow microtubule cylinder. Because β-tubulin's GTP is hydrolysed after incorporation, microtubules exhibit "dynamic instability" — stochastic switching between growth and rapid shrinkage — exploited during cell division to capture chromosomes. A third subunit, γ-tubulin, forms ring complexes that nucleate microtubules at centrosomes.

Clinical relevance

Tubulin is the target of important drugs. Colchicine binds tubulin and prevents polymerisation; the vinca alkaloids (such as vincristine) likewise inhibit assembly, while the taxanes (such as paclitaxel) stabilise microtubules. These agents are used in chemotherapy because rapidly dividing cancer cells are especially dependent on the mitotic spindle. Mutations in tubulin genes cause a range of neurodevelopmental disorders known as tubulinopathies.
