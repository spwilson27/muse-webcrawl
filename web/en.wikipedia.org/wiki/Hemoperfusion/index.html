Hemoperfusion

Hemoperfusion or haemoperfusion is a method of filtering the blood extracorporeally — outside the body — to remove toxic substances. Blood is passed over an adsorbent material, traditionally activated charcoal or resin beads in a cartridge, which binds toxins such as certain drugs and poisons.

Uses

Hemoperfusion was widely used in the 1970s and 1980s for drug overdose, particularly for substances such as theophylline, barbiturates and glutethimide, and remains used in some countries and in veterinary medicine. For most poisonings it has been largely superseded by high-flux hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapies, which clear many toxins as effectively with fewer complications.

Complications

Possible complications include thrombocytopenia (removal of platelets by the adsorbent), hypocalcaemia, hypoglycaemia and clotting of the extracorporeal circuit; modern coated adsorbent cartridges have reduced these effects.
