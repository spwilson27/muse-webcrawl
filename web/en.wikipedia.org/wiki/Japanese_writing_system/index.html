Japanese writing system

The modern Japanese writing system uses a combination of logographic characters, kanji (漢字), adopted from Chinese, and two syllabaries, hiragana (ひらがな) and katakana (カタカナ), collectively called kana. Written Japanese is often described as one of the most complex writing systems in use, because a single sentence normally mixes all three scripts, plus frequent use of Latin letters (rōmaji) and Arabic numerals.

Kanji

Kanji are used for most nouns and the stems of verbs and adjectives. Each character carries one or more readings: on'yomi, derived from historical Chinese pronunciations, and kun'yomi, native Japanese readings. The government maintains the jōyō kanji list of 2,136 characters taught in school and used in official publications.

Kana

Hiragana is used for grammatical endings, particles, and words without kanji; its cursive forms derive from whole characters written in abbreviated form. Katakana, more angular, is used for foreign loanwords, onomatopoeia, emphasis and scientific names. Each kana represents a mora; the basic inventories comprise 46 characters each, modified by diacritics (dakuten) and small characters.

Direction and punctuation

Texts may be written vertically (tategaki, top to bottom, columns right to left), as in most novels and newspapers' literary pages, or horizontally (yokogaki, left to right), as on the web. Pronunciation guides in small kana, furigana, are printed beside difficult kanji.

History

Writing arrived from China by the 5th century; the phonetic use of characters as man'yōgana in the 8th-century Man'yōshū led gradually to the kana syllabaries by the 9th–10th centuries. After the Second World War, reforms standardised character forms (shinjitai) and limited the kanji in general use.
