Richard McKay Rorty (October 4, 1931 – June 8, 2007) was an American philosopher and the central figure in the revival of pragmatism in late 20th-century philosophy. His book Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature (1979) attacked the idea that knowledge is an accurate representation of a mind-independent reality and helped reshape debates across the humanities.

Life and career

Rorty was born in New York City into a left-wing intellectual family; his parents were the writers and Trotskyist activists James Rorty and Winifred Raushenbush. He entered the University of Chicago at fifteen, took his PhD at Yale in 1956, and taught at Wellesley and then Princeton (1961–1982), where he became a leading analytic philosopher. After 1979 his work moved away from analytic philosophy toward a synthesis of pragmatism with Nietzsche, Heidegger and Derrida. He later held chairs at the University of Virginia (in English and comparative literature) and Stanford.

Philosophy

Rorty argued that philosophy should abandon the project of founding knowledge on representations that "mirror" nature, a picture he traced from Descartes and Locke through Kant to contemporary philosophy of mind and language. Drawing on Dewey, Wittgenstein, Sellars and Quine, he proposed instead a pragmatist view in which beliefs are tools for coping rather than copies of the world, and vocabularies are historically contingent creations.

In Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity (1989) he described the "liberal ironist" — someone who recognises the contingency of her own deepest convictions while remaining committed to reducing cruelty — and defended liberal democracy not as rationally grounded but as a tradition worth loyalty. His political essays, notably Achieving Our Country (1998), urged the American left toward reformist national pride and away from cultural pessimism.

Reception

Rorty was one of the most widely read and controversial philosophers of his generation, celebrated in literature and cultural studies departments and criticised by many analytic philosophers and by political critics who charged him with relativism and complacency about injustice — charges he rejected.
