Cáodòng (Chinese: 曹洞宗) is one of the five houses of Chan Buddhism, founded in Tang-dynasty China. It is named by combining characters from the names of its founders, Dongshan Liangjie (807–869) and his disciple Caoshan Benji (840–901). In Japan the school was transmitted by Eihei Dōgen in the 13th century and is known as Sōtō Zen, where it became one of the largest Buddhist denominations.

Teachings

The Caodong school is associated with Dongshan's teaching of the Five Ranks, which map the interplay of the absolute and the relative. In the Song dynasty, the Caodong master Hongzhi Zhengjue (1091–1157) systematised the practice of "silent illumination" (Chinese: mòzhào) — serene, objectless sitting meditation — in contrast to the koan-introspection methods of the rival Linji school. Caodong masters also compiled celebrated collections of verse commentaries on koans, including the Book of Equanimity.

History

After flourishing in the Tang and Song, the Chinese Caodong lineage declined in later dynasties and nearly died out, but was revived several times; the modern Chinese Caodong lineages derive largely from revivals in the Ming and Qing periods and from Japanese transmissions. Through Sōtō, Caodong teaching spread worldwide in the 20th century, notably through teachers such as Shunryū Suzuki in the United States.
