Soup kitchen

A soup kitchen is a place where food is offered to the hungry and homeless, usually for no cost, or sometimes at a below-market price. Frequently located in lower-income neighborhoods, soup kitchens are often staffed by volunteer organizations, such as church or community groups. Soup kitchens sometimes obtain food from a food bank free of charge, enabling them to feed many people at low cost.

History

Soup kitchens in the modern sense began to appear in the late 18th century. The scientist and inventor Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford, pioneered the feeding of the poor with cheap nutritious soups while working in Munich in the 1790s. During the Irish famine of the 1840s, soup kitchens were a major (and controversial) instrument of relief; in 1847 the British government funded them under the Soup Kitchen Act, at one point feeding some three million people daily.

In the United States, soup kitchens multiplied during the Great Depression of the 1930s; one of the most famous was opened in Chicago in 1931 by the gangster Al Capone, an early example of a private citizen attempting to fill the gap left by government relief. Many modern soup kitchens were founded during the economic downturns of the late 1970s and 1980s, and charitable meal provision remains a significant response to food insecurity.

Modern practice

Many modern soup kitchens serve full hot meals rather than only soup, and some also provide groceries, clothing, or referrals to social services. The largest are run by established charities and religious communities; Sikh temples operate the related institution of the langar, serving free meals to all visitors.
