Stunt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A stunt is an unusual, difficult, or dangerous physical feat performed for artistic purposes, typically in film, television, theatre, or live entertainment. Stunts are executed by trained stunt performers, who may double for actors in hazardous sequences or perform as specialists in their own right. Common categories include falls, fights, vehicle work, fire stunts, aerial and wire work, and water stunts.

History

Stunt work grew out of circus, vaudeville, and wild-west shows, and became a distinct profession with early cinema. Silent-film comedians such as Buster Keaton and Harold Lloyd performed their own dangerous gags, while specialist performers were hired for serials and westerns. As filmmaking industrialised, stunt work professionalised: performers like Yakima Canutt developed techniques — including the horse fall and the stagecoach transfer — that became standard practice, and stunt coordination emerged as a dedicated craft responsible for designing and safely executing action sequences.

Safety

Modern stunt work is governed by detailed planning, rehearsal, and safety equipment including airbags, decelerators, harnesses, fire gels, and breakaway props. Stunt coordinators assess risk, and performers are represented by unions such as SAG-AFTRA in the United States. Despite precautions, serious injuries and deaths have occurred on productions throughout film history, prompting periodic tightening of industry safety bulletins. Computer-generated imagery has replaced some of the most dangerous practical stunts, though practical stunt work remains central to many action films.

Recognition

Stunt performers have long campaigned for greater recognition, including an Academy Award category for stunt design, announced in 2025 to debut for films released in 2027. The Taurus World Stunt Awards were established in 2001 to honour stunt work internationally.
