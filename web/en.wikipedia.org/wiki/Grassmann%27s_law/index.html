Grassmann's law

Grassmann's law, named after the German philologist Hermann Grassmann, who described it in 1863, is a dissimilation rule in Ancient Greek and Sanskrit: when two aspirated consonants occur in successive syllables of a word, the first loses its aspiration (deaspirates).

Examples

In Greek, the root of the verb τίθημι (títhēmi, "I put") derives from an earlier *thi-thē- form in which the first aspirate was lost. Similarly, the future of τρέφω shows the pattern: underlying *threph-sō surfaces as θρέψω for the present stem but the aorist appears with a plain dental, as in ἔθρεψα vs. forms where aspiration reappears when not followed by another aspirate.

Significance

Grassmann's law is a classic example of dissimilation and played an important role in establishing the regularity of sound change in comparative Indo-European linguistics.
