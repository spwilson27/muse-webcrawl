Ōkubo Toshimichi (Japanese: 大久保 利通; 26 September 1830 – 14 May 1878) was a Japanese statesman and one of the samurai of Satsuma Domain who led the Meiji Restoration. He is counted, together with Saigō Takamori and Kido Takayoshi, as one of the "Three Great Nobles of the Restoration" (維新の三傑, Ishin no Sanketsu). After 1868 he became the most powerful figure in the new Meiji government, directing much of Japan's early modernisation before his assassination in 1878.

Early life

Ōkubo was born in Kagoshima, the castle town of Satsuma Domain, the eldest son of a low-ranking samurai. He grew up in the same neighbourhood as Saigō Takamori and studied with him at the domain school. After a period of disgrace and exile following factional disputes within the domain, Ōkubo rose under the patronage of the reformist lord Shimazu Nariakira and became a central figure in the movement to overthrow the Tokugawa shogunate.

Meiji government

Following the Boshin War and the establishment of the Meiji state in 1868, Ōkubo helped drive the abolition of the feudal domains in 1871. He served as a senior councillor and accompanied the Iwakura Mission (1871–1873) on its tour of the United States and Europe, an experience that convinced him Japan needed gradual, state-led industrialisation before constitutional government. On his return he blocked plans to invade Korea in the Seikanron debate of 1873, a decision that pushed Saigō Takamori out of the government.

As the first Home Minister from 1873, Ōkubo promoted industry through the policy of shokusan kōgyō, created a centralised police system, and consolidated the authority of the central government. He suppressed a series of samurai rebellions, culminating in the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 led by his former friend Saigō.

Assassination and legacy

On 14 May 1878, Ōkubo was assassinated in Tokyo at Kioizaka by a group of discontented former samurai from Ishikawa Prefecture, in what became known as the Kioizaka Incident. He is remembered as the architect of the modern Japanese state: austere, pragmatic and often ruthless, he laid much of the institutional groundwork that later leaders, including his protégé Itō Hirobumi, built upon.
