Boudica

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Thornycroft's bronze statue of Boudica and her daughters in their chariot, Westminster, London]

Boudica (died AD 60 or 61), also spelled Boadicea, was queen of the British Celtic Iceni tribe who led the largest revolt against Roman rule in Britain. When her husband Prasutagus died (c. AD 60), Rome annexed his client kingdom outright, confiscated Iceni lands, flogged Boudica and raped her daughters (per Tacitus and Dio). Her revolt burned the Roman towns of Camulodunum (Colchester), Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans) to their foundations — the burnt layer still found by archaeologists — and massacred their inhabitants, before the governor Suetonius Paulinus destroyed her vastly larger army at the Battle of Watling Street with some 10,000 men. Boudica died — by suicide (Tacitus) or illness (Dio) — and Roman Britain was governed thereafter with a gentler hand.

The revolt

The rising combined grievances: Seneca's abruptly recalled loans, the temple of Claudius at Colchester as symbol of occupation, disarmament and land seizures, veterans' abuses. Colchester fell undefended — its population massacred, the temple's last stand lasting two days; the Ninth Legion's infantry was ambushed and destroyed marching to relieve it. Londinium, already Britain's commercial hub, was abandoned by Suetonius and burned with (the sources say) its remaining inhabitants; Dio's atrocity catalogue (sacrifices in the groves of Andraste) is coloured but the destruction layers — red burnt earth across all three cities — are archaeology's confirmation of the revolt's ferocity.

Aftermath and memory

Watling Street — Suetonius' chosen ground, a defile anchoring his flanks — saw Roman discipline annihilate tribal numbers attacking uphill into pila and wedges; Tacitus' figures (80,000 Britons dead for 400 Roman) are conventional rhetoric over a real catastrophe. Forgotten for a millennium, Boudica was resurrected by the Renaissance recovery of Tacitus, glorified by the Victorians (Thornycroft's statue of her chariot — with the scythed wheels Dio's fancy added — stands by Westminster Bridge), and remains Britain's emblem of resistance and of the violence beneath empire.
