V12 engine

A V12 engine is a twelve-cylinder piston engine where two banks of six cylinders are arranged in a V configuration around a common crankshaft. V12 engines are more powerful than V6, V8, and V10 engines of the same displacement, and they run more smoothly: with firing impulses every 60° of crankshaft rotation and perfect primary and secondary balance, a V12 needs no balance shafts.

Design

Each bank of a V12 is effectively a straight-six, itself inherently balanced, so the complete engine has excellent refinement. The usual bank angle is 60°, which gives evenly spaced firing for a four-stroke engine. The layout's disadvantages are size, weight, cost and complexity, with two cylinder heads and double the valvetrain of an inline engine.

History and uses

The first V12 was built in 1904 by the Putney Motor Works in London for racing boats. V12s became prestigious in 1930s luxury cars (Packard, Cadillac, Lincoln) and in the classic grand tourers of Ferrari, whose Colombo and Lampredi V12s defined the marque from 1947. In aviation, the Rolls-Royce Merlin V12 powered the Spitfire, Hurricane, Lancaster and P-51 Mustang. Modern V12s survive in flagship models from Ferrari, Lamborghini, Aston Martin, Mercedes-Maybach and Rolls-Royce, though emissions rules and downsizing have made them rare.
