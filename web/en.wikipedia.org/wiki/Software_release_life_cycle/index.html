Software release life cycle

Software progresses through recognizable stages of maturity before and after release.

A software release life cycle is the sequence of stages of development and maturity for a piece of software, from its initial development through testing, its release to users, its supported lifetime of updates, and finally the end of support. The conventional stage names — pre-alpha, alpha, beta, release candidate, and stable (general availability) — originated in the software industry and were popularized by IBM's usage; conventions vary between projects, especially open-source ones.

Stages

Pre-alpha covers work before formal testing, including design, prototyping, and feature development; versions may be incomplete and unstable ("nightly" or "canary" builds in modern practice). Alpha is the first phase of formal testing, usually internal; the product is feature-incomplete or feature-complete but contains known bugs. Beta follows, when the software is feature-complete and is released to a limited set of outside testers (closed beta) or to the public (open beta) for real-world feedback; no major features are normally added during beta. A release candidate (RC) is a build believed to be final, which will become the stable release unless serious defects appear. The stable or general availability (GA) release is distributed to all customers.

After release

Following release, software typically receives maintenance updates (patches), minor versions, and security fixes during its supported life. Vendors announce end of life (EOL) when support ceases. Version numbering schemes (such as semantic versioning) communicate the nature of each release. Modern continuous-delivery practices blur the classical stages: web services and apps may use feature flags, canary releases, and perpetual "beta" labels, while games and consumer software have added stages such as early access. The term "gold" or "gone gold" — release to manufacturing (RTM) — dates from the era of physical media pressing.
