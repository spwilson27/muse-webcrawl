Nonviolence

Nonviolence is the personal practice of not causing harm to others under any condition. It may come from the belief that hurting people, animals and/or the environment is unnecessary to achieve an outcome, and may refer to a general philosophy of abstention from violence. As a political method, nonviolent resistance (also called civil resistance) seeks change through protest, non-cooperation and intervention without physical violence.

Philosophical and religious roots

The principle of ahimsa — non-harm toward all living beings — is central to Jainism, Buddhism and Hinduism. In the West, traditions range from the early Christian pacifism of groups such as the Quakers and Mennonites to the teachings of Leo Tolstoy, whose writings on Christian non-resistance influenced Mohandas Gandhi. Gandhi developed his philosophy of satyagraha ("truth force") during campaigns in South Africa and led India's independence struggle through mass nonviolent action, including the 1930 Salt March.

Nonviolence in practice

Martin Luther King Jr. adapted Gandhian methods to the American civil rights movement, articulating six principles of nonviolence. Scholars such as Gene Sharp catalogued 198 methods of nonviolent action and theorised that political power depends on the consent and obedience of the governed, which non-cooperation withdraws. Research by Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan found that, in the twentieth century, nonviolent campaigns for regime change succeeded roughly twice as often as violent ones — a finding summarised by the claim that no government can withstand a movement mobilising about 3.5% of the population.

Forms

Distinctions are often drawn between principled nonviolence, held as a moral or spiritual commitment, and pragmatic nonviolence, adopted as the most effective available strategy. Practices include pacifism, conscientious objection, civil disobedience, boycotts, strikes and constructive programmes of institution-building.
