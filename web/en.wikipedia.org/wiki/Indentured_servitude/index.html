Indentured servitude

An indenture contract bound a servant to work for a fixed term.

Indentured servitude is a form of labour in which a person is contracted to work without salary for the benefit of another person for a specific length of time. It is often rendered "indentured labour", especially in the British Empire. The employer buys the contract (the "indenture") from a sea captain who had transported the servant and incurred the costs on the voyage. The servant generally worked to repay the cost of passage, lodging, food, and training.

North America

Indentured servitude supplied much of the labour for European colonies in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In British America, a large share of white immigrants — historians estimate between one-half and two-thirds of those arriving in the Chesapeake colonies — came as indentured servants, typically signing contracts of four to seven years in exchange for passage. At the end of the term, servants might receive "freedom dues" of money, tools, or land. Conditions varied widely, and servants had limited legal recourse; terms could be extended for infractions such as running away or pregnancy. The system declined as enslaved African labour became more entrenched and economically favoured by planters.

Indian indenture system

After the abolition of slavery in the British Empire in the 1830s, a new system of indentured labour recruited over a million Indians to work on plantations in Mauritius, Fiji, the Caribbean, Natal, and elsewhere. Recruits signed agreements (girmit) for five-year terms, frequently under conditions of deception and hardship that critics described as a new form of slavery. The system was abolished in the early twentieth century under pressure from Indian nationalist campaigns led in part by Mahatma Gandhi. Descendant communities — Indo-Caribbean, Indo-Fijian, Indo-Mauritian — remain a lasting legacy.

Distinction from slavery

Unlike chattel slavery, indenture was legally temporary and the servant retained some legal personhood; servants were not, in law, hereditary property, and their children were born free. In practice, the line could blur, especially where employers could sell contracts and enforce terms with corporal punishment.
