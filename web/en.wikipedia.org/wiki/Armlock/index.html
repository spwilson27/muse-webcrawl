An armlock in grappling is a single or double joint lock that hyperextends, hyperflexes or hyper-rotates the elbow joint or shoulder joint. An armlock that hyper-extends the arm is sometimes called an armbar, and an armlock that hyper-rotates the arm is sometimes called an armcoil. Depending on the joint flexibility of the opponent, armlocks can be used to cause pain, force a submission, or, if applied with sufficient force, break or dislocate the targeted joint.

Armbar

The armbar (known in judo as juji-gatame) is a classic armlock in which the attacker traps the opponent's extended arm between their legs while lying perpendicular to the opponent's torso, using the hips as a fulcrum to hyperextend the elbow. Variations exist from the mount, the guard, and standing positions, and include the flying armbar, in which the attacker jumps into position. The armbar is one of the most common submissions in mixed martial arts and submission grappling.

Shoulder locks

Shoulder locks attack the shoulder joint by rotating the arm. In Brazilian jiu-jitsu the two most common are the americana (ude-garami), which flexes the shoulder with the arm bent upward, and the kimura, in which the arm is bent behind the back; the kimura is named after judoka Masahiko Kimura, who used it to defeat Hélio Gracie in 1951. The omoplata uses the attacker's legs to rotate the shoulder. Wrist-dependent variants and hammerlocks are also recognised in catch wrestling.

Legality and safety

Armlocks are legal in judo, Brazilian jiu-jitsu, sambo and mixed martial arts, though some rule sets restrict certain variants — for example, the International Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Federation prohibits some spinal and rotational leg locks at lower levels and restricts flying techniques. Because a fully applied armlock can fracture the humerus or dislocate the elbow in seconds, practitioners signal submission by tapping out, and referees may stop a contest when an arm is caught in a dangerous position.
