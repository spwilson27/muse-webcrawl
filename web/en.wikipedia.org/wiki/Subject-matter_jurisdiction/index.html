Subject-matter jurisdiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Subject-matter jurisdiction is a court's authority to hear and determine cases of a particular type or cases relating to a specific subject matter. It derives from the constitution and statutes constituting the court, and (unlike many defects) it cannot be waived or conferred by consent of the parties: a judgment rendered without subject-matter jurisdiction is void, and the objection may be raised at any stage, even by the court itself.

In the United States, federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction: Article III and congressional grants confine them principally to federal questions (28 U.S.C. § 1331) and diversity of citizenship between parties of different states above a statutory amount in controversy (§ 1332, over US$75,000), with supplemental jurisdiction over related state claims (§ 1367). State courts, by contrast, are generally courts of general jurisdiction, subject to allocations among specialised courts (probate, family, small claims). The doctrine polices federalism: parties cannot manufacture federal jurisdiction, and cases improvidently filed federally are remanded or dismissed.
