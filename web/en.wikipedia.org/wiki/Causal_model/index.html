Causal model

A causal model is a conceptual model that describes the causal mechanisms of a system. Causal models are mathematical models representing causal relationships within an individual system or population, and they facilitate inferences about causal relationships from statistical data. They allow questions about cause and effect to be answered using formal rules rather than intuition alone.

Types

Structural causal models

A structural causal model, developed largely by Judea Pearl, describes a system by a set of variables, structural equations expressing each variable as a function of its direct causes, and an associated causal diagram (a directed acyclic graph). Interventions are represented by Pearl's do-operator, which fixes a variable to a value and severs its incoming causal arrows.

Potential outcomes framework

The potential outcomes (Rubin causal model) framework, originating with Jerzy Neyman and formalized by Donald Rubin, defines a causal effect by comparing the outcomes a unit would exhibit under different treatments, only one of which is observed.

History

The earliest formal causal models were the path diagrams of geneticist Sewall Wright in the 1920s, later developed into structural equation modelling in economics and the social sciences. Interest revived in the 1990s and 2000s with work on causal graphs, Bayesian networks, and counterfactual reasoning.

Applications

Causal models are used in epidemiology, policy evaluation, econometrics, artificial intelligence, and the philosophy of science, where they underpin discussions of causality, counterfactuals, and mechanisms.
