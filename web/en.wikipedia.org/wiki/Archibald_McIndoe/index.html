Sir Archibald Hector McIndoe (4 May 1900 – 11 April 1960) was a New Zealand plastic surgeon who worked in Britain during the Second World War. He pioneered the treatment and rehabilitation of badly burned Royal Air Force airmen at the Queen Victoria Hospital, East Grinstead, Sussex, whose patients formed themselves into the Guinea Pig Club in recognition of the experimental nature of his reconstructive techniques.

Early life and career

McIndoe was born in Dunedin, New Zealand, studied medicine at the University of Otago, and went on to a fellowship at the Mayo Clinic in the United States. Moving to London in 1930, he worked with his cousin, the pioneering plastic surgeon Harold Gillies, and built a successful private practice before joining the RAF as a consultant in plastic surgery.

The burns unit at East Grinstead

At the outbreak of war in 1939 McIndoe took charge of a unit at the Queen Victoria Hospital, which became the principal centre for treating aircrew with severe burns, particularly to the face and hands, sustained when their aircraft caught fire. Recognizing that standard treatments such as tannic acid coagulation damaged healing tissue, he championed saline baths, gentle dressings, and staged reconstructive surgery using pedicled skin flaps — refining Gillies's tubed pedicle technique — together with new methods of eyelid and nasal reconstruction.

Rehabilitation

McIndoe understood that surgical repair alone would not restore men whose faces were disfigured. He insisted his patients wear their uniforms rather than hospital dress, allowed beer on the wards, and encouraged the town of East Grinstead to accept the men socially — it became known as "the town that did not stare". The Guinea Pig Club, founded by the patients in 1941 with McIndoe as president, provided mutual support during treatment and for decades afterwards. More than 600 airmen were treated by his unit.

Later life and honours

McIndoe was knighted in 1947 and served on the council of the Royal College of Surgeons, campaigning for plastic surgery's recognition as a specialty. He died in 1960, and his work is commemorated at East Grinstead, where the McIndoe Surgical Centre and a statue honour him and his patients.
