War elephant

A war elephant is an elephant trained and guided by humans for combat. War elephants were used in the ancient world, particularly in India, and later across South and Southeast Asia, the Mediterranean, and parts of Africa, from roughly the 4th century BC until the age of gunpowder. A rider called a mahout directed the animal, and elephants often carried a wooden tower (howdah) with archers or spearmen, or were armoured with metal plates.

History

Elephants were first tamed for war in India, where armies of the Mahajanapadas fielded them in large numbers. Western armies encountered them at the Battle of Gaugamela (331 BC) and, most famously, in the wars of Pyrrhus and of Hannibal, who led elephants over the Alps against Rome in 218 BC. At the Battle of Zama (202 BC), Scipio Africanus neutralised Hannibal's elephants with lanes in his formation and noise. Hellenistic kingdoms, especially the Seleucids and Ptolemies, maintained large elephant corps; the Seleucids obtained animals from India under a treaty with the Mauryan Empire.

In Asia, war elephants remained central to warfare for two millennia — used by Khmer, Burmese, Thai, Vietnamese, and Mughal armies — as command platforms, battering forces against gates, and shock weapons. Their effectiveness declined with disciplined infantry tactics and, decisively, with cannon and muskets, against which they became large targets; panicked elephants could trample their own side, a recurring weakness noted since antiquity. Elephants continued in military use as transport and engineering animals into the Second World War.
