William Harrison Ainsworth (4 February 1805 – 3 January 1882) was an English historical novelist. One of the most popular British novelists of the early Victorian period, he was for a time a rival in sales to Charles Dickens, and is associated with the "Newgate novels" that romanticised the lives of criminals.

Career

Ainsworth's breakthrough came with Rookwood (1834), a romantic tale of the highwayman Dick Turpin whose depiction of Turpin's ride to York entered popular mythology. Jack Sheppard (1839), serialised in Bentley's Miscellany alongside Dickens's Oliver Twist, was a sensation — and a scandal, blamed for glamorising crime and adapted repeatedly for the stage.

Historical novels

Ainsworth turned to more respectable historical subjects with The Tower of London (1840), Old St Paul's (1841), set during the Great Fire of London, and Windsor Castle (1842). He wrote some forty novels in total, edited magazines including Ainsworth's Magazine, and remained a prolific, if declining, literary presence until his death.

Legacy

Though his reputation faded after his death, Ainsworth's early works are credited with popularising the historical romance and shaping the legend of Dick Turpin.
