A trust is a legal relationship in which property (the trust fund) is held by one party, the trustee, for the benefit of another, the beneficiary, under terms set by the person who creates the trust, the settlor (or grantor/testator). The trustee holds legal title and owes fiduciary duties to manage the property solely in the beneficiaries' interests, while the beneficiaries hold equitable (beneficial) ownership. The trust is one of the most characteristic creations of English equity and a cornerstone of common-law property and succession planning.

Creation and requirements

A valid express trust generally requires certainty of intention, subject matter, and objects (the "three certainties"), a properly constituted transfer of property to the trustee, and compliance with any formalities—for example, trusts of land usually must be in writing. Trusts may be created during life (inter vivos) or by will (testamentary trusts). A valid trust must also satisfy rules against perpetuities and, in most contexts, have identifiable beneficiaries or a charitable purpose.

Types of trust

• Private express trusts: fixed trusts (set shares) and discretionary trusts (trustee chooses among a class).
• Charitable trusts: for purposes recognised as charitable; enforceable by the state and exempt from some rules.
• Resulting trusts: presumed or arising where property returns to the transferor.
• Constructive trusts: imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment or wrongdoing.
• Other common forms include bare trusts, protective trusts, unit trusts, REITs, and pension trusts.

Duties and uses

Trustees must act prudently, loyally, and impartially, keep accounts, and follow the trust instrument and statutes such as trustee investment legislation. Trusts are used for estate planning and avoiding probate, protecting vulnerable beneficiaries, holding business and investment assets, pension provision, charitable giving, and tax planning. Civil-law jurisdictions lack the native trust but often recognise foreign trusts under the Hague Trusts Convention (1985); analogous institutions include the German Treuhand and the French fiducie.
