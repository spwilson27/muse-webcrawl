Diyarbakır (Kurdish: Amed) is the largest Kurdish-majority city in Turkey and the capital of Diyarbakır Province. Situated on the banks of the Tigris River on a basalt plateau in southeastern Anatolia, it is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world and is renowned for its massive black basalt city walls, second in extent only to the Great Wall of China among surviving fortifications.

History

Known in antiquity as Amida, the city was contested by Romans and Persians, besieged famously in 359 CE as described by Ammianus Marcellinus, and later ruled by Arab, Turkmen, Mongol and Safavid powers before becoming part of the Ottoman Empire in the 16th century. Its name derives from the Arabic Diyār Bakr, "land of the Bakr tribe." The Diyarbakır Fortress and Hevsel Gardens Cultural Landscape was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015.

Demographics and culture

The city has a predominantly Kurdish population and is a cultural and political center for Kurds in Turkey. Historic landmarks include the Great Mosque of Diyarbakır (Ulu Cami), among the oldest in Anatolia, the ancient city walls with their inscribed towers, and the On Gözlü Köprü ("Ten-Eyed Bridge") over the Tigris. The Sur district, the old city within the walls, suffered heavy destruction during clashes in 2015–16.
