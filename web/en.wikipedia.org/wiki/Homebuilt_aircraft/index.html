Homebuilt aircraft

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homebuilt aircraft, also known as amateur-built aircraft or kit planes, are aircraft built by individuals for whom this is an avocation, rather than for commercial sale. Most are constructed from kits manufactured by companies such as Van's Aircraft, Zenith Aircraft and Glasair Aviation, while some are built from published plans or from the builder's own design.

Regulation

In the United States, amateur-built aircraft are certificated by the Federal Aviation Administration in the "experimental" category. The builder must fabricate and assemble the major portion of the aircraft — the so-called "51 per cent rule" — for education or recreation. The aircraft carries a special airworthiness certificate with operating limitations, and may not be used for commercial purposes, although it may be flown for personal transport and, with restrictions, for hire-related uses such as flight instruction in some jurisdictions. Other countries have similar schemes administered by national authorities or delegated organisations; in the United Kingdom, the Light Aircraft Association oversees most homebuilt types under a Permit to Fly.

Construction

Kits range from bundles of raw materials with plans to quick-build kits with pre-assembled major components. Common construction methods include aluminium monocoque (as in the Van's RV series), steel tube and fabric, wood, and moulded composites. Build times typically run from several hundred to several thousand hours. Support for builders is provided by organisations such as the Experimental Aircraft Association (EAA), whose annual AirVenture fly-in at Oshkosh is the movement's largest gathering.

Popular types

The Van's RV series are the most numerous homebuilts, with more than ten thousand completed. Other popular designs include the Lancair and Glasair composite aircraft, the Rutan Long-EZ, Kitfox and Zenith STOL designs, and the RotorWay kit helicopter.

Safety

Accident rates for amateur-built aircraft have historically been higher than for certified production aircraft, reflecting the variability of construction quality, unique systems, and the risks of flight testing; rates have improved with builder education, transition training and standardised flight-test programmes.
