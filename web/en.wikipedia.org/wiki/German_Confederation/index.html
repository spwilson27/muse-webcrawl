The German Confederation (German: Deutscher Bund) was an association of 39 predominantly German-speaking sovereign states in Central Europe, created by the Congress of Vienna in 1815 to replace the former Holy Roman Empire, which had been dissolved in 1806. It lasted until 1866, when it was destroyed by the rivalry between its two dominant members, Austria and Prussia.

Structure

The Confederation was a loose league of sovereign princes and free cities rather than a federal state. Its only central organ was the Federal Assembly (Bundestag) at Frankfurt am Main, a permanent congress of ambassadors presided over by Austria. Only parts of the Austrian Empire and of Prussia belonged to the Confederation; other members included the kingdoms of Bavaria, Saxony, Hanover and Württemberg, the electorate of Hesse, numerous duchies and principalities, and the four free cities of Frankfurt, Hamburg, Bremen and Lübeck.

History

Conservative statesmen, above all Austria's Klemens von Metternich, designed the Bund partly as an instrument of order against liberal and national movements, a role codified in the repressive Carlsbad Decrees of 1819. At the same time, economic integration proceeded within it through the Prussian-led Zollverein customs union (from 1834), which excluded Austria.

In the revolutions of 1848 the Bundestag was displaced by the elected Frankfurt Parliament, which drafted a constitution for a united Germany; but the old order was restored by 1850 after Austria forced Prussia to back down in the Punctation of Olmütz. Thereafter Austro-Prussian "dualism" paralysed the Confederation. When the two powers quarrelled over the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein, war followed in 1866. Prussia's victory in the Austro-Prussian War led to the dissolution of the Confederation; Prussia annexed several of its northern members and in 1867 organised the remaining northern states into the North German Confederation, the direct precursor of the German Empire of 1871.
