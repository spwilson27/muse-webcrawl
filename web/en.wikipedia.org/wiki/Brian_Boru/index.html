Brian Boru (Middle Irish: Brian Bóruma mac Cennétig; c. 941 – 23 April 1014) was an Irish king who ended the long domination of the High Kingship of Ireland by the Uí Néill dynasty. Brian built on the achievements of his father, Cennétig mac Lorcáin, and brother, Mathgamain, ruling first as King of Munster and then, from 1002, as High King of Ireland. He is best remembered for his victory at the Battle of Clontarf in 1014, at which he was killed. His byname Bóruma is traditionally linked to the cattle tribute (bórama) exacted from subject kingdoms, or to the fort of Beal Boru near his birthplace at Killaloe.

Rise to power

Brian belonged to the Dál gCais, a rising kindred of Thomond (north Munster). After his brother Mathgamain seized the kingship of Munster and was killed in 976, Brian defeated the Norse of Limerick at the Battle of Sulcoit and took the kingship in 978, defeating and killing the rival King of Munster, Máel Muad. He then campaigned against Leinster and Connacht, and in a series of expeditions (999–1002) subdued Máel Sechnaill mac Domnaill, the Uí Néill High King, who formally acknowledged Brian's supremacy in 1002.

High kingship

As High King, Brian exercised an authority over the whole island unmatched by any predecessor: he toured Ireland receiving hostages and tribute, rebuilt churches and monasteries, restored learning and law, and was styled in the Book of Armagh imperator Scottorum — "emperor of the Irish". His court propagated his deeds in the propaganda tract Cogadh Gaedhel re Gallaibh ("The War of the Irish with the Foreigners").

Clontarf and death

In 1013 the King of Leinster, Máel Mórda, and the Norse king of Dublin, Sitric Silkbeard, rebelled, drawing in allies from the Hebrides and Orkney. Brian's army met them at Clontarf, outside Dublin, on Good Friday, 23 April 1014. The battle was won by Brian's forces, but Brian — too old to fight, and praying in his tent — was killed, according to tradition by the fleeing Viking warrior Brodir. His victory is often popularly described as ending Viking power in Ireland, though historians note that Norse towns remained and that the battle was chiefly about supremacy within Ireland. After his death the High Kingship reverted to the Uí Néill, and Ireland returned to competing provincial kingships.
