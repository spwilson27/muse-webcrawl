Coroza

The coroza was a tall, conical cap of pasteboard or stiffened cloth worn by persons condemned by the Spanish and Portuguese Inquisitions, most conspicuously by penitents and heretics paraded in the public ceremony of the auto-da-fé. Together with the sambenito, the penitential garment with which it was usually paired, the coroza was intended to mark the wearer publicly with shame and to announce the nature of the offence of which they had been convicted.

Description and symbolism

The coroza was typically a pointed cone, sometimes around a metre high, made of cardboard or canvas, and painted with devices indicating the sentence: flames rising upward for those condemned to be burned, flames reversed (pointing downward, the so-called fuego revolto) for those who had repented and would be garrotted before burning or otherwise spared the fire, and crosses or other symbols for lesser penitents. Inscriptions or caricatures of the condemned might also be painted on the cap and on the accompanying sambenito.

Those reconciled to the Church in effigy, who had died or fled, were represented in procession by effigies wearing corozas, carried alongside the condemned. After an auto-da-fé, the sambenitos of the penitent — and in some places painted coroza depictions — were hung in churches as perpetual memorials of infamy, a practice that extended disgrace to the families of the condemned for generations.

Use in the autos-da-fé

Autos-da-fé were held in Spain from the late fifteenth century and in Portugal and the Spanish American and Asian colonies — including tribunals at Lima, Mexico City, Cartagena, and Goa — into the eighteenth century. The procession order, the green cross, the ranks of familiares, and the condemned in their sambenitos and corozas formed one of the most theatrical spectacles of the early modern Catholic world, frequently depicted in prints and in the famous painting Auto-da-fé in the Plaza Mayor of Madrid (1683) by Francisco Rizi. The coroza's shape, resembling a bishop's mitre turned to infamy, was an intentional blasphemous parody for those condemned for Judaizing and other heresies.

Legacy

Surviving corozas are rare, since they were cheaply made and perishable; examples and images are held in museums of the Inquisition in Lima and elsewhere. The word has passed into Spanish as a synonym for humiliation, and the cap is a recurring visual emblem of the Inquisition in literature and film.
