Faiyum mummy portraits

Mummy portraits or Fayum mummy portraits are a type of naturalistic painted portrait on wooden boards attached to upper-class mummies from Roman Egypt. They belong to the tradition of panel painting, one of the most highly regarded forms of art in the ancient classical world. About 900 portraits have survived, mostly found in the Faiyum Basin, particularly at Hawara, er-Rubayat and Antinoopolis; the exceptionally dry climate preserved the wood and pigment.

Technique and style

The portraits date from the 1st century BC to the mid-3rd century AD. They were painted in two techniques: encaustic (pigment suspended in hot wax), which gives rich, luminous colour, and tempera, yielding a flatter, more matte effect. The sitters — men, women and children, often named in Greek inscriptions — are depicted frontally, dressed in Roman fashion with jewellery and hairstyles that help date the panels. The portraits were bound into the mummy wrappings over the face of the deceased, combining Egyptian funerary practice with Roman portraiture.

Significance

Long prized as the finest surviving examples of ancient panel painting, the portraits are now held by museums worldwide, including the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, the Louvre, the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the British Museum. They offer an unusually vivid record of the multiethnic society of Roman Egypt — Egyptians, Greeks and Romans — and of ancient painting technique, otherwise almost entirely lost.
