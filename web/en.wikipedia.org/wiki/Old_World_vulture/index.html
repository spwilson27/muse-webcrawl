Old World vultures are vultures found in Europe, Asia, and Africa, belonging to the family Accipitridae, which also includes eagles, buzzards, kites, and hawks. They are not closely related to the superficially similar New World vultures of the Americas; the resemblance between the two groups is a famous example of convergent evolution driven by a shared scavenging lifestyle.

Characteristics

Unlike New World vultures, which locate carrion largely by smell, Old World vultures find food almost entirely by eyesight, watching the ground and each other from soaring flight. They have strong hooked beaks for tearing flesh but relatively weak feet, poorly suited to carrying prey. Many species have bare or sparsely feathered heads, an adaptation that keeps the plumage cleaner when feeding inside carcasses. Their stomach acid is highly corrosive, allowing them to feed safely on decaying meat that carries anthrax, botulism, and other pathogens.

Species

The group comprises about sixteen species, including the griffon vulture (Gyps fulvus), the cinereous vulture (Aegypius monachus), the Egyptian vulture (Neophron percnopterus), the bearded vulture or lammergeier (Gypaetus barbatus), the lappet-faced vulture (Torgos tracheliotos), and the critically endangered white-rumped vulture (Gyps bengalensis) of South Asia.

Conservation

Many Old World vultures have suffered catastrophic declines. In South Asia, populations of Gyps vultures fell by more than 95 percent beginning in the 1990s, a collapse traced to the veterinary drug diclofenac, which causes kidney failure in vultures that feed on treated livestock carcasses. Veterinary diclofenac has since been banned in several countries. African vultures face poisoning, poaching for traditional medicine, and collisions with power lines and wind turbines. Conservation programmes include captive breeding, "vulture restaurants" providing safe carcasses, and anti-poisoning campaigns.
