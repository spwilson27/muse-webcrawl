Tidal stream generator

A tidal stream generator, often called a tidal energy converter, is a machine that extracts energy from moving masses of water, in particular tides, although the term is often used in reference to machines designed to extract energy from the run of a river or from tidal currents. Certain types of these machines function very much like underwater wind turbines and are thus often referred to as tidal turbines.

Operation

Tidal stream generators draw energy from the kinetic energy of tidal currents, which are highly predictable, following the gravitational cycles of the moon and sun. Because seawater is about 800 times denser than air, tidal turbines can be much smaller than wind turbines for the same power output, though they must withstand much greater forces. Devices are typically installed in fast-flowing channels, straits and around headlands where tidal flow is concentrated.

Types

Designs include horizontal-axis turbines resembling wind turbines; vertical-axis turbines; oscillating hydrofoils that flap with the flow; Venturi-effect devices that funnel water through a turbine; and Archimedes screws and tidal kites. Most grid-scale development has focused on horizontal-axis turbines mounted on the seabed.

Projects

The first commercial-scale tidal turbine, SeaGen, a 1.2 MW twin-rotor device, operated in Strangford Lough, Northern Ireland, from 2008 to 2019. The MeyGen project in the Pentland Firth, Scotland, is among the largest tidal stream arrays, exporting power from multiple 1.5 MW turbines. Other test sites include the European Marine Energy Centre in Orkney and the Bay of Fundy in Canada, where currents are among the strongest in the world. Environmental concerns include the risk of collision for marine animals and effects on sediment movement, which monitoring at early sites has sought to quantify.
