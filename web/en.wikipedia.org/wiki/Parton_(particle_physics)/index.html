Parton (particle physics)

In deep inelastic scattering, a lepton scatters from a single parton within the proton.

In particle physics, the parton model is a model of hadrons, such as the proton and neutron, proposed by Richard Feynman in 1969. It describes a hadron as composed of point-like constituents, the partons, and allows high-energy collisions to be analysed as the scattering of a probe from a single, effectively free parton.

History

The model was motivated by deep inelastic scattering experiments at SLAC in the late 1960s, which showed that electrons scatter from point-like objects inside the proton. The partons were soon identified with the quarks of Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig, together with gluons. James Bjorken's prediction of scaling in the structure functions, and its approximate confirmation, was a key early success.

Framework

In the infinite-momentum frame, time dilation slows the internal interactions of the hadron, so during a brief high-energy collision each parton — carrying a fraction x of the hadron's momentum — behaves as if free. Cross-sections factorise into hard scattering cross-sections for individual partons and parton distribution functions (PDFs), which give the probability of finding a parton of a given type with momentum fraction x at a given energy scale.

Parton distribution functions

PDFs cannot yet be computed from first principles in quantum chromodynamics and are instead extracted from global fits to experimental data, but their evolution with energy scale is predicted by the DGLAP equations. Precise PDFs are essential for predicting production rates at hadron colliders such as the Large Hadron Collider.
