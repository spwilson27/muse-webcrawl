Wuffingas

The Wuffingas, Uffingas or Wuffings were the ruling dynasty of the Kingdom of East Anglia, an Anglo-Saxon kingdom in early medieval England, during the 6th, 7th and 8th centuries. The dynasty took its name from Wuffa, described in genealogies as the grandfather of Rædwald, the most powerful of the Wuffing kings. The Wuffingas claimed descent from the god Woden.

Origins

According to the genealogies preserved in the Anglian collection and by the historian Nennius, the Wuffing line descended from Wuffa, son of Wehha. Bede, in his Ecclesiastical History of the English People, names Tytila as Wuffa's son and Rædwald's father. The dynasty's heartland lay in the area of the River Deben in south-east Suffolk, around Rendlesham and the royal burial ground at Sutton Hoo, where the famous ship burial of about 625 — widely associated with Rædwald — was excavated in 1939.

Principal kings

  • Rædwald (reigned c. 599 – c. 624/625): the first king of the East Angles to exercise overlordship over other English kingdoms; Bede lists him as a bretwalda. He was baptised in Kent but famously maintained altars to both Christ and the old gods. His victory at the Battle of the River Idle (616/617) made Edwin king of Northumbria.
  • Eorpwald: Rædwald's son, converted to Christianity but murdered by a pagan, Ricberht, who ruled for about three years.
  • Sigeberht (c. 630 – c. 634): restored Christianity to East Anglia, founded a school, and later abdicated to a monastery; he was dragged from it to lead the army against Penda of Mercia and killed.
  • Anna (c. 636 – 654): a devout king whose family produced several saints, including his daughter Æthelthryth of Ely; killed fighting Penda.
  • Æthelhere, Æthelwald, Ealdwulf and later kings ruled as East Anglia came increasingly under Mercian dominance.

Christianity and culture

The Wuffing court was a conduit for Christianity into eastern England: Sigeberht admitted the Burgundian bishop Felix, and Anna's family founded monasteries at Cnobheresburg and Ely. The dynasty's wealth and continental connections are displayed in the Sutton Hoo treasures, which include Byzantine silver, Merovingian coins and Swedish-style regalia, suggesting the Wuffingas' possible dynastic links with Scandinavia.

Decline

After the death of Ealdwulf in 713, the succession becomes obscure; the Wuffing line may have continued in attenuated form, but East Anglia fell under the control of Mercia and then, in the 9th century, was conquered by the Danes, its last king, Edmund, being killed in 869 and venerated as Saint Edmund the Martyr.
