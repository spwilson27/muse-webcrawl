Avermectin

The avermectins are a series of drugs and pesticides used to treat parasitic worms and insect pests. They are a class of macrocyclic lactone derivatives with potent anthelmintic and insecticidal properties, naturally occurring as fermentation products of the soil bacterium Streptomyces avermitilis.

Discovery

Streptomyces avermitilis was isolated from a soil sample collected in Japan in the 1970s by a team led by Satoshi Ōmura at the Kitasato Institute. The sample was sent to Merck laboratories, where William C. Campbell's group demonstrated the compounds' extraordinary activity against parasites. Ōmura and Campbell shared half of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their work on avermectin-derived therapy.

Mechanism and derivatives

Avermectins bind glutamate-gated chloride channels in invertebrate nerve and muscle cells, causing paralysis and death of the parasite; these channels are absent in vertebrates, contributing to the drugs' safety margin. The best-known derivative is ivermectin, a semisynthetic mixture used worldwide against river blindness (onchocerciasis), strongyloidiasis and other helminth infections, and extensively in veterinary medicine. Other members include abamectin and emamectin (used in agriculture), doramectin, eprinomectin and selamectin.
