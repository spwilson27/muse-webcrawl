An acid is a substance that produces hydrogen ions (protons) in aqueous solution, or more generally donates protons (Brønsted–Lowry definition) or accepts electron pairs (Lewis definition). Acids taste sour, turn litmus red, react with bases to form salt and water, and react with many metals and carbonates.

Common acids include hydrochloric acid (in stomach juice), sulfuric acid (industrial workhorse), citric acid (in citrus fruit), acetic acid (vinegar) and carbonic acid (in rain and fizzy drinks). Acid strength — how completely the acid ionises — varies enormously and is measured on the pH scale.

Definitions

Three definitions dominate teaching: Arrhenius (1880s): an acid releases H⁺ in water; Brønsted–Lowry (1923): an acid is a proton donor; Lewis (1923): an acid accepts an electron pair. Each wider definition covers the earlier one's examples and more.

Everyday and industrial acids

Sulfuric acid is made in greater quantity than any other chemical, for fertilisers, refining and batteries. Acid rain — dilute sulfuric and nitric acids from polluted air — damages buildings, lakes and forests.
