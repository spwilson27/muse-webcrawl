Nitrogen narcosis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A diver at depth on compressed air

Nitrogen narcosis (also called inert gas narcosis, the rapture of the deep, or the Martini effect) is a reversible alteration in consciousness that occurs while diving at depth with breathing gases containing nitrogen. It produces a state similar to alcohol intoxication or nitrous oxide inhalation and can happen during shallow dives, but usually becomes noticeable at depths beyond about 30 metres (100 ft).

Causes and mechanism

Narcosis results from the increased partial pressure of nitrogen breathed under pressure, following Henry's law: more gas dissolves in the body's tissues, including the lipids of nerve membranes. In line with the Meyer–Overton hypothesis, the narcotic potency of gases correlates with their lipid solubility; nitrogen is mildly soluble and thus mildly narcotic, whereas helium is essentially non-narcotic, which is why deep commercial and technical divers use helium mixtures such as heliox and trimix. Other gases, including oxygen and carbon dioxide, also have narcotic effects at pressure.

Signs and symptoms

Effects progress with depth: mild euphoria and overconfidence, impaired judgement, reasoning, and short-term memory, slowed reactions, fixation on a single task, and poor decisions; at greater depths, confusion, hallucinations, terror or mania, and eventually unconsciousness and death. A divers' rule of thumb — the "Martini effect" — likens each 15 m (50 ft) of depth to drinking one martini on an empty stomach. Jacques Cousteau popularized the phrase "rapture of the deep" (l'ivresse des grandes profondeurs). Individual susceptibility varies widely and from day to day in the same diver; cold, fatigue, exertion, alcohol, and carbon dioxide retention worsen the effect.

Management

Narcosis itself is not directly harmful and there is no tolerance or after-effect: symptoms clear within minutes of ascending to a shallower depth, because the cause is the ambient pressure. The danger lies in the impaired behaviour it provokes — divers have removed regulators, ignored air supplies, or descended beyond planned limits. Training agencies therefore set maximum depths for air diving (commonly 30–40 m for recreational divers) and teach recognition of symptoms and immediate ascent as the response.
