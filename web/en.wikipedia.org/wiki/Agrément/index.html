Agrément

[Image: A newly appointed ambassador presenting credentials to a head of state, the final step after agrément]

Agrément (from French, "approval" or "consent") is the formal agreement by a receiving state to accept a named individual as the diplomatic representative — normally ambassador or high commissioner — of a sending state. Under Article 4 of the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations (1961), the sending state must make certain that the agrément of the receiving state has been given for the person it proposes to accredit, and the receiving state is not obliged to give reasons for refusing it.

Procedure

The process is usually conducted confidentially before any public announcement. The sending state's foreign ministry transmits the nominee's name and curriculum vitae through diplomatic channels, and the receiving state investigates and responds, typically within a few weeks. Only after agrément is granted is the appointment announced, the ambassador issued with letters of credence, and travel arranged. Because enquiries are secret, a refusal can be handled quietly and the sending state can propose another candidate without public embarrassment.

Refusals and related concepts

Refusals of agrément are uncommon but occur for reasons including the nominee's past statements or activities, intelligence concerns, or wider political disputes. A related concept is persona non grata: under Article 9 of the Vienna Convention, a receiving state may at any time declare a member of a mission unacceptable, requiring the sending state to recall them — the mechanism used to expel diplomats. Consular officers are accredited by a different instrument, the exequatur. See also Diplomatic mission and Diplomatic rank.
