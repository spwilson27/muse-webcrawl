Transitive relation

In mathematics, a binary relation R on a set X is transitive if, for all elements a, b, c in X, whenever R relates a to b and b to c, then R also relates a to c. In mathematical notation:

∀ a, b, c ∈ X: (aRb ∧ bRc) ⇒ aRc

Transitivity is a key property of both partial orders and equivalence relations.

Examples

  • "Is greater than", "is at least as great as", and "equals" are transitive relations on the real numbers: if a > b and b > c then a > c.
  • "Is a subset of" is transitive on sets.
  • "Is an ancestor of" is transitive.
  • "Divides" is transitive on the integers.

Non-examples

Not all relations are transitive. "Is the father of" is not transitive: if A is the father of B and B is the father of C, A is not the father of C. "Beats" in rock-paper-scissors is also non-transitive. A relation that is not transitive need not be intransitive (where aRc never holds when aRb and bRc): a relation may simply fail transitivity in some cases.

Related properties

Combining transitivity with other properties defines important classes of relations: a transitive, reflexive relation is a preorder; adding antisymmetry gives a partial order; a transitive, reflexive, symmetric relation is an equivalence relation. The transitive closure of a relation is the smallest transitive relation containing it.

Counting transitive relations

There is no simple closed formula for the number of transitive relations on a set with n elements; values are catalogued in the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences.
