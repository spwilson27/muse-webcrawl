Asexual reproduction is a type of reproduction that does not involve the fusion of gametes or a change in the number of chromosomes. The offspring that arise by asexual reproduction from either a single cell or a multicellular organism inherit the genome of their single parent; they are, except for rare mutations, genetically identical clones. Asexual reproduction is the primary form of reproduction for single-celled organisms such as archaea and bacteria.

Types

Common forms include binary fission, in which a cell divides into two; budding, in which a new organism grows out of the parent's body, as in hydra and yeast; fragmentation, in which an organism splits into pieces that each grow into a new individual; vegetative propagation in plants, by runners, bulbs, tubers and rhizomes; sporogenesis, the formation of spores; and parthenogenesis, in which an embryo develops from an unfertilised egg, as in some insects, reptiles and fish.

Advantages and disadvantages

Asexual reproduction allows a population to increase rapidly when conditions are favourable, requires no mate, and passes on 100% of a parent's genes. Its chief disadvantage is the lack of genetic variation: a genetically uniform population may be wiped out by a disease or environmental change to which all members are equally susceptible, and harmful mutations cannot be purged as readily as in sexual populations.

Alternation of generations

Many organisms, including plants, algae and fungi such as the Ascomycota, alternate between sexual and asexual phases in their life cycles, gaining the benefits of both strategies.
