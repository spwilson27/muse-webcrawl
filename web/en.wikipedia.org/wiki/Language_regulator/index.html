Language regulator

A language regulator is an institution—often an academy or a government body—that purports to set or influence standards for the usage of a language, including its spelling, grammar, vocabulary and pronunciation. The activity of such bodies is an instance of linguistic prescription, and their rulings may be advisory or legally binding, particularly in education, public administration and broadcasting.

Notably, English has no official regulator; usage is codified descriptively by dictionaries and style guides. Some countries regulate language mainly through laws on official use, such as France's Toubon Law, rather than through an academy.

Functions

Regulators commonly publish official dictionaries and grammars, approve or coin terms for new concepts (neologisms), standardize orthography, and rule on contested usages. Their effectiveness varies widely: official coinages sometimes displace loanwords, while in other cases they are ignored by the public. Critics argue that natural language change cannot be controlled by decree, whereas supporters point to the value of a stable standard for education, administration and interregional communication.

History

The earliest European language academies were founded in Italy and France in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: the Accademia della Crusca published its first dictionary in 1612, and Cardinal Richelieu founded the Académie Française in 1635. Spain followed with the Real Academia Española in 1713. Comparable institutions later spread to many other countries, especially in connection with nation-building and the standardization of national languages.
