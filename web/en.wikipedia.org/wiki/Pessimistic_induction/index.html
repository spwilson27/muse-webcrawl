Pessimistic induction

Field: Philosophy of science

Key proponent: Larry Laudan

Related: Scientific realism, no-miracles argument

The pessimistic induction (also called the pessimistic meta-induction) is an argument in the philosophy of science, most famously formulated by Larry Laudan in his 1981 paper "A Confutation of Convergent Realism". It challenges scientific realism — the view that our best scientific theories are approximately true and that their central terms genuinely refer.

The argument

Laudan assembled a list of once-successful scientific theories that were later rejected and whose central theoretical terms are now regarded as non-referring: the phlogiston theory of combustion, the caloric theory of heat, theories of the luminiferous aether, humoral medicine, and others. These theories were empirically successful in their day — they made correct predictions — yet by current standards they were false.

The pessimistic induction then proceeds as a meta-level induction over the history of science: since many past successful theories turned out to be false, we should expect, by induction, that our current successful theories are probably false too, and that their central terms (electrons, genes, curved spacetime) may not refer. Success, the argument concludes, is no reliable guide to truth.

Responses

Realists have replied in several ways. Selective realists argue that only the parts of past theories actually responsible for their successes were retained (e.g. Fresnel's equations survive inside Maxwell's theory), supporting a "divide and conquer" strategy. Structural realists hold that what is preserved across theory change is mathematical structure rather than ontology. Others dispute Laudan's list, arguing that some cited theories were not genuinely successful, or not genuinely rejected in the relevant respects.
