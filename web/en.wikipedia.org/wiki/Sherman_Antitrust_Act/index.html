Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890

Sherman Antitrust Act
EnactedJuly 2, 1890
Codified15 U.S.C. §§ 1–7
Named forSenator John Sherman of Ohio

The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 is a United States antitrust law which prescribes the rule of free competition among those engaged in commerce. It was the first federal statute to limit cartels and monopolies, and today it forms the core of antitrust policy in the United States.

Provisions

Section 1 declares illegal "every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States, or with foreign nations." Section 2 makes it a felony to monopolize, or attempt to monopolize, any part of interstate commerce. Violations can bring criminal fines and imprisonment as well as private treble-damage actions.

History

Passed with only one dissenting vote in the Senate, the Act was a response to the great industrial trusts of the Gilded Age, such as John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil. Early enforcement was weak and the Act was famously used against labour unions in cases such as Loewe v. Lawlor (the Danbury Hatters case, 1908). Its teeth were shown in Northern Securities Co. v. United States (1904) and the 1911 dissolutions of the Standard Oil and American Tobacco trusts, which also produced the "rule of reason" interpretation.

Later development

The Sherman Act was supplemented by the Clayton Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act, both of 1914. Landmark later cases include United States v. AT&T, which led to the 1984 breakup of the Bell System, and United States v. Microsoft Corp. (2001).
