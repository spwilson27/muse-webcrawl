Botflies, also known as warble flies, heel flies and gadflies, comprise the family Oestridae of flies whose larvae are obligate parasites of mammals, including livestock and, occasionally, humans. Adult botflies do not feed significantly and are short-lived; the parasitic larval stage is the biologically dominant phase of the life cycle.

Biology

Different subfamilies exploit different tissues. Horse botflies (Gasterophilus) attach eggs to the host's hair; the larvae are ingested during grooming and develop in the stomach. Cattle grubs (Hypoderma) migrate through the body and form swellings ("warbles") under the skin of the back. Sheep nasal botflies (Oestrus ovis) deposit live larvae in the nostrils, where they develop in the sinuses.

The human botfly, Dermatobia hominis of Central and South America, uses a remarkable strategy: the female glues her eggs to a mosquito or other blood-feeding fly, and when the carrier lands on a warm-blooded host the larvae hatch and burrow into the skin, producing a painful boil-like lesion (a form of myiasis).

Impact

Infestation of livestock reduces weight gain, milk yield and hide value, and heavy infestations can seriously debilitate animals. Human cases are mostly travel-associated and are treated by removing the larva, traditionally after suffocating it by occluding its breathing pore.

Control

Control in livestock relies on systemic insecticides timed to kill larvae early in their migration, avoiding damage caused by dying larvae near the spinal cord or oesophagus in cattle.
