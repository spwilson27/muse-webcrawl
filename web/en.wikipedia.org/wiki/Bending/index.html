Bending

In applied mechanics, bending (also known as flexure) characterises the behaviour of a slender structural element subjected to an external load applied perpendicularly to a longitudinal axis of the element. A structural element undergoing bending is termed a beam. When a beam bends, one side is stretched (tension) while the other is compressed, and along the neutral axis the material is neither stretched nor compressed.

Theory

The classical Euler–Bernoulli beam theory provides a means of calculating the load-carrying and deflection characteristics of beams. It assumes that cross-sections remain plane and perpendicular to the bent axis, giving a simple relationship between the bending moment M, the curvature of the beam, and the product EI of the elastic modulus and the second moment of area. The Timoshenko beam theory refines the model by including shear deformation and rotary inertia, which matters for short, deep beams.

For small deflections, the normal stress at a distance y from the neutral axis is proportional to y, with maximum stress at the outer fibres. If the stress exceeds the yield point the beam deforms plastically, forming a plastic hinge in the limit.

Applications

Bending analysis underlies the design of beams, slabs, springs, shafts, and aircraft wings. In metal forming, bending is also a manufacturing process: sheet metal is plastically deformed along a straight axis in press brakes to produce angles and channels, with the minimum bend radius governed by the material's ductility and the phenomenon of springback.
