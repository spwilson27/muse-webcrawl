The age of the Solar System is estimated at about 4.568 billion years. The figure is derived primarily from radiometric dating of meteorites, whose primitive compositions preserve a record of the earliest solids that condensed from the solar nebula, rather than from dating rocks of the Earth itself, whose surfaces have been reworked by geology.

Evidence

The oldest dated material in the Solar System is found in calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions (CAIs) within carbonaceous chondrite meteorites. Lead–lead isochron dating of CAIs gives ages of about 4.567–4.568 billion years, taken as the starting point of Solar System formation. Iron meteorites, fragments of the cores of early planetesimals, yield similar ages using the hafnium–tungsten chronometer, showing that planetary building blocks began accreting and differentiating within the first few million years.

The age of the Earth, famously estimated by Clair Cameron Patterson in 1956 from lead isotopes in meteorites and terrestrial samples, is about 4.54 billion years — slightly younger than the CAIs because Earth took tens of millions of years to accrete and was later reset in part by the Moon-forming impact. Lunar samples returned by the Apollo missions and models of nebular collapse are consistent with these figures.
