The Moscone–Milk assassinations were the killings of San Francisco Mayor George Moscone and Supervisor Harvey Milk, who were shot and killed in San Francisco City Hall by former Supervisor Dan White on November 27, 1978. White, a conservative former police officer and firefighter, had resigned his seat on the Board of Supervisors ten days earlier, then sought to be reappointed; when Moscone refused, influenced in part by Milk's opposition, White entered City Hall through a basement window to avoid metal detectors and murdered both men. The killings and the lenient verdict in White's trial profoundly affected San Francisco and the American gay rights movement.

Background

Dan White was elected to the Board of Supervisors in 1977 representing District 8, the same election in which Harvey Milk, one of the first openly gay elected officials in the United States, won in District 5. White and Milk clashed repeatedly — over a psychiatric facility for troubled youth in White's district, and over gay rights measures, though White had also supported some of Milk's initiatives. Citing financial strain, White resigned on November 10, 1978, then, after pressure from constituents and police interests, asked Mayor Moscone to reappoint him. Moscone initially agreed but reconsidered after lobbying by Milk and other liberal supervisors who wanted a more progressive appointee.

The murders

On the morning of November 27, 1978, Moscone planned to announce a different appointee. White entered City Hall through an open basement window, carrying his police revolver and extra ammunition, thereby bypassing the metal detectors. He first went to Moscone's office, argued with him, and then shot him four times, twice in the head. White then walked to his former office, reloaded, asked Milk to step inside, and shot him five times, including two shots to the head at close range. Board President Dianne Feinstein heard the shots, discovered Milk's body, and announced the deaths to stunned reporters — "As President of the Board of Supervisors, it's my duty to make this announcement: both Mayor Moscone and Supervisor Harvey Milk have been shot and killed." Feinstein, as board president, succeeded Moscone as acting mayor.

Trial and aftermath

White surrendered to police shortly afterward. At his 1979 trial, his attorneys argued diminished capacity, presenting psychiatric testimony and evidence of depression; the press dubbed part of the argument the "Twinkie defense," although junk food was offered only as a symptom of his decline, not its cause. The jury convicted White of voluntary manslaughter rather than murder, sentencing him to seven years and eight months in prison, of which he served about five. The verdict outraged San Francisco's gay community: on May 21, 1979, protests escalated into the White Night riots, in which City Hall was attacked and police cars burned; police retaliated with a raid on a gay bar in the Castro. White was released in 1984 and died by suicide in October 1985. Milk's assassination cemented his status as a martyr and icon of the gay rights movement; his life was later portrayed in Gus Van Sant's film Milk (2008).
