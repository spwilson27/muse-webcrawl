Cursus honorum

The cursus honorum (Latin for "course of honours") was the sequential order of public offices held by aspiring politicians in the Roman Republic and the early Roman Empire. It formalized the mixed military and political career expected of a citizen of senatorial rank, and was regulated by law — most importantly the lex Villia annalis (180 BC) and later reforms under Sulla — which set minimum ages and intervals between offices.

The offices

The ladder began, typically after ten years of military service as a military tribune, with the quaestorship (minimum age 30 under Sulla), which administered finances and conferred membership of the Senate. Next came the optional offices of aedile (responsible for public buildings, markets, and games) or, for plebeians, tribune of the plebs. The praetorship (minimum age 39) conferred imperium and judicial duties, and after serving, praetors usually governed a province as propraetor. The highest regular office was the consulship (minimum age 42), held by two men for a single year. Above even the consuls in prestige stood the censorship, elected every five years, which conducted the census and reviewed the roll of the Senate.

Significance

The fixed sequence restricted — but did not eliminate — the ambition of Rome's aristocracy. A politician who held offices suo anno ("in his own year", at the earliest legal age) was marked out as exceptionally successful; Cicero, a "new man" without consular ancestors, prided himself on having done so. Skipping or repeating steps, as in the extraordinary commands of Pompey or the repeated consulships of Marius, was a sign either of crisis or of dangerous personal power, and violations of the sequence figure prominently in accounts of the Republic's fall.
