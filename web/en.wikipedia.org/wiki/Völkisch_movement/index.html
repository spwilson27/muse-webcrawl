Völkisch movement

The völkisch movement (German: Völkische Bewegung) was a German nationalist movement active from the late 19th century through the Nazi era. Centred on the notion of Volk — a people understood in terms of shared blood, language, culture and soil — the movement combined ideas of ethnic nationalism, romanticism, racial theory and antisemitism, and it formed an important ideological seedbed from which National Socialism later drew.

The term derives from the German word Volk ("folk", "people"), and its adherents — often calling themselves Deutschvölkische ("German-folkish") — sought a supposedly pure German national community free of what they saw as alien influences, especially Jewish influence in finance, the press and the arts.

Origins

The movement grew out of 19th-century German romantic nationalism, the writings of figures such as Johann Gottlieb Fichte, and the broader European völkisch publishing boom. Publishers such as Eugen Diederichs promoted folk tales, neopaganism and Germanic mythology, while groups like the Germanenorden (founded 1912) and the Thule Society mixed occultism with racial politics. Antisemitic parties of the 1880s and 1890s, and the Pan-German League, gave the current a political organisational form.

Ideology

Key themes included the idealisation of the peasantry as the bearer of national virtue, the equation of "blood and soil" (Blut und Boden), hostility to urban modernity and liberal capitalism, and the belief in a hierarchy of races. Thinkers such as Paul de Lagarde, Julius Langbehn and Houston Stewart Chamberlain — author of The Foundations of the Nineteenth Century (1899) — gave the movement intellectual respectability in some circles.

Influence on Nazism

After the First World War the splintered völkisch parties and leagues provided members, symbols and ideas to the young Nazi Party; Adolf Hitler absorbed much of this milieu in Munich. Under the Third Reich many völkisch organisations were absorbed or dissolved, and after 1945 the term became inseparable from the crimes of the regime. Historians generally treat the movement as a crucial precondition for the rise of Nazism, while noting that it was never a single unified party.
