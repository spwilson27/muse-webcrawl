Northern Dobruja

Northern Dobruja (Romanian: Dobrogea de Nord) is the part of the historical region of Dobruja situated in Romania, between the lower Danube River and the Black Sea. Its counterpart, Southern Dobruja, lies in northeastern Bulgaria. Northern Dobruja comprises the modern Romanian counties of Constanța and Tulcea.

Geography

The region covers about 15,570 square kilometres. In the north lies the Danube Delta, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Europe's largest wetlands, while the interior is a low, dry plateau of loess-covered hills used largely for agriculture. The principal city and Black Sea port is Constanța, Romania's largest seaport; other towns include Tulcea, Medgidia, and Mangalia. The Danube–Black Sea Canal crosses the region, shortening shipping routes between the river and the sea.

History

Dobruja was part of the Roman and Byzantine worlds in antiquity — the Greek colonies of Histria, Tomis (modern Constanța), and Callatis were founded on its coast — and later a contested zone between the Bulgarian Empire, the Byzantine Empire, and the principality of Wallachia. It came under Ottoman rule in the fifteenth century. Following the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 and the Treaty of Berlin, Northern Dobruja was awarded to Romania, giving the young Romanian state its Black Sea coastline; Southern Dobruja went to Bulgaria. The division was adjusted in 1913, when Romania took the south as well, but the Treaty of Craiova in 1940 restored Southern Dobruja to Bulgaria, confirming the present frontier.

Population

Northern Dobruja is ethnically diverse. Alongside the Romanian majority, it is home to Turks, Tatars, Lipovan Old Believers in the delta, Greeks, Armenians, and others — a legacy of its position at the meeting point of empires and trade routes.
