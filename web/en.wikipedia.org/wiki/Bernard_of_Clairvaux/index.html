Bernard of Clairvaux

Bernard of Clairvaux (1090 – 20 August 1153), venerated as Saint Bernard, was a French abbot and a major leader in the revitalisation of Benedictine monasticism through the nascent Cistercian Order. Founding abbot of Clairvaux Abbey, he was the dominant spiritual and political voice of Latin Christendom in the first half of the 12th century, preaching the Second Crusade and shaping papal politics. He was canonised in 1174 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1830.

Life

Born into the Burgundian nobility, Bernard entered the austere new monastery of Cîteaux in 1112 or 1113, bringing with him a large group of companions and relatives. In 1115 he was sent to found Clairvaux, whose growth under his abbacy — to hundreds of monks and dozens of daughter houses — made the Cistercians the leading monastic order in Europe. Bernard intervened in the papal schism of 1130 on behalf of Innocent II, opposed the teachings of Peter Abelard, securing his condemnation at Sens in 1141, and wrote the rule's commendation for the Knights Templar (In Praise of the New Knighthood).

The Second Crusade

Commissioned by Pope Eugene III — himself a former monk of Clairvaux — Bernard preached the Second Crusade at Vézelay in 1146 with electrifying effect, recruiting King Louis VII of France and Emperor Conrad III. The crusade's failure in 1148 was a heavy blow, and Bernard's last years were shadowed by it; his On Consideration, addressed to Eugene, remains a classic of pastoral and papal reflection.

Writings and legacy

Bernard's sermons — especially the 86 Sermons on the Song of Songs — and treatises such as On Loving God made him the pre-eminent mystical theologian of his century, earning him the title "Mellifluous Doctor" for his eloquence. He is traditionally credited with adding the final invocation to the Salve Regina and with popularising affective devotion to the humanity of Christ and to the Virgin Mary.
