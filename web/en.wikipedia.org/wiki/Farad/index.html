Farad

Unit system: SI derived unit
Unit of: Capacitance
Symbol: F
Named after: Michael Faraday
In SI base units: kg−1·m−2·s4·A2

The farad (symbol: F) is the unit of electrical capacitance in the International System of Units (SI). It is named after the English physicist Michael Faraday. A capacitor has a capacitance of one farad when a charge of one coulomb changes its potential by one volt: 1 F = 1 C/V. Equivalently, the farad may be expressed in SI base units as s4·A2·m−2·kg−1.

Scale

The farad is a very large unit for practical purposes. Capacitors encountered in electronics typically range from picofarads (pF, 10−12 F) through nanofarads and microfarads (µF, 10−6 F) to millifarads. A one-farad capacitor charged to one volt stores half a joule of energy. Supercapacitors, with capacitances of hundreds or thousands of farads, are a notable exception used for energy storage. The capacitance of the Earth considered as an isolated sphere is only about 710 microfarads.

History

The term "farad" was first proposed by the engineers Latimer Clark and Charles Tilston Bright in honour of Faraday, initially (in 1861) for a unit of quantity of charge; by 1873 the British Association had adopted the farad as the unit of capacitance. In 1881 the International Congress of Electricians in Paris formally approved the name. The farad became an SI derived unit with a special name when the SI was established in 1960.

Related concepts

Capacitance is the ratio of the change in electric charge on a conductor to the change in its electric potential. The reciprocal of capacitance is elastance, occasionally measured in the informal unit "darafs". The farad should not be confused with the faraday, an older unit of charge equal to the charge of one mole of electrons (approximately 96,485 coulombs).
