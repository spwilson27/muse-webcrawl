Gifted education

Gifted education (also known as gifted and talented education, talented and gifted programs, or TAG) is a broad group of special practices, procedures, and theories used in the education of children who have been identified as gifted or talented.

The main approaches to gifted education are enrichment and acceleration. Enrichment broadens and deepens the curriculum for identified students without moving them ahead in the standard sequence, while acceleration allows gifted children to progress through material at a faster pace, including grade skipping, early entrance to school or university, and advanced placement courses.

Identification

Students are commonly identified through intelligence tests, achievement tests, teacher or parent nomination, and portfolios of work. Cut-off scores vary widely: many programs use an IQ score in the top 2–5 percent of the population. Critics argue that identification procedures can under-represent minority and low-income students, and researchers continue to debate the balance between test-based and holistic identification.

Forms of provision

Provision may take the form of pull-out programs, special classes, magnet schools, cluster grouping within regular classrooms, mentorships, and extracurricular competitions such as mathematics olympiads. Some jurisdictions mandate gifted services by law; others leave provision to local discretion. The Marland Report of 1972 gave an influential definition of giftedness in the United States, recognizing ability in academics, creativity, leadership, and the arts.
