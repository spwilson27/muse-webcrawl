Zoonosis

Also calledZoonotic disease
DefinitionInfectious disease transmissible from non-human animals to humans
ExamplesRabies, Ebola, avian influenza, Lyme disease, toxoplasmosis, COVID-19
Share of emerging diseasesRoughly 60–75% of emerging infectious diseases in humans

A zoonosis (plural zoonoses) is an infectious disease caused by a pathogen — a virus, bacterium, parasite or prion — that has jumped from a non-human animal to a human. Zoonotic transmission may occur through direct contact with animals, through food or water, or via vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks. The reverse process, human-to-animal transmission, is sometimes called reverse zoonosis or anthroponosis.

Transmission and reservoirs

Many zoonoses circulate silently in wildlife reservoir hosts such as bats, rodents and wild birds, spilling over into people through hunting, farming, deforestation, the wildlife trade or peri-domestic contact. Domesticated animals can act as intermediate hosts, as with pigs and poultry in influenza ecology. Once a pathogen adapts to sustained human-to-human transmission it may emerge as a new human disease, historically including measles, smallpox relatives and HIV/AIDS.

Public health significance

Zoonoses account for a large share of known and newly emerging human infections. Examples include rabies (almost invariably fatal once symptomatic), plague, anthrax, brucellosis, leptospirosis, Ebola and Marburg virus diseases, MERS, SARS and COVID-19, as well as foodborne infections such as salmonellosis and campylobacteriosis. Control strategies follow the "One Health" model, integrating human medicine, veterinary medicine and environmental science: animal vaccination, surveillance at the human–animal interface, food hygiene and vector control.

History

The domestication of animals during the Neolithic greatly expanded human exposure to animal pathogens, and many classical epidemic diseases are thought to have zoonotic origins. The term derives from the Greek zōon (animal) and nosos (disease); the 19th-century physician Rudolf Virchow is credited with popularising the concept.
