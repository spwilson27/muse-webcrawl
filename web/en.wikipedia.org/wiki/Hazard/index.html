Hazard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hazard is a source or situation with the potential to cause harm — including injury, ill health, damage to property or the environment, or a combination of these. In safety science a hazard is distinguished from risk: hazard describes the intrinsic potential for harm, while risk combines the likelihood that the harm will occur with its severity. The identification and control of hazards is the foundation of occupational safety and health, environmental protection, food safety and disaster management.

Classification

Physical hazards

Sources of energy that can cause harm: electricity, noise, vibration, radiation (ionising and non-ionising), extreme temperatures, pressure, heights and moving machinery.

Chemical hazards

Substances that are toxic, corrosive, flammable, explosive or otherwise harmful, including gases, vapours, dusts and fumes. The Globally Harmonized System (GHS) classifies chemical hazards and standardises labels and safety data sheets.

Biological hazards

Bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and other biological agents that can cause infection, allergy or toxicity — from moulds and bloodborne pathogens to pandemic viruses.

Ergonomic and psychosocial hazards

Poorly designed workstations, repetitive strain, heavy lifting, and organisational factors such as excessive workload, bullying and stress.

Natural hazards

Geophysical and weather-related events: earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, floods, hurricanes, wildfires, droughts and landslides. A natural hazard becomes a disaster when it intersects with human vulnerability and exposure.

Hazard identification and control

Standard practice proceeds through hazard identification, risk assessment, and the application of controls following the hierarchy of controls: elimination of the hazard, substitution with something less hazardous, engineering controls, administrative controls, and finally personal protective equipment as the last line of defence. Techniques such as job safety analysis, HAZOP studies and HACCP systematise the process in different industries.

Etymology

The word derives from Old French hasard, a game of dice, itself from Arabic az-zahr ("the dice"); from its gaming sense of chance it acquired the meaning of risk and danger. The separate concept of "moral hazard" in economics refers to the tendency of insured parties to take greater risks.
