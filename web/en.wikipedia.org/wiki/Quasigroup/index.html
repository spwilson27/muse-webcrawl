Quasigroup

The operation table of a quasigroup is a Latin square.

In mathematics, especially in abstract algebra, a quasigroup is an algebraic structure resembling a group in that "division" is always possible. Quasigroups differ from groups mainly in that the binary operation need not be associative, and — in the general definition — need not have an identity element. Formally, a quasigroup is a set Q with a binary operation ∗ such that for every a and b in Q there exist unique elements x and y with a ∗ x = b and y ∗ a = b. Equivalently, the equations a ∗ x = b and y ∗ a = b are always uniquely solvable ("Latin square property").

Loops

A quasigroup with an identity element is called a loop. Every group is an associative loop, but loops need not be associative: the best-known example is the set of nonzero octonions under multiplication. Certain loops, called Moufang loops, satisfy a weakened form of associativity and arise in projective geometry and physics.

Examples

  • Any group is a quasigroup, since a ∗ x = b implies x = a−1b.
  • The integers with subtraction form a quasigroup (not a loop).
  • Any Steiner triple system defines an idempotent, commutative quasigroup.
  • The multiplication table of a finite quasigroup is exactly a Latin square, and vice versa.

Applications

Quasigroups appear in statistics (experimental design via Latin squares), coding theory, cryptography, and the study of non-associative geometries. The theory developed in the 20th century through the work of Ruth Moufang and others on the connections between loops and projective planes.
