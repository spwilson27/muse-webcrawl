Daigo Fukuryū Maru (第五福龍丸, "Lucky Dragon No. 5") was a Japanese tuna fishing boat with a crew of 23 men that was contaminated by nuclear fallout from the United States Castle Bravo thermonuclear weapon test at Bikini Atoll on 1 March 1954.

The Castle Bravo incident

The Castle Bravo device yielded about 15 megatons, far more than expected, and wind carried fallout over the vessel, which was outside the officially warned danger zone. The crew suffered acute radiation syndrome after being exposed to radioactive ash for hours before returning to Yaizu, Japan, on 14 March. The radioman, Aikichi Kuboyama, died on 23 September 1954, becoming the first recognised fatality of a hydrogen bomb.

Consequences

The incident caused a diplomatic crisis between Japan and the United States, fears about contaminated tuna in Japanese markets, and a surge in the Japanese anti-nuclear movement. The United States paid compensation to the crew and their families. The episode is widely cited as part of the inspiration for the film Godzilla (1954).

Preservation

After years of neglect, the vessel was restored and since 1976 has been exhibited at the Daigo Fukuryū Maru Exhibition Hall in Tokyo.
