East Semitic languages

The East Semitic languages are one of the branches of the Semitic language family, spoken in ancient Mesopotamia from the third millennium BCE onward. The branch is defined by its divergence from the rest of Semitic and is known from cuneiform texts. All East Semitic languages are extinct.

Languages

The principal East Semitic language is Akkadian, the language of the Akkadian Empire and later of Babylonia and Assyria, attested from about 2500 BCE until its gradual displacement by Aramaic and final disappearance as a written language around the first century CE. Akkadian's two major dialects, Babylonian in the south and Assyrian in the north, are known from hundreds of thousands of clay tablets.

A second language, Eblaite, is attested in the royal archives of Ebla in Syria (c. 2400 BCE) and is sometimes classified as a dialect of Akkadian or as a sister language. Some scholars also assign other fragmentarily known languages of the region to the branch.

Characteristics

East Semitic differs from other Semitic branches in several innovations, including a distinctive set of verbal prefixes, a stative conjugation, and extensive grammatical influence from the unrelated Sumerian language, from which Akkadian borrowed vocabulary, the cuneiform writing system and certain word-order patterns. In contrast to the West Semitic languages, East Semitic lost some of the guttural consonants of Proto-Semitic.
