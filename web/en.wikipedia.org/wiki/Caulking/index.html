Caulking

Caulk or caulking is a material used to seal joints or seams against leakage in various structures and piping. The oldest form of caulk consisted of fibrous materials driven into the wedge-shaped seams between boards on wooden boats and ships. Cast iron sewerage pipe joints were formerly sealed with molten lead.

Modern caulks

Modern caulking compounds are flexible sealing materials used to close air- and water-leakage gaps in buildings and other structures. Common types include silicone, polyurethane, acrylic latex ("painter's caulk") and butyl rubber, supplied in tubes applied with a caulking gun or in squeeze tubes. Caulks differ in flexibility, adhesion, paintability and resistance to water and ultraviolet light, and are chosen according to the application — for example, silicone around baths and windows, and paintable acrylic along interior trim.

In shipbuilding

Traditional caulking on wooden vessels drove oakum (tarred hemp or jute fibre) into the seams between hull planks with a caulking iron and mallet, then sealed ("payed") the seam with pitch or putty. The process made the hull watertight while allowing the planks some movement. The term survives in the expression "caulking off", a sailor's nap, derived from sleeping on deck seams.

Energy efficiency

Sealing air leaks around windows, doors and penetrations with caulk is a standard low-cost measure for improving the energy efficiency of buildings, reducing draughts and heating and cooling costs.
