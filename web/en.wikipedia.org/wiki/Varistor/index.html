Varistor

A varistor (a portmanteau of varying resistor) is a surge-protecting electronic component with an electrical resistance that varies with the applied voltage. It has a nonlinear, non-ohmic current–voltage characteristic similar to that of a diode. Unlike a diode, however, it has the same characteristic for both directions of traversing current. Most varistors are metal-oxide varistors (MOVs), made of a ceramic mass of zinc oxide grains in a matrix of other metal oxides, forming a network of microscopic junctions.

A disc-type metal-oxide varistor used for surge protection.

Operation

At normal voltages the varistor presents a very high resistance and, connected in parallel with the protected circuit, draws negligible current. When a transient overvoltage — such as a lightning-induced surge or a switching spike — raises the voltage above the device's clamping level, its resistance collapses within nanoseconds and it shunts the surge current away, limiting the voltage seen by the load. Surge protectors, power strips and mains input stages commonly use MOVs, often combined with fuses and thermal protection because repeated or excessive surges degrade the ceramic and can cause failure.

History and types

Early varistors were based on silicon carbide and were used in telephone networks from the 1930s; zinc-oxide MOVs, developed in the 1970s, offered a much sharper clamping characteristic. Multilayer varistors provide low-voltage, surface-mount protection for data lines and circuit boards.
