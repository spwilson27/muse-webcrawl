Parachute

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Colourful round and ram-air parachutes descending against a blue sky]
Parachutes in descent.

A parachute is a device used to slow the motion of an object through the atmosphere by creating drag, usually made of light, strong fabric such as nylon formed into a canopy. Leonardo da Vinci sketched a pyramidal parachute in 1485; Louis-Sébastien Lenormand jumped from a tower with one in 1783, and André-Jacques Garnerin made the first parachute descent from a balloon in 1797. Modern parachutes range from emergency and military systems to ram-air \'wings\' used in skydiving and precision spacecraft recovery.

Development

Early canopies were rigid or semi-rigid; Garnerin\'s frameless silk canopy (with violent oscillations, later tamed by a vent) began modern design. World War I brought observation-balloon parachutes and late-war aircrew issues; World War II created mass airborne forces (paratroopers), airborne life-saving, and silk\'s strategic importance.

Types and uses

Round parachutes serve cargo and ejection seats (Martin-Baker systems have saved thousands of aircrew); ram-air parafoils give skydivers steerable, flareable landings for sport and special-forces insertion (HAHO/HALO). Spacecraft from Apollo to Crew Dragon and sample-return capsules rely on staged parachutes; whole-aircraft parachutes (Cirrus CAPS) save light planes.

Skydiving

Sport parachuting began post-1945 with surplus gear; modern skydivers exit at 10,000–14,000 ft, freefall near 190 km/h in a belly-to-earth position, and deploy around 2,500–3,000 ft, with automatic activation devices as backup. Wingsuits cut vertical speed while racing forward — the extreme edge of the sport.
