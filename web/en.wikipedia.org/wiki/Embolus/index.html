An embolus (plural emboli; from Greek ἔμβολος, "wedge" or "plug") is an unattached mass that travels through the bloodstream and is capable of clogging arterial capillary beds at a site distant from its point of origin. By contrast, a thrombus is a clot that forms and remains at its site of origin. The process of an embolus lodging in a vessel is called an embolism; if the embolus is a blood clot, it is a thromboembolism.

Types

  • Thromboembolus – a fragment of thrombus, most commonly from a deep-vein thrombosis of the leg travelling to the lungs (pulmonary embolism), or from the heart (e.g. in atrial fibrillation) travelling to the brain, limbs, or viscera.
  • Atheroembolus – cholesterol crystals and plaque debris, which may shower into small vessels after vascular instrumentation or spontaneously ("blue toe syndrome").
  • Fat embolus – globules of fat released after long-bone fractures or orthopaedic surgery, causing fat embolism syndrome with respiratory and neurological signs.
  • Air (gas) embolus – bubbles entering the circulation from surgery, trauma, central lines, or decompression sickness.
  • Amniotic fluid embolism – a rare, catastrophic complication of childbirth.
  • Septic embolus – infected material, as from endocarditis, seeding abscesses.
  • Tumour and foreign-body emboli – including material from intravenous procedures.

Clinical significance

The consequences of embolism depend on the vessel occluded and the tissue supplied: cerebral embolism causes ischaemic stroke; pulmonary embolism can cause hypoxaemia, right-heart failure, and death; limb embolism threatens tissue viability ("the six Ps": pain, pallor, pulselessness, paraesthesia, paralysis, perishing cold). Treatment includes anticoagulation, thrombolysis, surgical or catheter embolectomy, and management of the source. Prevention focuses on treating the origin — for example anticoagulation in atrial fibrillation and prophylaxis against venous thromboembolism after surgery.

History

The concept was developed in the 19th century, notably by Rudolf Virchow, who coined the terms embolus and embolism and described the relationship between venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.
