Oral candidiasis

Oral candidiasis
Other names: Oral thrush, oral candidosis
Cause: Candida species, usually Candida albicans
Treatment: Topical or systemic antifungals

Oral candidiasis, commonly called oral thrush, is a fungal infection of the mouth caused by overgrowth of Candida species, most often Candida albicans, a yeast that normally colonises the mouth harmlessly. It is an opportunistic infection that appears when local or systemic defences are disturbed.

Signs and forms

The classic pseudomembranous form presents as soft white plaques on the tongue, palate and buccal mucosa that can be wiped off, leaving a red, sometimes bleeding surface. Other forms include erythematous (red) candidiasis, denture-related stomatitis, angular cheilitis at the mouth corners, and chronic hyperplastic candidiasis, a persistent white patch that does not wipe away.

Risk factors

Risk factors include infancy and old age, wearing dentures, inhaled or systemic corticosteroids, recent antibiotics, dry mouth, diabetes, smoking and immunosuppression — notably HIV/AIDS, where thrush was an early recognised marker, and chemotherapy. Symptoms range from none to soreness, burning and altered taste.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported if needed by microscopy or culture of a swab. Treatment uses topical antifungals such as nystatin suspension or miconazole gel for mild disease, and oral fluconazole for extensive, recurrent or immunocompromised cases, together with correction of predisposing factors such as denture hygiene.
