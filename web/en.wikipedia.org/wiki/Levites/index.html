The Levites (Hebrew: לְוִיִּם, Ləviyyîm) are members of the Hebrew Tribe of Levi, descendants of Levi, the third son of Jacob and Leah. In the Hebrew Bible the Levites were set apart for religious service: they assisted the priests (the Kohanim, Aaron's descendants), cared for the Tabernacle and later the Temple in Jerusalem, served as musicians, singers, gatekeepers, and teachers, and carried the Ark of the Covenant.

Biblical account

According to the Book of Numbers, because the Levites were dedicated to God's service in place of the firstborn of Israel, they received no share in the division of the Land of Israel. Instead they were granted 48 Levitical cities scattered among the other tribes and supported by a tithe (ma'aser) of the people's produce. During the Exodus and the wilderness wanderings they camped around the Tabernacle and transported its components, with the clans of Gershon, Kohath, and Merari each assigned specific duties.

In the Temple and after

Under David and Solomon the Levites were organised into divisions of singers, porters, and officials; the Psalms are associated with Levitical choirs such as the sons of Korah and Asaph. After the destruction of the Second Temple in 70 CE, sacrifices ceased, but Levitical status (Levi) is still inherited patrilineally in Judaism: Levites are called second to the Torah reading, wash the hands of the Kohanim before the priestly blessing, and retain certain other honours and restrictions.

The Levite in parable

A Levite figures in Jesus's Parable of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10), where a priest and a Levite pass by a wounded man before a Samaritan stops to help — a story told in answer to the question "Who is my neighbour?"
