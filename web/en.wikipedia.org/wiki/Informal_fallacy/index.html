Informal fallacy

An informal fallacy is an error in reasoning that arises from the content, context, or wording of an argument rather than from its logical form. Unlike formal fallacies, which can be detected by examining an argument's structure alone, informal fallacies are deductively invalid (or inductively weak) because of what the premises say, what they omit, or how the language shifts meaning.

Classification

Textbooks traditionally group informal fallacies into families. Fallacies of relevance offer premises that are emotionally persuasive but logically irrelevant: ad hominem attacks, appeals to pity, force, popularity, or tradition, the straw man, and the red herring. Fallacies of presumption smuggle an unjustified assumption into the premises: begging the question, false dilemma, loaded question, and false cause. Fallacies of ambiguity exploit shifts in the meaning of words or phrases, such as equivocation and amphiboly. Inductive weaknesses, such as hasty generalisation and faulty analogy, are often grouped here as well.

Study

The study of fallacies goes back to Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations. In the 20th century, C. L. Hamblin's Fallacies (1970) criticised the textbook treatment and helped launch modern informal logic, in which fallacies are analysed as failed moves in dialogue rather than simply invalid arguments. Contemporary argumentation theory stresses that many so-called fallacies are context-dependent: an appeal to authority, for instance, may be reasonable or fallacious depending on the circumstances.
