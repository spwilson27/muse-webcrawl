Single displacement reaction

A single-displacement reaction, also known as a single-replacement reaction, is a type of chemical reaction where an element reacts with a compound and takes the place of another element in that compound. The general form is:

A + BX → AX + B

This reaction occurs when A is more reactive than B, as shown by the reactivity series. Typically, single displacement reactions are categorized as metal replacement reactions or halogen replacement reactions.

Metal replacement

In a metal replacement reaction, a free metal displaces a less reactive metal from its compound. For example, when a strip of zinc metal is placed in an aqueous solution of copper(II) sulfate, zinc displaces copper, forming zinc sulfate solution and depositing solid copper:

Zn + CuSO4 → ZnSO4 + Cu

Similarly, iron nails placed in copper sulfate solution become coated with copper, and copper placed in silver nitrate solution deposits silver crystals while the solution turns blue from copper nitrate. Whether a displacement occurs can be predicted from the reactivity (activity) series, which ranks metals from most to least reactive: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, hydrogen, copper, silver, gold.

Halogen replacement

Halogens also undergo displacement: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide from its salt. For instance, chlorine gas bubbled through a solution of potassium bromide produces bromine and potassium chloride:

Cl2 + 2 KBr → 2 KCl + Br2

The reactivity of the halogens decreases down the group: fluorine > chlorine > bromine > iodine.

Related reactions

Acids react with reactive metals in a similar pattern, producing a salt and hydrogen gas — for example, zinc with hydrochloric acid gives zinc chloride and hydrogen. Single displacement contrasts with double displacement (metathesis) reactions, in which two compounds exchange partners and no free element is produced or consumed.
