Free radical theory of ageing

Free radicals can damage cellular structures, contributing to ageing.

The free radical theory of ageing states that organisms age because cells accumulate free radical damage over time. A free radical is any atom or molecule that has a single unpaired electron in an outer shell. While a few free radicals such as melanin are not chemically reactive, most biologically relevant free radicals are highly reactive. For most biological structures, free radical damage is closely associated with oxidative damage. Antioxidants are reducing agents, and limit oxidative damage to biological structures by passivating them from free radicals.

The theory was first proposed by Denham Harman in the 1950s. Harman suggested that ageing results from the accumulation of damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated as byproducts of normal metabolism, particularly within the mitochondria.

Mechanisms

Free radicals can damage lipids, proteins, and DNA. Oxidation of membrane lipids produces lipid peroxides, which generate chain reactions that propagate further damage. Oxidised proteins may lose enzymatic function, while oxidative DNA lesions, if unrepaired, can cause mutations. The mitochondrial version of the theory proposes a "vicious cycle" in which damaged mitochondria produce more ROS, accelerating decline.

Evidence and criticism

Some evidence supports the theory: animals engineered to produce more antioxidant enzymes in mitochondria, such as catalase targeted to mitochondria in mice, have shown extended lifespan in some studies. However, broad antioxidant supplementation trials in humans have generally failed to extend life, and some studies suggest ROS also serve as important signalling molecules, a concept known as mitohormesis. Modern views treat oxidative damage as one contributor among several to ageing rather than its sole cause, alongside telomere shortening, genomic instability, and cellular senescence.
