Metacontrast

Metacontrast is a form of backward visual masking in which the visibility of a briefly presented target stimulus is reduced by a second stimulus (the mask) that appears after the target, surrounds it, and shares its outer contour without overlapping it. For example, a disc presented for a few tens of milliseconds can be rendered nearly invisible by a ring presented shortly afterwards around it.

Characteristics

Suppression in metacontrast is strongest when the mask follows the target by roughly 50–100 milliseconds and when target and mask are close in space; unlike ordinary pattern masking, the mask does not cover the target's location. The effect produces a U-shaped function of suppression over stimulus onset asynchrony and is stronger for magnocellular (transient) pathway processing.

Theoretical significance

Metacontrast has been influential in theories of visual awareness. Because the target can influence behaviour — for instance through priming — while remaining subjectively invisible, the phenomenon is used to study unconscious perception and the neural correlates of consciousness. Leading explanations involve interrupted recurrent processing between visual areas such as V1 and higher object-recognition regions, and interactions between sustained and transient visual channels.
