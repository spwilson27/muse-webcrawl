Allopatric speciation

Allopatric speciation (from Greek allos, "other", and patris, "fatherland") is speciation that occurs when populations of a species become geographically isolated so that gene flow between them is prevented or greatly reduced. Over time, genetic drift and divergent natural selection lead the separated populations to evolve reproductive isolation, so that even if the barrier disappears they can no longer interbreed.

Mechanisms

Isolation may arise by vicariance — a barrier such as a rising mountain range, a new river or a shifting sea splits an existing range — or by dispersal, when a few individuals colonise a new area such as an island (a founder event; the extreme case is sometimes called peripatric speciation). Classic examples include Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands, the divergence of snapping shrimp species on either side of the Isthmus of Panama, and ring-distributed squirrel populations around the Grand Canyon.

Evidence and significance

Championed by Ernst Mayr in the mid-20th century, allopatric speciation is widely considered the most common mode of speciation in animals. Field patterns — sister species occupying adjacent but non-overlapping ranges — and laboratory experiments on reproductive isolation in isolated populations provide strong support, in contrast to the more contentious sympatric speciation, in which divergence occurs without a barrier.
