Floating-point unit

A floating-point unit (FPU), also called a math coprocessor, is a part of a computer system specially designed to carry out operations on floating-point numbers. Typical operations are addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and square root, as well as transcendental functions in some designs. Historically implemented as a separate chip — such as the Intel 8087 and its successors paired with x86 processors — the FPU has been integrated into the main processor die in virtually all modern CPUs.

TypeProcessor component
StandardIEEE 754

History

Early computers performed floating-point arithmetic in software, which was slow. Dedicated hardware appeared in mainframes of the 1950s and 1960s, and in the microcomputer era the FPU was sold as an add-on coprocessor — the 8087 (1980) for the 8086/8088, and later the 80387 for the 386. Intel integrated the FPU into the CPU with the 80486DX (1989), though the cut-down 486SX disabled it. The 1994 Pentium FDIV bug, an error in the FPU's division lookup table, led to a famous and costly recall.

Operation

Modern FPUs conform to the IEEE 754 standard for floating-point arithmetic, supporting single- and double-precision formats (and often half and extended precision), multiple rounding modes, and exceptions such as overflow, underflow, and division by zero. Contemporary designs typically contain several pipelined FPUs alongside SIMD vector units (such as SSE and AVX on x86, or NEON on ARM) that process multiple floating-point values per instruction.
