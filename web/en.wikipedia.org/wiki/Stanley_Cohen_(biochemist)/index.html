Stanley Cohen (biochemist)

Born: 17 November 1922, Brooklyn, New York, US
Died: 5 February 2020 (aged 97), Nashville, Tennessee, US
Known for: Discovery of epidermal growth factor (EGF) and nerve growth factor work
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1986)

Stanley Cohen (November 17, 1922 – February 5, 2020) was an American biochemist who, with Rita Levi-Montalcini, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1986 for the discovery of growth factors.

Career and discoveries

Cohen studied at Brooklyn College, Oberlin College and the University of Michigan, where he earned his PhD in 1948. After postdoctoral work in Colorado he joined Rita Levi-Montalcini at Washington University in St. Louis in 1953 to help identify the substance responsible for nerve growth. In the course of purifying nerve growth factor (NGF) from mouse salivary glands and snake venom, he isolated a second substance that caused newborn mice to open their eyes and erupt their teeth early; he identified it as a polypeptide and named it epidermal growth factor (EGF). He determined EGF's amino-acid sequence and showed it acts through a cell-surface receptor with protein-kinase activity — among the first links between growth factors, receptors, and the signalling pathways that, when deranged, drive cancer.

In 1959 Cohen moved to Vanderbilt University School of Medicine in Nashville, where he spent the rest of his career. In addition to the Nobel Prize, he received the Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize (1983), the Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research (1986) and the National Medal of Science (1986).
