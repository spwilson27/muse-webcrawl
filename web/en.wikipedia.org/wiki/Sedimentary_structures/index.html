Sedimentary structures

Cross-bedding, a primary sedimentary structure recording ancient current flow

Sedimentary structures are features formed in sediments and sedimentary rocks during or shortly after deposition, and sometimes later by chemical or biological activity. Because many structures record the conditions of the depositional environment — current direction and strength, water depth, exposure to air — they are among the most important tools of sedimentology and paleoenvironmental reconstruction.

Primary structures

Primary structures form during deposition. They include bedding and lamination; graded bedding, in which grain size decreases upward within a bed; cross-bedding produced by migrating ripples and dunes, whose inclined foresets indicate paleocurrent direction; ripple marks on bed surfaces; mud cracks formed by desiccation of exposed wet sediment; rain prints; and sole marks such as flute and groove casts preserved on the undersides of beds.

Secondary and biogenic structures

Soft-sediment deformation structures — load casts, flame structures, ball-and-pillow structures and convolute bedding — form when water-saturated sediment is deformed before lithification. Chemical structures include concretions, nodules and stylolites. Biogenic structures, or trace fossils, are produced by organisms: burrows, tracks, trails and borings, studied in the discipline of ichnology.

Use as way-up indicators

Many structures are asymmetric with respect to gravity and so tell geologists which way was "up" when the sediment was deposited. Graded beds, mud cracks tapering downward, and the truncations and tangential bases of cross-beds are classic way-up (younging) criteria, invaluable in structurally complex terrain where strata may be overturned.
