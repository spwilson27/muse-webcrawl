Bus station

A bus station, also known as a bus terminal or coach station, is a structure where city, intercity, or international buses stop to pick up and drop off passengers. Larger than a simple bus stop, which is merely a place on the roadside, a bus station typically provides platforms or bays, waiting areas, ticketing facilities, and passenger amenities, and often serves as an interchange with other modes of transport.

Types

Bus stations range from small rural terminals serving a handful of daily departures to major city facilities handling tens of thousands of passengers a day. Intercity stations are commonly used by long-distance coach operators such as Greyhound and FlixBus, while urban bus stations serve local transit networks. Many modern stations are integrated interchanges combining bus, rail, metro, taxi, and bicycle facilities.

Facilities

Typical facilities include numbered departure bays with sawtooth or drive-through layouts, enclosed waiting rooms, ticket offices and machines, departure boards, toilets, luggage services, shops, and staff offices. Larger terminals may include bus storage and maintenance areas. Famous examples include the Port Authority Bus Terminal in New York City — among the busiest bus stations in the world — and Preston bus station in England, a noted example of Brutalist architecture.

History

Purpose-built bus stations emerged in the 1920s and 1930s as motor coach networks expanded and operators sought central terminals comparable to railway stations. In Britain, companies such as coach operators and municipal transport departments built Art Deco and later modernist terminals; in the United States, Greyhound built distinctive Streamline Moderne stations across the country.
