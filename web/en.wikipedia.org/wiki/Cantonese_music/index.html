Cantonese music (Chinese: 广东音乐; pinyin: Guǎngdōng yīnyuè, literally "Guangdong music"), also called Yueyue (粵樂), is a style of traditional Chinese instrumental music originating in the Pearl River Delta region of Guangdong province. It crystallized in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in Guangzhou (Canton) and spread throughout the Cantonese-speaking diaspora.

History

Cantonese music grew out of the accompanimental music of Cantonese opera and of narrative singing, absorbing melodies from local folk song, Kunqu, and even Western popular and film music encountered in treaty-port Guangzhou. In the 1920s and 1930s, composers and performers such as He Liutang, Lü Wencheng, and Yi Jiansu shaped its repertoire and ensemble style in the teahouses and music clubs (played for pleasure by amateur "music societies") of Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Macau, and Shanghai.

Instrumentation and style

The classic ensemble is built around the gaohu — a higher-pitched erhu developed in the 1920s by Lü Wencheng, who also pioneered steel strings and a distinctive Cantonese bowing style — together with the yangqin (hammered dulcimer) and qinqin or pipa; these three form the "three pieces" (san jian tou). Larger ensembles add erhu, dizi, xiao, ruan and other instruments in the "five pieces" (wu jian tou) grouping. The music is generally bright, flowing and lyrical, built on pentatonic scales, with characteristic ornamentation and the two common modes zhengxian and yifan.

Repertoire

Famous pieces include Autumn Moon over a Calm Lake (平湖秋月), composed by Lü Wencheng; Stepping on High (步步高); The Hungry Horse Rattles Its Bells (餓馬搖鈴); Dragon Boat Race / Rain Beating on Plantain Leaves (雨打芭蕉); and Joyful Song (娛樂昇平). Many pieces depict scenes of southern life and nature.

Legacy

Cantonese music was inscribed on China's national list of intangible cultural heritage in 2006. It remains a staple of Chinese orchestras worldwide, and its melodies permeated early Cantopop and Hong Kong film scores, helping define the sound of 20th-century Cantonese culture.
