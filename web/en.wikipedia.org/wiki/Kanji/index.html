Kanji

Kanji (漢字, pronounced [kaɲdʑi]) are the logographic Chinese characters adapted from the Chinese script used in the Japanese writing system. They are used alongside the Japanese syllabic scripts hiragana and katakana. The Japanese term kanji means "Han characters".

History

Chinese characters arrived in Japan via Korea, probably around the 5th century CE, on seals, coins, mirrors and official documents. Over time the Japanese adapted the characters both for their sound values — in the man'yōgana system of the Man'yōshū — and for their meanings, assigning native Japanese readings (kun'yomi) alongside the borrowed Sino-Japanese readings (on'yomi).

Readings

Most kanji have at least two readings: the on'yomi, derived from the Chinese pronunciation at the time of borrowing, and the kun'yomi, the native Japanese word for the concept the character represents. For example, 水 ("water") is read sui in on'yomi and mizu in kun'yomi.

Official lists

Post-war reforms simplified many character forms (shinjitai). The Japanese government maintains the jōyō kanji list of characters for general use, expanded in 2010 to 2,136 characters, taught through primary and secondary school. Beyond this list, jinmeiyō kanji are permitted in personal names, and thousands more characters exist in dictionaries.

Stroke order

Kanji are written following a conventional stroke order, generally from top to bottom and left to right, which aids legibility and correct character structure. Characters are built from a set of radicals (bushu), which also serve as the organising principle of kanji dictionaries.
