Quebec nationalism

Quebec nationalism (French: nationalisme québécois) is a political and cultural movement that asserts the distinctiveness of the French-speaking people of Quebec within (or, for its separatist wing, outside) Canada. It has been the dominant force in Quebec politics since the 1960s and is expressed in language policy, distinct-society demands and, at its peak, campaigns for sovereignty.

History

After the British conquest of 1760, French-Canadian identity survived through the Church, the civil code and language. The Quiet Revolution of the 1960s under Jean Lesage transformed the movement into a modern, secular, state-led project: nationalisation of hydroelectricity, creation of the Caisse de dépôt, and expansion of the francophone middle class. René Lévesque founded the sovereigntist Parti Québécois, which won power in 1976 and passed the Charter of the French Language (Bill 101).

Sovereignty referendums

Quebec held two referendums on sovereignty-association (1980) and on sovereignty with an economic partnership (1995); both were defeated — the second by the narrowest of margins, 50.58% to 49.42%. The federal Clarity Act (2000) set conditions for any future secession vote. Since the mid-2000s the sovereigntist movement has declined in electoral terms, while nationalism has been partly rechannelled into debates over identity, secularism (Bill 21) and immigration under the Coalition Avenir Québec.
