Acetylcholinesterase inhibitor

An acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (AChEI) is a chemical or drug that inhibits the enzyme acetylcholinesterase from breaking down acetylcholine, thereby increasing both the level and duration of action of this neurotransmitter. Such compounds include medicines, insecticides, and some of the most toxic nerve agents known.

Medical uses

Reversible inhibitors — including donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine — are used to treat the cognitive symptoms of Alzheimer's disease and other dementias, compensating for the loss of cholinergic neurons. Pyridostigmine and neostigmine are used in myasthenia gravis and to reverse neuromuscular blockade after surgery.

Insecticides and nerve agents

Organophosphate and carbamate insecticides (such as malathion and carbaryl) inhibit acetylcholinesterase in insects and, at sufficient dose, in humans. Chemical weapons such as sarin, VX, and the Novichok agents are potent, essentially irreversible inhibitors; poisoning causes a cholinergic crisis of excessive secretions, bronchoconstriction, convulsions, and respiratory failure, treated with atropine and oxime reactivators such as pralidoxime.

Mechanism

Acetylcholinesterase normally hydrolyses acetylcholine into acetate and choline within microseconds at cholinergic synapses. Carbamate and organophosphate inhibitors carbamylate or phosphorylate the active-site serine of the enzyme, the latter undergoing "aging" that makes inhibition permanent.
