Greece occupies the southern tip of the Balkan Peninsula in southeastern Europe, with territory that also includes thousands of islands scattered in the Aegean and Ionian Seas. It borders Albania, North Macedonia, and Bulgaria to the north and Turkey to the east. Greece has one of the longest coastlines in the world — some 13,676 km — owing to its deeply indented shores and archipelagos; only a fraction of its estimated 6,000 islands, islets, and rocks are inhabited, with the largest being Crete, followed by Euboea.

Terrain

About four-fifths of Greece is mountainous. The Pindus mountain range forms the spine of the mainland, running from the northwest toward the Gulf of Corinth and continuing, submerged, into the islands of Kythera and Crete. Mount Olympus, at 2,918 m the country's highest peak, was the mythical home of the Greek gods. Major lowland areas include the Thessalian plain, the Macedonian plain around Thessaloniki, and the plains of Thrace. Rivers are generally short and seasonal; the longest entirely in Greece is the Haliacmon, while the Evros (Maritsa) forms much of the border with Turkey.

Climate

Greece has a predominantly Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, moderated on the coasts by the sea. Alpine conditions prevail in the high mountains, and the northern interior experiences colder winters with snow. The country is one of Europe's most seismically active, and summer wildfires are a recurring hazard.

Regions and islands

The mainland divides traditionally into regions including Attica (containing Athens), the Peloponnese — joined to the mainland by the Isthmus of Corinth, now cut by the Corinth Canal — Thessaly, Epirus, Macedonia, and Thrace. Island groups include the Ionian Islands, the Cyclades, the Dodecanese, the Sporades, and the North Aegean islands, together with Crete and Euboea.
