Geodesics in general relativity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In general relativity, a geodesic generalizes the notion of a "straight line" to curved spacetime. Importantly, the world line of a particle free from all external, non-gravitational forces is a particular type of geodesic. In other words, a freely moving or falling particle always moves along a geodesic.

The geodesic equation

The path of a test particle is determined by the geodesic equation,

d²xμ/dτ² + Γμαβ (dxα/dτ)(dxβ/dτ) = 0,

where τ is proper time, xμ are the spacetime coordinates, and Γμαβ are the Christoffel symbols built from the metric tensor. The equation can be derived from the Einstein field equations via the geodesic postulate, or from an action principle in which the particle extremizes its proper time.

Types of geodesics

Geodesics are classified by the sign of their tangent vector's norm: timelike geodesics are followed by massive particles, null (lightlike) geodesics are followed by photons and other massless particles, and spacelike geodesics cannot be followed by any physical object. Null geodesics determine the causal structure of spacetime — the light cones — and underlie phenomena such as gravitational lensing and the Shapiro time delay.

Applications

Geodesic motion in the Schwarzschild geometry explains the anomalous precession of Mercury's perihelion and the existence of an innermost stable circular orbit around black holes. In the Kerr geometry of a rotating black hole, frame dragging alters the geodesic structure. Gravitational redshift and the orbits of stars around the compact object Sagittarius A* are also modeled as geodesic motion.
