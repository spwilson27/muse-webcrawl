Julius Martov (born Yuliy Osipovich Tsederbaum; 24 November 1873 – 4 April 1923) was a Russian revolutionary and the leader of the Menshevik faction of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party (RSDLP). A close early collaborator of Vladimir Lenin, he became the Bolshevik leader's chief socialist rival and the most prominent advocate of a democratic, pluralist Marxism in Russia.

Early life and Iskra

Martov was born in Constantinople to a middle-class Russian Jewish family and raised in Odessa and St Petersburg. Expelled from university for political activity, he was arrested and exiled for organising Jewish workers in Vilnius, where he pioneered "agitation" among workers through pamphlets such as On Agitation. In 1900 he joined Lenin and Alexander Potresov in founding the newspaper Iskra ("The Spark") and its journal Zarya, becoming one of its editors and most prolific writers.

The 1903 split

At the Second Congress of the RSDLP in Brussels and London in 1903, Martov and Lenin clashed over the definition of party membership: Martov proposed a looser formula that would open the party to sympathisers, while Lenin demanded a narrow party of professional revolutionaries. Martov's formula actually won the vote on that clause, but subsequent votes on the editorial board and central committee — after some delegates walked out — gave Lenin's supporters a majority, hence the names Bolsheviks ("majorityites") and Mensheviks ("minorityites") for the factions Martov thereafter led. He refused to serve on the reduced Iskra board and became the Mensheviks' leading theorist and journalist.

Revolution and opposition

Martov spent much of the period to 1917 in exile in Western Europe, opposing the First World War as an internationalist. Returning to Russia after the February Revolution of 1917, he led the Menshevik-Internationalists, demanding a negotiated peace and an all-socialist coalition government. He opposed the Bolshevik seizure of power in October 1917; at the Congress of Soviets, Martov's plea for a broad socialist government was rejected, and he led the Menshevik walkout. In the Constituent Assembly and then from the margins, he defended civil liberties and legal opposition while also condemning White counter-revolution, supporting the Red Army against the Whites in the Civil War.

Exile and death

As the Bolsheviks progressively suppressed the Mensheviks, Martov, gravely ill with tuberculosis, was permitted to leave Soviet Russia in 1920. From Berlin he edited the journal Sotsialisticheskiy Vestnik (Socialist Courier), which became a key source on Soviet affairs, and co-founded the "Two-and-a-Half International" of socialist parties. He died at a sanatorium in Schömberg in the Black Forest on 4 April 1923, aged 49.
