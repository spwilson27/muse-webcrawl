Swamp rock

Swamp rock is a genre of rock music that emerged in the late 1960s, blending rock and roll with rhythm and blues, country, funk, and the sounds associated with Louisiana and the American South — particularly swamp blues and swamp pop. The term evokes the humid, murky atmosphere of the bayou; its archetypal practitioners were Creedence Clearwater Revival and singer-songwriter Tony Joe White.

Characteristics

Swamp rock is typically built on a laid-back but insistent groove, with twangy or tremolo-laden guitar, harmonica, fiddle, and earthy, story-telling lyrics about rural Southern life — bayous, floods, voodoo, alligators, and hard times. Despite the regional imagery, many of its defining acts were not Southern: Creedence, famously, hailed from El Cerrito, California, and adopted a Louisiana persona on records such as "Born on the Bayou" and "Green River".

Key artists and songs

Creedence Clearwater Revival's run of hits in 1969–70 ("Proud Mary", "Bad Moon Rising", "Green River", "Down on the Corner") defined the sound commercially. Tony Joe White brought an authentic Louisiana voice with "Polk Salad Annie" (1969) and wrote "Rainy Night in Georgia". Other artists associated with the style include The Band (whose roots-rock shared swamp rock's textures), Little Feat, J. J. Cale, Dale Hawkins (whose "Susie Q" Creedence covered), swamp pop veteran Bobby Charles, and later performers such as John Fogerty in his solo career and Lucinda Williams.

Related styles

Swamp rock is closely related to swamp blues (Slim Harpo, Lightnin' Slim, Lazy Lester, recorded for J. D. Miller's Excello sessions in Crowley, Louisiana), swamp pop (the lilting R&B-country hybrid of south Louisiana and southeast Texas), Southern rock, and Americana. Critics sometimes treat "swamp rock" loosely as a mood rather than a strict genre, applying it to any recording that conjures the bayou.
