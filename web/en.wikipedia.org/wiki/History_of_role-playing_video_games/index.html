History of role-playing video games

The history of role-playing video games (RPGs) begins in the mid-1970s on university mainframes, where students inspired by the tabletop game Dungeons & Dragons (1974) created text-based dungeon crawls such as pedit5 and dnd on the PLATO system. The genre commercialised on home computers with Akalabeth (1979), Wizardry and Ultima (both 1981), split into Western and Japanese traditions on consoles with Dragon Quest (1986) and Final Fantasy (1987), and expanded into massively multiplayer online games and action RPGs in the 2000s.

Early RPGs rendered dungeons as simple wireframe graphics.

Early mainframe and home computer RPGs

Programmers on the PLATO educational network created some of the first graphical dungeon games. On personal computers, Richard Garriott's Akalabeth: World of Doom and the Ultima series, Andrew Greenberg and Robert Woodhead's Wizardry, and the Might and Magic and Gold Box series adapted dice rolls, character classes and experience points from tabletop play. EVOLUTION: text adventures with statistics, such as The Temple of Apshai (1979), bridged adventure and RPG genres.

The Japanese console tradition

Yuji Horii's Dragon Quest (1986) streamlined the genre for the Famicom with command menus and a prewritten party; Hironobu Sakaguchi's Final Fantasy (1987) followed. The two series defined the JRPG: narrative-driven, turn-based, and exported worldwide, culminating in global hits such as Final Fantasy VII (1997) and the Pokémon series (1996), the best-selling RPG franchise of all time.

1990s diversification

On PC, isometric story-driven games — Fallout (1997), Baldur's Gate (1998), Planescape: Torment (1999) — revived Western RPGs, while Blizzard's Diablo (1997) popularised real-time action RPG combat. Tactical RPGs (Final Fantasy Tactics), roguelikes and early online worlds (Ultima Online, 1997) broadened the field.

Online and modern era

EverQuest (1999) and World of Warcraft (2004) made MMORPGs a mass medium. Single-player epics such as The Elder Scrolls V: Skyrim (2011), The Witcher 3 (2015) and Baldur's Gate 3 (2023), alongside soulslike games following Demon's Souls (2009), kept the genre central to the industry, while indie developers revived turn-based and retro styles.
