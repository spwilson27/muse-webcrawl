Manhatta (1921) is an American short experimental documentary film directed by the painter Charles Sheeler and the photographer Paul Strand. Considered one of the first American avant-garde films, it presents a day-in-the-life portrait of Manhattan, New York City — its skyscrapers, harbors, bridges, ferries, steam, and crowds — through 65 shots arranged in a loosely chronological arc from dawn to dusk.

Content and title

The film's title derives from "Manna-hata," the Lenape name often rendered "island of many hills," and its intertitles quote Walt Whitman's poem "Mannahatta." Its static, formally composed photography — views looking down from skyscrapers, smoke drifting between towers, commuters streaming from ferries — treats the modern city as both spectacle and machine.

Significance

Manhatta is regarded as a foundational "city symphony" film, preceding the better-known European examples of the genre such as Berlin: Symphony of a Metropolis (1927). It was selected for preservation in the United States National Film Registry in 1995. A restored print, recovered after the original negative was long thought lost, has been widely exhibited in retrospectives of early experimental cinema.
