Earth's shadow

Earth's shadow (or Earth shadow) is the shadow that Earth itself casts through its atmosphere and into outer space, toward the antisolar point. During the twilight period (both early dawn and late dusk), the shadow's visible fringe — sometimes called the dark segment or twilight wedge — appears as a dark, diffuse band low on the horizon, most distinct when the sky is clear and the horizon unobstructed.

Appearance

Just after sunset, as the Sun sinks below the western horizon, Earth's shadow rises in the eastern sky as a blue-grey arc opposite the Sun. Above it glows a pinkish band known as the Belt of Venus, caused by backscattered reddened sunlight in the upper atmosphere. The shadow rises and expands as the Sun sets further, eventually merging with the darkness of the night sky. The same phenomenon occurs in reverse before sunrise in the western sky.

Geometry

The shadow is a cone extending about 1.4 million kilometres from Earth away from the Sun. When the Moon passes through this cone, a lunar eclipse occurs. Earth's shadow is also visible from high altitude and from space, where astronauts aboard the International Space Station can observe the terminator and the shadowed side of the planet.
