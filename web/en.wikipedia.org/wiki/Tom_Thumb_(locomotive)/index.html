Tom Thumb (locomotive)

TypeSteam locomotive
Designer / builderPeter Cooper
Built1829–1830
OperatorBaltimore and Ohio Railroad
ConfigurationVertical boiler, 2-2-0

Tom Thumb was the first American-built steam locomotive used on a common-carrier railroad. Designed and built by inventor and industrialist Peter Cooper in 1829–30, it ran on the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad (B&O) and demonstrated the viability of steam traction on the B&O's sharply curved early track.

Design

Cooper's tiny locomotive—of about one horsepower—used a vertical boiler with tubes made, legend holds, from rifle barrels. An anthracite-burning engine drove the wheels through gears and belts, and a fan blower driven by steam maintained the fire's draft. The machine weighed about a ton and could pull a passenger car at speeds up to roughly 18 mph (29 km/h).

The horse race

The locomotive is famous for an impromptu race on 28 August 1830 against a horse-drawn car on the B&O's line between Baltimore and Ellicott's Mills. Tom Thumb initially pulled ahead of the horse, but a belt slipped off a pulley, causing the blower to stop and the locomotive to lose steam; despite frantic repairs, the horse won. Although the race was lost, the demonstration convinced B&O directors of steam's potential, and the railroad committed to steam power the following year, offering a prize that led to the York and Phineas Davis's locomotive designs.

Legacy

The original locomotive was not preserved, but a replica built for the B&O's 1927 Fair of the Iron Horse is displayed at the B&O Railroad Museum in Baltimore. The Tom Thumb race remains one of the most famous anecdotes in American railroad history, symbolizing the transition from horse-drawn to steam transport.
