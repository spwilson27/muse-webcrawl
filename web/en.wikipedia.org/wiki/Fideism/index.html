Fideism

Fideism is a standpoint or epistemological theory which maintains that faith is independent of — and in some cases superior to — reason, and that religious belief cannot be justified by rational argument. The term derives from the Latin fides, "faith". Strong fideism holds that reason can actively conflict with faith; weaker forms hold simply that religious truths are accepted on faith rather than demonstrated.

History

Attitudes resembling fideism can be traced to Tertullian, whose rhetoric ("I believe because it is absurd") is often quoted in simplified form, and to medieval and Reformation thinkers who stressed the limits of reason after the Fall. Blaise Pascal argued that matters of religion belong to the "reasons of the heart". In the 19th century Søren Kierkegaard emphasised a "leap of faith", and in the 20th century Ludwig Wittgenstein's remarks on religious language inspired a controversial interpretation dubbed "Wittgensteinian fideism". The Roman Catholic Church officially rejected strong fideism, notably in the First Vatican Council's teaching that God can be known with certainty by reason, while affirming the necessity of faith.
