Armand Vallin Feigenbaum (April 6, 1922 – November 13, 2014) was an American quality control expert and businessman. He was the founder of the concept of total quality control, later known as total quality management, and originated the analysis of the "cost of quality" and the idea of the "hidden plant" — the proportion of factory capacity wasted by not getting things right first time.

Career

Feigenbaum joined General Electric after graduating from Union College, and rose to become the company's first corporate director of quality. He earned a master's degree and a PhD from the MIT Sloan School of Management. In 1968 he founded General Systems Company, an engineering and management consultancy in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, which he led for the rest of his life. He served as president of the American Society for Quality Control and was a founding member of the International Academy for Quality.

Contributions

His book Total Quality Control (first published as Quality Control: Principles, Practice and Administration in 1951; retitled in 1961) laid out quality as a company-wide responsibility rather than a specialist inspection function. Feigenbaum's three steps to quality — quality leadership, modern quality technology, and organisational commitment — and his classification of quality costs into prevention, appraisal, internal failure and external failure costs became standard elements of quality management, and strongly influenced the Japanese quality movement of the post-war decades.
