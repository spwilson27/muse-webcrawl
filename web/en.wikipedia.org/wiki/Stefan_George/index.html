Stefan Anton George (12 July 1868 – 4 December 1933) was a German symbolist poet and translator of Dante, Shakespeare and Baudelaire. One of the most influential German-language poets of the early twentieth century, he gathered around himself the George Circle (George-Kreis), a group of writers, scholars and artists united by devotion to his vision of an aristocratic, spiritually renewed Germany.

Life and work

George was born at Büdesheim near Bingen, the son of a wine merchant. After travels in London, Paris and Vienna — where he met Stéphane Mallarmé and the Viennese poet Hugo von Hofmannsthal — he published his first collection, Hymnen (1890), and founded the journal Blätter für die Kunst (1892), the organ of his circle. His collections Algabal (1892), about a decadent Roman emperor, and Das Jahr der Seele ("The Year of the Soul", 1897) established his reputation for formal, hieratic verse in a deliberately idiosyncratic orthography and typography designed by Melchior Lechter.

The death in 1903 of the young Maximilian Kronberger, whom George idealised as "Maximin", became the centre of a quasi-religious cult in the circle and of the collection Der siebente Ring (1907). Later volumes — Der Stern des Bundes (1914) and Das neue Reich (1928) — prophesied a "Secret Germany" (Geheimes Deutschland) of spiritual elites. Members of his circle included the historians Ernst Kantorowicz and Percy Gothein and, fatefully, Claus von Stauffenberg, who would attempt to assassinate Hitler in 1944 and drew part of his ethos from George's "Secret Germany".

Later years

The Nazis courted the elderly poet in 1933, offering him honours and the presidency of a reconstituted poetry academy; George declined, left Germany, and died in Minusio, Switzerland, in December 1933. His poetry, with its cult of beauty, hierarchy and the charismatic leader, remains the subject of debate over its relationship to German conservatism and National Socialism.
