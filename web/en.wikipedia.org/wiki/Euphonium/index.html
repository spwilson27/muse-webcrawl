The euphonium is a medium-sized, three- or four-valve, often compensating, conical-bore, tenor-voiced brass instrument that derives its name from the Ancient Greek word εὔφωνος (euphōnos), meaning "well-sounding" or "sweet-voiced". It is pitched in B♭, sounding a major ninth below its written part in the treble clef, and is a mainstay of concert bands, brass bands, and military bands.

History

The euphonium was invented in 1843 by Ferdinand Sommer of Weimar, Germany, as the "euphonion", building on earlier valved tenor brass designs such as the tenor cornett and the ophicleide family. It spread rapidly through military and brass bands in Germany, France, and Britain. In British brass bands the euphonium is a featured solo instrument, often called "the cello of the brass band" for its warm, singing tone.

Construction

Modern professional euphoniums usually have four piston or rotary valves; the fourth valve lowers the pitch by a perfect fourth. Compensating systems, in which extra tubing is engaged when the fourth valve is used in combination with others, correct the intonation problems of the low register. The instrument's conical bore distinguishes it from the cylindrical-bore trombone and gives it a darker, mellower sound than the smaller baritone horn, with which it is frequently confused.

Repertoire

Although the euphonium has a modest orchestral presence — notable parts include the "Bydlo" movement of Mussorgsky's Pictures at an Exhibition (usually played on tuba or euphonium), Holst's suites, and works by Strauss — it has an extensive solo and band repertoire, including concertos by Joseph Horovitz, Philip Sparke, and Peter Graham, and celebrated arrangements of cello and vocal works.
