Kharasch–Sosnovsky reaction

The Kharasch–Sosnovsky reaction is a radical oxidation of an allylic C–H bond to an allylic benzoate using a copper catalyst and a perester, most commonly tert-butyl peroxybenzoate. The reaction was reported by Morris S. Kharasch and George Sosnovsky in 1958 and is one of the earliest examples of a selective allylic C–H functionalisation.

Reaction

In the standard protocol an alkene is heated with tert-butyl peroxybenzoate in the presence of a catalytic amount of a copper(I) salt, such as copper(I) chloride or copper(I) bromide. The product is the allylic benzoate ester, formed with the allylic C–H bond replaced by a C–O bond. Cyclic alkenes such as cyclohexene give a single allylic product because of symmetry, whereas unsymmetrical acyclic alkenes may give mixtures of regioisomers.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds by a radical pathway. The copper(I) catalyst reduces the perester to a tert-butoxy radical and benzoate, generating copper(II). The tert-butoxy radical abstracts an allylic hydrogen atom, producing a resonance-stabilised allyl radical. This radical is then trapped by the copper(II) benzoate species — formally by ligand transfer or by formation of an allyl–copper intermediate that undergoes reductive elimination — delivering the allylic benzoate and regenerating copper(I).

Asymmetric variants

In the 1990s, enantioselective versions of the reaction were developed using chiral bis(oxazoline)–copper complexes as catalysts. These asymmetric Kharasch–Sosnovsky reactions convert cyclic alkenes into allylic benzoates with useful levels of enantioselectivity and have been applied in natural product synthesis.
