Lent (Latin: Quadragesima, "Fortieth") is a solemn religious observance in the Christian liturgical calendar, commemorating the 40 days Jesus spent fasting in the desert and enduring the temptation of Satan, as described in the Gospels of Matthew, Mark and Luke. It begins on Ash Wednesday and ends before Easter Sunday, and is a period of prayer, fasting, repentance and almsgiving in preparation for the celebration of the Resurrection at Easter.

Duration and practice

In Western Christianity, Lent spans six weeks (46 days); the six Sundays are not counted as fast days, giving 40 fasting days in imitation of Christ's fast and of other biblical periods of forty: Moses on Mount Sinai, Elijah's journey, and the forty years of Israel's wandering. Eastern Christians observe Great Lent, beginning on Clean Monday and counting the days differently, with Holy Week treated as a distinct fast. Traditionally, Catholics were required to fast (one full meal and two small meals) on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday and to abstain from meat on Fridays; historically the fast was stricter, excluding meat, eggs and dairy for the whole season, and its relaxation produced customs such as using up rich foods on Shrove Tuesday (Mardi Gras) with pancakes and carnival festivities.

Observances

Many Christians "give up" a luxury or habit for Lent, or take on additional prayer, scripture reading and charitable works. Churches veil statues and images, omit the singing of the Gloria and Alleluia, and use violet vestments. Devotions particular to the season include the Stations of the Cross, the Lenten vespers and, in many cultures, processions during Holy Week. In the Eastern Orthodox Church, the services of Great Lent are marked by prostrations, the reading of the Great Canon of Andrew of Crete and the Liturgy of the Presanctified Gifts.

History

Preparatory fasts before Easter developed in the early Church: by the time of the First Council of Nicaea (325) a forty-day fast was widely established. Its length and strictness varied by region for centuries before becoming broadly standardised in the medieval West.
