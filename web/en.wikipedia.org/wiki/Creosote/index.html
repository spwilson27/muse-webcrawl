Creosote

Creosote has long been used to preserve railway sleepers (ties)

Creosote is a category of carbonaceous chemicals formed by the distillation of various tars and by pyrolysis of plant-derived material, such as wood or fossil fuel. The best known are coal-tar creosote, a dark oily liquid used as a wood preservative, and wood-tar creosote, once widely used in medicine. The name derives from the Greek kreas (flesh) and sōtēr (preserver), reflecting its early use as an antiseptic.

Coal-tar creosote

Coal-tar creosote is produced by distilling coal tar and consists largely of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) together with phenols and cresols. Pressure-impregnation of timber with creosote — a process industrialised in the nineteenth century — protects wood against fungi, insects, and marine borers, and has been used extensively for railway sleepers, telegraph and utility poles, pilings, and fencing.

Health and environmental concerns

Creosote is toxic, and coal-tar creosote is classified as a probable human carcinogen; long-term exposure is associated with skin damage and cancers in workers. Because it leaches from treated timber into soil and water, its sale to consumers and many uses have been restricted or banned in the European Union and elsewhere, though industrial use continues under regulation in many countries, including the United States.

Wood-tar creosote

Distilled principally from beechwood tar, wood-tar creosote was historically used as a disinfectant, expectorant, and treatment for toothache and tuberculosis, and as a meat preservative. Pharmaceutical use has largely ceased owing to toxicity concerns.

The creosote bush

The name "creosote" is also applied to the creosote bush (Larrea tridentata), an unrelated desert shrub of the southwestern United States and Mexico whose resinous leaves have a tar-like odour after rain.
