Slag

Slag is a by-product of smelting and refining ores and metals. It is a mixture of metal oxides and silicon dioxide, often with metal sulfides and other compounds, that floats on molten metal and is removed during processing. Slags are produced in blast furnaces making iron, in steelmaking converters and electric arc furnaces, and in the smelting of copper, lead, and other non-ferrous metals.

Formation and composition

During smelting, fluxing agents such as limestone are added to the charge. The flux reacts with gangue minerals and impurities to form a liquid slag of lower density than the metal, which separates and can be tapped off. Blast furnace slag is principally calcium and magnesium silicates and aluminates; steelmaking slags are more basic and contain significant iron oxide. Controlling slag chemistry protects furnace linings, removes sulfur and phosphorus, and governs metal quality.

Uses

Once considered waste, slag is now widely used. Ground granulated blast-furnace slag is a supplementary cementitious material that improves concrete durability. Air-cooled slag serves as road aggregate and railway ballast. Slag is also used in cement manufacture, as a soil amendment and phosphate fertilizer (basic slag), in mineral wool insulation, and in land reclamation. Historic slag heaps remain landscape features of old industrial regions.

Environmental aspects

Some slags, particularly from non-ferrous smelting, can leach heavy metals and require controlled disposal, though iron and steel slags are generally regarded as low-risk and extensively recycled.
