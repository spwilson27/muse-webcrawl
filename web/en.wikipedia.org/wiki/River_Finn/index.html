The River Finn (Irish: An Fhinn) is a river in the north-west of Ireland. It rises in Lough Finn in County Donegal and flows eastwards through the town of Ballybofey and the Finn Valley, crossing the border into County Tyrone, Northern Ireland, before joining the River Mourne at Lifford and Strabane to form the River Foyle. The Finn is about 40 miles (64 km) long and is noted for its Atlantic salmon fishing.

Course

From Lough Finn, a narrow lake beneath the Blue Stack Mountains, the river flows through Fintown and the steep-sided Finn Valley to the twin towns of Ballybofey and Stranorlar, divided by the river and joined by a bridge. Continuing east, it passes Castlefin, then crosses into Northern Ireland, where it flows past Clady before meeting the Mourne just below Lifford and Strabane; the combined waters form the Foyle, which flows on to Derry and Lough Foyle.

Fishing

The Finn is regarded as one of Ireland's productive salmon rivers, historically noted for its spring salmon run and later grilse runs; it also holds brown trout, and its tributaries such as the Reelan River are important spawning streams. Declining Atlantic salmon stocks led to catch-and-release rules and conservation measures on the river in recent decades. Parts of the river are designated a Special Area of Conservation under the EU Habitats Directive, recognising its salmon, otter, and freshwater habitat interest.

History

The Finn Valley was the heartland of the Cineál Conaill branch of the Northern Uí Néill and later of the O'Donnell lordship of Tyrconnell. The river forms part of the historic boundary between County Donegal and County Tyrone and, since 1921, an international border for a stretch of its lower course. The Finn Valley Railway once followed the river from Stranorlar towards Strabane.
