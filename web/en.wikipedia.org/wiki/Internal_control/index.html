Internal control

Internal control, as defined by accounting and auditing, is a process for assuring of an organization's objectives in operational effectiveness and efficiency, reliable financial reporting, and compliance with laws, regulations and policies. A broad concept, internal control involves everything that controls risks to an organization.

The COSO framework's five interrelated components of internal control.

COSO framework

The Committee of Sponsoring Organizations of the Treadway Commission (COSO) published its Internal Control — Integrated Framework in 1992, updated in 2013. It defines five components:

  1. Control environment — the set of standards, processes and structures providing the basis for carrying out internal control, including integrity, ethical values, and organizational structure.
  2. Risk assessment — identifying and analyzing risks to the achievement of objectives.
  3. Control activities — actions established through policies and procedures, such as authorizations, reconciliations, segregation of duties, and physical safeguards.
  4. Information and communication — generating and sharing relevant, quality information internally and externally.
  5. Monitoring activities — ongoing and separate evaluations to ascertain that controls are present and functioning.

Roles and responsibilities

Management is responsible for designing, implementing and maintaining internal control; the board of directors and audit committee provide oversight; internal auditors evaluate effectiveness; and all personnel contribute through their duties. External auditors assess internal control over financial reporting where required.

Regulation

In the United States, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX) requires management of public companies to assess and report on internal control over financial reporting (Section 404), with auditor attestation for larger issuers. Similar requirements exist internationally, and the concept underpins standards such as COBIT for IT controls.

Limitations

Internal control provides reasonable, not absolute, assurance: it can be defeated by human error, faulty judgment, collusion, and management override, and its design is subject to cost–benefit considerations.
