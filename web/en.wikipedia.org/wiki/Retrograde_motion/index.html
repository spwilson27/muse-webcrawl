Apparent retrograde motion

Apparent retrograde motion is the apparent motion of a planet in a direction opposite to that of other bodies within its system, as observed from a particular vantage point. While planets ordinarily appear to move eastward (prograde) against the background stars, the superior planets periodically appear to slow, stop, reverse to the west for some weeks, then stop again and resume their eastward course.

Explanation

The effect is an optical consequence of relative motion: when Earth, moving faster in its inner orbit, overtakes an outer planet such as Mars, the line of sight to the planet sweeps backwards against the more distant stars. In antiquity the phenomenon was a central puzzle of astronomy; Greek astronomers accounted for it with epicycles in the geocentric model, while the heliocentric model of Copernicus explained it simply as a perspective effect. Retrograde loops are largest and most frequent for Mars, whose orbit is closest to Earth's; Mercury and Venus, being inferior planets, show their own retrograde phases around inferior conjunction.

Other uses

"Retrograde" also describes real (not merely apparent) backwards motion: a body orbiting or rotating opposite to the usual direction is in retrograde orbit or rotation, as with Venus's rotation and several irregular moons and exoplanets. In astrology, Mercury retrograde has become a popular cultural phrase, though it has no scientific standing.
