Skates are cartilaginous fish belonging to the family Rajidae in the order Rajiformes. Together with the stingrays and sawfishes they form the rays (superorder Batoidea), the flattened relatives of sharks. Skates are found in oceans worldwide, from shallow coastal waters to depths of nearly 3,000 metres, and are especially diverse in cold and temperate seas.

Anatomy

Skates have a flattened, typically diamond- or rounded-shaped body formed by enlarged pectoral fins fused to the head. Unlike stingrays, skates lack a venomous stinging spine; many instead carry rows of sharp thorn-like denticles along the tail and back. The tail is generally thick and fleshy, bearing two small dorsal fins and often a small caudal fin, and is not used for defence. The eyes sit on top of the head with spiracles behind them that draw in water for the gills, which open on the underside. The mouth contains numerous small, pavement-like teeth suited to crushing molluscs, crustaceans and small fish.

Reproduction

Skates are oviparous: females lay leathery egg cases — the familiar "mermaid's purses" that wash up on beaches — each containing a single embryo nourished by a yolk sac. This contrasts with the live-bearing stingrays. Egg cases have horn-like projections at the corners that anchor them to the seabed. Skates mature slowly, grow to large sizes (the common skate complex historically exceeded 2 metres in disc width) and produce few offspring, making them highly vulnerable to overfishing.

Conservation and fisheries

Skates are caught for their edible "wings" (pectoral fins) in European and Asian markets and taken as bycatch in trawl fisheries. Several species, including the common skate (Dipturus batis complex) and the barndoor skate, have suffered severe declines and are listed as Critically Endangered or Endangered by the IUCN. Slow recovery of barndoor skate populations in the northwest Atlantic after fishing closures is often cited as evidence that management can work.
