Utagawa Hiroshige (Japanese: 歌川 広重; 1797 – 12 October 1858), born Andō Tokutarō, was a Japanese ukiyo-e artist, considered the last great master of the tradition and Japan's pre-eminent landscape printmaker. Together with Katsushika Hokusai, he defined the genre of meisho-e (pictures of famous places), depicting the scenery of Edo and the great highways of Japan with a lyrical sensitivity to season, weather, and atmosphere.

Life and career

Hiroshige was born in Edo (modern Tokyo) into a samurai fire-warden family and inherited his father's post at the fire station of the shogun's castle. He studied under Utagawa Toyohiro and received the Utagawa school name in 1812. For years he produced conventional actor and warrior prints while keeping his fire-brigade duties, but his career transformed with The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (1832–33), a series of landscapes along the great coastal highway, whose commercial success established him as a leading artist. He went on to produce an estimated 8,000 designs in more than 500 series.

Major works

  • The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (Hōeidō edition, 1832–33)
  • The Sixty-nine Stations of the Kiso Kaidō (with Keisai Eisen)
  • Famous Views of the Sixty-odd Provinces (1853–56)
  • One Hundred Famous Views of Edo (1856–58), completed after his death with Hiroshige II
  • Numerous bird-and-flower prints (kachō-e) and fan prints

Style and influence

Hiroshige's mature work is characterised by bold cropping, high or absent horizons, subtle colour gradation (bokashi), and evocative depictions of rain, snow, and mist — sometimes called "Hiroshige blue" for his extensive use of imported Prussian blue pigment. His prints profoundly influenced European artists of the Japonisme movement: Vincent van Gogh made oil copies of two designs from One Hundred Famous Views of Edo, and his compositions informed Impressionist and Post-Impressionist landscape painting. He died in Edo during a cholera epidemic in 1858.
