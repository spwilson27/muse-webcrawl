Clare Island

Clare Island at the entrance to Clew Bay, County Mayo.

Clare Island (Irish: Cliara or Oileán Chliara) is a mountainous island guarding the entrance to Clew Bay in County Mayo, Ireland. It is famous as the home of the 15th-century pirate queen Gráinne O'Malley (Grace O'Malley). The island had a population of about 130 at recent census counts. It is also one of the best-documented islands in Ireland biologically: the Clare Island Survey of 1909–1911, organised by Robert Lloyd Praeger, was a pioneering multidisciplinary study of the island's flora and fauna, and a New Survey of Clare Island has been published in multiple volumes since the 1990s.

Geography

Clare Island is the largest of the Clew Bay islands, lying about 5.6 kilometres (3.5 miles) off the Mayo coast near Louisburgh. Its highest point is Knockmore (462 m), and the great cliffs of its north-western coast rise over 300 metres. The island's harbour is on the sheltered eastern side, where the ferry from Roonagh Pier docks. A lighthouse, built in 1816 and decommissioned in 1965, stands on the northern cliffs.

History

The island was a stronghold of the Ó Máille (O'Malley) clan. Clare Island Castle, a tower house near the harbour, was one of the strongholds of Gráinne O'Malley (c. 1530 – c. 1603), the celebrated chieftain and seafarer. The Cistercian Clare Island Abbey, founded in the 12th century and rebuilt by the O'Malleys, contains medieval wall paintings and the O'Malley Tomb, the reputed burial place of Gráinne O'Malley.

Before the Great Famine of the 1840s the island supported a population of over 1,600; like other western communities it declined sharply through emigration during the following century.

The Clare Island Survey

Between 1909 and 1911, the naturalist Robert Lloyd Praeger organised more than 100 scientists to catalogue the island's geology, archaeology, and living things; their findings, published in the Proceedings of the Royal Irish Academy, recorded over 6,000 species. The Royal Irish Academy's New Survey of Clare Island, launched in the 1990s, revisited the work a century later as a baseline for measuring environmental change.

Access

Ferries run year-round from Roonagh Pier near Louisburgh, taking about 20 minutes. The island offers accommodation, a café and bar, a yoga and meditation centre at the former lighthouse property, and walking routes including the climb of Knockmore and the coastal path to the lighthouse.
