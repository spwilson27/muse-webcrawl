Flax, also known as common flax or linseed, is a flowering plant, Linum usitatissimum, in the family Linaceae. It is cultivated as a food and fibre crop in regions of the world with temperate climates. Textiles made from flax are known in English as linen and are traditionally used for bed sheets, underclothes and table linen. Its oil, known as linseed oil, is an edible oil obtained by expeller pressing and sometimes solvent extraction.

Description and cultivation

The flax plant grows 0.9–1.2 m tall with slender stems and pale blue five-petalled flowers 15–25 mm in diameter. The fruit is a round, dry capsule containing several glossy brown seeds. Flax is one of the oldest cultivated plants: spun, dyed flax fibres found in a cave in Georgia date to about 30,000 years ago, and the plant was domesticated in the Fertile Crescent. It was grown extensively in ancient Egypt, where linen was used for clothing and mummification wrappings.

Uses

Fibre varieties are grown for linen and for industrial bast fibre used in composites, while seed varieties (linseed) are grown for oil and for flaxseed, which is rich in the omega-3 fatty acid alpha-linolenic acid and is sold whole, ground or as a supplement. Linseed oil is a drying oil used in paints, varnishes, linoleum and wood finishes. Major producers include Kazakhstan, Russia, Canada and China. Flax remains the national flower emblem of Northern Ireland and appears on the badge of the city's linen heritage in Belfast.
