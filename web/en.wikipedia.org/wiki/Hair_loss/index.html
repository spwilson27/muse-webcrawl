Hair loss

Other namesAlopecia; baldness (of the scalp)
SpecialtyDermatology
Common typesAndrogenetic alopecia, alopecia areata, telogen effluvium, tinea capitis
TreatmentsMinoxidil, finasteride, hair transplantation, corticosteroids (for alopecia areata)

Hair loss, also known as alopecia or baldness, refers to the partial or complete loss of hair from part of the head or body. The severity and pattern vary widely. Hair loss is common: roughly half of men show significant male-pattern hair loss by age 50, and a substantial minority of women experience female-pattern thinning, particularly after menopause.

Types and causes

The most common form is androgenetic alopecia (pattern hair loss), a genetically determined sensitivity of hair follicles to dihydrotestosterone (DHT) that gradually miniaturises follicles. Alopecia areata is an autoimmune disorder producing round patches of loss and, in extreme forms, loss of all scalp hair (alopecia totalis) or all body hair (alopecia universalis). Telogen effluvium is diffuse shedding triggered by stress, illness, childbirth or nutritional deficiency, and is usually temporary. Other causes include fungal infection (tinea capitis), traction and styling damage, scarring (cicatricial) alopecias, thyroid disease, iron deficiency and chemotherapy.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually clinical, based on the pattern of loss and examination of the scalp, supplemented where needed by pull tests, trichoscopy, blood tests and scalp biopsy.

Treatment

Topical minoxidil and oral finasteride (for men) are the best-established treatments for pattern loss, slowing progression and producing modest regrowth; neither restores a full head of hair, and benefits reverse on stopping. Hair transplantation redistributes DHT-resistant follicles from the back of the scalp. Alopecia areata may be treated with corticosteroids and, in severe cases, JAK inhibitors such as baricitinib. Cosmetic options include wigs, hairpieces and scalp micropigmentation.

Psychological and cultural aspects

Hair carries strong cultural and religious significance, and its loss is associated with measurable effects on self-image and quality of life. Shaved heads, by contrast, are voluntarily adopted in many religious traditions and subcultures.
