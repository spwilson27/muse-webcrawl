Thoor Ballylee is a fortified Norman tower house in County Galway, Ireland, best known as the summer home of the poet William Butler Yeats between 1919 and 1929. The name derives from the Irish túr, meaning tower, and the adjacent townland of Ballylee.

History

The tower was built in the fifteenth or sixteenth century by the de Burgo (Burke) family as part of the castle of Ballylee. It stands beside the Streamstown River, close to the former home of Yeats's patron Lady Gregory at Coole Park and to the town of Gort.

Yeats's tower

Yeats first saw the tower in 1915 and purchased it in 1917 for £35. He undertook extensive restoration work, renovating the four storeys connected by a winding stone stair in the thickness of the wall. He and his wife Georgie (George) spent their first summers there from 1919, with their children Anne and Michael; Michael was born in the tower in 1921.

The tower became a powerful symbol in Yeats's later poetry, appearing in the volumes The Tower (1928) and The Winding Stair (1933). His poem "To Be Carved on a Stone at Thoor Ballylee" records his restoration of the building. Yeats wrote of it: "I declare this tower is my symbol; I declare / This winding, gyring, spiring treadmill of a stair is my ancestral stair."

Later history and museum

After Yeats's last visit in 1929, the tower fell into ruin, its roof lost, and was restored in the 1960s as a Yeats museum. It has suffered flood damage on several occasions, including severe flooding in 2009. Today it is maintained by the Yeats Thoor Ballylee Society and opened to visitors in the summer months, housing exhibitions on the poet's life and work.
