Freedom of religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Freedom of religion or religious liberty is the principle that supports the freedom of an individual or community, in public or private, to manifest religion or belief in teaching, practice, worship and observance, including the freedom to change religion and to hold no religion. It is protected by Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (whose Human Rights Committee's General Comment 22 elaborates it), by the First Amendment in the United States (free exercise, paired with non-establishment), and by Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights, under which manifestation may be limited only as prescribed by law and necessary in a democratic society.

The right's frontiers are perpetually litigated: religious dress (the French and Turkish headscarf cases), conscientious objection (military service, medical procedures), religious arbitration and family law, blasphemy and hate-speech laws, proselytism, and the autonomy of religious bodies in employment. Global monitoring (Pew Research Center studies) finds government restrictions and social hostilities involving religion at high levels in the 2010s–2020s, with religious minorities — Christians in parts of the Middle East, Muslims in India and China (Uyghurs), Jews amid rising antisemitism, Ahmadis, Baháʼís, Jehovah's Witnesses in Russia — facing the severest pressures in different regions.
