Jeanne Antoinette Poisson, Marquise de Pompadour (29 December 1721 – 15 April 1764), commonly known as Madame de Pompadour, was a member of the French court. She was the official chief mistress (maîtresse-en-titre) of King Louis XV from 1745 to 1751, and remained influential as a court favourite and patron of the arts until her death in 1764, exerting considerable influence over the king's policies and appointments.

Early life

Jeanne Antoinette Poisson was born in Paris into a wealthy bourgeois family; her father, François Poisson, was a banker who fled the country over debts when she was a child, and she was raised under the protection of the financier Charles François Paul Le Normant de Tournehem, who educated her thoroughly in music, art, theatre, and literature. A fortune-teller reportedly predicted she would win the heart of a king, and she was nicknamed "Reinette" ("little queen"). In 1741 she married Tournehem's nephew, Charles Guillaume Le Normant d'Étiolles, and became a celebrated hostess of Parisian salons.

Mistress of Louis XV

Jeanne Antoinette attracted the king's attention at a masked ball at Versailles in February 1745, held to celebrate the Dauphin's marriage; by September 1745 she had been installed at court and granted the estate of Pompadour, taking the title Marquise de Pompadour. Louis granted her extensive apartments at Versailles and later made her a duchess and a lady-in-waiting to the queen. She founded the king's private theatre, the Théâtre des Petits Cabinets, performing in plays with courtiers, and entertained the king's inner circle at residences including Choisy and Bellevue.

Influence and patronage

Though her position as the king's lover waned around 1750, Pompadour remained his confidante and a decisive political broker, influencing ministerial appointments and, notably, supporting the Diplomatic Revolution of 1756 that allied France with Austria against Prussia and Britain in the Seven Years' War. She was a great patron of the Enlightenment: she protected Voltaire and Diderot, helped secure royal privilege for the Encyclopédie, and supported artists such as François Boucher and Jean-Baptiste Pigalle. She championed French luxury manufactures, notably the porcelain works she helped transfer to Sèvres, whose famous pink glaze is still called rose Pompadour, and she promoted the founding of the École Militaire. Her health was fragile, and she died of tuberculosis at Versailles in 1764, aged 42.

Legacy

Pompadour became the archetype of the powerful royal mistress: admired as a tastemaker who shaped Rococo art and French decorative style, and blamed by critics for the extravagance of the court and the disasters of the Seven Years' War. The phrase attributed to her — "Après nous, le déluge" ("After us, the flood") — became a byword for the heedless luxury of the ancien régime.
