The Loos Memorial is a Commonwealth War Graves Commission memorial in France commemorating more than 20,000 British and Commonwealth soldiers who were killed in the area during the First World War and who have no known grave. It forms the sides and back of Dud Corner Cemetery at Loos-en-Gohelle in the Pas-de-Calais department.

Background

The memorial primarily covers the Battle of Loos (25 September – 8 October 1915), the largest British offensive of 1915 and the first in which the British used poison gas, as well as the later fighting in the sector through to the end of the war, including the final advance in 1918.

The name "Dud Corner" is believed to refer to the large number of unexploded enemy shells found in the vicinity after the Armistice. The cemetery contains over 1,800 burials, the majority of them unidentified.

Design

The memorial was designed by Sir Herbert Baker, with sculpture by Ernest Blakemore. Panels bearing the names of the missing surround the cemetery on three sides. It was unveiled on 4 August 1930 by General Sir Nevil Macready.

Among those commemorated on the memorial are several Victoria Cross recipients and the poet Charles Hamilton Sorley. Rudyard Kipling's son, John Kipling, was long believed to be commemorated here, as he was killed at Loos and his body was not identified at the time.
