V engine

A V engine, or Vee engine, is a common configuration for internal combustion engines in which the cylinders are arranged in two banks set at an angle to each other, forming a "V" shape when viewed from the end of the crankshaft. Both banks share a common crankshaft. V engines are shorter and more compact than equivalent inline engines, and the layout provides a stiff, balanced structure, especially in larger cylinder counts.

History

The first V-configuration engine, a V-twin, appeared on Daimler vehicles in 1889. V8 engines were produced by Antoinette in France from 1904 for aircraft and racing boats, and Cadillac introduced the mass-produced V8 to automobiles in 1914. V12 engines became prestigious in luxury cars and aircraft between the wars, while the post-war American market made the V8 its signature configuration.

Configurations

V engines are named by cylinder count: V-twin (V2), V4, V6, V8, V10, V12, and larger counts for ships and locomotives up to V24. The angle between banks — the V-angle — influences balance and compactness: 90° is typical for V8s, 60° for V6s and V12s, and narrow-angle designs such as Volkswagen's VR6 reduce the angle to around 15°, allowing a single cylinder head.

Balance and smoothness

Some configurations are inherently well balanced: the V12 has excellent primary and secondary balance, while a crossplane V8 trades some vibration for its characteristic exhaust note. Other layouts, such as 90° V6s, require balance shafts or split crankpins to run smoothly. Compared with the flat engine, the V layout has a higher centre of gravity but a shorter length.

Applications

V engines have powered cars, motorcycles, aircraft, boats, locomotives and stationary machinery. In modern passenger cars they are increasingly displaced by turbocharged inline-fours, but V6 and V8 engines remain common in trucks, performance cars and marine use.
