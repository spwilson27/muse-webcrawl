The Greek Civil War (Greek: Εμφύλιος Πόλεμος, "the Civil War") took place from 1946 to 1949 between the Greek government army — backed first by Britain and then by the United States — and the Democratic Army of Greece (DSE), the military branch of the Communist Party of Greece (KKE), supported by Yugoslavia, Albania and Bulgaria. It was the first major armed conflict of the Cold War era and a principal trigger of the Truman Doctrine.

Origins

The war grew out of the struggle between the communist-led EAM/ELAS resistance and rival groups, and the British-backed government-in-exile, during the Axis occupation of Greece. Clashes erupted in the Dekemvriana in Athens in December 1944, weeks after liberation, and were ended provisionally by the Varkiza Agreement (February 1945). The ensuing "White Terror" against the left, the KKE's boycott of the March 1946 elections, and the return of King George II after a contested plebiscite pushed the communists into full-scale insurgency from late 1946, proclaiming a Provisional Democratic Government in December 1947.

Course

The DSE, under Markos Vafiadis, waged guerrilla war from mountain strongholds in the north, with supply lines into the communist Balkan states through which — under the wartime "percentages agreement" — Stalin largely left Greece to the West. Britain, financially exhausted, handed responsibility to the United States, which in March 1947 announced the Truman Doctrine and poured money, equipment and advisers into the Greek army under General James Van Fleet. In 1948–49 the reorganised government army, commanded by Field Marshal Alexandros Papagos, cleared the Peloponnese and central Greece. The Tito–Stalin split of 1948 led Yugoslavia to close its border to the DSE in July 1949, cutting its main lifeline. Final offensives took the Grammos and Vitsi strongholds in August 1949; the KKE announced a ceasefire on 16 October 1949.

Aftermath

Tens of thousands died, some 700,000 people were displaced internally, tens of thousands of communist refugees fled to the Eastern bloc, and thousands of children were evacuated by both sides — a lasting source of bitterness. The war entrenched a right-wing, anti-communist state: the KKE remained banned until 1974, and political exiles were only broadly allowed to return in the 1980s, when the PASOK government formally recognised the wartime Resistance and the conflict as a civil war rather than a "bandit war."
