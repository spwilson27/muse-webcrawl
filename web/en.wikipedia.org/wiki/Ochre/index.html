Ochre

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ochre (American English) or ocher is a natural clay earth pigment consisting of ferric oxide, clay and sand, ranging in colour from yellow (yellow ochre, based on limonite) through orange and red (red ochre, based on hematite) to brown. Ochres are among the oldest pigments used by humans and remain in use in artists' paints today.

Use in prehistory

Use of ochre is documented from the Middle Stone Age of Africa more than 100,000 years ago: engraved ochre pieces and ochre-processing kits have been found at Blombos Cave in South Africa. Ochre was widely used in Upper Palaeolithic cave painting — for example at Lascaux and Altamira — as a body paint and sunscreen, in burials, and possibly as an adhesive ingredient and hide preservative. Aboriginal Australian cultures have used ochre for tens of thousands of years in art, ceremony and trade over long distances.

Types

Yellow ochre (goethite/limonite) turns red on heating as water is driven off and hematite forms; this process may have been deliberately used by early humans. Red ochre is naturally rich in hematite. Purple and brown ochres, umber (containing manganese oxides) and sienna (from Italy) are related earth pigments. Synthetic iron-oxide pigments, such as Mars yellow and Mars red, now supplement or replace natural ochres in paints.

Historical use

Ochre was a staple of classical, medieval and Renaissance painting and of traditional building decoration: the yellow- and red-ochred houses of Roussillon in France (where the pigment was mined at Rustrel and Roussillon) and of colonial architecture are famous. Ochre (ωχρα) takes its name from the Greek word for "pale yellow".
