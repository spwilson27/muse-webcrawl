Dick Turpin

Image: Dick Turpin and Black Bess

Richard Turpin (bapt. 21 September 1705 – 7 April 1739) was an English highwayman whose exploits were romanticised following his execution in York for horse theft. He became the subject of legend after his death, portrayed as an elegant and gallant outlaw in English ballads and popular theatre of the 18th and 19th centuries.

Life

Turpin was born at Hempstead, Essex, the son of a farmer and innkeeper, and was apprenticed to a butcher. He became involved with a gang of smugglers and deer thieves (the Gregory Gang) in Essex in the early 1730s, whose activities escalated to burglary and violent home invasions. As the gang was broken up, Turpin turned to highway robbery around Epping Forest, at times in partnership with Tom King. In 1737 a reward was offered for his capture, and after accidentally shooting King during an attempted arrest, Turpin fled north.

Capture and execution

Living in Yorkshire under the alias John Palmer, Turpin was arrested in 1738 after shooting a gamecock and threatening a man. While imprisoned at York Castle, he wrote to his brother asking for help; the letter was recognised by his former schoolteacher, James Smith, who identified the handwriting. Turpin was convicted of horse theft and hanged at Knavesmire, York, on 7 April 1739.

Legend

The famous episode of Turpin's overnight ride from London to York on his mare Black Bess was invented by the novelist William Harrison Ainsworth in his 1834 novel Rookwood, which transformed Turpin into a dashing folk hero. The ride had earlier been attributed to other highwaymen. Turpin's exploits have since featured in numerous ballads, plays, films, and television series.
