Sleep hygiene

Sleep hygiene is a behavioural and environmental practice designed to promote good quality sleep. The term was popularised in the late 1970s by psychologist Peter Hauri, who developed a set of guidelines for helping patients with mild to moderate insomnia. Sleep hygiene recommendations are a standard part of sleep medicine education, although research indicates that hygiene education alone is usually insufficient to treat chronic insomnia, for which cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the recommended first-line treatment.

Recommendations

Commonly advised practices include:

  • Maintaining a regular sleep and wake schedule, including at weekends.
  • Keeping the bedroom cool, dark and quiet, and reserving the bed for sleep.
  • Avoiding caffeine, nicotine and alcohol in the hours before bed.
  • Limiting daytime naps, and keeping any nap short and early.
  • Getting regular exercise, but not vigorous exercise immediately before bedtime.
  • Reducing exposure to bright light and electronic screens in the evening.
  • Establishing a relaxing pre-sleep routine, and getting out of bed if unable to sleep rather than lying awake.

Assessment

Sleep hygiene is assessed clinically through discussion and instruments such as the Sleep Hygiene Index, a 13-item self-report scale that measures how often a person engages in behaviours that are detrimental to sleep. Understanding of an individual's habits guides targeted advice within broader insomnia treatment.

Evidence

Systematic reviews find modest and inconsistent effects of sleep hygiene education as a standalone therapy, and several specific recommendations rest on limited evidence. Nevertheless, because the practices are low-risk and generally consistent with healthy living, they remain widely recommended in public health campaigns on sleep, and form one component of CBT-I alongside stimulus control and sleep restriction.
