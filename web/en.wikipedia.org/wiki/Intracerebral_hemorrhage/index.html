Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a type of intracranial bleed that occurs within the brain tissue or ventricles. It is a form of haemorrhagic stroke, distinguished from ischemic stroke, which is caused by blockage of a blood vessel. Intracerebral hemorrhage accounts for roughly 10–15% of strokes but a disproportionate share of stroke deaths, with one-month mortality around 40%.

Causes

The most common cause is chronic high blood pressure, which damages small penetrating arteries; deep hemorrhages in the basal ganglia, thalamus, pons and cerebellum are characteristic. In older people, cerebral amyloid angiopathy predisposes to lobar hemorrhages. Other causes include anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy, bleeding disorders, vascular malformations, aneurysms, tumours, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, sympathomimetic drugs such as cocaine, and head trauma.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms develop suddenly and often progress over minutes to hours as the hematoma expands: headache, vomiting, focal neurological deficits such as limb weakness or speech disturbance, seizures and deteriorating consciousness. Large bleeds raise intracranial pressure and can cause herniation and death.

Diagnosis and treatment

Non-contrast CT of the head is the standard emergency investigation, showing the location and size of the hematoma; CT angiography may identify a "spot sign" predicting expansion or an underlying vascular lesion. Treatment is urgent and largely supportive: rapid blood pressure management, reversal of anticoagulation, neurosurgical consultation, and care in a stroke or intensive care unit. Surgery may be used for cerebellar hemorrhage with deterioration, or for selected superficial bleeds; trials of minimally invasive evacuation have shown mixed results. Prevention centres on blood pressure control.
