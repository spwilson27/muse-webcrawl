The monarchy of Denmark is a constitutional institution and the official head of state of the Kingdom of Denmark, which includes Denmark proper together with the autonomous territories of Greenland and the Faroe Islands. The Danish monarchy is one of the oldest continuing monarchies in the world, tracing its lineage back more than a thousand years to the Viking kings; the current royal house is the House of Glücksburg.

History

Denmark was unified as a kingdom in the 10th century under kings such as Gorm the Old and Harald Bluetooth, who is credited with the Christianisation of the Danes. For centuries the monarchy was elective; it became hereditary in 1660, when Frederick III established one of Europe's most complete absolutist regimes, codified in the King's Law (Lex Regia) of 1665. Absolutism ended in 1849, when Frederick VII signed the June Constitution, establishing a constitutional monarchy. The succession law was changed in 2009 by referendum to absolute primogeniture, so the monarch's eldest child inherits regardless of sex.

Constitutional role

Under the Constitutional Act of 1953, executive authority is formally vested in the monarch but exercised by the government; the monarch appoints the prime minister, presides over the Council of State, formally signs legislation, and represents Denmark abroad. By convention these acts follow ministerial advice, and ministers bear political responsibility. The monarch also serves as supreme commander of the Danish Defence and, until constitutional separation, was head of the Church of Denmark.

Recent reigns

Margrethe II, who ascended in 1972, became Denmark's longest-reigning current monarch and was widely respected as an artist and translator. In her 2023 New Year address she announced her abdication — the first by a Danish monarch in nearly 900 years — and on 14 January 2024 she signed the declaration of abdication, and her elder son succeeded as Frederik X.
