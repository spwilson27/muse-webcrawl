Technological determinism

Technological determinism is a reductionist theory that assumes a society's technology determines the development of its social structure and cultural values. The term, believed to have been coined by American sociologist and economist Thorstein Veblen (1857–1929), describes the view that technological change proceeds along a largely autonomous path and that its effects on society are the primary driver of historical transformation.

Hard and soft determinism

Scholars commonly distinguish between two versions of the thesis. Hard determinism holds that technology develops independently of social concerns and that society is forced to adapt to and is shaped by technological change. Soft determinism acknowledges that social and political factors influence which technologies are developed and adopted, but maintains that technology remains the strongest single influence on social change. Many contemporary writers in science and technology studies reject hard determinism in favour of accounts — such as the social construction of technology (SCOT) and actor–network theory — that stress the mutual shaping of technology and society.

Proponents and examples

The philosopher Karl Marx is often read as a technological determinist, since he argued that changes in productive technology drive changes in social relations — famously summarised in his observation that "the hand-mill gives you society with the feudal lord; the steam-mill society with the industrial capitalist." Media theorist Marshall McLuhan's dictum "the medium is the message" is a classic statement of the position applied to communications media. Historian Lynn White Jr. argued that the stirrup transformed medieval warfare and thereby feudal society. Twentieth-century writers such as Jacques Ellul, in The Technological Society, portrayed technology as an autonomous, self-augmenting system beyond human control.

Criticism

Critics argue that technological determinism ignores the social, economic, and cultural choices involved in developing and deploying technology, and that the same technology can produce different outcomes in different societies. Historians of technology such as Thomas P. Hughes and Wiebe Bijker have emphasised contingency, path dependence, and the role of users and institutions. The debate remains central to discussions of the internet, automation, and artificial intelligence, where claims that a technology will "inevitably" transform work, politics, or education are frequently described — often pejoratively — as deterministic.
