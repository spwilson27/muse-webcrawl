Psychotherapy

Psychotherapy (also called psychological therapy, talk therapy or talking therapy) is the use of psychological methods, especially regular personal interaction, to help a person change behaviour, increase happiness and overcome problems. Psychotherapists — who may be psychiatrists, psychologists, counsellors or other licensed clinicians — treat mental disorders and emotional difficulties through hundreds of distinct techniques, delivered individually, in groups, with couples or families, and increasingly online.

Major schools

Psychodynamic therapy descends from psychoanalysis, exploring unconscious conflict and early relationships. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), developed by Aaron Beck and drawing on behaviour therapy, targets maladaptive thoughts and behaviours and has the largest evidence base for anxiety and depression. Humanistic therapies, such as Carl Rogers' person-centred therapy, emphasise empathy, unconditional positive regard and self-actualisation. Other major approaches include dialectical behaviour therapy, EMDR for trauma, family and systemic therapy, and existential therapy.

Evidence and common factors

Meta-analyses generally find psychotherapy effective, with moderate effect sizes across many conditions. Research on "common factors" finds that the therapeutic alliance, empathy and client expectations account for much of the outcome variance across schools — the so-called Dodo bird verdict that bona fide therapies produce broadly comparable results, though specific techniques outperform for particular disorders (for example exposure therapy for phobias and PTSD).

Regulation and delivery

Regulation varies widely: titles such as "psychotherapist" are protected in some jurisdictions and unregulated in others. Therapy may be provided in private practice, hospitals, community mental-health services and digital platforms, and is often combined with medication for moderate-to-severe illness.
