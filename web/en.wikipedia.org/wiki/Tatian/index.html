Tatian of Adiabene, or Tatian the Syrian or Tatian the Assyrian (Latin: Tatianus; Greek: Τατιανός; Syriac: ܛܛܝܢܘܣ; c. 120 – c. 180 AD), was an Assyrian Christian writer and theologian of the 2nd century.

Tatian's most influential work is the Diatessaron, a Biblical paraphrase, or "harmony", of the four gospels that became for a long period of time the standard text of the four gospels in the Syriac-speaking churches, from which it was translated into many languages.

Tatian is also known for his writings To the Greeks (or Oratio ad Graecos, "Address to the Greeks"). This lengthy work is an exposition of the non-Greek origins of philosophy, yet most of it is a bitter denunciation of everything Greek (in translation: "All is spurious and untrustworthy, for all things with you have a great appearance only").

Life

Concerning the date and place of his birth, little is known beyond what he tells about himself in his Oratio ad Graecos, chap. xlii: he was born in "the land of the Assyrians", a designation that may refer to the ancient lands of Assyria, including Mesopotamia, though the precise extent of the designation is debated. However, such an identity would make him one of the first ethnic Assyrians of historical note, alongside perhaps Lucian of Samosata.

He travelled to Rome and first encountered Christianity there, where he also met Justin Martyr, to whom he became a pupil. It was around this time, roughly in the 160s, that Tatian composed his harmony of the gospels, the Diatessaron.

After the death of Justin, c. 165, Tatian was expelled from the church in Rome following accusations of heresy — specifically of following the Encratites, an ascetic sect that rejected marriage and abstained from meat and wine. Irenaeus also says Tatian became a follower of the Gnostic teacher Valentinus, though other sources suggest his divergence was more modest.

Tatian subsequently journeyed eastwards and is said to have founded a school of theology among his fellow Assyrians and in Mesopotamia in general. The date and place of his death are unknown, though he is believed to have died around 180 AD, possibly in Adiabene.

Works

Tatian's chief work, and the earliest known harmony of the Gospels, is the Diatessaron, a title that means "through four". The work wove the four canonical gospels into a single continuous narrative. It remained the standard gospel text of the Syriac church for centuries, until superseded by the Peshitta in the 5th century. Several other works are attributed to Tatian but are now lost.

His Oratio ad Graecos ("Address to the Greeks", c. 165) is a zealous defence of Christianity against Greek paganism, denouncing Greek philosophy, art, and culture as inferior to the "barbarian" wisdom of Moses and the prophets, whose antiquity Tatian argued proved their truth.

Theology

Tatian's theology combined elements of his teacher Justin's Logos doctrine with a strong ascetic tendency. His Encratite leanings led him to regard matter and marriage with suspicion, positions the wider church rejected. Later writers, including Eusebius, treated him cautiously: praising the Diatessaron's industry while questioning his orthodoxy.
