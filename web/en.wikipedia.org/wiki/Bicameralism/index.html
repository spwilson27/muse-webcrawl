Bicameralism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bicameralism is a type of legislature divided into two separate assemblies or houses, known as a bicameral legislature. The two houses are often styled the upper house and the lower house, differing in powers, size, mode of election and term length. Bicameralism contrasts with unicameralism, in which all legislators deliberate in a single assembly.

The arrangement originated in medieval estates assemblies and was rationalised in federal and constitutional theory: the lower house represents the people by population, while the upper house may represent territorial units (as in the United States Senate), provide revision and delay, or represent special interests. As of the 2020s, roughly two-fifths of the world's national legislatures are bicameral, including those of most large federations.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argue that a second chamber improves scrutiny, tempers hasty legislation, protects minorities and states' rights, and embodies a further check within the legislature itself. In federations, equal representation of states in an upper house was often the price of union — the Connecticut Compromise of 1787 joined proportional representation in one American house to equal representation in the other. Critics object that second chambers duplicate work, cause deadlock, and may rest on undemocratic appointment or election; several countries, including Denmark (1953), Sweden (1970) and New Zealand (1950), abolished their upper houses.

Variations

“Strong” bicameralism gives both houses substantial and roughly equal powers (the United States, Switzerland, Australia, Italy); “weak” bicameralism subordinates the upper house, which may delay but not block most legislation (the United Kingdom's House of Lords since the Parliament Acts). Money bills are commonly reserved to the lower house.
