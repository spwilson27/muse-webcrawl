Hollywood blacklist

The Hollywood blacklist was the practice of denying employment to entertainment industry professionals during the mid-20th century in the United States, on the basis of their alleged membership in or sympathy with the Communist Party, or on their refusal to assist federal investigations into the party's activities. The blacklist involved the implementation of industry-wide practice — the denial of employment to entertainment industry professionals — rather than a single formal list, and it ruined the careers of hundreds of actors, screenwriters, directors, musicians, and others.

Origins

In 1947, the House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) held hearings into alleged Communist influences in the motion picture industry. Ten writers and directors — the Hollywood Ten — refused to answer questions about their political affiliations, citing the First Amendment, and were cited for contempt of Congress, fined, and imprisoned. In November 1947, studio executives issued the Waldorf Statement, announcing the suspension of the Ten and pledging not to employ Communists or anyone refusing to deny membership — the formal beginning of the blacklist era.

Operation and effects

During the late 1940s and 1950s, publication of lists in outlets such as Red Channels, combined with congressional and state-level investigations and pressure from organizations like the American Legion, extended the blacklist to radio and television. Those named were typically forced to appear before committees and "name names" to be cleared for work. Some blacklisted artists worked under pseudonyms or through fronts — the screenwriter Dalton Trumbo famously wrote scripts under other names, winning Academy Awards he could not publicly claim — while others left the United States to work in Europe, among them director Joseph Losey and actor Charlie Chaplin, who was effectively exiled in 1952.

End of the blacklist

The blacklist began to crack around 1960, when Trumbo received open screen credit for Exodus and Spartacus. Through the 1960s the practice faded, though its effects on individuals were lasting, and the era remains a central episode in debates over artistic freedom, political loyalty tests, and the Second Red Scare presided over in the Senate by Joseph McCarthy.
