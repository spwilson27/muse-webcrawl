Moa were nine species (in six genera) of flightless birds endemic to New Zealand. The two largest species, the South Island giant moa (Dinornis robustus) and the North Island giant moa (Dinornis novaezealandiae), reached about 3.6 metres in height with neck outstretched and weighed as much as 230 kilograms. Unlike other flightless ratites, moa had entirely lost their wings, lacking even vestigial wing bones.

Biology and ecology

Moa were herbivores that browsed and grazed on twigs, leaves, fruits, and seeds, grinding them with a large gizzard containing gastroliths. They occupied habitats from alpine shrubland to lowland forest and were the dominant herbivores of New Zealand's ecosystems. Their principal natural predator was the enormous Haast's eagle. DNA studies show moa are most closely related to the flying tinamous of South America, indicating their ancestors flew to New Zealand. Females were much larger than males — in giant moa they could be up to 150% taller and 280% heavier, an extreme case of reversed sexual dimorphism initially leading taxonomists to classify the sexes as separate species.

Extinction

Moa were hunted to extinction by the Māori, who arrived in New Zealand in the late 13th century and prized moa for meat, eggs, bones, and feathers. All species were extinct by about 1445–1500, within roughly two centuries of human settlement — one of the fastest megafaunal extinctions known. The disappearance of the moa also doomed the Haast's eagle, which lost its main prey. Well-preserved moa remains, including bones, eggshells, feathers, and even desiccated soft tissue from swamps and dry caves, are held in museums worldwide.
