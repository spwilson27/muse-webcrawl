Beef aging

Beef aging (American English) or ageing is a process of preparing beef for consumption by storing it, usually under refrigeration, to improve its tenderness and flavour. During aging, naturally occurring enzymes break down connective tissue in the meat, while controlled moisture loss concentrates flavour. There are two principal methods: dry aging and wet aging.

Dry aging

Dry aging is the traditional method: primal cuts or whole sides of beef are hung in a refrigerated, humidity-controlled room, typically at 0–3 °C (32–38 °F) with relative humidity around 75–85%, for between one and five weeks — commonly around 21 to 35 days for premium steakhouses, and occasionally much longer. Air circulation prevents bacterial growth while an outer crust forms; this crust is trimmed away before the meat is cut into steaks, causing significant yield loss. Combined with evaporative moisture loss of up to 30%, this makes dry-aged beef considerably more expensive.

Dry aging produces distinctive flavours often described as nutty, buttery, roasted or mildly funky, sometimes compared to blue cheese, resulting from enzymatic activity and controlled oxidation of fats. Only high-quality, well-marbled beef is generally considered suitable, because the process relies on even fat distribution to protect the meat.

Wet aging

In wet aging, beef is vacuum-sealed in plastic shortly after slaughter and aged in its own juices under refrigeration, usually for a few days to several weeks. The anaerobic environment prevents moisture loss, so yields are high and the process can take place during ordinary distribution. Wet-aged beef tends to have a more pronounced bloody, metallic or sour flavour note and lacks the concentrated, nutty character of dry-aged beef, but it is far more economical; the great majority of beef sold in supermarkets is wet-aged.

Enzymatic action

The tenderising effect of aging is largely due to endogenous proteolytic enzymes, principally the calpains and cathepsins, which weaken the myofibrillar structure of the muscle in the first days after slaughter and resolve rigor mortis. Flavour development continues over longer periods through lipid oxidation and the breakdown of proteins into flavour-active amino acids and peptides.
