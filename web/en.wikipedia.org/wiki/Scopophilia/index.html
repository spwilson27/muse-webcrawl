Scopophilia

Scopophilia (from Greek skopein, "to look at", and philia, "love") is the pleasure in looking. In psychoanalytic theory it refers to the erotic pleasure derived from looking at another person or object of desire, and it is closely related to voyeurism. The term was used by Sigmund Freud in his Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality (1905), where scopophilia (in German, Schaulust) appears as one of the component drives of sexuality: looking is an active aim, whose passive counterpart is exhibitionism.

In psychoanalysis

For Freud, the scopophilic drive takes other people as objects and subjects them to a controlling and curious gaze. Jacques Lacan later developed the concept of "the gaze" (le regard) as one of the forms of the objet petit a: in the scopic drive, the subject is not the master of vision but is itself caught within the field of the visible.

In film theory

The concept became central to film studies through Laura Mulvey's influential 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema", published in Screen. Mulvey argued that classical Hollywood cinema structures looking around two pleasures: scopophilia — taking other people as objects of erotic visual stimulation — and narcissistic identification with the screen image of the (usually male) protagonist. She coined the term "the male gaze" for the way mainstream film positions the spectator as masculine and the woman as image, "to-be-looked-at-ness". Her essay helped found feminist film theory and remains a standard reference in discussions of spectatorship, voyeurism and the gaze, as well as a target of criticism and revision by later theorists.
