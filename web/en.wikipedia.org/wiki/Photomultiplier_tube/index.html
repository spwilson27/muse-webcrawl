Photomultiplier tube

Photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), also called photomultipliers or photoelectric multipliers, are extremely sensitive detectors of light in the ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared ranges of the electromagnetic spectrum. They multiply the current produced by incident light by as much as 108, enabling the detection of individual photons.

Principle of operation

A PMT is an evacuated glass tube containing a photocathode, a series of electrodes called dynodes, and an anode. A photon striking the photocathode ejects an electron by the photoelectric effect. This photoelectron is accelerated onto the first dynode, where its impact liberates several secondary electrons; these are accelerated onto the next dynode, and the cascade repeats across typically 9–14 dynodes. The resulting pulse of millions of electrons at the anode constitutes a measurable signal for a single detected photon. Gains depend on the high voltage (hundreds to thousands of volts) applied across the dynode chain.

Applications

Because of their sensitivity, speed, and low noise, photomultipliers have been used in scintillation counters and particle physics detectors (including the huge water Cherenkov detectors such as Super-Kamiokande, which uses thousands of large PMTs), medical imaging (gamma cameras and PET scanners), astronomy, analytical instruments such as spectrophotometers and flow cytometers, and night-vision devices. Although solid-state devices such as avalanche photodiodes and silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) have displaced PMTs in many roles, PMTs remain preferred for very large-area, ultra-low-light detection.
