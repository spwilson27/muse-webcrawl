Nyingma

The Nyingma school (Tibetan: རྙིང་མ་, "the ancient ones" or "old school") is the oldest of the four major schools of Tibetan Buddhism, tracing its origins to the first dissemination of Buddhism in Tibet in the eighth century under King Trisong Detsen. Its founding figures are the Indian tantric master Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche), the scholar-monk Śāntarakṣita, and the king himself, who together established Samye, Tibet's first monastery, around 779 CE.

Teachings and literature

The Nyingma tradition classifies Buddhist teaching into nine vehicles (yānas), culminating in Dzogchen, the "Great Perfection", which its adherents regard as the direct path to recognizing the nature of mind. Its canon includes texts transmitted from the imperial period (kama, the "long transmission") and revealed treasures (terma) said to have been concealed by Padmasambhava and unearthed by later "treasure revealers" (tertöns). Great systematizers include Longchenpa (1308–1364), whose Seven Treasuries remain central, and Jigme Lingpa (1730–1798), author of the Longchen Nyingthig cycle.

Organization

Unlike the Gelug school, Nyingma historically had no single central authority or head; it comprises numerous monastic and lay lineages, with important seats including Mindrolling, Dorje Drak, Shechen, and Kathok monasteries. A nominal head of the school was appointed only in exile, in India after 1959. The tradition is notable for its strong tradition of non-celibate yogic practitioners (ngakpa) alongside monastics.
