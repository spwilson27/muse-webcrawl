The Kosovo myth (Serbian: Косовски мит, Kosovski mit), also known as the Kosovo covenant (Косовски завет, Kosovski zavet) or Kosovo legend, is a Serbian national myth based on a legend that grew up around the Battle of Kosovo of 1389. It is a central theme of Serbian folklore and epic poetry, and a cornerstone of modern Serbian national identity.

The legend

According to the legend, on the eve of the battle against the Ottoman army of Sultan Murad I, Prince Lazar Hrebeljanović was visited by a prophet — in some versions an angel, in others Saint Elijah in the form of a grey falcon — who offered him a choice between an earthly kingdom (victory in the battle, but ruin for his people afterwards) and a heavenly kingdom (defeat and death, but eternal glory and the survival of the faith). Lazar chose the heavenly kingdom, cursing any Serb who would not come to fight at Kosovo.

"Whoever is a Serb and of Serb birth, and of blood and origin, and comes not to the Battle of Kosovo, may he never have the progeny his heart desires, neither son nor daughter..." — from the Kosovo cycle

Other elements of the cycle include the hero Miloš Obilić, who feigned defection and assassinated Sultan Murad in his tent; the alleged treason of Vuk Branković, who is said to have withdrawn his troops; the Kosovo Maiden, who searched the battlefield for her betrothed among the wounded; and the deaths of the nine Jugović brothers with their father, old Jug Bogdan.

Development

The earliest accounts of the battle were sparse and contradictory; the myth crystallised over centuries through the oral epic tradition, carried by guslars (singers to the gusle) and preserved in monastic centres. In the 19th century the folk songs were collected and published by Vuk Karadžić, and the Kosovo cycle became the moral foundation of the Serbian national revival. The 600th anniversary of the battle in 1989, marked by a mass commemoration at Gazimestan, coincided with the rise of Slobodan Milošević and a renewed political mobilisation of the myth.

Interpretation

Historians note that the myth's central motifs — the conscious choice of a heavenly kingdom, Obilić's assassination of the sultan, and Branković's betrayal — are largely later constructions not supported by contemporary sources. Scholars such as Noel Malcolm have treated the cycle as literature rather than history, while others emphasise its genuine role in sustaining Serbian identity under Ottoman rule. The myth remains politically potent in disputes over Kosovo's status.
