Ba'athism

Ba'athism (from Arabic baʿth, "renaissance" or "resurrection") is an Arab nationalist ideology that promotes the development and creation of a unified Arab state through the leadership of a vanguard party over a socialist government. It was founded by Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and Zaki al-Arsuzi in Syria in the 1940s.

FoundersMichel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, Zaki al-Arsuzi
Founded1940s, Damascus, Syria
IdeologyArab nationalism, Arab socialism, pan-Arabism, secularism
SloganUnity, Freedom, Socialism

Principles

The Ba'ath Party motto "Unity, Freedom, Socialism" expressed its program: unity of the Arab nation across colonial borders, freedom from foreign imperialism, and a distinctively Arab socialism. Aflaq, a Christian, treated Islam as a cultural achievement of the Arab nation, making the ideology largely secular. The party organized as a hierarchical vanguard with regional (national) and national (pan-Arab) commands.

Power in Syria and Iraq

Ba'ath parties took power in Iraq in 1963 and again in 1968, and in Syria in 1963. A 1966 Syrian coup split the movement into rival Syrian- and Iraqi-dominated parties that became bitter enemies. In Iraq the party ruled under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr and then Saddam Hussein until the 2003 invasion. In Syria, Hafez al-Assad's Corrective Movement of 1970 concentrated power, continued by his son Bashar al-Assad until the fall of the Assad regime in December 2024.

Criticism and legacy

In practice Ba'athist governments became authoritarian one-party states marked by personality cults, security services, and suppression of Islamists, Kurds, and other opponents. Critics argue Ba'athism's pan-Arab project failed, leaving national regimes that used its slogans to legitimize rule. See also Arab nationalism and pan-Arabism.
