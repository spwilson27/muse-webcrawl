Race and ethnicity in the United States census

The United States census has included questions on race and ethnicity since the first census of 1790. The categories have changed in every census, reflecting shifting social, political and scientific understandings. The data are used for apportionment, civil-rights enforcement, and the allocation of federal funds.

Standards

Since 1977 the Office of Management and Budget's Statistical Policy Directive No. 15 has set the minimum categories. The 1997 revision defined five races — White; Black or African American; American Indian or Alaska Native; Asian; and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander — and treated Hispanic or Latino as an ethnicity, with respondents able to select more than one race. A 2024 revision combined race and ethnicity into a single question, added a Middle Eastern or North African category, and requires more detailed data collection by default.

History

Early censuses distinguished free white males, free white females, other free persons, and slaves; the 1890 census used subdivisions such as "mulatto", "quadroon" and "octoroon". Enumerators assigned race by observation until self-identification became standard in the later twentieth century. The 2000 census first allowed respondents to mark more than one race; the 2020 census showed a sharp rise in multiracial responses, partly because of improved question design.
