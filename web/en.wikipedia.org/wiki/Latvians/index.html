Latvians

Latvians (Latvian: latvieši) are a Baltic ethnic group native to Latvia, speaking Latvian, an Indo-European language of the Baltic branch. Ethnic Latvians number roughly 1.2 million in Latvia — about 62 percent of the country's population — with significant diaspora communities in the United States, Canada, Australia, Germany, Ireland, and the United Kingdom.

History

The Latvians descend from Baltic tribes — including the Latgalians, Semigallians, Selonians, and Curonians — who merged under German, Swedish, Polish, and Russian rule between the 13th and 18th centuries. Serfdom was abolished in the 19th century, and a National Awakening produced a Latvian literary language, press, and cultural institutions, leading to the proclamation of independent Latvia in 1918. Soviet and Nazi occupations and mass emigration in 1944 greatly reduced the population; independence was restored in 1991.

Culture

Latvian culture is noted for its folk songs (dainas), of which over a million texts have been collected, and for the national Song and Dance Festival. Traditional celebrations include Jāņi (Midsummer). Most Latvians are historically Lutheran, with Catholic and Orthodox minorities; today a large share are unaffiliated. Latvian language and culture are protected by law as central to the identity of the Latvian state.
