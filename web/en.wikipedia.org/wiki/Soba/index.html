Soba

Chilled soba on a zaru draining basket

Soba (そば or 蕎麦, "buckwheat") are Japanese noodles made of buckwheat flour and sometimes wheat flour (such as in a ni-hachi, "two-eight," 20:80 wheat-to-buckwheat mixture). They are roughly as thick as spaghetti, brownish-gray in color, and served either chilled with a dipping sauce (tsuyu) or hot in a soup-like broth, often with toppings such as tempura, sliced scallions, or nori.

History

Buckwheat has been cultivated in Japan since prehistory, but soba noodles in their present form became popular in the Edo period (1603–1868), when Tokyo (Edo) developed a culture of fast-food stalls and soba shops serving the working population. A tradition holds that eating soba on New Year's Eve (toshikoshi soba) symbolizes longevity and letting go of the year's hardships, since the noodles break easily.

Varieties and serving

Regional varieties include Shinshū soba from Nagano, Izumo soba from Shimane, and cha soba flavored with green tea. Jūwari soba is made with 100% buckwheat. Cold styles include mori soba and zaru soba, eaten by dipping the noodles into tsuyu; after the meal, the starchy cooking water (sobayu) is mixed with the remaining sauce and drunk. Hot dishes include kake soba in broth, tempura soba, kitsune soba with sweetened abura-age, and tanuki soba with tempura batter flakes. Buckwheat hull flour is also used in soba-gaki dumplings and in galettes in other cuisines.

Nutrition

Soba are a source of protein, manganese, thiamine, and the flavonoid rutin; pure buckwheat noodles are naturally gluten-free, though commercial varieties often contain wheat.
