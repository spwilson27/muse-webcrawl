Electrospray ionization

Electrospray ionization (ESI) is a technique used in mass spectrometry to produce ions using an electrospray in which a high voltage is applied to a liquid to create an aerosol. It is especially useful in producing ions from macromolecules because it overcomes the propensity of these molecules to fragment when ionized. ESI is a "soft" ionization technique that leaves molecules largely intact and often produces multiply charged ions, extending the effective mass range of analysers to very large biomolecules.

Mechanism

Analyte solution flows through a needle held at several kilovolts relative to a counter-electrode. The strong electric field disperses the liquid into a fine spray of charged droplets (the Taylor cone regime). As solvent evaporates—assisted by heated gas—the droplets shrink until Coulomb repulsion overcomes surface tension at the Rayleigh limit and they fission into smaller droplets. Ultimately, gas-phase ions are released, either by complete evaporation (charged-residue model) or by direct ejection from the droplet surface (ion-evaporation model).

History

Malcolm Dole demonstrated molecular beams of macroions in the 1960s, and John Fenn's group at Yale developed ESI for mass spectrometry of large molecules in the 1980s. Fenn shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the development of soft desorption ionization methods for mass spectrometric analyses of biological macromolecules.

Applications

ESI is the dominant ionization method for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS), enabling proteomics, metabolomics, pharmaceutical analysis, and intact-protein mass measurement. Variants include nanoelectrospray, which uses microlitre-per-minute-scale flows for high sensitivity with scarce samples.
