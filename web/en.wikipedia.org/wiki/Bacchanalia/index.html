Bacchanalia

The Bacchanalia were unofficial, privately funded popular Roman festivals of Bacchus, the Greco-Roman god of wine, freedom, intoxication and ecstasy. Based on the Greek Dionysia, the rites were initially held in secret three times a year and open only to women, but later spread to men and became much more frequent.

History and suppression

According to the historian Livy, the cult was introduced into Rome from southern Italy around 200 BC. By 186 BC it had grown dramatically, allegedly drawing in thousands of initiates, including men and members of the elite. Livy portrays the rites as a cover for crime and conspiracy, and his account drove the Roman Senate to act.

In 186 BC the Senate issued the Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus, a decree preserved on a bronze inscription, which effectively banned the Bacchanalia throughout Italy except in certain carefully controlled cases. The cult was forced into small groups of no more than five worshippers meeting with senatorial permission. Despite the crackdown — Livy claims some 7,000 people were implicated — worship of Bacchus continued in more private or officially tolerated forms.

Legacy

In modern usage, "bacchanalia" has come to mean any uninhibited, drunken or orgiastic celebration. The festivals are frequently depicted in Renaissance and Baroque art, notably by Titian and Poussin.
