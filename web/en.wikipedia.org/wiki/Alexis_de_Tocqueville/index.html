Alexis de Tocqueville

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A portrait of Alexis de Tocqueville, the young French aristocrat who analysed American democracy]
A portrait of Alexis de Tocqueville, the young French aristocrat who analysed American democracy

Alexis Charles Henri Clérel, comte de Tocqueville (29 July 1805 – 16 April 1859) was a French diplomat, political scientist, historian and politician, best known for Democracy in America (1835/1840) and The Old Regime and the Revolution (1856). His analysis of American democracy — its equality of conditions, voluntary associations, tyranny of the majority and 'individualism' — remains foundational to political science.

America

Sent with Gustave de Beaumont in 1831 to study American prisons, Tocqueville spent nine months travelling the young republic, interviewing everyone from President Andrew Jackson to frontiersmen. Democracy in America — half celebration, half warning — saw equality as providential and irresistible, feared soft despotism of a tutelary state over atomised citizens, and praised townships, juries, churches and associations as democracy's schools and safeguards. Its treatment of slavery and Native American dispossession as democracy's crimes was unusually clear-eyed.

Later career and the Old Regime

Tocqueville served as a deputy (1839–51) and briefly as foreign minister (1849), opposed Louis-Napoléon's coup, and withdrew to write The Old Regime and the Revolution (1856), arguing that the Revolution completed, rather than reversed, Bourbon centralisation — the 'Tocqueville thesis' of continuity.

Influence

Neglected in France for a century, Tocqueville was revived after the Second World War as liberalism's prophet against totalitarianism; in America he is quoted by politicians of every party and anchors debates on civil society (Robert Putnam's Bowling Alone), religion in public life and democratic decay.
