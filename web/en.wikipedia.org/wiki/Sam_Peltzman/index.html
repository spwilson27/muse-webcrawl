Sam Peltzman

Born: 1940, New York City, U.S.

Fields: Economics, regulation, industrial organisation

Institutions: University of Chicago Booth School of Business

Known for: Peltzman effect; economic theory of regulation

Sam Peltzman (born 1940) is an American economist and the Ralph and Dorothy Keller Distinguished Service Professor Emeritus of Economics at the University of Chicago Booth School of Business. He is known for his work on the economics of regulation, government policy, and the unintended behavioural responses to safety regulation.

Career

Peltzman received his PhB from the City College of New York in 1960 and his PhD in economics from the University of Chicago in 1965. Before joining the Chicago Booth faculty in 1973, he taught at the University of California, Los Angeles. He served as editor of the Journal of Political Economy and directed the George J. Stigler Center for the Study of the Economy and the State.

Research

In a celebrated 1976 paper, Peltzman developed a general economic theory of regulation, extending George Stigler's work by modelling how regulators balance the interests of producers and consumers. His 1975 study of automobile safety regulation argued that mandatory safety devices could be partly offset by changes in driver behaviour — an idea that came to be known as the Peltzman effect, a form of risk compensation.

He also conducted influential research on banking, the market for pharmaceuticals and drug regulation, the size and growth of government, and the effect of regulation on industry prices and innovation.

Selected publications

"Toward a More General Theory of Regulation" (1976); "The Effects of Automobile Safety Regulation" (1975); "How Efficient Is the Market for Professional Licenses?" and works on the economics of higher education.
