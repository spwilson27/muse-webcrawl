In mathematics, homology is a general way of associating a sequence of algebraic objects, such as abelian groups or modules, to other mathematical objects such as topological spaces. Homology groups capture information about the "holes" of a space in each dimension.

Overview

To a topological space X one assigns a chain complex of groups Cn connected by boundary operators ∂ with ∂∘∂ = 0. The n-th homology group is the quotient Hn(X) = ker ∂n / im ∂n+1: cycles modulo boundaries. For example, a sphere S² has H₀ = ℤ, H₂ = ℤ, and vanishing higher homology, while a torus has H₁ = ℤ² corresponding to loops around its two holes.

History

Homology grew out of Henri Poincaré's foundational 1895 paper Analysis Situs, which introduced Betti numbers and torsion coefficients. In the 1920s Emmy Noether and others recast these invariants as groups, and the axiomatic theory of Eilenberg and Steenrod (1945–52) unified the various constructions, such as simplicial and singular homology.

Applications

Homology is fundamental in algebraic topology and, via homological algebra, throughout modern mathematics. Cohomology, its dual theory, underlies characteristic classes and much of algebraic geometry; persistent homology is used in topological data analysis.
