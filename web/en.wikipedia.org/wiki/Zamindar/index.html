Zamindar

A zamindar (from Persian zamīn, "land", + -dār, "holder") was a hereditary landholder in South Asia — particularly in Mughal and British India — who held large estates and the right to collect revenue or rent from the cultivators on them. Zamindars ranged from powerful, quasi-autonomous chiefs ruling thousands of villages to modest local proprietors.

Under the Mughals

In the Mughal Empire, zamindars were a broad category of local landholding elites who collected land revenue on behalf of the state and maintained order in their territories, retaining a share of the collections. Their rights were hereditary but conditional on loyalty and payment; the state could and did intervene against recalcitrant zamindars.

Under British rule

The East India Company's Permanent Settlement of 1793, introduced by Lord Cornwallis in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, transformed zamindars into proprietors of their estates in the English sense, with revenue demand fixed in perpetuity — provided payments were made punctually, failing which estates could be auctioned. Zamindari estates became the landed aristocracy of eastern India, while other regions of British India used different systems, such as ryotwari and mahalwari settlements.

Abolition

After independence, both India and Pakistan moved to abolish the zamindari system as part of land reform. In India, state-level zamindari abolition acts of the early 1950s extinguished the intermediary rights between the state and the cultivator, compensating the former zamindars; East Pakistan (now Bangladesh) abolished the system under the East Bengal State Acquisition and Tenancy Act of 1950. The reforms reshaped rural society, though implementation and their effect on land inequality varied widely.
