SN 1572, known as Tycho's Supernova or Tycho's Nova, was a Type Ia supernova that appeared in the constellation Cassiopeia in November 1572. One of only about eight supernovae in the Milky Way ever visible to the naked eye, it rivalled Venus in brightness and was visible in daylight for about two weeks. Its careful measurement by the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe helped shatter the Aristotelian doctrine that the heavens above the Moon were unchanging.

Observation and significance

The new star was seen across Europe and recorded as far away as China and Korea. Tycho, then 25, observed it from 11 November 1572 and published his measurements in De nova stella ("On the new star", 1573) — the work that popularised the term "nova" for such events. Crucially, he found that the object showed no measurable diurnal parallax, placing it far beyond the Moon, in the supposedly perfect and immutable celestial realm. Along with the comet of 1577, whose parallax he also measured, the supernova was a landmark in the breakdown of the Aristotelian cosmos and the Scientific Revolution.

The remnant

The expanding remnant, about 8,000–9,800 light-years away, is a classic Type Ia remnant: a roughly circular shell some 20–30 light-years wide, bright in radio (catalogued as 3C 10) and X-rays, with filaments of shocked gas and evidence of cosmic-ray acceleration. In 2004 astronomers identified a Sun-like star, Tycho G, near the remnant's centre, proposed as the surviving donor companion of the exploded white dwarf; in 2008, light echoes from the original explosion were spectroscopically observed, confirming the event was a normal Type Ia supernova.
