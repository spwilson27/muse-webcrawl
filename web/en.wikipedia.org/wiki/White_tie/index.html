White tie

White tie, also called full evening dress or tails, is the most formal dress code in Western evening wear. It is worn for occasions after 6 p.m. such as state banquets, formal balls, opera premieres and the Nobel Prize ceremony. For men it consists of a black tailcoat worn over a white waistcoat and a white bow tie, from which the code takes its name. The standard female equivalent is a full-length ball gown.

White tie: black tailcoat, white marcella waistcoat, wing-collared shirt and white bow tie.

Components for men

  • Tailcoat — a black or midnight-blue wool coat with peaked lapels faced in silk, cut away at the front and falling in two tails at the back; it is left unbuttoned.
  • Trousers — matching trousers with a single stripe of silk braid down each leg (two stripes in some American usage), worn with braces rather than a belt.
  • Shirt — a white dress shirt with a stiff marcella (piqué) bib front, a detachable wing collar and single cuffs fastened with cufflinks.
  • Bow tie — a white marcella bow tie, traditionally tied by hand.
  • Waistcoat — a low-cut white marcella waistcoat, single- or double-breasted, cut so that it does not show below the front of the tailcoat.
  • Footwear — black patent leather court shoes (opera pumps) or polished Oxford shoes, with black socks.
  • Accessories — shirt studs and cufflinks, often of mother-of-pearl; a white pocket square. Decorations, orders and medals may be worn where the occasion permits, as are a black top hat and white gloves in the most traditional settings.

History

White tie developed from the court dress of the late eighteenth century. Around 1800 the frock coat and tailcoat became fashionable formal wear, and by the Victorian era the black tailcoat with white waistcoat and tie had crystallised as the uniform of the gentleman in the evening. It remained standard formal wear through the Edwardian period. After the First World War the shorter dinner jacket of the black tie code — itself originally considered informal — progressively displaced white tie for most occasions, and after the Second World War full evening dress retreated to the most ceremonial events.

Occasions

White tie survives at state dinners at the White House and European courts, the Nobel Prize award ceremony in Stockholm (where it is obligatory for laureates and guests), Viennese balls, May Balls at Cambridge, doctoral conferment ceremonies in Finland and Sweden, and certain livery company and diplomatic functions. Invitations specify "white tie" explicitly; it ranks above black tie, which in turn is more formal than a lounge suit.

Women's dress

For women, white tie calls for a full-length evening or ball gown, often with long gloves, and hair worn up; tiaras may be worn by married women where the occasion and local custom allow. There is no exact female counterpart to the regulated male uniform, and conventions vary by country and event.
