Scrum (software development)

The Scrum framework: work proceeds in fixed-length sprints

Scrum is an agile framework for developing, delivering, and sustaining complex products, most widely used in software development. Within Scrum, work is organised into short, fixed-length iterations called sprints (usually one to four weeks), at the end of which a usable increment of the product is produced. Scrum is defined by the Scrum Guide, written by its creators Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland, and is the most popular of the agile methods that followed the 2001 Agile Manifesto.

History

The term comes from a 1986 Harvard Business Review article by Hirotaka Takeuchi and Ikujiro Nonaka, "The New New Product Development Game", which compared cross-functional product teams to a rugby scrum. Sutherland created the first Scrum team in 1993, and he and Schwaber presented the framework publicly in 1995. The name of this article, Scrum (software development), distinguishes the framework from the rugby formation.

Accountabilities, events, and artifacts

A Scrum team has three accountabilities: the Product Owner, who owns and orders the product backlog to maximise value; the Scrum Master, who coaches the team and removes impediments; and the Developers, who build the increment. The framework's events are the sprint itself, sprint planning, the daily scrum (a 15-minute stand-up meeting), the sprint review with stakeholders, and the sprint retrospective for process improvement. Its artifacts are the product backlog, the sprint backlog, and the product increment, each with an associated commitment (product goal, sprint goal, and definition of done). Scrum is founded on empiricism — transparency, inspection, and adaptation — and the Scrum Guide is deliberately kept minimal, leaving teams to add complementary practices.

Reception

Scrum has been adopted far beyond software, in fields from education to marketing, and large-scale variants such as SAFe, LeSS, and Scrum@Scale adapt it to many teams. Critics argue that in practice Scrum is often implemented bureaucratically ("Scrum-but", cargo-cult stand-ups, sprint metrics used as performance targets) and that its fixed cadence fits some kinds of work poorly; supporters credit its simplicity and inspection rhythm for its durability.
