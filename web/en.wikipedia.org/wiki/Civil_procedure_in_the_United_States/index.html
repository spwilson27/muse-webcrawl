Civil procedure in the United States consists of the rules and standards by which courts conduct civil trials — lawsuits seeking damages, injunctions, or other non-criminal relief — as distinct from criminal procedure. Federal courts follow the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, adopted in 1938, which merged law and equity and introduced a unified "civil action"; most states have modelled their own rules on them.

Stages of a case

A case typically begins with the plaintiff filing a complaint and serving process on the defendant, who answers or moves to dismiss. Under the plausibility standard of Twombly and Iqbal, the complaint must state a claim that is plausible on its face. The parties then engage in discovery — interrogatories, depositions, document requests, and expert disclosures — which is unusually broad in the US system. Many cases end in settlement or summary judgment; the remainder go to trial, by jury where the Seventh Amendment or state law provides, followed by possible appeal and enforcement of the judgment.

Distinctive features

Features often noted by comparative lawyers include extensive discovery, the class action (Rule 23), contingent-fee representation, punitive damages, and the general "American rule" that each side bears its own attorneys' fees. Under the Erie doctrine, federal courts sitting in diversity apply state substantive law but federal procedural law. Large-scale fraud litigation, such as the class actions following the scandals described under accounting scandals and the Madoff collapse (handled largely through a bankruptcy trustee's adversary proceedings), illustrate the system's mechanisms for mass claims.
