Abortion

Abortion is the termination of a pregnancy by removal or expulsion of an embryo or fetus. An abortion that occurs without intervention is known as a miscarriage or spontaneous abortion. When deliberate steps are taken to end a pregnancy, it is called an induced abortion. Modern medical methods use medication or surgical procedures.

Methods

Medical abortion uses drugs — most commonly mifepristone followed by misoprostol — and is widely used in the first trimester and increasingly in the second. Surgical methods include vacuum aspiration, used in early pregnancy, and dilation and evacuation (D&E), used later. The World Health Organization recommends access to safe methods performed by trained providers; when performed legally by trained professionals, abortion carries low risks of major complications.

Incidence

Estimates by the Guttmacher Institute and WHO put the number of induced abortions worldwide at about 73 million per year during 2015–2019, corresponding to a rate of roughly 39 per 1,000 women aged 15–49. Rates have declined in developed regions since the 1990s, largely attributed to increased contraceptive use.

Law and regulation

Abortion law varies widely by jurisdiction, from broad legality on request within gestational limits (as in much of Europe and Canada, which has no federal abortion law) to near-total prohibition in a small number of countries. In the United States, the 1973 Roe v. Wade decision recognized a constitutional right to abortion, which was overturned by Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization in 2022, returning regulation to the individual states.

Ethics and debate

Public debate centres on the moral status of the fetus, the bodily autonomy of the pregnant person, and the proper role of the state. Positions are commonly described as "pro-choice" (supporting legal access) and "pro-life" (supporting restriction). Religious traditions differ in their teachings, and views within traditions vary.
