Quaternion

Quaternion
Type: Hypercomplex number system
Introduced: 1843
Inventor: William Rowan Hamilton
Dimension: 4 over the real numbers

In mathematics, the quaternions are a number system that extends the complex numbers. Quaternions were first described by the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton in 1843 and applied to mechanics in three-dimensional space. Hamilton defined a quaternion as the quotient of two vectors in three-dimensional space, or equivalently as the quotient of two oriented lines in space.

Initially regarded as mysterious and of dubious practicality, quaternions eventually found applications in describing spatial orientation and rotation. They were largely displaced in many applications by matrices and Euler angles until their resurgence in the late 20th century, particularly in computer graphics, signal processing, robotics, and flight dynamics.

Definition

A quaternion is generally represented in the form a + bi + cj + dk, where a, b, c, and d are real numbers, and i, j, and k are the fundamental quaternion units. The units satisfy the multiplication rules:

i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1

Unlike the multiplication of real or complex numbers, quaternion multiplication is not commutative: ij = k but ji = −k. This non-commutativity makes the quaternions an example of a noncommutative division algebra, and indeed the first such system discovered.

History

Hamilton later claimed that the idea came to him on 16 October 1843 as he walked along the Royal Canal in Dublin with his wife, and that he was so struck by the discovery that he carved the fundamental formula into the stone of Brougham Bridge. A plaque there now commemorates the event.

The quaternions prompted the development of vector analysis; Josiah Willard Gibbs and Oliver Heaviside extracted the dot product and cross product of vectors from Hamilton's quaternion product, giving the modern formalism of three-dimensional vector calculus.

Conjugation, norm, and inverse

The conjugate of a quaternion q = a + bi + cj + dk is q* = a − bi − cj − dk. The norm of q is |q| = √(a² + b² + c² + d²), and every nonzero quaternion has a multiplicative inverse q⁻¹ = q* / |q|². Unit quaternions — those of norm 1 — form a group under multiplication and provide a convenient representation of rotations in three dimensions.

Applications

Unit quaternions avoid the gimbal lock problem that afflicts Euler angles and require fewer parameters than rotation matrices, so they are widely used in computer graphics, animation, spacecraft attitude control, and robotics. They also appear in number theory, where they are used in proofs of Lagrange's four-square theorem.
