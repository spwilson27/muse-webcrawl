Duress in English law

Duress in English law is a complete common law defence to criminal liability, and a ground for rescinding contracts. A person acts under duress when unlawful threats of death or serious injury leave them no real choice but to commit an offence, or when illegitimate pressure vitiates consent to a contract. Duress is unavailable as a defence to murder and attempted murder.

Criminal duress

The modern test derives from R v Graham (1982): (1) was the defendant impelled to act as he did because he believed that if he did not, he or another would be killed or seriously injured; and (2) would a sober person of reasonable firmness, sharing the defendant's characteristics, have responded in the same way? The threat must be of death or serious injury and sufficiently immediate, and the defendant must not have voluntarily associated with criminals in a way that exposed him to compulsion (R v Hasan [2005]). In R v Howe (1987) the House of Lords held duress is no defence to murder, and in R v Gotts (1992) extended this to attempted murder.

Duress of circumstances

A related necessity-like defence, duress of circumstances, arises where the surrounding facts — rather than a human threat — compel the offence, as in cases of dangerous driving to escape attack.

Contract law

In contract, duress — including economic duress, developed in cases such as Universe Tankships v ITWF (1983) — renders a contract voidable where illegitimate pressure coerced entry into it, leaving the victim without a practical alternative.
