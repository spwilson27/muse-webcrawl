Risk-free rate

The risk-free rate of return, usually shortened to the risk-free rate, is the theoretical rate of return of an investment with zero risk of financial loss. It represents the interest an investor would expect from an absolutely risk-free investment over a specified period of time, and it serves as a foundational input in finance: asset-pricing models such as the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) build the expected return of risky assets as the risk-free rate plus a risk premium.

Proxies in practice

Because no investment is truly riskless, practitioners use the yields of high-grade government securities as proxies. Common choices are short-term United States Treasury bills for short horizons and longer-dated Treasury bonds for valuation work; German Bunds are frequently used in the euro area. Interbank offer rates and overnight indexed swap (OIS) rates have also been used, particularly for discounting derivatives, and after the 2008 financial crisis multi-curve frameworks largely replaced LIBOR-based discounting with OIS discounting at near-risk-free rates.

Limitations

The concept is an idealisation. Government bonds carry inflation risk and reinvestment risk, and even sovereign default is not impossible, as episodes such as the euro-area debt crisis showed: Greek government yields rose far above German ones, demonstrating that "government bond" does not automatically mean risk-free. Moreover, the appropriate risk-free rate depends on the currency and horizon of the cash flows being valued. Analysts therefore distinguish the nominal risk-free rate from the real (inflation-adjusted) rate and match maturities when discounting.

Applications

The risk-free rate is used in the CAPM, in Black–Scholes option pricing, in calculating the Sharpe ratio (which measures excess return over the risk-free rate per unit of volatility), and as the starting point for building discount rates in discounted cash flow valuations, where a company's cost of equity is estimated by adding risk premia to a government bond yield.
