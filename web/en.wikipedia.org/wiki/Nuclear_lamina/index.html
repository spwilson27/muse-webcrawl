Nuclear lamina

The nuclear lamina is a dense fibrillar network inside the cell nucleus of eukaryotic cells. It lies beneath the inner nuclear membrane and is composed mainly of intermediate filament proteins called lamins, together with many lamina-associated proteins. Its thickness is roughly 30–100 nanometres.

Functions

The lamina provides mechanical support to the nucleus and helps maintain its shape. It anchors chromatin and nuclear pore complexes, participates in the organisation of the genome by tethering lamina-associated domains of chromatin, and plays roles in DNA replication, transcription regulation, and the assembly and disassembly of the nucleus during cell division. During mitosis in many animals, phosphorylation of lamins triggers the reversible breakdown of the lamina and nuclear envelope.

Lamins and disease

Vertebrates express A-type lamins (lamin A and C, encoded by the LMNA gene) and B-type lamins (lamin B1 and B2). Mutations in lamin genes and lamina-associated proteins cause a group of diseases known as laminopathies, which include Emery–Dreifuss muscular dystrophy, dilated cardiomyopathy and Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, a premature-ageing disorder caused by a mutant form of lamin A called progerin.
