Hashemites

The Hashemites (Arabic: الهاشميون, romanized: al-Hāshimiyyūn), also called the House of Hashim, are an Arab royal family that has ruled parts of the Middle East since 1916. The family belongs to the Dhawu Awn clan of the Banu Qatadah branch of the Banu Hashim tribe, and claims descent from Hashim ibn Abd Manaf, the great-grandfather of the Islamic prophet Muhammad.

History

Hashemite sharifs served as rulers of Mecca from the 10th century until 1924, nominally under various imperial overlords. In 1916, Hussein bin Ali, Sharif of Mecca, launched the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire in alliance with Britain, encouraged by the McMahon–Hussein correspondence promising Arab independence.

Kingdoms

After the First World War, Hussein's sons were placed on thrones: Faisal became King of Iraq (1921–1933) and Abdullah became Emir, then King, of Transjordan (later Jordan). Hussein proclaimed himself King of the Hejaz in 1916 but lost the holy cities to Ibn Saud's forces in 1924–25. The Iraqi branch of the family was extinguished in the 14 July 1958 revolution, in which King Faisal II and his family were murdered.

Jordan

The Jordanian Hashemites survived the assassination of Abdullah I (1951), the brief reign of Talal, and the long rule of Hussein (1952–1999), who steered the kingdom through wars of 1967, Black September, and peace with Israel in 1994. His son Abdullah II has reigned since 1999. The family retains the custodianship of Islamic holy sites in Jerusalem under the 1994 treaty.
