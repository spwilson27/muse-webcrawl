Environmental sex determination is a type of sex determination in which the sex of an offspring is established by environmental conditions during development rather than by genetic factors such as sex chromosomes. The best-known mechanism is temperature-dependent sex determination (TSD), in which the incubation temperature of eggs during a sensitive period of development determines whether the embryo develops as male or female.

Mechanisms

TSD occurs in all crocodilians, most turtles, some lizards, and the tuatara. Patterns vary: in many turtles, warm eggs produce females and cool eggs males; in crocodilians, females are produced at low and high temperatures and males at intermediate ones. Other environmental cues can also determine sex — in the marine worm Bonellia viridis, larvae that settle on a female become males; in some fish, social rank or population density influences sex; and in certain plants and invertebrates, photoperiod or nutrition plays the role.

Significance

Because a single environmental variable can skew the sex ratio of an entire cohort, environmental sex determination makes some species vulnerable to climate change: warming beaches, for example, have produced strongly female-biased sea turtle populations in parts of the Great Barrier Reef. The evolution of ESD is thought to be advantageous when the environment affects the fitness of males and females differently.
