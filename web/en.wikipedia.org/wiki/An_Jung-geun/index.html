An Jung-geun (Korean: 안중근; 2 September 1879 – 26 March 1910) was a Korean independence activist who assassinated Itō Hirobumi, the former Resident-General of Korea and former Prime Minister of Japan, at Harbin railway station in Manchuria on 26 October 1909. He is revered in South Korea as a national hero and patriot, while in Japan he has historically been regarded as a terrorist, a contrast that has periodically caused diplomatic friction between the two countries.

Early life

An was born in Haeju, Hwanghae Province, into a prosperous family. He was educated in classical Chinese literature and was known in his youth for his marksmanship. He later converted to Roman Catholicism, taking the baptismal name Thomas. After Japan imposed the Eulsa Treaty of 1905, which made Korea a Japanese protectorate, An became involved in the independence movement, running schools in northwestern Korea. When armed resistance groups (the "righteous armies") formed after the forced abdication of Emperor Gojong in 1907, An joined one operating in Manchuria and the Russian Maritime Province, rising to a lieutenant-general's rank. Before his assassination mission, he and eleven comrades cut off part of a finger and wrote "Korean independence" in blood on a flag — an oath known as the Danji Dongmaeng.

Assassination of Itō Hirobumi

An held Itō, architect of the protectorate, responsible for Korea's loss of sovereignty and listed fifteen charges against him, including the murder of Empress Myeongseong and the coercion of the Eulsa Treaty. On the morning of 26 October 1909, as Itō arrived at Harbin station to meet a Russian official, An fired seven shots from a pistol, three of which struck Itō, who died shortly afterwards. An shouted "Corea Ura!" (Korean: 대한 만세, "Long live Korea!") in Russian and was arrested by Russian guards.

Trial and execution

Handed over to the Japanese, An was tried by a Japanese colonial court in Lüshun (Port Arthur). He insisted he be treated as a prisoner of war — a soldier of the Korean resistance army — rather than a common criminal, and used his trial statements to expound his vision of peace in East Asia, which he set out in an unfinished manuscript, A Treatise on Peace in the East, arguing for cooperation among Korea, Japan, and China. He was sentenced to death and hanged on 26 March 1910. His calligraphy, produced prolifically in his final months, is treasured in Korea.

Legacy

An was posthumously awarded the Order of Merit for National Foundation by South Korea in 1962. A memorial hall stands in Seoul, and a memorial was opened in Harbin in 2014 — welcomed by Seoul and criticised by Tokyo, illustrating his contested memory. His remains, buried near the prison in Lüshun, have never been recovered despite joint search efforts.
