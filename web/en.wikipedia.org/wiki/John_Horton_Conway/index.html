John Horton Conway FRS (26 December 1937 – 11 April 2020) was an English mathematician active in the theory of finite groups, knot theory, number theory, combinatorial game theory and coding theory. He was emeritus professor at Princeton University and is known to the wider public as the inventor of the cellular automaton called the Game of Life.

Cambridge career

Born in Liverpool, Conway studied at Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, where he took his PhD under Harold Davenport. At Cambridge he discovered three new sporadic simple groups (the Conway groups) while studying the Leech lattice, co-authored the ATLAS of Finite Groups (1985) — central to the classification of finite simple groups — and made conjectures linking the Monster group to modular functions that became "monstrous moonshine".

Games and numbers

In combinatorial game theory Conway founded the theory summarised in On Numbers and Games (1976), which introduced the surreal numbers — a vast number system containing the reals and the ordinals. His Game of Life (1970), popularised by Martin Gardner in Scientific American, became the most famous cellular automaton ever devised. Among many other inventions are the Doomsday algorithm for the day of the week, the look-and-say sequence, the FRACTRAN programming language, and the Free Will theorem (with Simon Kochen).

Princeton and death

Conway moved to Princeton in 1987 as John von Neumann Professor. A legendary lecturer and puzzle-setter, he died on 11 April 2020 of complications from COVID-19, aged 82.
