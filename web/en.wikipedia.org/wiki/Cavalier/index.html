Cavalier

The term Cavalier was first used by Roundheads as a term of abuse for the wealthier royalist supporters of King Charles I of England and his son Charles II during the English Civil War, the Interregnum, and the Restoration (1642 – c. 1679). It was later adopted by the Royalists themselves. Although it originated as a pejorative term for a gallant, it later became a blanket term for a Royalist.

Etymology

"Cavalier" derives from the Spanish caballero and French chevalier, both meaning a horseman or knight. Parliamentarian pamphleteers used it to suggest a swaggering, haughty, drunken and licentious foreign-style courtier. The Royalists answered by calling their opponents "Roundheads", after the short-cropped hair of London apprentices and Puritan soldiers.

Appearance and stereotype

The archetypal Cavalier wore his hair long and curled, with a wide-brimmed beaver hat turned up on one side and decorated with an ostrich plume, a lace collar or falling band, richly coloured clothing and bucket-top boots. Portraits by artists such as Anthony van Dyck helped fix this image of aristocratic elegance. The stereotype contrasted with the plain dress and godly discipline attributed to Parliament's supporters, though in reality dress and allegiance were far less sharply divided.

Notable Cavaliers

Leading Cavalier commanders included Prince Rupert of the Rhine, the King's nephew, famed for his daring cavalry charges; Sir Ralph Hopton; and the Earl of Newcastle. After the Restoration the Cavalier Parliament (1661–1679) dominated English politics and passed legislation penalising the King's former opponents and religious dissenters.

Other uses

In early modern fortification a cavalier is a raised work inside a bastion. In the American South, "Cavalier" later described the settler aristocracy of colonial Virginia, and it remains the nickname of the University of Virginia's sports teams, the Virginia Cavaliers.
