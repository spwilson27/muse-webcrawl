Nucleus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The cell nucleus is a membrane-bound organelle found in eukaryotic cells. It contains most of the cell's genetic material, organised as DNA complexed with proteins into chromatin and chromosomes, and it coordinates cell activities including growth, metabolism, protein synthesis and division. It is usually the most prominent organelle in the cell.

Structure

The nucleus is enclosed by the nuclear envelope, a double membrane pierced by nuclear pore complexes that regulate traffic of RNA and proteins. Inside, the nucleolus is the site of ribosomal RNA synthesis and ribosome assembly. The DNA–protein complex, chromatin, exists as loosely packed euchromatin (actively transcribed) and dense heterochromromatin (largely silent), condensing into visible chromosomes during division.

Function

The nucleus stores the genome and controls gene expression: transcription produces messenger RNA there, which is exported through the pores for translation in the cytoplasm. During Mitosis (in most eukaryotes) the envelope breaks down and chromosomes are segregated by the spindle.

Variations

Most cells have one nucleus, but skeletal muscle fibres and some other cells are multinucleate, mammalian red blood cells lose their nucleus as they mature, and ciliates carry two kinds of nuclei. The nucleus was described by Robert Brown in 1831.
