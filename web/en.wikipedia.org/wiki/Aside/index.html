TypeDramatic device
DefinitionA brief remark by a character intended for the audience, unheard by other characters
RelatedSoliloquy, monologue, breaking the fourth wall

An aside is a dramatic device in which a character speaks briefly to the audience — or sometimes to another character — in words that, by convention, the other characters on stage do not hear. Asides reveal a character's true thoughts or intentions and often create dramatic irony or comic effect.

Use in theatre

The aside differs from the soliloquy, in which a character alone on stage addresses the audience at length, and from the monologue, which is addressed to other characters. Asides were especially common in classical and Renaissance drama and in 19th-century melodrama: Shakespeare's villains, such as Iago and Richard III, confide their schemes to the audience in asides, while in Restoration and Augustan comedy asides delivered wit and social commentary. In performance, the aside is often signalled by a turn toward the audience, a step downstage, or a hand raised to the mouth.

Later use

Naturalistic theatre of the late 19th and 20th centuries largely abandoned the aside as artificial, though it survived in comedy, pantomime, musical theatre, and farce. Its descendants include the direct-to-camera address in film and television — from Ferris Bueller's Day Off to mockumentary interviews — which breaks the fourth wall in a similar spirit.
