Ray Milton Dolby (January 18, 1933 – September 12, 2013) was an American engineer and inventor best known for devising the Dolby noise-reduction system for audio recording and for founding Dolby Laboratories. His companding noise-reduction circuits, first marketed in 1965, dramatically reduced tape hiss in professional recording and were later adapted for consumer cassette decks, cinema sound and broadcast audio.

Early life and career

Dolby grew up in San Francisco and, while still a teenager, worked after school at Ampex, the pioneering tape-recorder company, where he contributed to early videotape recording research. He studied electrical engineering at Stanford University and, after military service, earned a PhD in physics from the University of Cambridge in 1961 as a Marshall Scholar.

Dolby Laboratories

In 1965 Dolby founded Dolby Laboratories in London, introducing the Dolby A-type noise reduction system for professional studios the same year. The simpler B-type system followed for consumer equipment in 1968 and became a standard feature of cassette decks. Dolby Stereo, launched for cinemas in the 1970s, transformed film sound, and the company's later digital formats — Dolby Digital and Dolby Atmos — became dominant standards in cinema and home entertainment.

Honours and legacy

Dolby received numerous honours, including the U.S. National Medal of Technology (1997), an Academy Award for scientific and technical achievement, Grammy and Emmy awards, and induction into the National Inventors Hall of Fame. He was appointed an Officer of the Order of the British Empire in 1987. He died in San Francisco in 2013, having suffered from Alzheimer's disease and leukaemia.
