Battle of Bạch Đằng (938)

The Battle of Bạch Đằng was a naval battle fought in 938 CE on the Bạch Đằng River in northern Vietnam, near modern Haiphong. The Vietnamese forces of Ngô Quyền decisively defeated an invasion fleet of the Chinese state of Southern Han. The victory ended roughly a thousand years of Chinese imperial domination of Vietnam and opened the way to an independent Vietnamese state; Ngô Quyền proclaimed himself king in 939 and established his capital at Cổ Loa. The battle is famous for Ngô Quyền's tactic of planting a field of iron-tipped wooden stakes beneath the tidal river waters.

Background

After the fall of the Tang dynasty, northern Vietnam (then the Tĩnh Hải circuit) enjoyed de facto autonomy under the Khúc clan and then Dương Đình Nghệ. In 937 Dương Đình Nghệ was assassinated by his subordinate Kiều Công Tiễn, who appealed to Southern Han for support. Ngô Quyền, son-in-law of Dương Đình Nghệ, raised forces to oppose the usurper, killed Kiều Công Tiễn, and prepared to meet the Southern Han fleet sent under Prince Liu Hongcao (Lưu Hoằng Tháo).

Battle

Knowing the Han fleet would enter on the tide, Ngô Quyền had thousands of sharpened wooden stakes, tipped with iron, driven into the riverbed at the mouth of the Bạch Đằng, invisible at high water. Small Vietnamese boats skirmished with the Han warships and then feigned retreat, drawing the fleet upriver past the stake field. When the tide turned and fell, the heavy Han ships were impaled and stranded on the exposed stakes; Vietnamese forces attacked with boats and from the banks, setting fires and routing the enemy. Liu Hongcao was killed, and the Southern Han emperor, receiving the survivors, abandoned the invasion.

Legacy

The victory is a founding event in Vietnamese national history. The same river and the same stake tactic were used again by Trần Hưng Đạo to destroy the Mongol Yuan invasion fleet in 1288. Ngô Quyền's capital at Cổ Loa and his mausoleum remain sites of commemoration, and the battle is a staple of Vietnamese school curricula and national mythology.
