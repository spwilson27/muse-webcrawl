The Barton Swing Aqueduct is a moveable navigable aqueduct at Barton upon Irwell in Greater Manchester, England. It carries the Bridgewater Canal across the Manchester Ship Canal, swinging open on a central pivot to allow large ships to pass beneath. It is the first—and, in Britain, the only—swing aqueduct in the world, and is regarded as one of the great feats of Victorian civil engineering.

History

The aqueduct replaced a masonry arch aqueduct, designed by James Brindley, that had carried the Bridgewater Canal over the River Irwell since 1761. When the Manchester Ship Canal was built along the Irwell valley in the 1890s, the old aqueduct's arches were too low for the large ocean-going ships the new canal was built to serve, so it was demolished in 1893 and replaced by the swing aqueduct, designed by the ship canal's engineer Edward Leader Williams and built by Andrew Handyside and Company of Derby. The aqueduct was completed in 1893 and came into use when the ship canal opened on 1 January 1894.

Design and operation

The aqueduct is a steel trough about 100 metres (330 ft) long and 5.5 metres (18 ft) wide, holding some 1.8 metres (6 ft) depth of water—about 800 tonnes of water in total. It pivots horizontally on an island in the middle of the ship canal, supported on 64 rollers, and is turned by hydraulic machinery controlled from a tower overlooking the crossing. Gates at each end of the trough, and matching gates on the canal banks, close before the aqueduct swings, so that the trough swings full of water while only a small quantity is lost from the ends of the canal.

Immediately alongside stands the Barton Road Swing Bridge, which carries the road over the ship canal in the same manner; both structures are operated from the same control tower. The aqueduct is a Grade II* listed structure.
