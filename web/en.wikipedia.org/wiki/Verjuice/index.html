Verjuice

Verjuice (from Middle French vert jus, "green juice") is a highly acidic juice made by pressing unripe grapes, crab-apples or other sour fruit. Sometimes lemon or sorrel juice, and herbs such as tarragon, are added to change the flavour. It was widely used in medieval and early modern European cooking, and remains in use in Middle Eastern cuisines.

History

Verjuice was a staple of medieval European kitchens, used much as vinegar or lemon juice is today — to sharpen sauces, deglaze pans and season meats. It appears throughout medieval cookbooks such as Le Viandier and The Forme of Cury. Unlike vinegar, it could be produced in wine-growing regions from thinnings of unripe grapes, and unlike citrus it kept well through winter. Its use in northern Europe declined as lemons became cheaper and more widely available, though French verjus never wholly disappeared.

Production and use

Modern verjuice is made from grapes picked before ripening, when acidity is high and sugar low, pressed and lightly filtered. Because it shares the flavour base of wine without the alcohol, chefs use it in place of wine or vinegar in dressings, marinades and sauces where a gentle acidity is wanted; it is a traditional accompaniment to rich meats and features in Iranian cooking (as ab-ghooreh) in stews and salads. In Lebanon and Syria, juice of unripe grapes and of sour plums is similarly used.

Revival

From the 1980s, Australian and Californian wine producers began bottling verjuice commercially, and it has been adopted by contemporary chefs as a mild alternative to vinegar that does not clash with wine served at the table.
