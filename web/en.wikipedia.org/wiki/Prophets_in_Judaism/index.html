Prophets in Judaism

In Judaism, a prophet (Hebrew: נָבִיא‎, navi, plural nevi'im) is a person chosen by God to speak on His behalf to Israel, delivering messages of warning, consolation, moral teaching, and sometimes prediction. The second book of the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) is called Nevi'im (Prophets) after them. According to rabbinic tradition, there were forty-eight male and seven female prophets whose messages were recorded for all generations, though many more prophesied whose words were not preserved.

The Nevi'im, the prophetic books of the Tanakh

The prophetic books

The Nevi'im is divided into the Former Prophets — Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings, historical narratives in which prophets such as Samuel, Nathan, Elijah, and Elisha act — and the Latter Prophets: the three major prophets Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel, and the Book of the Twelve Minor Prophets (Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi).

Moses

Moses occupies a unique position: Jewish tradition holds that no prophet arose in Israel like Moses, who spoke with God "face to face" and received the Torah. In Maimonides' Thirteen Principles of Faith, the supremacy of Moses' prophecy and the truth of prophecy in general are fundamental articles. All later prophets are understood as calling Israel back to the covenant given through Moses rather than founding a new one.

Female prophets

The Talmud (Megillah 14a) lists seven prophetesses: Sarah, Miriam, Deborah, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah, and Esther. Deborah also served as a judge of Israel, and Huldah authenticated the book of the Law found in the Temple in the reign of Josiah.

End of prophecy

Rabbinic Judaism teaches that prophecy departed from Israel after the last prophets, Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi, in the early Second Temple period; thereafter, guidance came through the sages and the interpretation of Scripture, though a residual "echo" (bat kol, "daughter of a voice") was sometimes acknowledged. Prophecy is expected to return in the messianic age.
