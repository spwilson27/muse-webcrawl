Anti-aircraft warfare

Anti-aircraft warfare (AAW), also known as air defence, is the counter to aerial warfare: the measures designed to destroy, damage or hinder enemy aircraft, helicopters, drones and missiles. It includes ground-based and naval gun systems, surface-to-air missiles, electronic warfare, and the sensors and command networks that coordinate them. Historically the field was dominated by flak — from the German Fliegerabwehrkanone — the explosive shells fired by anti-aircraft guns.

History

Anti-aircraft fire began almost as soon as military aircraft did. During the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, Krupp guns were adapted to shoot at French balloons. In the First World War, dedicated anti-aircraft artillery, sound locators and early predictors defended cities and fleets against aeroplanes and Zeppelins. The Second World War brought radar-directed fire, heavy batteries such as the German 8.8 cm Flak, and the proximity fuze, which greatly increased shell lethality. Britain's defence during the Blitz and the Battle of Britain integrated guns, searchlights, barrage balloons and fighter aircraft into a single system.

Missile era

From the 1950s, guided surface-to-air missiles (SAMs) progressively replaced heavy guns at medium and high altitude: the Soviet S-75 famously shot down a U-2 spyplane in 1960, and SAMs shaped the air wars over Vietnam and the Middle East. Guns survived at low altitude — increasingly as radar-guided self-propelled systems and close-in weapon systems (CIWS) aboard ships — while portable MANPADS missiles gave infantry an air-defence capability.

Modern air defence

Contemporary integrated air and missile defence networks combine early-warning radar, fighters, layered SAM systems (short-, medium- and long-range) and anti-ballistic-missile interceptors such as Patriot and THAAD. New challenges include stealth aircraft, cruise missiles, hypersonic weapons and swarms of inexpensive drones, which have revived interest in gun-based and directed-energy (laser) defences to keep the cost of interception below the cost of the threat.
