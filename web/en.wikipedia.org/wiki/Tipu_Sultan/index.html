Tipu Sultan (20 November 1750 – 4 May 1799), also known as Fateh Ali Tipu and commonly called the Tiger of Mysore, was the ruler of the Sultanate of Mysore in South India from 1782 until his death in 1799. The eldest son of Hyder Ali, he succeeded his father as de facto ruler of Mysore after the Second Anglo-Mysore War had begun, and fought the British East India Company through the Third and Fourth Anglo-Mysore Wars. He was killed defending his capital, Srirangapatna, in 1799.


Tipu Sultan, the "Tiger of Mysore"

Early life and accession

Tipu was born at Devanahalli, the son of Hyder Ali, a military officer who had risen to become the de facto ruler of Mysore. He received training in military tactics, horsemanship and languages, and saw active service in the First Anglo-Mysore War while still a teenager. On Hyder Ali's death in December 1782, Tipu succeeded him and continued the war, which ended with the Treaty of Mangalore (1784).

Wars with the British

Tipu's attack on Travancore in 1789 provoked the Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792), in which a coalition of the British, the Marathas and the Nizam of Hyderabad defeated him. Under the Treaty of Seringapatam (1792) he ceded about half his territory and paid a large indemnity, surrendering two of his sons as hostages. In the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799), British forces under General George Harris stormed Srirangapatna, where Tipu was killed on 4 May 1799.

Administration and innovation

Tipu introduced administrative and economic reforms, a new calendar and coinage, and promoted sericulture and trade missions. He is noted for his pioneering use of iron-cased rockets in warfare — the Mysorean rockets later influenced the British Congreve rocket — and for the mechanical toy known as Tipu's Tiger. His tiger motif appeared on his throne, arms and coinage. Assessments of his rule, including his treatment of religious minorities in Malabar and Coorg, remain subjects of historical debate.
