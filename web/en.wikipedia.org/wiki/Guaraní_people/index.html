Guaraní people

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Guaraní are a group of culturally related indigenous peoples of South America, historically settled in the region between the Paraná, Paraguay, and Uruguay rivers — modern Paraguay, north-eastern Argentina, southern Brazil, and parts of Bolivia and Uruguay. Their language, Guaraní, is one of the most widely spoken indigenous languages of the Americas and is co-official in Paraguay, where it is spoken by the majority of the population, including many non-indigenous Paraguayans.

History

Guaraní society was semi-sedentary and agricultural, cultivating manioc, maize, and sweet potatoes, and organised into extended-family communities led by chiefs (mburuvicha) and shamans. Their mythology speaks of a search for the "Land Without Evil" (Yvy marã e'ỹ), which drove migrations across the continent.

From 1609 the Jesuits established mission settlements, the reducciones, among the Guaraní, famous for their communal organisation, music, and stone churches, and for protecting inhabitants — imperfectly — from Portuguese slave raiders (bandeirantes). The Guaraní War of 1754–1756 followed the Treaty of Madrid's attempt to transfer mission land to Portugal. After the Jesuits' expulsion in 1767 the missions declined. Guaraní soldiers formed a large part of Paraguay's army in the catastrophic Paraguayan War (1864–1870).

Culture and modern situation

Today several distinct Guaraní peoples survive, including the Ava-Guaraní, Mbyá, Pai Tavytera, and the Kaiowá of Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil — roughly 280,000 people in total. Many communities face severe pressure over land, as cattle ranching and soy farming have taken over their traditional territories; the Kaiowá in particular have campaigned for restitution of their tekoha (ancestral lands). Guaraní culture has profoundly shaped Paraguay's national identity, cuisine (such as chipa and tereré), and music, and the language's survival as a majority vernacular is a rare case in the Americas.
