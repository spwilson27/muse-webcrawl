Lord of the manor

The lord of the manor, in medieval and early modern England and parts of Europe, was the landholder of a rural estate known as a manor. The lord held the manor from a superior lord or directly from the Crown, exercised rights over the manorial lands and tenants through a manorial court, and retained certain privileges — such as rights to minerals, markets, and mills — long after the feudal system itself decayed. Lordships of the manor still exist in English law as a form of incorporeal property and are bought and sold today, though they carry no political power.

History

The manorial system developed in the early Middle Ages out of the late Roman villa system and the Frankish and Anglo-Saxon organisation of rural estates. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, all land in England was held ultimately of the King, and great tenants-in-chief subinfeudated parcels to mesne lords, who in turn were lords of individual manors. A manor typically comprised the lord's demesne lands, the holdings of free and unfree (villein) tenants, and common land and waste.

The lord presided, through his steward, over the manorial court — the court baron for free tenants and the customary court for copyholders — which regulated agricultural practice, recorded surrenders and admissions of customary tenancies, and enforced the customs of the manor. Rents were gradually commuted from labour services to money payments, and copyhold tenure was finally abolished by the Law of Property Act 1922, effective 1926, which enfranchised copyholds into freehold.

Modern status

The title "lord of the manor" is not a title of nobility or of the peerage; it is a property right (an incorporeal hereditament) that may be held by a person or a corporation, and several lordships may be divided among co-owners. The Manorial Society of Great Britain estimates that many thousands of lordships exist. Since 2003, lordships of the manor could no longer be newly noted against registered land titles at HM Land Registry after the transitional period of the Land Registration Act 2002 expired in October 2013, though the rights themselves were not extinguished.

Manorial rights retained by lords of the manor may include ownership of the soil of common land, mines and minerals, sporting rights, and rights to hold markets and fairs granted by charter. In a small number of cases, the lord of the manor remains the patron of an ecclesiastical living or retains ceremonial functions in the locality.
