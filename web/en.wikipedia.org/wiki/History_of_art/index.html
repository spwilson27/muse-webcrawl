History of art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Art history ranges from Palaeolithic cave painting to contemporary art.

The history of art is the study of painting, sculpture, architecture and the other visual arts across human cultures and periods, from prehistoric cave painting — such as the works at Chauvet and Altamira, over 30,000 years old — to contemporary art. As an academic discipline, art history emerged in the 19th century from antiquarianism and the biographical tradition of Giorgio Vasari's Lives of the Artists (1550), and was formalised by scholars such as Johann Joachim Winckelmann, Jacob Burckhardt, Heinrich Wölfflin and Erwin Panofsky.

Outline of periods

Conventional Western narratives move from ancient Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Greek and Roman art, through Byzantine and medieval art (Romanesque and Gothic), to the Renaissance, Mannerism, Baroque and Rococo, then Neoclassicism and Romanticism. The later 19th century brought Realism, Impressionism and Post-Impressionism; the 20th century exploded into modern movements — Fauvism, Cubism, abstraction, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism, Pop art and Minimalism — and then contemporary and postmodern practices. Parallel histories cover Islamic art, the arts of China, Japan and India, African art, and the Indigenous arts of the Americas and Oceania.

Methods and debates

Art historians employ formal analysis, iconography and iconology, social history of art, semiotics, feminism, postcolonial critique and technical study of materials. Since the 1970s the discipline has questioned its canon — the hierarchy of "fine" over "decorative" art, the marginalisation of women artists and non-Western traditions — and expanded toward visual culture studies. Figures such as Linda Nochlin ("Why Have There Been No Great Women Artists?", 1971) reshaped its questions.
