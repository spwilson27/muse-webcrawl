Perpendicular Gothic

Perpendicular Gothic
Period: c. 1330s–1540s
Region: England
Preceded by: Decorated Gothic
Key features: Vertical lines, panel tracery, fan vaults, four-centred arches

Perpendicular Gothic (also Perpendicular English or Rectilinear Gothic) is the third and final phase of English Gothic architecture, following Early English and Decorated Gothic. It developed from about the 1330s and lasted into the 1540s, making it unique to England — no equivalent style appeared on the European continent, where Flamboyant Gothic evolved instead.

Characteristics

The style takes its name from its strong emphasis on vertical lines, especially in window tracery, where straight upright mullions continue to the top of the window in a grid-like pattern called panel tracery. Windows became very large, with thinner walls between them, flooding interiors with light. Other hallmarks include the four-centred (Tudor) arch, flattened vault profiles, elaborate fan vaults, and the use of heraldic decoration and stained glass.

History

Early examples appear at Gloucester Cathedral, where the choir was remodelled in the 1330s–1350s, and in the work of royal masons such as William Wynford and Henry Yevele. The style flourished in parish churches funded by the wool trade, in Oxford and Cambridge college chapels, and in great works such as King's College Chapel, Cambridge (1446–1515), St George's Chapel, Windsor, and Henry VII's Chapel at Westminster Abbey, famous for its fan vaulting.

Legacy

Perpendicular elements survived into the Tudor period and influenced later Gothic Revival design. The term "Perpendicular" was coined by the antiquarian Thomas Rickman in his 1812 classification of English Gothic styles.
