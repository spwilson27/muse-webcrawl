The 1896 United States presidential election was the 28th quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, November 3, 1896. Republican nominee William McKinley, governor of Ohio, defeated Democratic nominee William Jennings Bryan. The election is generally considered a realigning election that began the Fourth Party System, inaugurating a period of Republican dominance that lasted until 1932.

Background

The Panic of 1893 had plunged the country into a severe depression, discrediting the Democratic administration of Grover Cleveland. The dominant question was the monetary standard: whether the dollar should be backed by gold alone or by "free silver" — the unlimited coinage of silver at a ratio of 16 to 1 — which debtors and farmers hoped would inflate prices and ease debts.

Nominations and the Cross of Gold

At the Democratic convention in Chicago, the 36-year-old Bryan electrified delegates with his "Cross of Gold" speech ("You shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold") and won the nomination; the Populist Party also endorsed him. McKinley, the leading advocate of the protective tariff and the gold standard, was nominated by the Republicans under the management of Mark Hanna.

Campaign

Bryan revolutionised campaigning by travelling some 18,000 miles and speaking to millions, while McKinley conducted a "front porch campaign" from Canton, Ohio, receiving organised delegations. Hanna raised unprecedented corporate funds and flooded the country with pamphlets. Bryan swept the South and much of the West, but McKinley carried the populous industrial states of the Northeast and Midwest.

Results and legacy

McKinley won 271 electoral votes to Bryan's 176 and 51.0% of the popular vote. The election consolidated a Republican coalition of business, industry and much of the urban working class, marginalised the Populists, and settled the currency question in practice; the Gold Standard Act followed in 1900. Bryan remained a major Democratic figure, nominated again in 1900 and 1908.
