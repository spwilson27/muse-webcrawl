Organisation of Islamic Cooperation

[Image: Emblem of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, featuring a crescent surrounding a globe centred on the Kaaba]

The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (OIC; Arabic: منظمة التعاون الإسلامي; French: Organisation de la coopération islamique) is an intergovernmental organisation founded in 1969 with 57 member states across four continents, most of them with significant Muslim populations. It describes itself as "the collective voice of the Muslim world" and works to safeguard members' interests and promote international peace and harmony. Its permanent secretariat is in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia.

History

The OIC was established following an arson attack on the Al-Aqsa Mosque in Jerusalem in August 1969; the first Islamic Summit, held in Rabat, Morocco, in September 1969, created the organisation, and its charter was adopted in 1972. Originally named the Organisation of the Islamic Conference, it adopted its present name in 2011. A revised charter (2008) expanded its stated aims to include human rights, good governance, and countering defamation of Islam.

Structure and activity

The organisation's main organs are the Islamic Summit of heads of state (held every three years), the Council of Foreign Ministers, and the General Secretariat headed by a secretary-general. Subsidiary bodies include the Islamic Development Bank and the Islamic Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (ICESCO). The OIC holds a permanent observer mission to the United Nations and has frequently coordinated member states' positions on issues such as the Israeli–Palestinian conflict — where it supports a Palestinian state with East Jerusalem as its capital — Kashmir, and the treatment of Muslim minorities such as the Rohingya.

Membership and criticism

Members include most Arab League states (many of which also historically participated in pan-Arab politics), Turkey, Iran, Pakistan, Indonesia, and states in Africa and Central Asia; Syria's membership was suspended in 2012. Critics argue that internal divisions limit the OIC's effectiveness and that its resolutions are non-binding; supporters regard it as a significant forum of the Global South and the second-largest intergovernmental organisation after the United Nations.
