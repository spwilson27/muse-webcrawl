The residents of the District of Columbia — the federal district containing Washington, the capital of the United States — lack full voting representation in the United States Congress, a situation often summarised by the slogan on the District's licence plates: "End Taxation Without Representation." Although D.C. residents pay federal taxes, serve in the military and outnumber the populations of Vermont and Wyoming, they elect no senators and only a non-voting delegate to the House of Representatives. The issue has generated a long-running statehood movement.

Constitutional background

Article I of the U.S. Constitution grants Congress exclusive legislative authority over a federal district "not exceeding ten miles square." When the capital moved to the District in 1800, its residents lost the voting rights they had held as residents of Maryland and Virginia. Because representation in the Constitution is allocated to states, courts have generally held that full congressional representation for the District requires either statehood or a constitutional amendment.

Partial gains

The Twenty-third Amendment (1961) gave the District electors in the Electoral College — as many as the least populous state — and residents first voted for president in 1964. Since 1971 the District has elected a non-voting delegate to the House, who may serve on and vote in committees but cannot vote on final passage. The District of Columbia Home Rule Act (1973) established an elected mayor and council, though Congress retains the power to override local legislation and controls the District's budget process. A proposed District of Columbia Voting Rights Amendment, passed by Congress in 1978, would have treated D.C. as a state for representation purposes, but it expired in 1985 having been ratified by only 16 of the required 38 states.

Statehood movement

Advocates argue that Congress can admit the residential parts of the District as the 51st state by ordinary statute, shrinking the federal district to the monumental core. The House of Representatives passed statehood bills (the Washington, D.C. Admission Act) in 2020 and again in 2021, but they stalled in the Senate. Opponents question the constitutionality of statutory statehood and raise the Twenty-third Amendment's grant of electors to the remaining federal district. Residents have consistently supported statehood in referendums, most recently by about 86% in 2016.
