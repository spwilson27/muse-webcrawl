Spontaneous fission

Spontaneous fission (SF) is a form of radioactive decay in which an unstable atomic nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei (fission fragments) and generally several neutrons, without any external trigger. Because it releases neutrons, spontaneous fission is a significant neutron source and competes as a decay mode only for very heavy nuclei.

Physics

In the liquid-drop model, fission occurs when a heavy nucleus deforms until electrostatic repulsion between its two halves overcomes the strong nuclear force holding it together. In induced fission this deformation is supplied by an absorbed neutron; in spontaneous fission the nucleus tunnels quantum-mechanically through the fission barrier from its ground state. The probability of spontaneous fission rises steeply with atomic number and the ratio Z²/A.

Rates and examples

Spontaneous fission was discovered in 1940 by Georgy Flyorov and Konstantin Petrzhak in uranium-238, whose spontaneous fission half-life is about 8 × 1015 years — long, but measurable because each event releases energetic fragments. For heavier nuclides the rate increases dramatically: californium-252 has a spontaneous fission branch of about 3% and a total half-life of 2.65 years, making it a widely used neutron source emitting about 2.3 × 1012 neutrons per second per gram. Plutonium-240's spontaneous fission (roughly 5.7 × 105 fissions per second per kilogram) produces background neutrons that complicate the design of nuclear weapons and limit the use of reactor-grade plutonium in gun-type designs. For superheavy elements such as fermium-258, spontaneous fission is the dominant decay mode and limits the stability of the heaviest synthesized nuclei.

Applications and significance

Compact californium-252 sources are used to start up nuclear reactors, for neutron activation analysis, neutron radiography and well logging. In geochronology, the tracks left by spontaneous fission of uranium-238 in minerals are used for fission-track dating. Spontaneous fission neutrons also contribute to the neutron background in underground physics experiments.
