Dispersed knowledge

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dispersed knowledge is the notion that no single agent has all the information needed to make decisions in a social system; instead, relevant knowledge is spread across many individuals, much of it local, tacit, and specific to particular times and places. The concept is central to the work of economist Friedrich Hayek, especially his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society".

Hayek's argument

Hayek argued that the economic problem of society is not allocating resources given complete information, but utilising knowledge that "is not given to anyone in its totality". Each person holds unique information about their own circumstances — local conditions, fleeting opportunities, practical know-how — that cannot be fully transmitted to a central authority in statistical form. Because much of this knowledge is tacit, it cannot even be articulated by its holder, let alone collected by planners.

The price mechanism

Hayek's proposed solution is the price system: prices act as signals that compress dispersed information. When tin becomes scarcer, its price rises, and users economise on tin without needing to know why it became scarce. Prices thus coordinate the plans of millions of people who know almost nothing about one another. Hayek called this a "marvel" of spontaneous coordination.

Relation to the socialist calculation debate

The dispersed-knowledge argument built on Ludwig von Mises's earlier critique in the socialist calculation debate: without market prices for capital goods, central planners lack a rational way to compare alternative uses of resources. Hayek shifted the emphasis from calculation itself to the epistemic problem — the knowledge planners would need is generated only within the market process and cannot exist independently of it.

Criticism and influence

Critics respond that modern computing, big data and planning within large firms (which internally operate as command economies of considerable size) complicate the argument, and that prices can fail to convey socially relevant information, as with externalities. The idea nonetheless remains influential in economics, organisation theory and discussions of decentralisation, and is related to concepts such as tacit knowledge (Michael Polanyi) and local knowledge in anthropology and development studies.
