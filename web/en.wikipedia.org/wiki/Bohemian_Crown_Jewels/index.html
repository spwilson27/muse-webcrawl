Bohemian Crown Jewels

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Crown of Saint Wenceslas with the royal orb and sceptre (image placeholder)

The Bohemian Crown Jewels, also called the Czech Crown Jewels (Czech: České korunovační klenoty), include the Crown of Saint Wenceslas (Svatováclavská koruna), the royal orb and sceptre, the coronation vestments of the kings of Bohemia, the gold reliquary cross, and Saint Wenceslas' sword. They are kept in a chamber within St. Vitus Cathedral at Prague Castle and are exhibited only on special occasions, roughly once every five years.

History

The Crown of Saint Wenceslas was made in 1346 for the coronation of Charles IV, who had it embellished with precious stones and dedicated it to Saint Wenceslas, the patron saint of Bohemia. The crown is decorated with 19 sapphires, 30 emeralds, 20 pearls, 44 spinels and a large ruby mounted in its cross.

The royal orb and sceptre date from the first half of the 16th century and are associated with Ferdinand I. During periods of danger the jewels were moved for safekeeping, including to Karlštejn Castle and, during the Second World War, hidden away in the Old Town Hall and elsewhere in Prague.

The crown chamber

The jewels are stored in the Crown Chamber above the St. Wenceslas Chapel in St. Vitus Cathedral. The chamber door, and the iron safe within it, have seven locks; the seven keys are held by seven different dignitaries, including the President of the Czech Republic, the Prime Minister, the Archbishop of Prague and the Mayor of Prague, all of whom must be present for the chamber to be opened.

Legend

A well-known legend holds that any usurper who places the crown on his head will die within a year. The story is often connected with Reinhard Heydrich, the Nazi Reichsprotektor of Bohemia and Moravia, who was said to have tried on the crown in 1941 and was assassinated the following year — although historians treat the episode as folklore rather than fact.

Exhibitions

Public exhibitions of the jewels are rare state occasions. In the modern era they have been displayed in 1993, 1998, 2003, 2008, 2013, 2016, 2018 and 2023, drawing long queues of visitors to Prague Castle. A permanent replica set is displayed in the Old Royal Palace.
