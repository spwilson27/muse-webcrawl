Hutong

Hutongs (simplified Chinese: 胡同; traditional Chinese: 衚衕; pinyin: hútòng) are narrow streets or alleys characteristic of northern Chinese cities, most famously Beijing. Formed by rows of siheyuan — traditional courtyard residences enclosed by grey-brick walls — hutongs represent the historic urban fabric of the capital and a distinctive way of communal life. The word is believed to derive from a Mongolian term for a well or water source, dating to the Yuan dynasty, when Beijing (then Dadu) was laid out on a formal grid.

History

When Kublai Khan established Dadu in the 13th century, the city was planned with broad avenues dividing residential districts; the lanes within those districts became the hutongs. Their widths and orientations reflected the rank and status of the residents under Ming and Qing planning codes, with the grandest lanes near the Forbidden City. By the early 20th century Beijing contained several thousand named hutongs, and hutong neighbourhoods housed everyone from imperial princes to rickshaw pullers.

Decline and preservation

During the later 20th century, and especially during the rapid development of the 1990s and 2000s, large numbers of hutongs were demolished to make way for wide roads, office towers, and apartment blocks; residents were relocated to suburban housing. The loss of much of the old city prompted growing preservation efforts. Areas such as Shichahai, Nanluoguxiang, and the lanes around the Drum and Bell Towers were designated conservation areas, and many surviving courtyards have been renovated as homes, boutique hotels, cafés, and tourist attractions, visited by pedicab tours.

Life in the hutongs

Hutong life was historically communal: neighbours shared wells, public toilets, and small shops, and the lanes functioned as social spaces for chess games, bird-keeping, and street food. The gates of courtyard houses — their size, colour, and the number of door studs — traditionally signalled the owner's rank. Names of hutongs preserve the city's history, recalling markets, temples, crafts, officials, and local legends.
