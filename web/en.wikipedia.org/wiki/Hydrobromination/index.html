Hydrobromination

Hydrobromination is the addition of hydrogen bromide (HBr) across a carbon–carbon double or triple bond. The reaction is a standard method of preparing alkyl bromides from alkenes and is notable in the history of organic chemistry because it can proceed by two different mechanisms that give opposite regiochemistry: ordinary ionic (electrophilic) addition follows Markovnikov's rule, whereas addition in the presence of peroxides follows a radical pathway and gives the anti-Markovnikov product — the so-called peroxide effect, or Kharasch effect.

Markovnikov (ionic) addition

In the absence of radical initiators, HBr adds to an alkene by electrophilic addition: the proton adds first to the less substituted carbon, generating the more stable carbocation, which is then captured by bromide. The bromine therefore ends up on the more substituted carbon. For example, propene gives 2-bromopropane. The reaction is subject to the usual carbocation rearrangements, and analogous ionic additions occur with HCl and HI.

Anti-Markovnikov (radical) addition

In 1933 Morris S. Kharasch and co-workers showed that traces of peroxides — often present in aged reagents or formed from atmospheric oxygen — completely reversed the regiochemistry of HBr addition. Under radical conditions, a bromine radical adds first to the alkene, generating the more stable carbon radical, which then abstracts a hydrogen atom from HBr to give the product and regenerate a bromine radical in a chain process. Bromine consequently bonds to the less substituted carbon: propene gives 1-bromopropane. The peroxide effect is unique to HBr among the hydrogen halides, because only for HBr are both propagation steps of the radical chain sufficiently exothermic; for HCl and HI the chain breaks down.

Applications

Hydrobromination is used in synthesis to convert alkenes into alkyl bromides, versatile intermediates for substitution, elimination, and organometallic (Grignard and organolithium) chemistry. The radical variant provides primary bromides that are otherwise difficult to obtain from terminal alkenes. HBr can also add twice to alkynes to give geminal dibromides under ionic conditions, or vicinal products stepwise under radical conditions.
