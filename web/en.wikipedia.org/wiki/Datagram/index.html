Datagram

A datagram is a basic transfer unit associated with a packet-switched network. Datagrams provide a connectionless communication service across a network: each datagram is an independent, self-contained message carrying enough information — source and destination network addresses — to be routed from source to destination without relying on earlier exchanges between the computers and the network.

History and terminology

The word "datagram" was coined by combining "data" and "telegram", and was adopted by the French CYCLADES network pioneer Louis Pouzin, whose datagram design influenced the development of the Internet protocol suite. The concept contrasts with virtual-circuit networks, in which a path is established before data flows.

Properties

Because datagrams are routed independently, they may arrive out of order, be duplicated, or be lost entirely; the service makes no delivery guarantees. Reliability, sequencing, and congestion control, if required, must be supplied by the endpoints — the essence of the end-to-end principle in network design. The Internet Protocol (IP) is a datagram service: each IP packet is routed independently. The User Datagram Protocol (UDP) exposes this datagram service directly to applications, favouring low latency and simplicity; applications such as DNS lookups, streaming media, and online games accept its unreliability in exchange for speed.

Datagrams vs. packets

Usage of the terms varies: "packet" is the generic name for a formatted unit of data carried by a packet network, while "datagram" is often reserved for packets delivered by an unreliable service, and "frame" for units at the link layer.
