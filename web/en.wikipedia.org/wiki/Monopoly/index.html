Monopoly

[Image: a single railway company sign dominating a station concourse, symbolising one sole supplier]

A monopoly is a Market structure in which a single seller supplies a good or service with no close substitutes, giving the firm market power: it is a price maker, able to raise Price by restricting output. Compared with competitive supply, monopoly pricing creates a deadweight welfare loss — output too low, price too high — besides transferring income from consumers to the firm, though the firm may also enjoy economies of scale.

Causes and policy

Monopolies arise from control of a key resource, patents and copyrights, network effects, high start-up costs, or the cost structure of a natural monopoly (water, electricity grids) where one network serves the market most cheaply. Historical examples include Standard Oil (broken up in 1911) and AT&T (broken up in 1984); multinational platform firms face modern monopoly scrutiny. Policy responses include antitrust enforcement and break-up, price regulation of natural monopolies, public ownership, and reducing barriers to entry. De Beers in diamonds and Microsoft in 1990s operating systems are standard case studies; pure monopolies are rare, and most analysis concerns degrees of market power along the spectrum to Oligopoly.
