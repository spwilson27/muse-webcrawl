An arrowslit (also arrow loop or balistraria) is a narrow vertical aperture in a fortification through which an archer can launch arrows or a crossbowman loose bolts at attackers while remaining largely protected. Narrow at the exterior face, the slit typically splays widely inside the wall, giving the defender room to move and a wide field of fire, while offering attackers only a minimal target.

Design

Arrowslits were cut into walls several feet thick, often with an internal recess or chamber so the archer could stand back from the opening. A horizontal sill at the bottom could be sloped to allow shooting downward at the wall's base. Vertical slits suited longbowmen; horizontal or cross-shaped openings better fitted crossbows, which are aimed in a horizontal position.

Shapes and development

Simple vertical slits appeared in European castles from the 12th century; later designs added a horizontal crosspiece (forming a cross slit), circular oillet holes at the ends for improved vision, inverted keyhole shapes, and fish-tail bases for a wider downwards arc. Highly developed arrowslits feature in castles such as Dover, Harlech and Beaumaris, where they command approaches in enfilade.

Use and decline

With the spread of gunpowder weapons, arrowslits were adapted or replaced by gun loops and gunports designed for handguns and cannon. The unrelated modern term "loophole" — a small opening through which an obligation can be escaped — derives from these apertures. Decorative false arrowslits also appear on later Gothic Revival buildings.
