Generic trademark

Also calledGenericised trademark, proprietary eponym
FieldTrademark law
ExamplesAspirin, escalator, thermos (in some jurisdictions)

A generic trademark, also known as a genericised trademark or proprietary eponym, is a trademark or brand name that, because of its popularity or market dominance, has become the generic term for a particular type of product or service, against the intentions of the trademark's owner. When this occurs, the owner may lose exclusive rights in the mark, a process sometimes called genericide.

Examples

Terms that began as trademarks but became generic in at least some jurisdictions include aspirin and heroin (both originally Bayer marks), escalator (Otis), thermos, zipper, dry ice, cellophane, and linoleum. Other marks, such as Kleenex, Band-Aid, and Google, are widely used generically in everyday speech but remain legally protected trademarks, and their owners actively police usage to prevent genericide.

Legal treatment

Under the United States Lanham Act, a registration may be cancelled if the mark becomes the generic name for the goods or services for which it is registered; courts ask whether the primary significance of the term to the relevant public is as a product category rather than a brand. Trademark owners commonly combat genericisation by using the mark as an adjective (e.g., "Kleenex brand tissues"), avoiding use as a noun or verb in their own materials, and running corrective advertising campaigns.
