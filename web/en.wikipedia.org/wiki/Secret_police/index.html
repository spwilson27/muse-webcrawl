Secret police

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Secret police are police, intelligence or security organisations that engage in covert operations against political opponents and dissidents, operating outside or above ordinary law. They are characteristic instruments of authoritarian and totalitarian states, used to surveil, intimidate, imprison and sometimes kill those the regime regards as enemies, and to deter opposition by the fear of omnipresent surveillance.

Notorious historical examples include Tsarist Russia's Okhrana; the Soviet Cheka and its successors (GPU, NKVD, KGB), which ran the Gulag and the Great Terror; Nazi Germany's Gestapo, dependent in practice on denunciations from the public; East Germany's Stasi, which employed roughly one informer for every few dozen citizens and left archives that post-reunification Germany opened to victims; and Romania's Securitate. Democratic states maintain intelligence services but subject them, at least formally, to law, budgets and oversight, and prohibit political policing of lawful dissent — the line contested in debates over mass surveillance.

Functions and effects

Secret police do more than repress: they inform rulers (often telling them what they wish to hear), staff the machinery of purges, and corrode social trust, an effect East Germans called Zersetzung when applied as psychological decomposition of dissidents' lives. After transitions, societies confront the archives — lustration, trials and truth commissions are alternative responses.
