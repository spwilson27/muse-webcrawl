The Lesser Key of Solomon, also known by its Latin title Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis ("Little Key of Solomon"), is an anonymous grimoire on demonology, compiled in the mid-seventeenth century from materials several centuries older. Though attributed to King Solomon, it is pseudepigraphical, like the related and older Key of Solomon (Clavicula Salomonis), from which it is distinct.

Contents

The work is divided into five books. The first, the Ars Goetia, is the most famous: it describes 72 demons—kings, dukes, princes, marquises, counts, knights, and presidents of Hell—each with a rank, a seal (sigil), an account of their powers, and the number of legions they command. Many of its entries derive from Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577) and ultimately from sources such as the Testament of Solomon. The second book, the Ars Theurgia-Goetia, deals with aerial spirits of the cardinal directions; the third, the Ars Paulina, with angels of the zodiac and hours; the fourth, the Ars Almadel, with a wax tablet used to contact angels; and the fifth, the Ars Notoria, a collection of prayers and orations for obtaining knowledge, related to the medieval Sworn Book of Honorius tradition.

Translations and influence

The Ars Goetia was translated into English by Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers and edited by Aleister Crowley, appearing in 1904 as The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King, with Crowley's prefatory remarks; this edition shaped the text's central place in modern Western occultism and ceremonial magic. A critical edition of the full Lemegeton was produced by Joseph H. Peterson (2001). The 72 Goetic demons recur throughout popular culture—in occult literature, games, anime, and fiction—and the grimoire is regularly cited in studies of early modern magic.
