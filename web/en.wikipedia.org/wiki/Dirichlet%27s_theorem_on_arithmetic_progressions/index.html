Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions

FieldAnalytic number theory
Proved byPeter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, 1837
StatementFor coprime a and d, the progression a, a+d, a+2d, … contains infinitely many primes

In number theory, Dirichlet's theorem states that for any two positive coprime integers a and d, there are infinitely many prime numbers of the form a + nd, where n is a non-negative integer — that is, the arithmetic progression a, a + d, a + 2d, … contains infinitely many primes. It was proved by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet in 1837.

Significance

The theorem generalises Euclid's theorem that there are infinitely many primes, and it shows that primes are distributed among the admissible residue classes modulo d in the strongest qualitative sense. A quantitative refinement, the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, shows the primes are asymptotically equidistributed among the φ(d) coprime classes.

Proof

Dirichlet's proof is regarded as the founding work of analytic number theory. It introduced Dirichlet characters and Dirichlet L-functions, showing that L(1, χ) ≠ 0 for non-principal characters χ; the cases where a and d are not coprime are excluded because then every term shares a common factor and at most one term can be prime.

Examples

The theorem implies infinitely many primes end in the digit 1, 3, 7 or 9 (progressions modulo 10), infinitely many primes of the form 4n + 3, and infinitely many of the form 6n + 5.
