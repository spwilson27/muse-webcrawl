Internal wave

Internal waves in the Strait of Gibraltar, revealed by sun glint in a satellite image.

Internal waves are gravity waves that oscillate within a fluid medium instead of at its surface. For a wave to propagate, the fluid must be stratified: its density must increase with depth due to changes in temperature and/or salinity. Internal waves occur in the ocean along the pycnocline and in the atmosphere along temperature inversions.

Properties

Because the density contrast across ocean layers is small, the restoring force is weak: internal waves have much larger amplitudes (tens to hundreds of metres), longer wavelengths, and slower periods (minutes to hours) than surface waves. Their frequency is bounded above by the Brunt–Väisälä (buoyancy) frequency of the stratification.

Generation and effects

Tides flowing over submarine ridges and continental shelf breaks are a major source, notably in the South China Sea (Luzon Strait) and the Strait of Gibraltar. Internal waves transport energy and momentum, drive mixing that supplies nutrients to surface waters, and can endanger submarines and offshore platforms; breaking internal waves contribute significantly to deep-ocean turbulence. Surface slicks aligned with the waves make them visible in satellite radar and sunglint images, and they can modulate conditions experienced by divers and ships.

Atmospheric internal waves

In the atmosphere, internal gravity waves are generated by flow over mountains (lee waves) and by convection; they transport momentum to the middle atmosphere and influence circulation patterns.
