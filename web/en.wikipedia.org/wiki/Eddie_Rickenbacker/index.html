Edward Vernon Rickenbacker (born Edward Rickenbacher; October 8, 1890 – July 23, 1973) was an American fighter pilot in World War I and a Medal of Honor recipient. With 26 aerial victories, he was the United States' most successful fighter ace of the war and is considered to have received the most decorations of any American serviceman in the conflict. He was also a race car driver, automotive designer and longtime head of Eastern Air Lines.

Early life and racing career

Born in Columbus, Ohio, to Swiss immigrant parents, Rickenbacker left school after his father's death to help support the family. He worked in automobile factories, took a correspondence course in engineering, and became a racing driver, competing in the Indianapolis 500 four times and setting speed records. When the United States entered World War I in 1917 he enlisted, hoping to fly, and initially served as a chauffeur before persuading superiors to assign him to flight training.

World War I

Rickenbacker joined the 94th Aero Squadron, the famous "Hat-in-the-Ring" squadron, in March 1918 and became its commanding officer in September. Flying SPAD XIII fighters, he scored 26 confirmed victories — including seven in a single month — and earned a record seven Distinguished Service Crosses. In 1930 one of those awards was upgraded to the Medal of Honor, presented by President Herbert Hoover, for an action on September 25, 1918, when he attacked seven German aircraft alone and shot down two.

Between the wars

After the war Rickenbacker published his memoir Fighting the Flying Circus (1919), founded the Rickenbacker Motor Company, and owned the Indianapolis Motor Speedway from 1927 to 1945. He became general manager and then president of Eastern Air Lines, building it into one of the most successful and conservatively managed American airlines.

World War II and the 1942 raft ordeal

During World War II Rickenbacker served as a civilian adviser and special representative of Secretary of War Henry Stimson, inspecting air bases worldwide. In October 1942 the B-17 carrying him on a mission to General Douglas MacArthur ditched in the Pacific Ocean after navigation failure. Rickenbacker and six companions survived 24 days adrift on rafts with almost no food or water before being rescued; the ordeal, which Rickenbacker recounted in his book Seven Came Through, became one of the war's best-known survival stories and influenced naval survival training.

Later life

Rickenbacker remained a prominent, outspoken conservative public figure. He retired from Eastern Air Lines in 1963 and published his autobiography in 1967. He died of pneumonia in Zürich in 1973 while seeking medical treatment for his wife Adelaide.
