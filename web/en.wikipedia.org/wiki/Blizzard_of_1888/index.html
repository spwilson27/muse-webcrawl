Blizzard of 1888

The Great Blizzard of 1888, also known as the Great Blizzard of '88 or the Great White Hurricane, was one of the most severe recorded blizzards in American history. The storm struck the northeastern United States from 11 to 14 March 1888, paralysing the East Coast from the Chesapeake Bay to Maine. Unrelated, the same year saw the separate "Schoolhouse Blizzard" of January 1888 on the Great Plains.

The storm

After a mild, rainy 11 March, temperatures plunged overnight as a deep coastal low intensified. Snow fell for more than a day and a half, whipped by winds gusting to 45 mph. Saratoga Springs, New York, recorded 58 inches; New Haven, Connecticut, 45 inches; New York City about 22 inches, but drifts of 20–40 feet buried streets and rail lines.

Impact

More than 400 people died, including some 200 in New York City, many of them found frozen in drifts days later. Telegraph lines collapsed, cutting communications; trains were stranded across the region; the East River froze so solidly that people walked across the ice between Manhattan and Brooklyn. Fires during the storm caused heavy damage because fire engines could not move. Some 200 ships were wrecked or grounded along the coast.

Legacy

The blizzard exposed the vulnerability of above-ground infrastructure. New York City accelerated plans to move telegraph and telephone wires underground and, impressed by Boston's new subway project, built its own underground rapid transit, which opened in 1904. The storm remains a benchmark for winter weather in the American Northeast.
