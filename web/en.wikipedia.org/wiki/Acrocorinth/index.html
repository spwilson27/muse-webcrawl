Acrocorinth

LocationCorinth, Corinthia, Greece
Elevation575 m (1,886 ft)
TypeAcropolis and fortress

Acrocorinth (Greek: Ακροκόρινθος, "Upper Corinth") is a steep monolithic rock overlooking the ancient city of Corinth, Greece. Rising about 575 metres above sea level, it served as the acropolis of ancient Corinth and, thanks to its defensible position, its secure water supply, and its command of the Isthmus of Corinth, it was successively fortified through the Byzantine, Frankish, Venetian, and Ottoman periods.

Antiquity

In classical times Acrocorinth held sanctuaries of Aphrodite, whose temple stood on the summit, as well as shrines of Helios, Demeter and Kore, and the spring of Upper Peirene within its walls. It was a key stronghold: garrisoned at various times by Macedonian kings, it was one of the "fetters of Greece" controlling access to the Peloponnese. The Romans under Lucius Mummius destroyed Corinth in 146 BC, but the citadel retained strategic importance.

Medieval and later history

The Byzantines refortified the site, and after the Fourth Crusade it became a seat of the Frankish lords of Corinth, who built the inner keep. It passed between Venetians and Ottomans several times; the Venetians strengthened its triple circuit of walls with bastions. Acrocorinth was surrendered to the Greeks during the Greek War of Independence in the 1820s.

The site today

Visitors enter through successive gates in the massive walls. Within are remains of the temple of Aphrodite (later converted into a church and then a mosque), a Frankish tower, cisterns, and the covered stairway descending to the Upper Peirene spring. The summit commands views across the Gulf of Corinth and the Saronic Gulf.
