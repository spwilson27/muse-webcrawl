Complementary currency

A complementary currency is a currency or medium of exchange that is not legal tender but supplements or complements a national currency within a defined community, region, or network. Such currencies encourage local spending, strengthen community ties, or mobilise unused resources.

Types

Examples include local currencies such as the Bristol Pound and the Swiss WIR franc (founded 1934 and still used by tens of thousands of businesses), time banks in which services are exchanged by the hour, and mutual credit systems such as LETS (Local Exchange Trading Systems), first developed in Canada in the 1980s. Some systems are backed by reserves of national currency, while others are created purely by mutual credit between members.

Rationale

Advocates argue that complementary currencies keep wealth circulating locally, cushion communities against national monetary shocks, and allow trade to continue during liquidity crises. Critics note their limited acceptability, administrative costs, and the tax and regulatory questions they raise.

History

Local emergency currencies proliferated during the Great Depression, including the stamp scrip inspired by Silvio Gesell's demurrage ideas, famously trialled in Wörgl, Austria in 1932–33. The movement revived from the 1980s onward with LETS, time banks, and transition-town currencies.
