View camera

A view camera is a large-format camera in which the lens forms an inverted image on a ground-glass screen directly at the film plane. The photographer views the image on the screen, then replaces the screen with the film holder to make the exposure. View cameras are the oldest type of camera still in common use and are characterized by their full range of movements.

Design

A typical view camera consists of a front standard holding the lens, a rear standard holding the ground glass and film, and a flexible bellows between them, mounted on a rail or flat bed. Common formats are 4×5 inch, 5×7 inch, and 8×10 inch sheet film. Two main families exist: the monorail camera, favored in studios for its extensive movements, and the field camera, which folds for portability and is used outdoors.

Movements

The lens and film planes can be tilted, swung, shifted, and raised or lowered independently. These movements let the photographer control perspective (correcting converging verticals in architecture, for example) and manipulate the plane of focus according to the Scheimpflug principle, achieving either extensive depth of field or deliberate selective focus.

Use

Because they are slow and deliberate to operate, view cameras are used mainly for architecture, landscape, still life, and fine-art photography, where their large negatives and precise control justify the effort. Digital backs can also be fitted to many models.
