Statutory instrument

A statutory instrument (SI) is the principal form in which delegated legislation is made in the United Kingdom. Statutory instruments are made under powers conferred by an Act of Parliament (the "parent Act") on a government minister, the Crown, or another body, and allow the details of the law to be filled in, updated, or brought into force without a new Act. Several thousand statutory instruments are made each year; the system is governed by the Statutory Instruments Act 1946.

Parliamentary control

Most statutory instruments are subject to one of two procedures in Parliament. Under the negative procedure, an instrument "made" (and usually laid before Parliament) becomes law unless either House resolves to annul it within 40 days — such a motion is called a "prayer". Under the affirmative procedure, an instrument cannot become law unless it is approved by resolution of both Houses (or, for some financial instruments, the House of Commons alone). Some instruments, such as commencement orders bringing parts of an Act into force, require no parliamentary procedure at all.

Scrutiny and criticism

Technical scrutiny of statutory instruments is undertaken by the Joint Committee on Statutory Instruments, which checks that instruments are within the powers granted, are clearly drafted, and do not impose charges or make unexpected use of the enabling power. Critics, most famously Lord Hewart in The New Despotism (1929), have warned that delegated legislation can transfer effective law-making to the executive; defenders reply that the volume and technicality of modern regulation makes it indispensable. So-called "Henry VIII clauses" — powers allowing ministers to amend primary legislation by instrument — attract particular controversy.

Numbering and publication

Statutory instruments are numbered sequentially each year (e.g., "SI 2024/123") and published officially; since 2010 the full text has been available on the legislation.gov.uk website. Devolved administrations in Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland make their own series of statutory instruments.
