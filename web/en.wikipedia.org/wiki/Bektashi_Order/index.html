Bektashi Order

The Bektashi Order (Turkish: Bektaşîlik) is a Sufi Islamic order (tariqa) that originated in 13th-century Anatolia and is named after the saint Haji Bektash Veli. Bektashism incorporates elements of Shia Islam — particularly reverence for Ali and the Twelve Imams — within a mystical framework, and historically was closely associated with the Janissary corps of the Ottoman Empire. Today its world headquarters is in Tirana, Albania.

Beliefs and practices

Bektashis seek the Divine through the "four gates" of religious law, the Sufi path, knowledge and ultimate truth, progressing through forty levels. Practices vary between branches: many Bektashis do not observe the five daily prayers or Ramadan fasting in conventional form, women participate unveiled and on equal terms in ceremonies, and the ritual meal (muhabbet) and confession to a spiritual guide (baba) are central. The order is organised hierarchically from lay adherents through dervishes and babas to the Dedebaba.

History

The order took shape around the shrine of Haji Bektash in Anatolia and was organised in its classical form by Balım Sultan in the early 16th century. As spiritual guides to the Janissaries, the Bektashis suffered when the corps was abolished in 1826. Suppressed along with all Sufi orders in Turkey in 1925, the order's leadership moved to Albania, where the community survived despite Albania's communist-era atheist ban (1967–1990). In Albania, Bektashis constitute a recognised religious community with a distinct identity, and plans have been announced for a small sovereign Bektashi entity in Tirana.
