Non-Aligned Movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Non-Aligned Movement (NAM) is a forum of states — today about 120 members — not formally aligned with or against any major power bloc. It emerged from the Asian–African Conference at Bandung, Indonesia (1955), and was formally founded at the Belgrade summit of 1961, led by Yugoslavia's Tito, India's Nehru, Egypt's Nasser, Ghana's Nkrumah and Indonesia's Sukarno, to give newly independent states a collective voice between the US and Soviet blocs during the Cold War and decolonisation.

History and role

NAM championed decolonisation, disarmament, a New International Economic Order in the 1970s, and anti-apartheid solidarity; its coherence was strained by members' own alignments (Cuba's Soviet ties, the Iran–Iraq war between members) and by the Cold War's end, which removed its founding rationale. It persists as the largest grouping of states after the UN itself, holding summits every few years (Kampala, 2024, most recently), coordinating developing-country positions on development, UN reform and Palestine, and overlapping with the Group of 77. Analysts debate whether "non-alignment" is being reinvented as "multi-alignment" in a US–China rivalry era.
