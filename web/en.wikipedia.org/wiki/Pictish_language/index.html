Pictish is the extinct language spoken by the Picts, the inhabitants of northern and eastern Scotland from late antiquity into the early Middle Ages. No connected Pictish text survives: the language is known only from place names and personal names in chronicles, a handful of words recorded by writers such as Bede and Adomnán, and some forty short inscriptions in the ogham alphabet, most of them still unintelligible.

Classification debate

For centuries Pictish was a scholarly battleground. Some 19th-century writers held it to be a non-Indo-European relic of pre-Celtic Europe. Kenneth Jackson's influential 1955 study proposed two languages: a Brittonic Celtic spoken language and an older non-Celtic one behind the ogham inscriptions. Most modern scholarship, notably Katherine Forsyth's reassessment, concludes that Pictish was simply a northern Brittonic language — a close relative of Welsh, Cumbric, and Breton, influenced by Goidelic (Gaelic) — and that the ogham difficulties arise from abbreviation, damage, and unusual spellings rather than a lost non-Celtic tongue.

Evidence

The place-name record is the richest source: elements such as pit- ("portion of land", in Pitlochry, Pittenweem), aber- ("river mouth", as in Aberdeen), and pert- (Perth) are characteristically Brittonic. Bede, writing about 731, lists Pictish as one of the five languages of Britain, alongside English, British, Irish, and Latin. A famous anecdote has Columba preaching to the Pictish king Bridei through an interpreter, implying Pictish differed from Gaelic.

Decline

After the Gaelic-speaking kingdom of Dál Riata and Pictland united under Kenneth MacAlpin in the 9th century (the kingdom of Alba), Gaelic spread at the expense of Pictish, which had probably died out by about 1100, leaving traces only in names and a small substrate vocabulary.
