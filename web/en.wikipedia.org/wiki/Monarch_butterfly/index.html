The monarch butterfly or simply monarch (Danaus plexippus) is a milkweed butterfly in the family Nymphalidae, famous for its bright orange wings veined and bordered with black, and for the long-distance seasonal migration of North American populations. Other common names include the milkweed, common tiger, wanderer, and black-veined brown. The monarch is the state insect of several U.S. states and one of the most widely recognised butterflies in the world.

Description and life cycle

Adults have a wingspan of 8.9–10.2 cm. The upperside is bright orange with thick black veins and wide black borders containing two rows of white spots; males have a black spot on a hindwing vein and slightly thinner veins than females. The vivid colouring is aposematic: monarch caterpillars feed exclusively on milkweed plants and sequester toxic cardiac glycosides (cardenolides), making both larvae and adults distasteful to predators. The viceroy butterfly, once thought a Batesian mimic, is now known to be unpalatable itself (a Müllerian co-mimic). Females lay single eggs on milkweed leaves; the yellow, black-and-white banded caterpillar passes through five instars, then pupates in a jade-green chrysalis with gold spots. Adults of breeding generations live only a few weeks.

Migration

Eastern North American monarchs migrate each autumn up to about 4,800 km (3,000 miles) to overwintering sites in oyamel fir forests in the mountains of central Mexico — a journey completed by a single long-lived "Methuselah" generation that enters reproductive diapause and lives up to eight months. In spring this generation begins the northward return, breeding successive generations that recolonise the United States and southern Canada. Western monarchs migrate to groves along the California coast. Monarchs navigate using a sun compass with circadian clocks located partly in their antennae. The Mexican overwintering colonies, discovered by science in 1975 (after tagging work begun by Fred and Norah Urquhart), are protected in the Monarch Butterfly Biosphere Reserve, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Distribution and status

Monarchs occur throughout the Americas and, having spread across the Pacific in the 19th century, in Australia, New Zealand and parts of southern Europe. Populations have declined sharply since the 1990s — the eastern overwintering area in Mexico has shrunk from a 1996–97 high of about 18 hectares to under 1 hectare in some recent years, and western counts have at times fallen below 2,000 butterflies — driven by loss of milkweed from herbicide use, habitat loss, logging at overwintering sites, and climate change. In July 2022 the IUCN listed the migratory monarch (D. p. plexippus) as Endangered; in December 2024 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing it as threatened under the Endangered Species Act.
