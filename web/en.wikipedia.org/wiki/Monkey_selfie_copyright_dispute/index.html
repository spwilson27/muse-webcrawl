Monkey selfie copyright dispute

The monkey selfie copyright dispute is a series of legal disputes about the copyright status of selfies taken by a Celebes crested macaque using equipment belonging to the British nature photographer David Slater. The photographs, taken in 2011 in the Tangkoko Reserve in Sulawesi, Indonesia, went viral: one monkey, later identified in litigation as Naruto, took photographs of itself with Slater's unattended camera. When the images appeared in a Wikimedia Commons upload and were reported by blogs and news outlets, Slater asserted copyright and demanded removal; Wikimedia refused, reasoning that a non-human animal cannot hold copyright and that Slater, not having taken the photographs, could not either.

Background

Slater spent days photographing the macaques, setting up a camera on a tripod with a remote release so the animals could trigger it themselves. The resulting images were widely published and initially credited under Slater's copyright. In 2014 the U.S. Copyright Office updated its guidance to state that works produced by nature, animals, or plants lack human authorship and are not copyrightable — citing a photograph taken by a monkey as an explicit example.

Litigation

In 2015, People for the Ethical Treatment of Animals (PETA) sued Slater and publisher Blurb in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, claiming Naruto held the copyright and that PETA should administer it for the monkey's benefit. The district court dismissed the suit in 2016, holding that animals lack statutory standing under the Copyright Act. The parties settled in 2017, with Slater agreeing to donate 25% of future revenue from the images to charities protecting crested macaques, but the Ninth Circuit nevertheless issued a ruling in 2018 affirming dismissal and criticizing PETA; it vacated the settlement-related portions and confirmed that animals cannot sue for copyright infringement.

Reactions and significance

The case became a leading public example in debates over authorship, animal rights, and works created without human authors. Similar questions have since arisen over art generated by artificial intelligence, where authorities have likewise required human authorship for copyright protection.
