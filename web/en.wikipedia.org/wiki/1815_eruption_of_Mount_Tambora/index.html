The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora, on the island of Sumbawa in present-day Indonesia, was the most powerful volcanic eruption in recorded human history, with a Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) of 7. The eruption column reached the stratosphere, more than 40 kilometres (25 mi) high, and ejected at least 37 cubic kilometres of material. The eruption caused a global climate anomaly known as the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816.

The eruption

Tambora had been dormant for centuries before showing signs of activity beginning in 1812. On the evening of April 5, 1815, a moderate eruption occurred, with detonations heard as far away as Jakarta. The eruption culminated on April 10–11: explosions were heard up to 2,600 km (1,600 mi) away, and ash fell as far as 1,300 km away. Pyroclastic flows destroyed the villages around the mountain, including the town of Tambora, wiping out the local Tambora culture and its language. The mountain's summit collapsed into a caldera 6–7 km wide and over a kilometre deep; Tambora lost about 1,400 m of height.

Human toll

At least 10,000–11,000 people were killed directly by the eruption on Sumbawa, and an estimated 49,000–60,000 more died across the region from famine and disease in the following months, making the total death toll at least 71,000 — the deadliest volcanic eruption in recorded history.

Global climate effects

The eruption injected an enormous quantity of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, forming a global aerosol veil that lowered average global temperatures by as much as 0.4–0.7 °C. In 1816 — the "Year Without a Summer" — frosts occurred in New England in every summer month, crops failed across Europe and North America, and food prices soared, causing the worst famine of the nineteenth century. Cultural effects included the gloomy summer of 1816 during which Mary Shelley wrote Frankenstein at Lake Geneva.
