Atmos clock

The Atmos clock is a brand of clock manufactured by Jaeger-LeCoultre in Switzerland that does not need to be wound manually. It gets the energy it needs to run from temperature and atmospheric pressure changes in the environment, and can run for years without human intervention.

History

The principle goes back to experimental "perpetual" clocks of the eighteenth century, including one by Cornelis Drebbel. The modern Atmos was developed by the Swiss engineer Jean-Léon Reutter, whose Atmos I design of 1928 was taken over by Jaeger-LeCoultre in the 1930s. Successive movements—most famously the calibre 519 and later 540 series—refined the design. Atmos clocks became traditional gifts of the Swiss Confederation to distinguished guests, and owners have included heads of state and popes.

Mechanism

Inside the clock is a hermetically sealed capsule filled with a mixture of gas and liquid ethyl chloride. As the temperature rises, the ethyl chloride vaporises and expands the capsule like a bellows; cooling contracts it. This motion winds the mainspring: a temperature variation of only one degree Celsius in the range between 15 °C and 30 °C, or a pressure variation of about 3 mmHg, supplies the clock with enough energy for about two days of operation.

The timekeeping element is a torsion pendulum—a heavy annular balance suspended from a thin wire—which swings very slowly, rotating back and forth with a period of one minute, minimizing energy losses. Because so little energy is needed, the mechanism experiences almost no wear, and Atmos movements are famed for their longevity, though the bellows eventually lose their gas and require service.
