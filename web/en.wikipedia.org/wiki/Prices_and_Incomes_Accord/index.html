The Prices and Incomes Accord was a series of agreements between the Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU) and the Australian Labor Party government of Bob Hawke and Paul Keating, beginning in 1983. Under the Accords, unions agreed to moderate wage claims in exchange for improvements to the "social wage" — tax cuts, superannuation, Medicare, family assistance and other benefits — and a voice in economic policy.

Background

Labor came to office in March 1983 amid stagflation, a severe drought and high unemployment. The first Accord (Mark I) restored centralised wage fixation through the Australian Conciliation and Arbitration Commission, traded wage restraint for the reintroduction of universal health insurance (Medicare), and helped cut inflation from around 11 per cent while supporting a return to growth without the wages breakout many feared.

Successive versions

Eight versions (Marks I–VIII) were negotiated between 1983 and 1996. Later Accords managed the response to the 1986 deterioration in the terms of trade — including discounted wage increases offset by tax cuts and the introduction of award superannuation, which grew into Australia's compulsory superannuation system — and gradually moved the industrial relations system toward enterprise bargaining, formalised in Accord Mark VII and the Industrial Relations Reform Act 1993.

Assessment

The Accord is widely credited with delivering a sustained fall in inflation and industrial disputation, funding a major expansion of the social safety net, and underpinning Australia's long expansion of the 1990s. Critics, including some on the left, argue it entrenched real wage restraint and demobilised the union movement; union density fell sharply during the Accord years. It remains a landmark example of a neo-corporatist incomes policy.
