Russkaya Pravda

The Russkaya Pravda (Old East Slavic: Правда роусьскаꙗ, "Rus' Justice" or "Rus' Truth") was the legal code of Kievan Rus and the subsequent Rus principalities. Compiled beginning in the 11th century, it is the principal source for the law and social structure of early medieval Rus.

Composition

The earliest layer, the Pravda of Yaroslav, is attributed to Yaroslav the Wise (early 11th century); his sons added the Pravda of the Yaroslavichi around 1072. The code survives in two main versions: the Short Version (Kratkaya Pravda) and the much longer Expanded Version (Prostrannaya Pravda) of the 12th–13th centuries, preserved in over a hundred manuscript copies, often within chronicles and legal miscellanies. It was rediscovered for scholarship by the historian Vasily Tatishchev in 1738.

Contents

The code consists largely of criminal and procedural law: schedules of fines (viry) payable for murder — differentiated by the victim's rank, with a prince's official valued far above a peasant — and for theft, arson, and damage to property. It marks a transition from blood feud toward monetary compensation: the Yaroslavichi's contribution abolished vengeance for murder in favour of fines. Provisions on the zakup (indebted labourer) and kholop (slave) illuminate Rus social hierarchy. Inheritance, loans, and commercial disputes in towns such as Kiev and Novgorod are also regulated.

Significance

Unlike contemporary Western codes, the Russkaya Pravda prescribes no death penalty, corporal punishment, or imprisonment — penalties are monetary. It influenced later law codes including the Lithuanian Statutes and remained in use in some regions into the 15th century, when it was superseded by the Sudebnik of 1497.
