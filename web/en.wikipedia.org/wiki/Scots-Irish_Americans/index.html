Scots-Irish Americans

Scots-Irish Americans (also called Scotch-Irish Americans, and Ulster Scots Americans) are Americans descended from Ulster Scots — largely Lowland Scots Presbyterians who settled in Ulster, Ireland, during the Plantation of Ulster in the 17th century, and who emigrated in large numbers to British North America in the 18th century. The term is used mainly in the United States to distinguish these Protestant settlers from later, predominantly Catholic Irish immigrants.

Migration

Between roughly 1717 and 1775, an estimated 200,000–300,000 Ulster Scots left Ireland for America, driven by rising rents (rack-renting), restrictions on Presbyterian worship and trade, drought, and linen-trade downturns. They entered chiefly through Philadelphia and New Castle, Delaware, and spread into the Pennsylvania backcountry, the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, and the Appalachian frontier of the Carolinas, Georgia, Tennessee, and Kentucky. Later waves continued into the 19th century, including after the Great Famine era, though distinct identity gradually merged into broader American frontier culture.

Culture and influence

The Scots-Irish are associated with Presbyterianism (and later Baptist and Methodist movements), log-cabin building, whiskey distilling traditions that fed into American bourbon and moonshine, and musical traditions — fiddle tunes and ballads — that are ancestral to Appalachian old-time and country music. Clan-based kin networks, a reputation for individualism and military service, and suspicion of central authority are recurring themes in writing about the group, popularised by works such as James Webb's Born Fighting (2004) and David Hackett Fischer's Albion's Seed (1989), which traces "borderlands" folkways to the Anglo-Scottish border country.

Notable figures

Depending on how ancestry is counted, numerous U.S. presidents have had Scots-Irish roots, among them Andrew Jackson, James K. Polk, James Buchanan, Chester A. Arthur, William McKinley, and Woodrow Wilson. Other prominent descendants include frontiersman Daniel Boone (of partly Ulster-Scots descent), Davy Crockett, Sam Houston, and Stonewall Jackson.
