Stateside Puerto Ricans

The Puerto Rican Day Parade in New York City.

Stateside Puerto Ricans (Spanish: puertorriqueños en Estados Unidos continentales) are Puerto Ricans living in the "mainland" or "stateside" United States — that is, in the 50 states and the District of Columbia, as distinct from the island of Puerto Rico. Puerto Ricans are U.S. citizens by birth under the Jones–Shafroth Act of 1917, so their movement to the mainland is internal migration rather than immigration.

Demographics

According to U.S. Census Bureau estimates, about 5.8 million people of Puerto Rican origin live stateside, compared with roughly 3.2 million on the island — meaning a clear majority of Puerto Ricans now live on the mainland. Historically concentrated in New York City, whose metropolitan area still hosts the largest Puerto Rican population of any mainland city, the diaspora has spread widely. Florida — especially Orlando and the I-4 corridor — has become a leading destination, followed by Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Illinois, Connecticut, Texas, and California.

History

Small communities formed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, including tobacco workers in Tampa and New York and sugar workers in Hawaii. The great wave of migration came after the Second World War, when cheap air travel and industrial recruitment drew hundreds of thousands to mainland factories and farms; this era, sometimes called the Great Migration (La Gran Migración), filled neighborhoods such as East Harlem ("Spanish Harlem" or El Barrio), the South Bronx, and Chicago's Humboldt Park. The New York-born second generation became known as Nuyoricans, a term later embraced as a cultural identity and artistic movement. Migration surged again in the 2010s, driven by Puerto Rico's debt crisis and, sharply, by Hurricane Maria in 2017.

Culture and politics

Stateside Puerto Ricans have shaped American music (salsa, Latin boogaloo, and reggaetón's mainland scenes), literature, and theater. Politically, they are a significant voting bloc in swing states such as Florida and Pennsylvania, and Puerto Ricans elected to Congress serve with full voting rights — unlike the island's resident commissioner. The annual Puerto Rican Day Parade in New York, first held in 1958, is among the largest ethnic parades in the United States.
