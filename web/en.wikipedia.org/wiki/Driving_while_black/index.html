Driving while black

"Driving while black" (DWB) is a sardonic description of racial profiling in the United States, referring to the perception that a motorist can be pulled over by a police officer largely because of racial bias rather than any apparent traffic violation. The phrase is a play on "driving while intoxicated" (DWI), the legal term for drunk driving, and implies that race itself is treated as an offence.

Origins and usage

The phrase entered wide circulation in the 1990s, when lawsuits and journalism documented the disproportionate stopping of Black motorists on highways such as Interstate 95 and the New Jersey Turnpike. The American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) published influential reports on the practice, including a 1999 report titled Driving While Black: Racial Profiling On Our Nation's Highways. Similar phrases — "driving while brown," "flying while Muslim," and others — have been coined by analogy.

Evidence

Large-scale studies of traffic-stop data, most notably the Stanford Open Policing Project's analysis of tens of millions of stops, have found that Black and Hispanic drivers are stopped and searched at higher rates than white drivers, while contraband is found on white drivers at equal or higher rates. The "veil of darkness" test — comparing the racial composition of stops in daylight, when race is visible, with stops after dark — has also been used as evidence that race influences stop decisions.

Legal and policy responses

A number of U.S. states have passed laws requiring the collection and publication of traffic-stop data, and some jurisdictions have limited pretextual stops for minor equipment violations. Landmark litigation, such as the New Jersey consent decree of 1999, placed state police under federal monitoring after findings of discriminatory enforcement along the Turnpike.
