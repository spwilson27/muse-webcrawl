Old Prussians

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Old Prussian tribes between the Vistula and the Neman, c. 1200.

The Old Prussians were a Baltic people who inhabited the lands on the south-eastern shore of the Baltic Sea, between the Vistula and the lower Neman, in the region later known as Prussia. They spoke Old Prussian, a now-extinct West Baltic language, and practised a pagan religion related to those of the Lithuanians and Latvians. Organised into tribes — including the Sambians, Natangians, Warmians, Bartians, Galindians and others — they never formed a single state.

History

Known to Tacitus and later writers as part of the peoples of the amber coast, the Prussians traded amber, furs and grain with their neighbours and resisted Christianisation for centuries; the missionary Adalbert of Prague was killed among them in 997, and Bruno of Querfurt in 1009. Polish attempts at conquest in the twelfth and early thirteenth centuries provoked Prussian raids in return, and in 1226 Duke Konrad of Masovia invited the Teutonic Order to subdue them. The ensuing Prussian Crusade, waged over some fifty years, combined conquest, castle-building and German and Polish colonisation. Major Prussian uprisings, especially the Great Prussian Uprising (1260–1274), were eventually crushed, and the surviving population was Christianised and progressively Germanised and Polonised.

Culture and language

Old Prussian religion centred on sacred groves, the god Perkūns (thunder) and a priestly class; its chief sanctuary was said to be at Romuva. The language, attested mainly in three small catechisms printed in Königsberg in the sixteenth century and in the Elbing Vocabulary, died out in the late seventeenth or early eighteenth century, hastened by plague in 1709–1711. The name "Prussia" was inherited by the German state that grew from the Teutonic Order's territory, though the later Prussians were German-speaking. Modern attempts to revive the Old Prussian language have been made since the late twentieth century, and a small neo-pagan movement, Romuva, looks partly to Old Prussian tradition.
