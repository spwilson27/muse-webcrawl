Höfuðlausn

Höfuðlausn (Old Norse for "Head-Ransom" or "Head's Redemption") is a skaldic poem attributed to Egill Skallagrímsson, the Icelandic poet and warrior of the 10th century. According to Egils saga, Egill composed the poem in a single night while under sentence of death in York, and recited it the following morning before King Eric Bloodaxe, who was so impressed that he spared Egill's life — ransoming his head with verse.

Content and form

The poem is a drápa of twenty stanzas composed in the runhent (end-rhymed) metre, unusual in Old Norse skaldic verse, which more typically employed dróttkvætt without end-rhyme. Egill may have adopted runhent under the influence of Old English rhyming poetry encountered during his stay in England. The poem praises Eric's battle deeds and generosity, opening with the poet's difficult journey westward to present his verse to the king.

The saga account

In Egils saga, Egill is shipwrecked on the Northumbrian coast and falls into the hands of Eric Bloodaxe, his enemy. His friend Arinbjörnn intercedes, and Egill is granted one night to compose a poem in the king's honour. A swallow chirping at his window — interpreted in the saga as a shapeshifted Óðinn or a hostile spirit — disturbs his work until Arinbjörnn drives it away. At dawn Egill recites the finished drápa, and Eric grants him his life, remarking that the poem has bought his head.

Authenticity

Scholars have debated whether the poem as preserved is genuinely Egill's work or a later composition placed in the saga. The surviving text of Höfuðlausn is transmitted in manuscripts of Egils saga, and its metre and vocabulary have been studied extensively in discussions of the early development of skaldic poetry.
