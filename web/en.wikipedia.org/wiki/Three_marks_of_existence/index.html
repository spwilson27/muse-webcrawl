Three marks of existence

All conditioned things arise and pass away: the mark of impermanence (anicca).

In Buddhism, the three marks of existence (Pali: tilakkhaṇa; Sanskrit: trilakṣaṇa) are three characteristics shared by all conditioned phenomena in samsara: dukkha (suffering, unsatisfactoriness, stress), anicca (impermanence) and anattā (non-self, the absence of an abiding essence). Direct insight into these three marks is held to uproot the ignorance that binds beings to rebirth and is a central theme of insight meditation (vipassanā).

The three marks

Dukkha

Dukkha denotes the suffering and unsatisfactoriness inherent in conditioned existence: physical and mental pain, the distress of change, and the deeper instability of anything formed by conditions, which cannot provide lasting satisfaction. The first of the Four Noble Truths states that "there is dukkha"; understanding it fully is the first task of the path.

Anicca

Anicca (Sanskrit: anitya) is impermanence: all conditioned things arise, alter and cease without exception. Nothing in the world of the five aggregates remains fixed even for two consecutive moments. Seeing this, the meditator loses fascination with phenomena that cannot be kept.

Anattā

Anattā (Sanskrit: anātman) is non-self: no conditioned phenomenon, taken singly or together, is or contains a permanent, independent "I" or essence. The teaching appears already in the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, the Buddha's second discourse, which examines each of the five aggregates (form, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness) and shows that none can be a self, since none is under one's control and all are subject to change.

Canonical formulation

A well-known summary occurs in the Dhammapada (verses 277–279): "All conditioned things are impermanent (sabbe saṅkhārā aniccā); all conditioned things are suffering (sabbe saṅkhārā dukkhā); all things are not-self (sabbe dhammā anattā)." Note that the third statement extends to "all dhammas", including the unconditioned, nirvana, whereas the first two are restricted to conditioned formations; some later systems accordingly debated how many marks there are and what exactly each covers.

Relation to practice

The three marks are not merely propositions to be believed but characteristics to be directly perceived. In the insight knowledges of Theravada meditation manuals, contemplation of arising and passing away matures into knowledge of dissolution, disenchantment (nibbidā) and equanimity toward formations, culminating in the path moments of awakening. In Mahayana traditions the marks are likewise fundamental, framed by the doctrine of emptiness (śūnyatā), and the Heart Sutra's negation of self is read against the same background.
