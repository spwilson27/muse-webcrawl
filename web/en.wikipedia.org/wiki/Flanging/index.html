Flanging

Flanging is an audio effect produced by mixing two identical signals together, one signal delayed by a small and gradually changing period, usually smaller than 20 milliseconds. This produces a swept comb filter effect: peaks and notches are produced in the resulting frequency spectrum, related to each other in a linear harmonic series. Varying the time delay causes these to sweep up and down the frequency spectrum. A flanger is an effects unit that creates the effect.

Origin

The name comes from the original mechanical method: two reel-to-reel tape machines playing the same recording in synchrony, with the engineer pressing a finger on the flange of one supply reel to slow it slightly, then releasing it. The resulting sweeping "whooshing" or jet-like sound was popularized in the 1960s. Earlier, Les Paul had experimented with variable-speed acetate recorders, and the effect appeared on records such as Toni Fisher's "The Big Hurt" (1959), often cited as the first prominent use of flanging. The Beatles and producer George Martin used related techniques extensively in 1966, alongside artificial double tracking, and engineer Ken Townsend's ADT system.

The track "Itchycoo Park" (1967) by the Small Faces and the Jimi Hendrix recording "Bold as Love" (1967) are frequently cited early examples of flanging in popular music.

Electronic and digital flangers

In the 1970s, the effect was reproduced with analog electronics using bucket-brigade delay chips, in units such as the Electro-Harmonix Electric Mistress and the MXR Flanger. Rack and pedal flangers offer controls for delay time, sweep rate (often by a low-frequency oscillator), sweep depth, feedback (which intensifies the resonance), and the mix of dry and processed signal. Modern implementations are commonly digital, in software plug-ins and multi-effects processors.

Comparison with similar effects

Flanging is related to phasing and chorus. Phasing uses all-pass filters to produce notches that are not harmonically related, while flanging's delay-based notches form a regular harmonic series. Chorus uses longer delays, typically 20–30 milliseconds, without the sweeping feedback character.
