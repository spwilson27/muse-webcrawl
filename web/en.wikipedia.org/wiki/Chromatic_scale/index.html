The chromatic scale is a musical scale with twelve pitches, each a semitone above or below its adjacent pitches. In twelve-tone equal temperament, the chromatic scale divides the octave into twelve equal semitones of 100 cents each, and it is the complete collection of pitches available in that tuning system.

Structure and notation

Ascending chromatic scales are conventionally written with sharps, and descending ones with flats, avoiding mixing accidentals where possible. Unlike diatonic scales, the chromatic scale has no tonic or inherent hierarchy: every pitch is equally spaced, which makes it a "non-diatonic" resource used for colour, passing notes, and modulation.

Use in music

Chromatic passing tones have enriched Western harmony since the Renaissance madrigal; by the Romantic era, composers such as Wagner extended chromatic harmony toward the dissolution of tonality. In the 20th century Arnold Schoenberg built the twelve-tone technique on the chromatic aggregate, treating all twelve pitch classes as equal. Chromatic runs are also idiomatic in jazz improvisation and in virtuoso instrumental display pieces such as Rimsky-Korsakov's "Flight of the Bumblebee".

Other temperaments

In just intonation and historical meantone systems, chromatic semitones are not all equal: the diatonic and chromatic semitones differ in size, and enharmonic notes such as C♯ and D♭ are distinct pitches. Extended instruments and split-key keyboards were sometimes built to accommodate these distinctions.
