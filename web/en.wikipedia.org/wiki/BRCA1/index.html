Breast cancer type 1 susceptibility protein is a protein that in humans is encoded by the BRCA1 gene. BRCA1 is a tumour suppressor protein found in the cells of the breast and other tissues, where it helps repair damaged DNA and, if the damage cannot be repaired, helps ensure the cell dies rather than becoming cancerous. Germline mutations in BRCA1 (and the related gene BRCA2) greatly increase the lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer and are the cause of most cases of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome.

Discovery

Evidence for a breast cancer susceptibility gene on chromosome 17 was reported in 1990 by Mary-Claire King's laboratory, which mapped the locus by linkage analysis in families with early-onset breast cancer. The gene itself was isolated in 1994, when a team led by researchers at Myriad Genetics and collaborators announced its cloning. The discovery triggered an early major controversy over gene patenting: Myriad's patents on BRCA1 and BRCA2 were challenged, and in 2013 the United States Supreme Court ruled in Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics that naturally occurring DNA sequences cannot be patented simply because they have been isolated.

Structure and function

The BRCA1 gene spans roughly 81 kilobases and contains 24 exons, producing a protein of 1,863 amino acids. Its key structural features include an N-terminal RING finger domain, which binds the partner protein BARD1 and confers E3 ubiquitin ligase activity; a central region containing nuclear localisation signals and binding sites for proteins such as RAD51, c-Myc and RB; a coiled-coil domain that binds PALB2, linking BRCA1 to BRCA2; and tandem C-terminal BRCT repeats that bind phosphorylated proteins involved in the DNA damage response.

BRCA1 is a central organiser of the repair of DNA double-strand breaks by homologous recombination. It promotes DNA end resection, recruits the PALB2–BRCA2–RAD51 repair machinery to break sites, and participates in checkpoint signalling through interactions with ATM, ATR and CHK2. Cells lacking functional BRCA1 cannot faithfully repair double-strand breaks and instead rely on error-prone pathways, producing the chromosomal instability characteristic of BRCA1-deficient tumours.

Clinical significance

More than a thousand distinct pathogenic variants of BRCA1 have been described. Women who inherit a harmful mutation have a lifetime breast cancer risk commonly estimated at 55–72% and an ovarian cancer risk of roughly 39–44%, far above population averages. Founder mutations are particularly common in Ashkenazi Jewish populations, where about 1 in 40 individuals carries one of three founder variants in BRCA1 or BRCA2.

Genetic testing for BRCA1 mutations is recommended for individuals with a strong family history of breast or ovarian cancer. Management options for carriers include enhanced surveillance, risk-reducing medication, and prophylactic mastectomy or salpingo-oophorectomy. BRCA1-deficient tumours are selectively sensitive to PARP inhibitors, a class of drugs that exploit the tumour's defective DNA repair — an example of synthetic lethality in cancer therapy. Biallelic (inherited from both parents) pathogenic variants can cause a subtype of Fanconi anaemia.
