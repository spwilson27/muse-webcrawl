History of Canada

The history of Canada covers the period from the arrival of Indigenous peoples thousands of years ago to the present. Indigenous societies across the territory had complex cultures, trade networks and languages long before European contact; Norse explorers reached Newfoundland around 1000 AD, but permanent European settlement began with France in the early 17th century.

Colonial era

Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec City in 1608, and New France developed along the St. Lawrence, its economy centred on the fur trade. After decades of war, Britain gained control of Canada in the Seven Years' War, confirmed by the Treaty of Paris (1763); the Quebec Act (1774) accommodated French Catholic society. The American Revolution brought Loyalist refugees north, and the War of 1812 was fought along the border. Rebellions in 1837–38 led to responsible government and the union of the Canadas.

Confederation and expansion

On 1 July 1867 the British North America Act united Ontario, Quebec, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia as the Dominion of Canada. Under its first prime minister, John A. Macdonald, Canada expanded west to the Pacific (British Columbia joined in 1871) and the Canadian Pacific Railway was completed in 1885. Indigenous peoples were dispossessed through treaties, reserves and the residential school system. Canada contributed heavily to both world wars, gaining international standing; the Statute of Westminster (1931) confirmed legislative autonomy, and the Constitution was patriated with the Charter of Rights and Freedoms in 1982. Quebec sovereignty referendums in 1980 and 1995 narrowly failed. Newfoundland, the last province to join, entered Confederation in 1949, and Nunavut was created in 1999.
