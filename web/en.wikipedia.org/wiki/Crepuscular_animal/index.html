Crepuscular animals

SubtypesMatutinal (dawn), vespertine (dusk)

Crepuscular animals are those active primarily during the twilight periods of dawn and dusk. The word derives from the Latin crepusculum, "twilight." Species active chiefly at dawn are termed matutinal, and those active chiefly at dusk vespertine; many species show both peaks, resting through the middle of the day and the night.

[Image: a deer active at twilight]

Adaptive significance

Twilight activity can balance competing pressures. Light levels are low enough to hinder visually hunting predators, yet high enough for the crepuscular animal itself to navigate and forage — many crepuscular species have eyes well adapted to dim light. In hot climates, dawn and dusk avoid the heat of midday; the pattern also reduces competition with strictly diurnal and nocturnal species. Biting insects such as mosquitoes, and their predators such as bats and fireflies, are classic dusk-active examples.

Examples

Many familiar mammals are crepuscular, including deer, rabbits, skunks, opossums, domestic cats, guinea pigs, and Syrian hamsters. Among birds, common nighthawks and many owls hunt at twilight, and moths are well-known vespertine insects. In captivity, artificial lighting and human disturbance can shift activity patterns, so the crepuscular label describes the natural rhythm under undisturbed conditions.
