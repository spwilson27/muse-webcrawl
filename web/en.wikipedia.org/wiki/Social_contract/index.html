Social contract

Social contract
FieldPolitical philosophy
Key figuresThomas Hobbes, John Locke, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, John Rawls
Core ideaPolitical authority rests on (actual or hypothetical) consent of the governed
[Image: Citizens gathered to sign a foundational charter document]

The social contract is a model in moral and political philosophy: individuals in a 'state of nature' surrender some freedoms to a sovereign or society in exchange for security and rights, so that legitimate authority rests on consent. Hobbes's Leviathan (1651) made the contract nearly absolute to escape a life 'solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short'; John Locke made government conditional and revocable; Rousseau recast it as the general will.

Modern forms

John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971) revived contract thinking with the 'original position' behind a veil of ignorance. Critics note historical contracts never happened and that actual societies' membership is unchosen; contractarians answer that the model is a device of representation, testing whether principles could be reasonably endorsed.
