Electric battery

An electric battery is a source of electric power consisting of one or more electrochemical cells with external connections for powering electrical devices. When a battery supplies power, its positive terminal is the cathode and its negative terminal is the anode. Electrons flow from the anode through an external circuit to the cathode, while ions move through an electrolyte inside the cell.

History

Alessandro Volta invented the first true battery, the voltaic pile, in 1800. The Daniell cell followed in 1836, the lead-acid battery was invented by Gaston Planté in 1859, and Georges Leclanché's cell of 1866 led to the dry cell. Nickel-cadmium, alkaline, and lithium-ion chemistries were commercialised over the twentieth century, with lithium-ion batteries — developed by John Goodenough, M. Stanley Whittingham and Akira Yoshino — winning the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Types

Primary batteries are single-use and are discarded when discharged; common types include zinc-carbon and alkaline cells. Secondary (rechargeable) batteries can be recharged by reversing the current, and include lead-acid, nickel-metal hydride and lithium-ion types, used in vehicles, portable electronics and grid storage.

Capacity and ratings

Battery capacity is measured in ampere-hours (Ah) or watt-hours (Wh). Voltage depends on cell chemistry: about 1.5 V for alkaline, 2 V per cell for lead-acid, and 3.6–3.7 V for lithium-ion. Performance falls with high discharge rates, low temperature, and age.

Environmental issues

Batteries contain toxic metals and corrosive materials, and many jurisdictions require recycling. Spent lithium-ion batteries are increasingly recycled to recover lithium, cobalt and nickel.
