The Old River Control Structure is a floodgate system constructed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers at the junction of the Mississippi River and the Atchafalaya River in Louisiana. Its purpose is to regulate the flow of water between the two rivers and prevent the Mississippi from changing course — an event geologists consider inevitable over time — away from Baton Rouge and New Orleans and down the shorter, steeper Atchafalaya channel to the Gulf of Mexico.

Background

By the early 20th century, the Atchafalaya was capturing an ever-growing share of the Mississippi's flow; hydrologist Harold Fisk warned in 1952 that a course change was imminent and would devastate the ports of Baton Rouge and New Orleans while flooding the Atchafalaya Basin. Congress authorized the project in the Flood Control Act of 1954. The completed complex maintains the distribution Congress mandated: roughly 70 percent of the combined flow down the Mississippi and 30 percent down the Atchafalaya, the split that existed in 1950.

Components

The complex comprises the Low Sill and Overbank structures (completed in 1963), the Auxiliary structure (completed in 1986, after the flood of 1973 nearly undermined the Low Sill structure), a lock for navigation, and the nearby Sidney A. Murray Jr. Hydroelectric Station (1990). The Morganza Spillway, part of the broader system, can divert additional floodwater into the Atchafalaya Basin.

Significance

Geologist John McPhee's widely read account "Atchafalaya" (in The Control of Nature, 1989) brought the structure's existential role to public attention: it is effectively the only thing keeping the Mississippi on its present course, and its failure during a major flood would reorder the geography and economy of south Louisiana within a few years.
