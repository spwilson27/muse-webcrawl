Cybernetics is an interdisciplinary field concerned with regulatory and purposive systems — their structures, constraints, possibilities, and control. It is the study of feedback, black boxes, and derived concepts such as communication and control in living organisms, machines, and organisations. The word comes from the Greek kybernētēs, "steersman" or "governor", and was popularised by Norbert Wiener in his 1948 book Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine.

History

The field crystallised around the Macy conferences (1946–1953), which brought together Wiener, John von Neumann, Warren McCulloch, Claude Shannon, Margaret Mead, Gregory Bateson, and others to explore circular causal and feedback mechanisms in biological and social systems. Earlier roots include James Clerk Maxwell's analysis of governors (1868), early work on servomechanisms, and wartime research on anti-aircraft fire control.

Second-order cybernetics

In the 1970s, Heinz von Foerster and others distinguished "second-order cybernetics", in which the observer is recognised as part of the system being observed — a shift influential in family therapy, management theory, and constructivist philosophy. Cybernetics contributed foundational ideas to control theory, systems theory, computer science, cognitive science, and robotics, even as many of its topics were absorbed into those more specialised disciplines.
