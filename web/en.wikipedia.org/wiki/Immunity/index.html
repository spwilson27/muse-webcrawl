Immunity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Immunity is the state of being protected against infectious disease. The immune system defends the body on two levels: innate immunity — the barriers and fast, general responses present from birth — and adaptive (acquired) immunity, the specific, memory-forming responses of lymphocytes that improve with exposure and underlie vaccination.

Innate and adaptive arms

Innate defences include skin and mucus, stomach acid, inflammation, phagocytes, natural killer cells and the complement system; they respond within minutes to hours to broad classes of threats. Adaptive immunity, mediated by B cells (which make antibodies) and T cells (which kill infected cells and coordinate responses), takes days to build on first exposure but leaves memory cells that respond faster and stronger on re-encounter — the basis of lasting immunity after infection or vaccination.

Types and problems

Immunity may be active (made by one's own system, after infection or vaccine) or passive (borrowed antibodies, as from mother to baby or antibody treatments). Failures run both ways: immunodeficiency (from HIV, chemotherapy or inherited defects) leaves the body open to infection, while excessive or misdirected immunity causes allergy and autoimmune disease. Herd immunity describes the indirect protection arising when enough of a population is immune to slow transmission.
