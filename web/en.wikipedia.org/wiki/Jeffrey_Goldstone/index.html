Goldstone, whose theorem links broken symmetries to massless particles.

Jeffrey Goldstone (born September 3, 1933) is a British theoretical physicist known for Goldstone's theorem, which states that when a continuous symmetry is spontaneously broken, massless particles — known as Nambu–Goldstone bosons — necessarily appear. His work on symmetry breaking in field theory became a foundation of the Standard Model of particle physics and of condensed-matter theory.

Early life and education

Goldstone was born in Manchester and educated at Manchester Grammar School and the University of Manchester, where he studied physics. He did his doctoral work at the Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge, under the supervision of Hans Bethe, completing his PhD in 1956, and held research positions at Cambridge and at Harvard before joining the MIT faculty in 1977, where he became Ford Professor of Physics and directed the Center for Theoretical Physics.

Goldstone's theorem

In a 1961 paper on field theories with "superconductor" solutions, Goldstone examined models in which a continuous symmetry of the Lagrangian is not respected by the ground state, and showed that such models imply the existence of massless spin-zero particles. Together with the parallel insights of Yoichiro Nambu, this result explained how symmetries could be hidden rather than manifest in nature. The mechanism of spontaneous symmetry breaking underlies the Higgs mechanism in electroweak theory (where the would-be Goldstone bosons are absorbed by gauge fields), the description of pions as near-Goldstone bosons of chiral symmetry in QCD, and phenomena such as phonons and magnons in solids.

Other work

Goldstone also contributed to the theory of many-body systems, solitons, and the application of path-integral methods, and collaborated on influential work concerning the quantum mechanics of particles with spin and the structure of nuclear and atomic systems. He is a fellow of the Royal Society and of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, and received the Dannie Heineman Prize for Mathematical Physics in 1985, shared for work on symmetry breaking.
