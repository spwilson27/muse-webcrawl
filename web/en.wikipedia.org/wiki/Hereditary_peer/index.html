Hereditary peer

The hereditary peers form part of the peerage in the United Kingdom. As of 2024 there are 805 hereditary peers, including dukes, marquesses, earls, viscounts and barons. Historically, hereditary peers automatically held seats in the House of Lords, but reforms have sharply reduced that right.

History

Peerages were originally feudal titles tied to land and counsel to the monarch. By the Peerage Act 1963, hereditary peers could disclaim their titles, and women could inherit some peerages and sit in the Lords.

House of Lords Act 1999

The House of Lords Act 1999 removed the automatic right of most hereditary peers to sit and vote. Ninety-two were retained: 90 elected by their fellow peers, plus the Earl Marshal and Lord Great Chamberlain as office holders. Vacancies among the 90 are filled by by-elections, a system later governments proposed to abolish.

Ranks and inheritance

Most peerages descend by male primogeniture under their letters patent, though some Scottish and older baronies can pass to daughters or through female lines. New hereditary peerages have rarely been created since 1964, except for members of the Royal Family.
