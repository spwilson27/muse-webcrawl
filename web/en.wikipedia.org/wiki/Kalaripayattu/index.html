Kalaripayattu, also known simply as Kalari, is an Indian martial art that originated in present-day Kerala, on the south-western coast of India. Frequently described as one of the oldest surviving martial arts in the world, it combines strikes, kicks, grappling, preset forms and weaponry with healing techniques and strikes to vital points (marma) drawn from Ayurvedic tradition. Training takes place in a kalari, a purpose-built training ground, often a sunken pit with an earthen floor.

History

Combat traditions in the region are ancient; Sangam literature of the early centuries CE describes warriors, duels and martial training in the Tamilakam, the cultural region that included modern Kerala. Legend attributes the art's creation to the warrior-sage Parashurama, said to have reclaimed the land of Kerala from the sea. The art took its mature form during the medieval period, when Kerala was divided among feuding chiefdoms (nadus) whose warrior retainers, including the Nair militias, settled disputes through formal duels called ankam. Heroic ballads such as the Vadakkan Pattukal (northern ballads) celebrate figures like Thacholi Othenan and Aromal Chekavar.

Under British colonial rule, Kalaripayattu was suppressed following the revolt of Pazhassi Raja and the disarmament of the Nairs in the early 19th century, and practice declined sharply. A revival began in the early 20th century, led by masters such as C. V. Narayanan Nair, Kottackal Kanaran Gurukkal and Chambadan Veetil Narayanan Nair, who re-established public kalaris and codified teaching lineages.

Practice

Training traditionally begins in childhood with meythari (body conditioning), including the distinctive low, wide stances and animal postures (vadivu) modelled on the elephant, horse, lion, boar, serpent, cat, cock and fish. Students progress to kolthari (wooden weapons such as the long staff and short stick), then ankathari (metal weapons, including the dagger, sword and shield, spear, and the flexible urumi sword), and finally verumkai (bare-handed combat), which includes locks, throws and strikes to vital points. Oil massage (uzhichil) is an integral part of conditioning, intended to keep the body supple.

Styles

Three broad regional styles are recognised: the northern style (vadakkan), emphasising weapons and acrobatic leaps, associated with the ballad country of North Malabar; the central style (madhya), a mixture of northern and southern elements; and the southern style (thekkan), emphasising bare-handed techniques and vital-point applications.

In popular culture

Kalaripayattu movements have influenced Kerala performance forms such as Kathakali and Theyyam, and its choreography has appeared in Indian and international cinema, video games and stage productions, contributing to a renewed global interest in the art.
