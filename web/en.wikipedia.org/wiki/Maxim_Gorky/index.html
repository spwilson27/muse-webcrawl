Alexei Maximovich Peshkov (Russian: Алексей Максимович Пешков; 28 March 1868 – 18 June 1936), better known as Maxim Gorky, was a Russian writer and political activist. Nominated five times for the Nobel Prize in Literature, he was the founder of the socialist realism literary method and a central figure of Soviet letters.

Life

Orphaned as a child and raised in poverty, Peshkov worked as a baker, dock hand, and railway watchman while wandering Russia; his first story, "Makar Chudra", appeared in 1892 under the pen name Gorky ("bitter"). His sketches and stories of tramps and the lower classes made him famous, and his play The Lower Depths (1902), premiered by the Moscow Art Theatre, became a worldwide success.

A supporter of the Bolsheviks and a friend of Lenin, Gorky nevertheless criticised the October Revolution in his Untimely Thoughts and left Russia in 1921, living mainly in Sorrento, Italy. He returned permanently in 1932 at Stalin's invitation, was fêted as the dean of Soviet literature, and presided over the 1934 Congress of Soviet Writers that proclaimed socialist realism. His novel Mother (1906) was canonised as the method's model, and his autobiographical trilogy (My Childhood, In the World, My Universities) is considered his finest work. He died in 1936 under circumstances that have remained debated.

Legacy

His birthplace Nizhny Novgorod was renamed Gorky from 1932 to 1990 in his honour, as were Gorky Park in Moscow and the Maxim Gorky Literature Institute.
