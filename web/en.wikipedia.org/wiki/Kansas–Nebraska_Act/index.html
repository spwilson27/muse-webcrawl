The Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 was an organic act of the United States Congress that created the territories of Kansas and Nebraska. Drafted by Democratic Senator Stephen A. Douglas of Illinois and signed into law by President Franklin Pierce on 30 May 1854, the act allowed the settlers of each territory to decide for themselves whether to permit slavery within their borders — a doctrine Douglas called popular sovereignty.

Provisions and background

Douglas sought to organise the vast unorganised territory west of Missouri and Iowa, in part to facilitate a transcontinental railroad with a Chicago terminus. Because southern senators demanded that the new territories be open to slavery, the act explicitly repealed the Missouri Compromise of 1820, which had prohibited slavery north of latitude 36°30′ in the Louisiana Purchase lands. Both Kansas and Nebraska lay north of that line.

Consequences

The act provoked outrage in the North, where it was seen as a capitulation to the "Slave Power". Pro- and anti-slavery settlers rushed into Kansas, producing rival governments and the violent guerrilla conflict known as Bleeding Kansas, a dress rehearsal for the Civil War. Politically, the act shattered the Whig Party and united anti-slavery Northerners in the new Republican Party, founded in 1854 in direct response. Abraham Lincoln's return to politics — including his Peoria speech of October 1854 attacking the act — launched the career that carried him to the presidency and set the stage for his famous 1858 debates with Douglas.
