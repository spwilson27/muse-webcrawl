Alexander Fleming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Fleming's original culture plate with the Penicillium mould colony and the zone of dead bacteria around it]
The 1928 plate: staphylococci killed around the contaminating mould colony.

Sir Alexander Fleming (1881–1955) was a Scottish physician and microbiologist best known for discovering the world's first broadly effective antibiotic substance, penicillin, from the mould Penicillium rubens in 1928 — an achievement for which he shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945 with Howard Florey and Ernst Boris Chain.

Born on a farm in Ayrshire, Fleming worked at St Mary's Hospital, London, where in the First World War he showed that antiseptics often harmed wounds more than helped. In 1922 he discovered lysozyme, the body's own antibacterial enzyme in tears and mucus — a rehearsal for his greater find.

The discovery

Returning from holiday in September 1928, Fleming found a culture plate of staphylococci contaminated by a mould, around which the bacteria had been killed while distant colonies grew normally. He cultured the mould, named its juice 'penicillin', and published in 1929 — but could not stabilise or mass-produce it, and the work stalled until the Oxford team took it up in 1939–40. The story is often told as pure luck; Fleming's prepared observation, and his earlier lysozyme work, made him the person most likely to notice it.

Warnings and legacy

Fleming warned publicly, as early as 1945, that using too little penicillin, or using it too briefly, would select resistant bacteria — 'the thoughtless person playing with penicillin treatment is morally responsible for the death of the man who succumbs to infection with the penicillin-resistant organism'. The prediction frames today's antimicrobial-resistance crisis. St Mary's laboratory is preserved as the Fleming Museum.
