Forced induction

Forced induction is the process of delivering air (or an air–fuel mixture) to an internal combustion engine at a pressure above atmospheric, using a compressor. Increasing the mass of air in the cylinders allows more fuel to be burned, producing more power from a given engine displacement than natural aspiration. The two main types of compressor are the turbocharger, driven by exhaust gases, and the supercharger, driven mechanically by the engine.

Turbochargers

A turbocharger uses a turbine powered by exhaust flow to drive a compressor on the same shaft. Turbochargers recover energy that would otherwise be wasted, but suffer from "turbo lag" — a delay in boost while the turbine spools up — mitigated by variable-geometry turbines, twin-scroll designs and electrically assisted turbos.

Superchargers

Superchargers (Roots, twin-screw and centrifugal types) are driven from the crankshaft and provide immediate boost, but draw parasitic power from the engine. Some engines combine both systems (twincharging).

Supporting systems

Compressed intake air heats up, reducing its density; intercoolers (charge-air coolers) are commonly fitted to cool it. Higher cylinder pressures demand stronger engine internals, careful fuel metering and knock control. Forced induction is also used in aviation piston engines to maintain power at altitude, and is standard in modern downsized automotive engines and nearly all diesel engines.
