Nosocomial infection

A nosocomial infection, also known as a hospital-acquired infection (HAI) or healthcare-associated infection, is an infection that is acquired in a hospital or other healthcare facility. To be classified as nosocomial, the infection must not have been present or incubating at the time of admission; infections occurring more than 48 hours after admission are typically considered nosocomial. Such infections occur worldwide and affect both developed and resource-poor countries, adding to morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.

Types

The most common types are urinary tract infections, usually associated with indwelling catheters; surgical site infections; ventilator-associated pneumonia; and bloodstream infections associated with central venous catheters. Frequently implicated organisms include the bacteria Staphylococcus aureus (including methicillin-resistant strains, MRSA), Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Clostridioides difficile, whose toxin-mediated colitis often follows antibiotic treatment. Fungi such as Candida species and viruses transmitted by blood or respiratory routes also cause healthcare-associated infections.

Causes and risk factors

Risk is increased by invasive devices that breach the skin or mucosal barriers, immunosuppression, prolonged hospital stays, intensive care, advanced age, and broad-spectrum antibiotic use, which disrupts normal flora and selects resistant organisms. Transmission occurs most often via the contaminated hands of healthcare workers, and also through contaminated surfaces, equipment, water systems, and air.

Prevention

Prevention rests on hand hygiene — the single most effective measure — together with aseptic technique, sterilisation and disinfection, isolation precautions for infectious patients, antimicrobial stewardship, environmental cleaning, and surveillance programmes with feedback of infection rates. Organised infection prevention and control teams, following guidelines from bodies such as the World Health Organization and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, have been shown to reduce infection rates substantially.
