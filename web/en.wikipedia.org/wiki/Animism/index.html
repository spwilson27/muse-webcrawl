Animism (from Latin anima, 'soul, spirit') is the belief that objects, places and creatures possess distinct spiritual essences. The anthropologist E. B. Tylor made it the centrepiece of his theory of religion's origin in Primitive Culture (1871): from dreams and death, early humans inferred souls, then spirits everywhere. Long treated by scholars as a 'primitive' stage, animism is now studied more respectfully as a relational ontology — prominent in many Indigenous traditions and in Japan's Shinto — in which personhood extends beyond humans.

New animism

Contemporary anthropologists (Nurit Bird-David, Philippe Descola) describe animist worlds as webs of social relations with non-human persons, challenging the nature/culture split. Elements of animist sensibility appear in modern environmental ethics and in philosophical panpsychism's revival.
