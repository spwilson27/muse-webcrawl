Delayed implantation

Delayed implantation, also called embryonic diapause, is a reproductive strategy used by over 130 species of mammals, as well as some other animals, in which an embryo does not immediately implant in the uterus after fertilisation. Instead, the blastocyst remains in a state of suspended development (diapause) for a period ranging from days to many months, so that birth can be timed to coincide with favourable environmental conditions or the end of lactation.

Mechanism

During diapause the blastocyst remains free-floating in the uterus, with cell division almost completely arrested. In many species the state is maintained by continued suckling of a previous offspring, which suppresses the hormonal signals (particularly involving prolactin and progesterone) needed for implantation; weaning or a seasonal cue such as photoperiod reactivates the embryo. In marsupials such as kangaroos and wallabies, diapause is a normal part of the reproductive cycle.

Occurrence

Well-known examples include roe deer (the only ungulate known to use it), bears, seals, mustelids such as the fisher and wolverine, mink, bats, and the nine-banded armadillo. Related dormant states occur in many invertebrates, fish and reptiles.
