Phenolphthalein is a chemical compound with the formula C20H14O4, widely used as an acid–base indicator. It is colourless in acidic and neutral solutions but turns a vivid pink (fuchsia) in alkaline solutions, with the transition occurring between about pH 8.5 and 9.0. In strongly basic solutions (above roughly pH 12) the pink colour fades again as the molecule converts to a colourless form.

Phenolphthalein was first synthesised in 1871 by the German chemist Adolf von Baeyer, who combined phthalic anhydride with two equivalents of phenol under acidic conditions — hence the name, a portmanteau of "phenol" and "phthalein".

Indicator mechanism

The colour change arises from changes in the molecule's structure with pH. Under acidic conditions phenolphthalein exists mainly as a closed lactone (H2In) that absorbs no visible light strongly. As the solution becomes basic, the lactone ring opens and the molecule loses two protons, forming the pink In2− ion with an extended conjugated system that absorbs green light around 553 nm. In very concentrated alkali, addition of hydroxide gives the colourless In(OH)3− form.

Uses

Phenolphthalein is a standard indicator for titrations of weak acids with strong bases, where the equivalence point lies on the basic side. It is also used in the Kastle–Meyer test for blood in forensic science, in pH-sensitive "colour change" toys and novelty inks, and as an indicator in concrete carbonation testing. It was formerly a common ingredient in over-the-counter laxatives, but was withdrawn from that use in the United States in 1999 after studies suggested a possible carcinogenic risk; it is classified by IARC as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B).
