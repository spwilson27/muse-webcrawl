The Yazidis (also Yezidis; Kurmanji: Êzidî) are an endogamous religious group indigenous to northern Mesopotamia, mainly the Sinjar mountains and Nineveh Plain of northern Iraq. They speak Kurmanji Kurdish and follow Yazidism, a monotheistic religion rooted in ancient Iranian and Mesopotamian traditions, centred on belief in one God and the Seven Angels headed by Tawûsî Melek, the Peacock Angel.

Religion

Yazidis believe God created the world and entrusted it to seven holy beings led by Tawûsî Melek, who is venerated as God's chief angel and regent of the world; outsiders' identification of the Peacock Angel with the devil has led to centuries of persecution under the slur of "devil worship". Yazidi society is organised into hereditary castes — sheikhs, pirs, and murids — and marriage outside the faith is not recognised. Sacred hymns (qewls) preserve the tradition orally. The temple complex at Lalish, containing the tomb of Sheikh Adi ibn Musafir, the 12th-century reformer of the faith, is the holiest site and the goal of pilgrimage.

History

Yazidi tradition coalesced around the Adawiyya Sufi order of Sheikh Adi at Lalish in the 12th–13th centuries, absorbing older regional beliefs. Under Ottoman rule and the emirates of the region, Yazidis suffered repeated campaigns of forced conversion and massacre, remembered in oral tradition as 72 firmans (persecutions). In 2014 the Islamic State attacked the Sinjar homeland in a campaign the United Nations recognised as genocide, killing thousands and enslaving thousands of women and children; hundreds of thousands were displaced and a large diaspora, especially in Germany, grew rapidly.

Demographics

Before 2014 most Yazidis lived in Iraq, with longstanding communities in Armenia (where they form the largest ethnic minority), Georgia, Syria, and Turkey. Estimates of the global population range from about 700,000 to 1.5 million.
