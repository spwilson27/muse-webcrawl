The Stuart period of British history lasted from 1603 to 1714, during the reigns of the House of Stuart. The period began with the accession of James VI of Scotland as James I of England, uniting the crowns of the two kingdoms, and ended with the death of Queen Anne and the succession of the Hanoverian George I.

Course of the period

The early Stuart court of James I and Charles I saw growing conflict between Crown and Parliament over finance, religion and royal prerogative. These tensions erupted in the Civil Wars of the 1640s, the execution of Charles I in 1649, and the republican Commonwealth and Protectorate under Oliver Cromwell (1649–1660). The monarchy was restored under Charles II in 1660; the Restoration era saw the Great Plague (1665), the Great Fire of London (1666), and the beginnings of organised party politics (Whigs and Tories).

The Catholic James II was deposed in the Glorious Revolution of 1688, replaced by William III and Mary II under the settlement of the Bill of Rights 1689. Under Anne, the Acts of Union 1707 united England and Scotland into the Kingdom of Great Britain, and Britain fought the War of the Spanish Succession. The Act of Settlement 1701 ensured that, on Anne's death without surviving children, the crown passed to the Protestant House of Hanover.

Society and culture

The Stuart century spanned the King James Bible (1611), the theatre before its closure under the Commonwealth, Restoration comedy, Inigo Jones's classical architecture and Christopher Wren's rebuilding of London, the founding of the Royal Society (1660), and the scientific revolution associated with Isaac Newton—while overseas it saw the first durable English colonies in North America and the Caribbean.
