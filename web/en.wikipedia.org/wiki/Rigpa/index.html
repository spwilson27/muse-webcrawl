Rigpa

Rigpa
Tibetanརིག་པ་ (Wylie: rig pa)
Sanskrit equivalentvidyā
TraditionDzogchen (Atiyoga), within Nyingma Tibetan Buddhism
MeaningKnowing, awareness; primordial, non-dual awareness

In Dzogchen, rigpa (Tibetan: རིག་པ་, Wylie: rig pa; Sanskrit: vidyā) is knowledge of the ground (gzhi), or primordial awareness. The opposite of rigpa is marigpa (avidyā), ignorance or delusion about the nature of reality, which is the root of samsara. Rigpa is described as an innate, non-conceptual, self-knowing awareness in which subject and object are not divided, and recognising it — typically through the "pointing-out instruction" of a qualified teacher — is the heart of Dzogchen practice.

Meaning and etymology

The Tibetan word rig pa means "to know" or "awareness," translating Sanskrit vidyā ("knowledge"). Dzogchen texts distinguish rigpa from ordinary conceptual consciousness (rnam shes): rigpa is primordial, unconstructed, and self-luminous, often compared to a mirror that reflects all appearances without being altered by them, or to the open sky in which clouds (thoughts and perceptions) arise and dissolve. Commentators distinguish several aspects, such as essence rigpa (empty), nature rigpa (luminous clarity), and compassionate responsiveness, mirroring the three qualities of the ground.

In Dzogchen practice

Dzogchen, the "Great Perfection," taught chiefly in the Nyingma school and in Bön, holds that rigpa is already present in all beings and does not need to be produced; practice consists in recognising and stabilising it. The preliminary practices (khrid) lead to direct introduction, after which practices such as trekchö ("cutting through" the solidity of dualistic experience) and tögal ("direct crossing," involving visionary experiences of light) develop and integrate the recognition. A well-known formulation by Garab Dorje summarises the path: direct introduction to one's own nature, deciding on one point, and gaining confidence in liberation.

Aspects and related terms

Texts speak of the "ground of being" (gzhi) whose energy manifests as rigpa, and classify rigpa into types such as the rigpa of the ground, effulgent rigpa, and rigpa of awareness. Related notions include lhündé (spontaneous presence) and ka dag (primordial purity). The term also names Rigpa, the international network of Buddhist centres founded by Sogyal Rinpoche, author of The Tibetan Book of Living and Dying, though that organisation is distinct from the concept itself.
