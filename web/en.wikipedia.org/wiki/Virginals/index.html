Virginals

The virginals is a type of harpsichord popular in Northern Europe during the late Renaissance and early Baroque periods. Despite the name, the instrument was played by both sexes. In 16th- and 17th-century England the word "virginals" was also used generically for any plucked stringed keyboard instrument, including the harpsichord and spinet.

Description

The virginals is a small, usually rectangular (occasionally polygonal) harpsichord with a single keyboard and a single set of strings running parallel to the keyboard, plucked by jacks set close to one end. The rectangular Flemish type, called the muselar virginals, had the keyboard placed towards the centre or right and produced a flute-like tone; the spinet virginals had the keyboard at the left. Cases were often richly decorated with paintings, printed papers, Latin mottoes, and inlaid marquetry.

History

The instrument was a favourite for domestic music-making in England and the Low Countries from about 1520 to 1700. Antwerp, under the Ruckers family of makers, was the great centre of production. In England the virginals became closely associated with the music of the Elizabethan and Jacobean keyboard school: composers such as William Byrd, John Bull, Giles Farnaby, and Orlando Gibbons wrote for it, and their music survives in collections such as the Fitzwilliam Virginal Book, Parthenia, and My Ladye Nevells Booke.

Etymology

The origin of the name is uncertain. It may derive from the Latin virga, a rod or jack, or from the instrument's association with young women players; the connection with Queen Elizabeth I, the "Virgin Queen," who was herself a player, is often mentioned but not proven.
