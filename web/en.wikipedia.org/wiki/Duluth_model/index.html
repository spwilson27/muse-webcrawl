Duluth model

The Power and Control Wheel, the best-known teaching tool of the Duluth model.

The Duluth model is a community-based programme for responding to domestic violence, developed in Duluth, Minnesota, from 1980 onwards by the Domestic Abuse Intervention Project (DAIP). It coordinates police, courts, probation, shelters and batterer-intervention groups around a shared analysis of abuse, and became one of the most widely adopted frameworks for domestic-violence intervention in the United States and internationally.

Power and Control Wheel

The model's best-known tool is the Power and Control Wheel, developed by Ellen Pence and colleagues from the accounts of battered women attending DAIP groups. The wheel places physical and sexual abuse on its rim and, inside, eight recurring tactics: coercion and threats; intimidation; emotional abuse; isolation; minimizing, denying and blaming; using children; male privilege; and economic abuse. A companion Equality Wheel describes the behaviours of a non-violent relationship, including negotiation, respect, trust and shared responsibility.

Approach

Under the Duluth model, agencies adopt policies such as mandatory or preferred arrest of the predominant aggressor, swift prosecution and court-mandated attendance at psychoeducational groups for offenders. The groups, usually lasting around 27 weeks, work through the themes of the wheel to challenge beliefs that support domination and to teach non-controlling behaviour.

Criticism and evaluation

The model has been criticised for its emphasis on gender and patriarchal power, which critics argue can understate other contributors to violence such as substance abuse or mental illness, and for limited fit with female perpetrators and same-sex relationships. Research evaluations of batterer-intervention programmes generally find modest effects on reoffending, and many jurisdictions now combine Duluth-style coordination with cognitive-behavioural and risk-assessment approaches.
