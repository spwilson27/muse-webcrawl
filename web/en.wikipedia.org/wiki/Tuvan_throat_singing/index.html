Tuvan throat singing

Tuvan throat singing, or khöömei (Tuvan: хөөмей), is a style of overtone singing practised by the Tuvan people of southern Siberia, in which a single vocalist produces two or more distinct pitches simultaneously — a low drone fundamental and a flute-like melody formed from its harmonics. Related traditions exist across Mongolia, Inner Mongolia, and the Altai region.

Styles

The principal styles are khöömei proper (a soft, wind-like tone), sygyt (a clear, piercing whistle-like melody), and kargyraa (a deep, growling style engaging the ventricular folds, in the "undertone" register). Singers traditionally imitate sounds of the natural world — wind, water, birds, and animals — reflecting an animist worldview in which sounds are imbued with spirits.

History and performers

Throat singing was historically a solitary pastoral art, performed by herders on the steppe. In the Soviet era it was professionalised by ensembles in Kyzyl, the Tuvan capital. Since the 1990s groups such as Huun-Huur-Tu, Yat-Kha, and Alash have brought it to international audiences, collaborating with Western musicians; the virtuoso Kongar-ool Ondar became its best-known ambassador before his death in 2013. In 2009, Mongolian and Chinese throat singing (khoomei) was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
