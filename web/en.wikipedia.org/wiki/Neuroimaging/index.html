Neuroimaging is the use of quantitative techniques to image the structure and function of the central nervous system, developed as an objective way of scientifically studying the healthy human brain in a non-invasive manner. Increasingly it is also used for quantitative research on brain disease and psychiatric illness. Neuroimaging is a highly multidisciplinary research field, drawing on neuroscience, psychology, medicine, physics, mathematics and computer science.

History

The first neuroimaging technique available to clinicians was the X-ray, followed in the 1920s by cerebral angiography and pneumoencephalography, both invasive and uncomfortable. The invention of computed tomography (CT) by Godfrey Hounsfield in the early 1970s revolutionised clinical practice by producing cross-sectional images of the brain. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) followed in the late 1970s and 1980s, providing far richer soft-tissue contrast without ionising radiation. Functional techniques — positron emission tomography (PET), single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and, from the early 1990s, functional MRI (fMRI) based on the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal — allowed researchers to map brain activity in living subjects.

Structural imaging

Structural imaging deals with the anatomy of the brain and is used to diagnose gross intracranial disease such as tumours, haemorrhage, stroke and traumatic injury. CT is fast and widely available; MRI gives superior detail and is the modality of choice for most elective investigations. Quantitative morphometry — measuring the volume and shape of brain structures — is used in research on neurodevelopment and neurodegeneration.

Functional imaging

Functional imaging measures aspects of brain activity, usually indirectly through changes in local blood flow, metabolism or oxygenation. fMRI is the workhorse of cognitive neuroscience because it is non-invasive and widely available, though its temporal resolution (seconds) is far slower than that of electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG), which record electrical and magnetic signals directly with millisecond resolution. PET uses radioactive tracers to image blood flow, glucose metabolism and the distribution of specific receptors and proteins, including the amyloid and tau deposits of Alzheimer's disease.
