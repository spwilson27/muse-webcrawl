Code of Hammurabi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Code of Hammurabi is a Babylonian legal text composed during 1755–1750 BC, in the reign of Hammurabi, the sixth king of the First Babylonian Dynasty. It is the longest, best-organised and best-preserved legal code from the ancient Near East, written in the Old Babylonian dialect of Akkadian purportedly by Hammurabi himself. The primary copy is inscribed on a basalt stele about 2.25 metres tall, topped by a relief of Hammurabi receiving the laws from the sun god Shamash; it was excavated at Susa in 1901 and is now in the Louvre, Paris.

Contents

The code's roughly 282 provisions (in the standard enumeration) cover family law, property, trade, agriculture, slavery and professional liability, graded strikingly by social class (free men, dependents and slaves received different penalties for the same act). It is famous for the principle of lex talionis — "an eye for an eye" — applied principally between social equals, alongside fines and, for some offences, death or mutilation. Provisions also place the burden of proof on accusers in capital cases and punish judges who alter decisions — suggesting concepts of proof and judicial accountability.

Significance

The code was not the first of its kind (the codes of Ur-Nammu and Lipit-Ishtar are older) nor a "constitution"; scholars debate whether it functioned as legislation, royal self-presentation, or a scribal treatise. Its recovery revolutionised study of ancient law and its possible parallels with biblical law, and it survives in popular culture as the emblem of written law's antiquity — an image of Hammurabi appears above one doorway of the US Supreme Court building among history's lawgivers.
