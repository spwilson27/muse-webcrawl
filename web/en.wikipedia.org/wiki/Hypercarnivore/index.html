Hypercarnivore

Hypercarnivore
DefinitionAnimal with a diet of more than 70% meat
ExamplesFelids, polar bear, orca, many raptors
ContrastsMesocarnivore (30–70%), hypocarnivore (<30%)

Felids such as the lion are obligate hypercarnivores.

A hypercarnivore is an animal that has a diet composed of more than 70% meat. The term, used in palaeobiology and ecology, distinguishes extreme meat specialists from mesocarnivores (30–70% meat, supplemented with fruit, fungi or other plant material) and hypocarnivores (less than 30% meat). Hypercarnivores are typically apex predators with anatomical adaptations for capturing, killing and processing animal prey.

Characteristics

Mammalian hypercarnivores tend to have reduced or lost grinding molars, prominent shearing carnassial teeth, strong jaws and, in predators of large prey, robust limbs and skulls. Because they depend almost entirely on prey, they are vulnerable to declines in prey populations. Well-known examples include all felids (cats are obligate carnivores that cannot synthesise certain nutrients, such as taurine, from plant matter), the polar bear among bears, wolves and other canids to a lesser degree, orcas, crocodilians, many raptors, and snakes.

In the fossil record

Hypercarnivory has evolved repeatedly — in extinct groups such as the sabre-toothed cats, creodonts, borhyaenids and the giant short-faced bear — and palaeontologists have noted a pattern in which hypercarnivorous lineages specialise over time and become disproportionately prone to extinction, a tendency sometimes discussed in connection with Van Valkenburgh's work on predator guilds. Large hypercarnivores such as Smilodon and the dire wolf disappeared in the late Pleistocene extinctions.
