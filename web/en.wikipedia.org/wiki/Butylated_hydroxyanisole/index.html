Butylated hydroxyanisole

IUPAC name2-tert-butyl-4-methoxyphenol and 3-tert-butyl-4-methoxyphenol
FormulaC11H16O2
E numberE320
AppearanceWaxy solid

Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA) is an antioxidant consisting of a mixture of two isomeric organic compounds, 2-tert-butyl-4-hydroxyanisole and 3-tert-butyl-4-hydroxyanisole. It is prepared from 4-methoxyphenol and isobutylene. It is sold as a waxy solid used as a food additive with the E number E320. The primary use for BHA is as an antioxidant and preservative in food, food packaging, animal feed, cosmetics, rubber, and petroleum products.

Uses

Since the mid-20th century BHA has been added to fats and fat-containing foods to retard rancidity caused by oxidation — it acts as a free-radical scavenger, breaking the chain reactions by which oxygen attacks lipids. It is commonly found in butter, cereals, baked goods, snack foods, chewing gum, dehydrated potatoes, and beer, and is frequently used together with the related antioxidant butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT). Typical permitted levels in food are around 0.02% of the fat or oil content.

Health concerns

BHA has long been the subject of safety debate. The U.S. National Toxicology Program lists it as "reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen" based on studies in which high doses fed to rats and hamsters produced tumours of the forestomach — an organ humans do not possess. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies BHA in Group 2B, "possibly carcinogenic to humans." At the same time, regulatory bodies including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration continue to permit its use at low levels, considering it generally recognised as safe at the doses used in food, and some research has suggested antioxidant or even anti-carcinogenic effects at low doses. California lists BHA under Proposition 65. Because of consumer concern, many manufacturers have reformulated products to reduce or remove BHA.

Chemistry

Commercial BHA is a mixture of the 3-tert-butyl (about 85%) and 2-tert-butyl (about 15%) isomers of 4-hydroxyanisole. The phenolic hydroxyl group donates a hydrogen atom to peroxyl radicals, terminating oxidation chains, while the bulky tert-butyl group shields the resulting phenoxy radical. BHA is soluble in fats and slightly soluble in water, and it survives baking temperatures better than BHT, though it can impart a faint phenolic odour at high concentrations.
