Event horizon

In astrophysics, an event horizon is a boundary in spacetime beyond which events cannot affect an outside observer. Most famously, it is the boundary around a black hole from within which light cannot escape: once a particle crosses the event horizon, it can never return, because all future paths lead toward the central singularity.

Black hole event horizons

For a non-rotating, uncharged black hole (the Schwarzschild solution), the event horizon is a sphere at the Schwarzschild radius, rs = 2GM/c² — about 3 km for a solar-mass object, and roughly 12 million km (about 0.08 AU) for Sagittarius A*, the four-million-solar-mass black hole at the Milky Way's centre, imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2022. Rotating (Kerr) black holes have more complex horizons, together with an outer ergosphere from which energy can still be extracted.

Other horizons

Analogous horizons arise elsewhere in general relativity: a uniformly accelerating observer has a Rindler horizon; an expanding universe has a cosmological event horizon (the greatest distance from which light emitted now will ever reach us); and de Sitter space possesses an observer-dependent horizon with an associated temperature. The study of event horizons led to black hole thermodynamics — the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy, proportional to horizon area — and to Hawking radiation, the slow quantum emission by which black holes are predicted to evaporate.
