Lojong

A Tibetan thangka, of the type used in contemplative practice

Lojong (Tibetan: བློ་སྦྱོང་, Wylie: blo sbyong, literally "mind training") is a contemplative practice in the Tibetan Buddhist tradition. It consists of a set of aphorisms or slogans, formulated in Tibet in the 12th century by Geshe Chekawa Yeshe Dorje, summarising the Mahayana Buddhist path to awakening through cultivating bodhicitta (the mind of enlightenment).

History

The lojong teachings are attributed in origin to the Indian master Atiśa (982–1054), who brought them to Tibet. They were transmitted in secrecy for several generations before being written down. Chekawa's root text, the Seven-Point Mind Training, organises the practice into seven points comprising fifty-nine slogans. The tradition was later brought into all schools of Tibetan Buddhism; the 19th-century master Jamgon Kongtrul included lojong texts in his Treasury of Key Instructions, helping to keep the practice alive.

The seven points

The seven points cover: the preliminaries; training in bodhicitta (both ultimate and relative, the latter including the practice of tonglen, "giving and taking"); transforming adversity into the path of awakening; integrating the practice into one's whole life; the criteria of proficiency in mind training; the commitments of mind training; and the precepts of mind training.

Famous slogans include "Drive all blames into one", "Be grateful to everyone", and "Don't ponder others' weaknesses". The slogans are intended to be memorised and applied moment by moment as antidotes to self-centred habits of thought.

In the West

Lojong has been widely taught in the West through commentaries by teachers such as Chögyam Trungpa, Pema Chödrön and others, and the slogans have been published as card decks and in numerous popular books on mindfulness and compassion practice.
