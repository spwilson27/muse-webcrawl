The Aqueduct of Segovia (Spanish: Acueducto de Segovia) is a Roman aqueduct in the Spanish city of Segovia, and one of the best-preserved elevated Roman aqueducts in the world. It carried water some 15 kilometres (9.3 mi) from the Fuente Fría river in the mountains to the city, and its towering two-tiered arcade of unmortared granite arches across the Plaza del Azoguejo has become the emblem of Segovia. It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1985 as part of the "Old Town of Segovia and its Aqueduct".

History

The aqueduct's exact construction date was long debated because the bronze inscriptions that once recorded it are lost; scholarly estimates have generally placed it in the late first or early second century AD, around the reigns of Domitian, Nerva, or Trajan, with recent research favouring a date in the early second century (c. AD 112–117). Local legend instead attributes its construction to the Devil, who in one night built the bridge for a young water-carrier in exchange for her soul, but missed finishing before dawn.

The aqueduct remained in use for centuries, supplying Segovia's fountains, baths, and later the Alcázar. Sections damaged in the eleventh century during the Muslim period were restored under the Catholic Monarchs in the late fifteenth century, when 36 arches were rebuilt under the direction of the prior of the Jeronymite monastery of El Parral. The water channel continued in municipal use, remarkably, into the modern era — parts of the system supplied water into the twentieth century — before pollution and deterioration led to its decommissioning and conservation campaigns in the late twentieth century.

Architecture

The channel runs about 15 km from the mountains, mostly underground or at ground level, before entering the city on the famous elevated section. The surviving arcade is about 813 metres long and rises to 28.5 metres at its highest point, built of roughly 20,400 granite blocks fitted together without mortar, using the stone's balance and friction alone. It consists of about 167 arches: a lower tier of single arches and, above much of its length, a second tier, with the water channel (specus) on top. A niche in the structure holds an image of the Virgin (traditionally Our Lady of Fuencisla), replacing a statue of Hercules said to have stood there in antiquity. The aqueduct could deliver an estimated flow of 20–40 litres per second to distribution tanks in the city.
