Deimos is the smaller and outer of the two natural satellites of Mars, the other being Phobos. With a mean radius of 6.2 km, it is one of the smallest known moons in the Solar System. Deimos was discovered by American astronomer Asaph Hall on 12 August 1877 at the United States Naval Observatory in Washington, D.C., a few days before his discovery of Phobos. It is named after Deimos, the Ancient Greek god and personification of terror and dread, who with his twin brother Phobos accompanied their father Ares into battle in Greek mythology.

Orbital characteristics

Deimos orbits Mars at a distance of about 23,460 km with a period of 30.3 hours, slightly longer than the Martian day (24.7 hours), so from the surface of Mars it rises in the east and takes several days to cross the sky. Its orbit is nearly circular and lies close to the planet's equatorial plane. Deimos is slowly receding from Mars under tidal forces, the opposite behaviour to Phobos, which is spiralling inward.

Physical characteristics

Deimos is a lumpy, irregular body measuring roughly 15 × 12.2 × 11 km. Like Phobos, it is composed of carbon-rich rock and ice and is covered in a thick layer of regolith. Its surface is notably smoother than that of Phobos because loose dust has partially filled many of its craters. The two named craters on Deimos are Swift and Voltaire, named after the writers Jonathan Swift and Voltaire, both of whom speculated about Martian moons before their discovery. The surface gravity is extremely low (about 0.003 m/s²), and the escape velocity is only about 20 km/h, so an object thrown from the surface could escape into space.

Origin

The origin of Deimos and Phobos is debated. Their composition and appearance resemble those of C-type (carbonaceous) asteroids, suggesting they may be captured asteroids; however, their near-circular, equatorial orbits are difficult to explain by capture. Alternative hypotheses propose that the moons accreted from debris produced by a giant impact on Mars.

Exploration

Deimos has been photographed by several Mars orbiters, including Viking, Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Express, and the UAE's Hope orbiter, which made close flybys in 2023 and obtained the most detailed views yet of its far side.
