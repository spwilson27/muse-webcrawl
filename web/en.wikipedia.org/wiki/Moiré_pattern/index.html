Moiré pattern


Moiré pattern formed by two concentric circle grids.

A moiré pattern (UK: /ˈmwɑːreɪ/, US: /mwɑːˈreɪ/) is a large-scale interference pattern that can be produced when an opaque ruled pattern with transparent gaps is overlaid on another similar pattern. For the moiré pattern to appear, the two patterns must not be completely identical, but rather displaced, rotated, or have slightly different pitch.

Moiré patterns appear in many situations. In printing, the printed pattern of dots can interfere with the image. In television and digital photography, a pattern on an object being photographed can interfere with the shape of the light sensors to generate unwanted artefacts. They are also sometimes created deliberately — in micrometers they are used to amplify the effects of very small movements.

Etymology

The term originates from moire (French: [mwaʁe]), a type of textile, traditionally of silk but now also of cotton or synthetic fibre, with a rippled or "watered" appearance. The word is derived from the French verb moirer, "to produce a watered appearance" (the textile known as watered silk).

Implications

Moiré patterns can be a problem in scanned images, printed halftones, and photographs of screens, where the sampling grid of the sensor interferes with fine periodic detail in the subject. Techniques to suppress them include shooting at different angles, applying blur filters, and using anti-aliasing in digital signal processing — a phenomenon related to aliasing and the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem.
