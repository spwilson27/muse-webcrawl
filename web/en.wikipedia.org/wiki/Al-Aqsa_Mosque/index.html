Al-Aqsa Mosque (al-Masjid al-Aqsa, 'the Farthest Mosque') is the congregational mosque on the southern side of the Temple Mount in Jerusalem, Islam's third-holiest site after Mecca and Medina. The Quran's account of Muhammad's Night Journey (Isra) names al-Masjid al-Aqsa as its destination; tradition adds his leading of the prophets in prayer there and ascension (Mi'raj) from the Rock beneath the neighbouring Dome of the Rock.

History

A first prayer house rose soon after the Muslim conquest (630s–690s); the building was repeatedly felled by earthquakes and rebuilt — by Umayyads, Abbasids, Fatimids, Ayyubids and Crusaders (who made it a Templar headquarters, 'Templum Solomonis'). An arson attack by a disturbed Australian tourist in 1969 damaged its ancient minbar — an outrage that helped spur the founding of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. The mosque gives its name to the 2000 uprising (Second Intifada, 'al-Aqsa Intifada') and remains a flashpoint.
