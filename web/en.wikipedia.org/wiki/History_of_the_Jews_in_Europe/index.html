History of the Jews in Europe

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the Jews in Europe spans more than two thousand years, from Jewish communities in the Roman Empire through medieval Ashkenazi and Sephardi cultures, expulsions and emancipation, the catastrophe of the Holocaust, and the smaller but reviving communities of the present day.

Antiquity and the Middle Ages

Jewish communities existed in Roman Europe, notably in Rome, Hispania, and Gaul. In the medieval period, Jews faced alternating toleration and persecution: they were invited to settle as merchants and financiers yet subjected to restrictions, blood libels, and violence, including massacres during the Crusades (1096) and the Black Death (1348–1351). Major expulsions included England (1290), France (1394), and Spain (1492), the latter driving Sephardi Jews to the Ottoman Empire, Italy, and the Netherlands. Ashkenazi Jewry flourished in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, which became the demographic center of world Jewry.

Emancipation and modernity

The Enlightenment and the French Revolution began Jewish emancipation: France granted citizenship in 1791, and other states followed unevenly through the 19th century. Jews entered professions, universities, and cultural life; movements such as Reform Judaism, the Haskalah (Jewish Enlightenment), and later Zionism reshaped Jewish identity. At the same time, modern racial antisemitism grew, exemplified by the Dreyfus affair in France and pogroms in the Russian Empire that drove mass emigration to the Americas.

The Holocaust and after

On the eve of World War II, Europe's Jewish population numbered about 9.5 million. Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered approximately six million Jews in the Holocaust, destroying centuries-old communities. After 1948 many survivors emigrated to Israel or the United States. Communist regimes in Eastern Europe suppressed Jewish religious life, though some communities revived after 1989. Today Europe's largest Jewish community is in France, followed by the United Kingdom and Germany, where immigration from the former Soviet Union revitalized communal life.
