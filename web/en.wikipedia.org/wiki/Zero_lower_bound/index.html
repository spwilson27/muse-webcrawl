Zero lower bound

Zero lower bound
FieldMonetary economics
ConstraintNominal interest rates cannot fall far below zero because cash yields zero
Key episodesJapan (1990s–), United States and Europe (2008–2022)
Related conceptsLiquidity trap, quantitative easing, negative interest rates

The zero lower bound (ZLB) is the idea that nominal interest rates cannot be reduced below zero — or not far below — because economic agents can always hold cash, which pays a nominal return of exactly zero. When a central bank's policy rate reaches the bound during a recession or deflation, conventional monetary policy loses its power to stimulate demand further, creating the conditions John Maynard Keynes described as a liquidity trap.

Why the bound exists

If deposit and bond rates were deeply negative, savers would withdraw funds and hoard banknotes, whose storage is inconvenient but not prohibitively costly. The costs and risks of storing cash mean the effective lower bound lies somewhat below zero — experiments after 2008 showed rates of about −0.5 to −0.75 per cent were workable — but the constraint is real. The bound matters most when inflation is low: the real interest rate equals the nominal rate minus inflation, so at zero nominal rates with falling prices, real rates actually rise, tightening policy when the economy needs the opposite.

Episodes

Japan first reached the bound in the late 1990s after its asset bubble burst, and the Bank of Japan pioneered quantitative easing in 2001. After the 2008 financial crisis, the US Federal Reserve held its funds rate at 0–0.25 per cent from December 2008 to 2015; the European Central Bank, Swiss National Bank, Sveriges Riksbank and the Bank of Japan later pushed rates modestly negative, combined with large-scale asset purchases.

Policy responses

Economists have proposed many escapes from the bound: quantitative easing to compress long-term rates and risk premia; forward guidance committing to keep rates low, thereby promising higher future inflation; negative-rate policies with tiering to protect banks; raising the inflation target; fiscal expansion; and more radical options such as abolishing large-denomination cash or moving to electronic money that can depreciate against paper currency. The ZLB literature, revived by Paul Krugman's analysis of Japan and formalised by Eggertsson and Woodford, reshaped mainstream macroeconomics after 2008.
