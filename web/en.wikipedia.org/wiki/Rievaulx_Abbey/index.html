Rievaulx Abbey (pronounced "REE-voh") was a Cistercian abbey in Rievaulx, near Helmsley, in North Yorkshire, England. Founded in 1132 by Walter Espec, it was the first Cistercian house established in the north of England and became one of the great abbeys of medieval England, renowned for its wealth from wool production and for the spiritual writings of its third abbot, Saint Aelred. Its extensive ruins, dominated by the soaring Early English choir, stand in the wooded valley of the River Rye within the North York Moors National Park.

History

The abbey was founded as a mission from Clairvaux Abbey in France, at the direction of Bernard of Clairvaux, on land granted by Walter Espec, lord of Helmsley. Its first abbot, William, was a former secretary of Bernard. Under Aelred (abbot 1147–1167), author of On Spiritual Friendship, the community grew to some 140 monks and 500 lay brothers, and Rievaulx founded several daughter houses in England and Scotland. The abbey's economy rested on sheep farming and the export of wool, supplemented by ironworking and lead mining in the surrounding moors; the monks engineered a canalised diversion of the River Rye past the site.

The stone church was rebuilt in an ambitious Gothic style in the early 13th century, its choir extended with an eastern transept arrangement unusual in English Cistercian architecture. By the late Middle Ages the community had shrunk considerably, and in 1538 the abbey was surrendered to the Crown during Henry VIII's Dissolution of the Monasteries, with an annual value recorded at about £278. The site passed to the Earl of Rutland and then to the Duncombe family, who in the 1750s created Rievaulx Terrace, a landscaped grass terrace above the valley, as a viewpoint over the romantic ruins.

The ruins

The remains include the church's nave walls, the twin transepts, and the exceptionally tall choir with its lancet windows; much of the cloister ranges survive, including the chapter house (with its shrine base of Abbot William), the refectory, and the abbot's lodging. The site, a Scheduled Monument and Grade I listed, is cared for by English Heritage and includes a visitor centre and museum.
