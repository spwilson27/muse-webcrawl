The Deutsche Mark (German: Deutsche Mark, abbreviated DM) was the official currency of West Germany from 1948 until 1990, and of the unified Federal Republic of Germany from 1990 until the adoption of the euro in 1999 (as book money) and 2002 (as cash). It was one of the world's most stable and trusted currencies, a symbol of West Germany's post-war economic miracle (the Wirtschaftswunder).

Deutsche Mark notes and coins, including the widely hoarded 1-Pfennig and 1-Mark pieces.

History

The Mark was introduced in the Western occupation zones on 21 June 1948 as part of the currency reform, replacing the worthless Reichsmark at a rate that wiped out most monetary savings but relaunched the economy overnight, ending rationing-era shortages when coupled with Ludwig Erhard's price liberalisation. The independent Deutsche Bundesbank (from 1957) pursued a famously strict anti-inflation policy, making the DM a major reserve currency, second only to the U.S. dollar. At German reunification in 1990, the DM replaced the East German mark.

Replacement by the euro

Germany gave up the Mark for the euro — a politically momentous step agreed in the Maastricht Treaty — at the irrevocable conversion rate of €1 = DM 1.95583. Euro notes and coins entered circulation on 1 January 2002, and the Mark ceased to be legal tender shortly after. Uniquely, the Bundesbank still exchanges DM notes and coins for euros indefinitely, and billions of Marks remain unredeemed as souvenirs or savings.
