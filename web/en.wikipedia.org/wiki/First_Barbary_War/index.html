First Barbary War

The First Barbary War (1801–1805), also known as the Tripolitan War, was the first of two Barbary Wars, in which the United States and Sweden fought against the four North African states known collectively as the "Barbary States". The cause of the war was pirates from the Barbary States seizing American merchant ships and holding the crews for ransom, demanding the US pay tribute to the Barbary rulers. President Thomas Jefferson refused to pay this tribute. Sweden had been at war with the Tripolitans since 1800.


The burning of USS Philadelphia in Tripoli harbour, 16 February 1804.

Background

After independence, American merchant shipping lost the protection of the Royal Navy and became prey to Barbary corsairs from Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Morocco. The United States, like European nations, had paid tribute for safe passage, spending a substantial share of its early federal budget on such payments. When Yusuf Karamanli, the Pasha of Tripoli, demanded an increased tribute in 1801 and, on its refusal, declared war by cutting down the flagstaff of the US consulate, Jefferson dispatched a naval squadron to the Mediterranean.

Course of the war

American squadrons under Commodores Richard Dale, Edward Preble, and Samuel Barron blockaded Tripoli and fought a series of actions. In October 1803 the frigate USS Philadelphia ran aground off Tripoli and was captured with her crew of over 300 men. On the night of 16 February 1804, Lieutenant Stephen Decatur led a daring raid into Tripoli harbour aboard the captured ketch Intrepid and burned the Philadelphia to deny her to the enemy — an exploit Admiral Nelson reportedly called "the most bold and daring act of the age".

In 1805 the American agent William Eaton, with a small force including Marine Lieutenant Presley O'Bannon, marched from Egypt across the desert, captured the port of Derna with local allies, and threatened Tripoli itself. Combined with the naval blockade, this pressured the Pasha to sign a peace treaty on 10 June 1805, ending the war: the US paid a ransom of $60,000 for the Philadelphia crew but no further tribute to Tripoli. The campaign is commemorated in the US Marine Corps hymn line "to the shores of Tripoli".

Aftermath

The war established the US Navy's early reputation and demonstrated that the United States would resist tribute demands, though payments to other Barbary states continued until the Second Barbary War of 1815 settled the matter definitively.
