Egg allergy

Egg allergy is an immune hypersensitivity to the proteins found in chicken eggs, and possibly goose, duck, or turkey eggs. Symptoms can be either rapid or gradual in onset. The latter can take hours to days to appear, while the former may include anaphylaxis, a potentially life-threatening condition that requires treatment with epinephrine. Other symptoms may include atopic dermatitis (eczema), inflammation of the esophagus (eosinophilic esophagitis), hives, and gastrointestinal upset.

Egg is one of the most common food allergens in young children.

Cause

Egg allergy is caused by an aberrant immune response to egg proteins, mediated in most cases by IgE antibodies. The major allergens are in the egg white: ovomucoid (Gal d 1), which is heat-stable and a marker for persistent allergy and reactivity to baked egg; ovalbumin (Gal d 2), the most abundant egg-white protein; ovotransferrin (Gal d 3); and lysozyme (Gal d 4). The main yolk allergen is alpha-livetin (Gal d 5), which is also implicated in bird–egg syndrome.

Prevalence and natural history

In the United States, egg allergy is the second most common food allergy in children after cow's milk. Prevalence estimates in young children range from about 0.5% to 2.5%. The majority of children outgrow egg allergy: older studies suggested about half by age 3–5, while more recent referral-population studies find slower resolution, with roughly two-thirds tolerant by adolescence. Many egg-allergic children tolerate extensively heated (baked) egg in foods such as muffins, and regular ingestion of baked egg may hasten tolerance.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis combines a clinical history with skin prick testing or serum egg-specific IgE; the oral food challenge is the gold standard. Management centers on avoiding egg, reading labels (egg must be declared on packaged foods in the US under FALCPA/FALCPA successor laws and in the EU under food information regulations), and carrying epinephrine for those at risk of anaphylaxis. Oral immunotherapy protocols using egg powder can raise the threshold dose in some patients. Egg allergy has implications for vaccines: influenza and yellow fever vaccines are produced in eggs, though current guidance holds that egg-allergic individuals can receive influenza vaccine with standard precautions.
