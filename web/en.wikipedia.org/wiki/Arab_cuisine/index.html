Arab cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Arab cuisine (Arabic: المطبخ العربي) is the cuisine of the Arab peoples of Western Asia and North Africa, a rich and varied culinary tradition shaped by the region's history of trade, empire, and agriculture from the Arabian Peninsula through the Levant and Egypt to the Maghreb. While there are strong regional differences, Arab cuisines share characteristic ingredients — wheat, rice, lamb, dates, chickpeas, olive oil, sesame, and warm spices such as cumin, cardamom, cinnamon, sumac, and za'atar — and characteristic forms, including the communal mezze of small dishes, flatbreads served at every meal, and elaborate hospitality rituals centred on coffee and tea.

History

The foundations of Arab cooking reach back to the pre-Islamic Arabian Peninsula, where dates, camel and sheep products, and flatbreads predominated, and to the ancient agricultural civilisations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, and the Levant, which contributed wheat, legumes, and olive cultivation. The early Islamic conquests and the Abbasid caliphate (8th–13th centuries) created a cosmopolitan court cuisine in Baghdad documented in medieval cookery books such as Ibn Sayyar al-Warraq's tenth-century Kitab al-Tabikh and the later Kitab al-Wusla ila l-Habib; ingredients and techniques flowed along trade routes bringing rice, sugar, citrus, aubergines, and spinach westward. The Ottoman Empire (16th–20th centuries) spread dishes such as kebabs, stuffed vegetables, baklava, and Turkish coffee across the Arab world, and the Columbian exchange later added tomatoes, potatoes, and peppers, now indispensable in dishes like shakshuka.

Regional varieties

Levantine cuisine (Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Palestine) is famed for mezze — hummus, muhammara, tabbouleh, fattoush, baba ghanoush — as well as kibbeh, falafel, and shawarma. Egyptian cuisine centres on bread, fava beans (ful medames), koshari (a mixed rice, lentil, and pasta dish), and molokhia. Maghrebi cuisine (Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya) features couscous, tagines, harissa chilli paste, and brik pastry. Gulf and Arabian Peninsula cuisine emphasises rice dishes such as kabsa and mandi, camel and lamb, dates, and spiced coffee (qahwa) served with elaborate ceremony. Iraqi cuisine is known for masgouf (grilled carp), dolma, and some of the oldest recorded recipes in the world; Yemeni cuisine for saltah, mandi, and honey-sweetened dishes.

Meals and customs

Bread — pita and other flatbreads such as markook, tannour, and msemen — accompanies virtually every meal and often serves as a utensil. Rice dishes and stews form main courses, and meals traditionally end with strong coffee or mint tea and sweets such as baklava, kunafa, ma'amoul, and qatayef, the latter especially associated with Ramadan. Islamic dietary law structures food practice: pork and alcohol are prohibited (halal rules govern slaughter), and during Ramadan the fast is broken at sunset with iftar, often beginning with dates and soup. Coffee culture — from the Arabic qahwa that gave European languages the word "coffee" — remains a central social ritual across the Arab world.
