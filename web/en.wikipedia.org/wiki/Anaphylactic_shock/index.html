Overview

Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially life-threatening systemic allergic reaction that occurs rapidly after exposure to a trigger. It involves the immune system — usually IgE-mediated release of histamine and other mediators from mast cells and basophils — causing some combination of skin symptoms (hives, flushing, angioedema), airway swelling and bronchospasm, gastrointestinal symptoms and cardiovascular collapse (anaphylactic shock).

Triggers and management

Common triggers are foods (peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, milk, egg), insect stings, medications (antibiotics, anaesthetic agents) and latex; exercise and idiopathic cases also occur. The only first-line treatment is adrenaline (epinephrine) given intramuscularly into the thigh, repeated as needed — antihistamines do not treat the dangerous features. People at risk are prescribed adrenaline auto-injectors (such as EpiPen) and taught action plans; biphasic reactions (a second wave hours later) justify observation after severe episodes.
