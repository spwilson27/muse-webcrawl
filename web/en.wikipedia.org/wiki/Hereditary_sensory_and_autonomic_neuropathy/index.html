Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy

Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathy
SpecialtyNeurology, medical genetics
SymptomsLoss of pain and temperature sensation, painless injuries and ulcers, autonomic dysfunction
CausesGenetic mutations affecting sensory and autonomic neurons
TypesHSAN I–VIII (classification varies)

Hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN) are a group of rare inherited disorders of the peripheral nervous system characterised by degeneration of sensory and autonomic neurons. The hallmark is loss of sensation — particularly of pain and temperature — which leads to painless injuries, chronic skin ulcers, bone infections and, in severe cases, self-mutilation and loss of digits. Autonomic involvement may cause abnormal sweating, unstable blood pressure, gastrointestinal problems and, in some types, absence of tears and of the normal pain response from infancy.

Classification

HSANs are classified into several types by age of onset, inheritance pattern and gene. HSAN type I is the commonest adult form, usually autosomal dominant, caused in many families by mutations in SPTLC1 or SPTLC2, genes of sphingolipid synthesis that produce toxic deoxysphingolipids damaging sensory neurons. Type II is recessive and begins in childhood. Type III is familial dysautonomia (Riley–Day syndrome), occurring mainly in people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry and caused by mutations in ELP1 (IKBKAP). Types IV and V involve congenital insensitivity to pain: type IV (with anhidrosis, inability to sweat) is caused by mutations in NTRK1, the receptor for nerve growth factor, while type V is associated with mutations in NGF.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms typically begin in the feet: loss of pain and temperature perception, painless burns and wounds, foot ulcers that heal poorly, and Charcot (neuropathic) joints. Motor weakness is absent or mild in early disease in several types. In the congenital forms, infants may bite their tongues and fingers once teeth erupt, suffer repeated fractures unnoticed, and — in type IV — develop dangerous fevers because they cannot sweat.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis rests on clinical examination, nerve conduction studies (often normal for motor fibres with absent sensory responses), skin biopsy showing reduced intraepidermal nerve fibre density, and genetic testing. There is no cure; management is protective: daily inspection of the feet, protective footwear, prompt treatment of injuries and infections, and education to compensate for the absent warning function of pain.
