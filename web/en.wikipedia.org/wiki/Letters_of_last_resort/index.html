Letters of last resort

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The letters of last resort are four handwritten letters, one kept aboard each of the Royal Navy's ballistic missile submarines, written by the Prime Minister of the United Kingdom. They contain orders on what action to take in the event that the British government has been destroyed by a nuclear strike and both the prime minister and their designated alternate ("second person") have been killed or incapacitated. The letters are considered among the most secret documents in the British state, and their contents have never been revealed.

Procedure

Within days of taking office, each new prime minister writes the letters by hand and they are sealed in envelopes, placed in nested safes aboard the four submarines of the nuclear deterrent fleet (the Vanguard class, to be succeeded by the Dreadnought class). When a prime minister leaves office, the letters are destroyed unopened and replaced by the successor's. The existence of the letters was publicly confirmed in a 2008 BBC Radio 4 programme, The Human Button.

Before opening the letter, a submarine commander must establish by various checks that the government has ceased to function — traditionally including whether BBC Radio 4 has stopped broadcasting. Only if the commander is satisfied that the chain of command has been destroyed is the safe opened.

Options

According to reporting and official hints, the prime minister chooses among a limited set of broad options, believed to include: retaliate with nuclear weapons; do not retaliate; use the commander's own judgement; or place the submarine under allied command (for example that of the United States or Australia, if either still exists). The letters embody the paradox of nuclear deterrence: they must be real enough to make a retaliatory threat credible, yet they are intended never to be opened.

Cultural references

The letters of last resort have inspired fiction and drama, including David Greig's play and radio drama treatments, and they feature in discussion of the ethics of nuclear weapons and the "human button" problem — the chain of individual judgements connecting political decision to nuclear launch.
