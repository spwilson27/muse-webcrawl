Flash smelting

TypePyrometallurgical process
Developed1940s, Outokumpu, Finland
ProductsCopper matte, nickel matte
FeedDried sulphide concentrates

Flash smelting (Finnish: liekkisulatus) is a smelting process for sulphur-containing ores, principally chalcopyrite, developed by the Finnish company Outokumpu in the 1940s and first operated commercially at Harjavalta in 1949. It is one of the two dominant modern processes for extracting copper from sulphide concentrates, the other being bath smelting such as the Isasmelt/Ausmelt process.

Process

Dried, finely ground sulphide concentrate is injected with oxygen-enriched air (or pure oxygen) through a burner at the top of a reaction shaft. The sulphide particles ignite and burn in suspension — "in a flash" — oxidising iron and sulphur and releasing enough heat to make the process nearly autothermal. Molten droplets collect in a settler hearth at the bottom, separating into a copper-rich matte layer and an iron-silicate slag. Off-gases, rich in sulphur dioxide, are cooled and sent to a sulphuric acid plant, greatly reducing emissions compared with older reverberatory furnaces.

Advantages

Flash smelting uses the fuel value of the sulphides themselves rather than external fuel, achieves high throughput in a compact furnace, produces a strong, steady stream of SO2 suitable for acid manufacture, and yields a high-grade matte that reduces the load on subsequent converting. Variants include the Outotec (now Metso) flash smelting furnace and the INCO flash furnace, which uses pure oxygen.
