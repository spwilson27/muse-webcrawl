Scanian Law

A medieval manuscript of the Scanian Law

The Scanian Law (Danish: Skånske Lov, Swedish: Skånelagen) was the provincial law code that applied in the Danish provinces of Scania (Skåne), Halland, Blekinge, and the island of Bornholm. It is the oldest of the Danish provincial laws and one of the earliest legal texts written in a Scandinavian vernacular language.

History

The law was compiled in the early 13th century, around 1202–1216, during the reign of King Valdemar II of Denmark. Unusually for its time, it was written in Danish rather than Latin, making it accessible beyond the clergy. A Latin paraphrase, the Liber legis Scaniae, was prepared by Archbishop Anders Sunesen of Lund around 1202–1215 for those trained in Roman and canon law.

Content

The code covers inheritance, marriage, criminal offences, compensation tariffs (weregild-like payments), land ownership, and ecclesiastical matters. It reflects a transitional legal culture combining customary Germanic practice with the growing influence of the Church and royal authority.

Later history

After the Treaty of Roskilde in 1658, Scania, Halland, and Blekinge were ceded to Sweden. Swedish law was gradually imposed, and the Scanian Law was formally replaced in the region by the Swedish national law code during the 1680s, although local customs persisted longer in practice.
