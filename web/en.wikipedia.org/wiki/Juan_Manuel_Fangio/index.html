Juan Manuel Fangio (June 24, 1911 – July 17, 1995), nicknamed El Chueco ("the bowlegged one") and El Maestro ("The Master"), was an Argentine racing driver who dominated the first decade of Formula One, winning the World Drivers' Championship five times with four different teams—a record that stood for 46 years until surpassed by Michael Schumacher in 2003.

Early career

Born in Balcarce to Italian immigrant parents, Fangio left school to work as a mechanic and began racing in South America's gruelling long-distance road races (Turismo Carretera), winning the Argentine national championship in 1940 and 1941 and surviving the marathon Buenos Aires–Lima race. Backed by the government of Juan Perón, he moved to European Grand Prix racing in 1949, winning races at San Remo, Pau, and elsewhere.

Formula One championships

Fangio won his first world title with Alfa Romeo in 1951. After a serious neck injury at Monza in 1952 cost him a season, he won consecutive championships in 1954 (with Maserati and Mercedes-Benz), 1955 (Mercedes), 1956 (Ferrari), and 1957 (Maserati). His 1957 German Grand Prix at the Nürburgring—where he recovered from a botched pit stop to overtake the Ferraris of Mike Hawthorn and Peter Collins, breaking the lap record repeatedly—is widely considered one of the greatest drives in motorsport history. His winning percentage (24 victories in 51 championship starts, about 47%) remains the highest in Formula One history.

Retirement and later life

Fangio retired in 1958 after the French Grand Prix, famously being kidnapped in Havana by Cuban rebels of Fidel Castro's movement before that year's Cuban Grand Prix and released unharmed. He became an ambassador for Mercedes-Benz Argentina, and the museum bearing his name in Balcarce preserves his cars and trophies. He died in Buenos Aires in 1995.
