The Limes Germanicus (Latin for Germanic frontier) is the name given in modern times to a line of frontier (limes) fortifications that bounded the ancient Roman provinces of Germania Inferior, Germania Superior and Raetia, dividing the Roman Empire and the unsubdued Germanic tribes from the years 83 to about 260 AD. At its height, the limes stretched from the North Sea outlet of the Rhine to near Regensburg on the Danube. The Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes, with a length of 568 km (353 mi), was one of the longest archaeological monuments in Europe.

Structure

The frontier comprised the Lower Germanic Limes along the Lower Rhine, the Upper Germanic Limes running from the Rhine near Rheinbrohl across the Taunus and Wetterau to the Main and on to Lorch, and the Rhaetian Limes continuing to the Danube near Eining. It included around 900 watchtowers and 120 forts of various sizes, joined in the Upper Germanic section by a continuous wooden palisade, later supplemented by ditches and, in Raetia, a stone wall about 3 metres high.

History

After the disaster of the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest (AD 9), Rome abandoned the conquest of Germania east of the Rhine. Under Domitian, expansion into the Agri Decumates between the Rhine and Danube shortened the frontier, and the limes was progressively fortified under Trajan, Hadrian and Antoninus Pius, advancing to a forward line under Antoninus. During the crisis of the third century, Alemannic pressure led to the abandonment of the Agri Decumates around AD 259/260 (the Limesfall), and the frontier retreated to the Rhine–Iller–Danube line.

Legacy

Excavated and reconstructed sites include the Saalburg fort near Bad Homburg. In 2005 the Upper Germanic-Rhaetian Limes was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the transnational "Frontiers of the Roman Empire" property, joined by the Lower Germanic Limes in 2021.
