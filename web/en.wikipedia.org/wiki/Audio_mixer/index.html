Audio mixer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audio mixer (also called a mixing console, mixing desk, or sound board) is an electronic device for combining, routing, and changing the level, tone, and dynamics of multiple audio signals. Mixers are central equipment in recording studios, live sound reinforcement, broadcasting, and DJ performance.

Structure

A mixer is organized into identical channel strips, one per input. A typical strip provides a microphone preamplifier with gain control and phantom power, equalization controls, auxiliary sends feeding effects or monitor mixes, a pan control positioning the signal in the stereo field, and a channel fader. Channels are summed onto buses feeding the main stereo (or surround) outputs, subgroup masters, and recording outputs; meters display signal levels to prevent overload.

Analog and digital mixers

Analog consoles process signals entirely in the analog domain and are prized for their sound and immediacy, from classic studio desks by Neve and SSL to small live mixers. Digital consoles convert inputs to digital audio and process them with DSP, gaining features impossible or impractical in analog: total recall of settings, onboard effects and dynamics on every channel, scene automation, remote control over networks, and integration with digital audio workstations. DJ mixers are specialized small mixers with crossfaders and cueing systems for blending recorded music.

Applications

Front-of-house engineers use mixers to balance a live band for the audience while separate monitor mixes serve the performers; recording engineers use them to combine microphones and instruments into stereo mixes; broadcast mixers handle speech, music, and playback sources for radio and television. Software mixers inside DAWs now replicate the console's functions for much studio work.
