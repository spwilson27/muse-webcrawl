Great Wall of Gorgan

The Great Wall of Gorgan is a Sasanian-era defensive system located near Gorgan in north-eastern Iran, in the south-eastern corner of the Caspian Sea. It is sometimes known in Persian as the "Red Snake" (Sadd-e Surkh) because of the colour of its bricks. At roughly 195 km (121 mi) long, it is the longest ancient defensive wall in Iran and among the longest such monumental barriers of the ancient world, second only to the systems of walls in China.

Description

The wall runs from the Caspian Sea coast eastwards towards the Pishkamar Mountains, largely following the Gorgan River. It consists of a fired-brick wall, an accompanying ditch and a chain of more than 30 forts at regular intervals. A system of canals fed water from the Gorgan River along the line of the wall, supplying the brick kilns during construction and the garrisons thereafter. More than a hundred brick kilns have been identified along the wall in the plains areas.

History

Archaeological investigation, including radiocarbon and optically stimulated luminescence dating carried out by an Iranian–British team from 2005 onwards, has shown that the wall was built in the 5th or possibly 6th century AD, during the late Sasanian period. It guarded the fertile Gorgan plain — the granary of north-eastern Iran — against the nomadic peoples of the Central Asian steppe, in particular the Hephthalites, and later the Göktürks.

The forts along the wall could have housed tens of thousands of soldiers; the largest, of around 40 hectares, resemble the legionary fortresses of the Roman world in their internal division into barrack-like ranges. The scale of the work — an estimated 200 million bricks — testifies to the organisational capacity of the Sasanian state.
