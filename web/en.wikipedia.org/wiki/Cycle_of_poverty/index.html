Cycle of poverty

The cycle of poverty or poverty trap is the concept that poverty, once started, is likely to continue unless there is outside intervention. It argues that the poor do not have access to the resources — financial capital, education, social connections — required to escape poverty, and that poverty is consequently passed from one generation to the next, often described as persisting for at least three generations before a family accumulates sufficient resources to break out.

Mechanism

In economics, a poverty trap is a self-reinforcing mechanism in which low income prevents investment in health, education or productive assets, which in turn keeps income low. Children raised in poverty tend to experience worse nutrition and healthcare, lower educational attainment, and restricted access to better-paid employment; as adults they are more likely to raise their own children in poverty. Poor households may also face higher costs for credit, food and housing (sometimes called the "poverty premium"), and lack savings to buffer shocks such as illness or job loss.

Breaking the cycle

Proposed interventions include public investment in education and healthcare, microfinance, conditional cash transfers such as Brazil's Bolsa Família, asset-building programmes, and anti-discrimination measures. Debates continue over the relative weight of structural factors versus individual or cultural explanations; the older "culture of poverty" thesis associated with Oscar Lewis has been widely criticised for victim-blaming.
