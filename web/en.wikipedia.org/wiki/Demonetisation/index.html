Demonetisation

Demonetisation is the act of stripping a currency unit of its status as legal tender. It occurs whenever the prevailing currency is replaced with a new one, whenever particular denominations are withdrawn from circulation, or when a country abandons one monetary standard (such as the gold standard) for another. The opposite process is remonetisation.

Examples

The most prominent recent case was the 2016 Indian banknote demonetisation, in which the Government of India withdrew the ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes — about 86% of the cash in circulation by value — announcing goals of curbing black money, counterfeit currency and corruption, and promoting digital payments. The move caused prolonged cash shortages and queues at banks, and its economic effects remain debated. Other notable episodes include the European transition to the euro, in which legacy national currencies were demonetised in 2002, Zimbabwe's demonetisation of its dollar in 2015, and North Korea's 2009 currency revaluation.

Rationale and criticism

Governments demonetise to replace worn currency, unify monetary systems, fight counterfeiting or the informal economy, or revalue after hyperinflation. Critics note that sudden demonetisation can disrupt trade, hurt cash-dependent and rural populations, and impose large short-term costs.
