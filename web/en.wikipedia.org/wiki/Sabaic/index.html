Sabaic

Sabaic, also known as Sabaean, was an Old South Arabian language spoken by the Sabaeans of the kingdom of Saba in southwestern Arabia, in what is now Yemen. It is the best-attested of the Old South Arabian languages and was in use from roughly the early 1st millennium BC until the 6th century AD. Its corpus of over ten thousand inscriptions — dedicatory, legal, building and funerary texts, plus documents written on wooden sticks — makes it the principal source for the history of pre-Islamic South Arabia.

Attestation and dialects

Sabaic is divided into early, middle and late periods. Middle Sabaic, the language of the great monumental inscriptions of the Sabaean and later Himyarite kingdoms, is the standard form; later inscriptions show monotheistic formulas as Judaism and then Christianity spread in South Arabia in the 4th–6th centuries. The language was written in the consonantal musnad script of 29 signs, usually right-to-left or boustrophedon in early texts. Thousands of minuscule documents on palm-leaf stalks and wooden sticks, recording everyday contracts and letters, were deciphered only in the late 20th century.

Linguistic features

Like other Semitic languages, Sabaic builds words from triliteral roots and uses suffixed and prefixed verb conjugations. A characteristic feature is the use of the letter h- and later ʾ- as causative prefixes, and the suffixed definite article -n. Sabaic differs from Arabic in several respects, including its article and its treatment of certain consonants, and the classification of the Old South Arabian languages within Semitic — whether as a separate "South Semitic" branch with Arabic or otherwise — remains debated.
