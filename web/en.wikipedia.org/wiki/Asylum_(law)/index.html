Asylum (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Asylum is a legal protection granted by a state to a foreign national who has fled their country because of a well-founded fear of persecution — on grounds of race, religion, nationality, political opinion or membership of a particular social group, per the 1951 Refugee Convention's definition of a refugee — or, under regional and national extensions, because of war, generalised violence, torture or inhuman treatment (subsidiary/complementary protection).

Procedure and politics

Asylum seekers apply at borders or in-country, are screened and interviewed, and receive status determination; recognised refugees gain residence and integration rights, while rejected applicants face return (subject to non-refoulement, which also bars return to torture absolutely under human-rights law). Systems strain under mass arrivals (the 2015–16 European movement of people, the Venezuelan displacement, post-2021 Afghan and Ukrainian flows — Ukrainians receiving temporary protection in the EU rather than individual asylum). Policy debates revolve around processing speed, deterrence and offshore processing (Australia's Pacific model; the UK's Rwanda scheme, abandoned in 2024), safe-third-country rules, and the burden-sharing quarrels of the EU's Dublin system and its 2024 pact reforms. Diplomatic asylum (in embassies) is recognised chiefly in Latin American practice.
