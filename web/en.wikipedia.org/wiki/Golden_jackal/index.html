The golden jackal (Canis aureus), also called the common jackal, is a medium-sized canid native to southeastern Europe, central and southern Asia, and the Middle East. It is a wolf-like canid closely related to the grey wolf, but smaller and lighter in build, with shorter legs and a shorter tail. Its coat is generally a grizzled golden-brown, varying with the season and region.

Distribution and habitat

Golden jackals occupy a wide range of habitats, from dry deciduous forest and open grassland to agricultural land and the fringes of towns. Since the late 20th century the species has been expanding northward and westward in Europe, with vagrants recorded far beyond its traditional range, including the Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, and the Baltic states. Genetic studies have shown that European and Asian populations form a single species, distinct from the African golden wolf.

Behaviour and diet

Golden jackals are social animals living in family units centred on a monogamous breeding pair, sometimes accompanied by grown offspring that help raise pups. They are opportunistic omnivores, feeding on small mammals, birds, reptiles, fruit, and carrion, and they frequently scavenge near human settlements. Pairs maintain territories and communicate with characteristic howling choruses.

Conservation

The species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN thanks to its wide distribution and large, stable population, estimated at more than 150,000 mature individuals in Europe and Asia combined. It is nevertheless persecuted in some areas as a livestock predator and hunted under national game laws.
