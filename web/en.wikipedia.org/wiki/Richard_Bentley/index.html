Richard Bentley (27 January 1662 – 14 July 1742) was an English classical scholar, critic, and theologian. Regarded as the founder of historical philology in England and one of the greatest classical scholars of his age, he was Master of Trinity College, Cambridge, from 1700 until his death, and the first Englishman to be recognised throughout Europe as a leading authority on Greek and Latin literature.

Early life and rise

Bentley was born at Oulton near Leeds and educated at Wakefield grammar school and St John's College, Cambridge. After serving as headmaster of Spalding school and as chaplain and tutor in the household of Edward Stillingfleet, dean of St Paul's, he published in 1691 his Epistola ad Millium, appended to an edition of the chronicle of John Malalas, which displayed his learning to European scholars while he was still in his twenties.

Scholarship

As the first Boyle lecturer (1692), Bentley used Newtonian physics to argue for the existence of God in The Folly and Unreasonableness of Atheism, corresponding with Newton himself. His Dissertation upon the Epistles of Phalaris (1697–1699), written in the "Battle of the Books" controversy, demolished the supposed letters of the Sicilian tyrant Phalaris and established modern methods of textual and historical criticism; it made his reputation and remains a landmark of classical scholarship.

Bentley's editions and emendations covered Horace, Terence, Manilius and others. His discovery of the lost Greek letter digamma explained metrical irregularities throughout Homer and became one of the most famous triumphs of conjectural criticism. His 1732 edition of Milton's Paradise Lost, claiming to correct a corrupt text and an interfering editor, was widely ridiculed and damaged his reputation with the public.

Master of Trinity

Appointed Master of Trinity College in 1700 and Regius Professor of Divinity in 1717, Bentley greatly raised the college's scholarly standards and promoted the building of its observatory and the study of science, but his imperious administration provoked decades of litigation with the fellows, who repeatedly attempted to deprive him of the mastership. He retained office until his death in 1742.
