Lateral thinking is a manner of solving problems using an indirect and creative approach, typically through viewing the problem in a new and unusual light. The term was coined in 1967 by the Maltese psychologist and author Edward de Bono, who contrasted it with "vertical thinking" — conventional, step-by-step logical reasoning that proceeds within an established pattern.

Concept

De Bono argued that the mind is a self-organising pattern-making system: it builds efficient routines but tends to keep using them even when they block a solution. Lateral thinking deliberately escapes the dominant pattern — by challenging assumptions, entering the problem at a different point, or using provocation. His famous analogy: vertical thinking digs the same hole deeper; lateral thinking digs a hole somewhere else.

Techniques

De Bono proposed a toolkit of formal techniques, including:

  • Provocation (PO) — deliberately stating an impossible or absurd idea (e.g. "cars should have square wheels") and using "movement" to extract a workable principle from it.
  • Random entry — using a randomly chosen word or object as a fresh starting point for generating ideas.
  • Challenging assumptions — questioning why things are done the usual way, including assumptions not normally recognised as assumptions.
  • The Six Thinking Hats (1985) — a parallel-thinking framework directing a group to adopt one mode (facts, feelings, caution, benefits, creativity, process) at a time.

Reception

Lateral thinking became a widely taught business and education technique, and the phrase entered general English usage for solving problems "sideways." Critics note that the concept overlaps with earlier work on creativity and divergent thinking, and that controlled evidence for the specific techniques' effectiveness is limited, though the general value of reframing problems is broadly accepted.
