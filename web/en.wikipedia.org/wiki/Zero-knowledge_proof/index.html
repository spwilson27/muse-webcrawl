Zero-knowledge proof

In cryptography, a zero-knowledge proof is a method by which one party (the prover) can prove to another party (the verifier) that a given statement is true, while avoiding conveying any information apart from the fact that the statement is indeed true.

Definition

A zero-knowledge proof must satisfy three properties:

  • Completeness — if the statement is true, an honest verifier will be convinced by an honest prover.
  • Soundness — if the statement is false, no cheating prover can convince an honest verifier, except with negligible probability.
  • Zero-knowledge — the verifier learns nothing beyond the truth of the statement; formally, the verifier's view can be simulated without the prover's secret.

History and examples

The concept was introduced in 1985 by Shafi Goldwasser, Silvio Micali and Charles Rackoff in their paper "The Knowledge Complexity of Interactive Proof Systems." Classic illustrative examples include the "Ali Baba cave" story, in which a prover demonstrates knowledge of a secret password opening a door inside a cave without revealing the password, and the graph three-colouring protocol.

Interactive proofs can be made non-interactive using the Fiat–Shamir heuristic. Practical constructions include the Schnorr protocol for proving knowledge of a discrete logarithm, and the modern succinct systems known as zk-SNARKs and zk-STARKs.

Applications

Zero-knowledge proofs are used in authentication protocols, anonymous credentials, electronic voting, and blockchain systems. Cryptocurrencies such as Zcash use zk-SNARKs to shield transaction details, while "ZK-rollups" on Ethereum use validity proofs to scale transaction throughput. They are also discussed for nuclear warhead verification, where inspectors must confirm an object's identity without learning classified design information.
