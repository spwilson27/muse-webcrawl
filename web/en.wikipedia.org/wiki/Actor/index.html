Actor

An actor is a person who portrays a character in a performance — in theatre, film, television, radio, voice work or other storytelling media. The actor's work is to embody the character's actions, speech and emotions so as to bring a script or story to life for an audience. The term traditionally applied to men, while "actress" referred to women; in contemporary usage "actor" is commonly used for performers of all genders.

History

Acting originated in religious ritual and ancient theatre. In ancient Greece the word for actor, hypokrites, meant "one who answers"; Thespis is traditionally credited as the first person to speak as a character separate from the chorus, giving rise to the term "thespian". Women were excluded from the stage in many early traditions — female roles in Shakespeare's England were played by boys — and were only gradually admitted, from the 17th century onward in much of Europe. The 19th and 20th centuries saw acting become a respected profession, with stars of stage and screen achieving mass celebrity.

Techniques

Approaches to acting include classical technique, focused on voice, movement and external craft, and psychological approaches derived from Konstantin Stanislavski's system, later developed in the United States as "the Method" by teachers such as Lee Strasberg, Stella Adler and Sanford Meisner. Other influential schools include Bertolt Brecht's epic theatre, which aimed at critical detachment, and physical theatre traditions. Actors typically train through drama schools, conservatoires, workshops and rehearsal.

Work and industry

Actors are cast through auditions and casting directors, work under directors, and may be represented by agents and unions such as SAG-AFTRA in the United States or Equity in the United Kingdom. Employment is famously irregular; most actors combine performance with other work, while a few reach leading roles in film and theatre, recognised by awards such as the Academy Awards, Tony Awards and BAFTAs.
