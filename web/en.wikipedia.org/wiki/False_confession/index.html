False confession

A false confession is an admission of guilt for a crime for which the confessor is not responsible. False confessions can be induced through coercion, mental disorder, intoxication, diminished capacity, or deceptive interrogation techniques, and they are a documented cause of wrongful convictions. Research suggests that false confessions contributed to roughly a quarter or more of the convictions later overturned by DNA evidence in the United States.

Types

Psychologist Saul Kassin and colleagues proposed an influential threefold classification:

  • Voluntary false confessions are offered without external pressure, sometimes from a desire for notoriety, to protect the real culprit, or as a result of mental illness. Famous crimes attract them in quantity: more than 200 people are said to have confessed to the Lindbergh kidnapping.
  • Coerced-compliant false confessions are given by suspects who know they are innocent but confess to escape an intolerable interrogation, end the stress, or gain a promised benefit.
  • Coerced-internalised false confessions occur when a vulnerable suspect, worn down by suggestion and false claims about the evidence, comes to believe — at least temporarily — that he or she may actually have committed the crime.

Causes

Modern accusatorial interrogation methods, notably the Reid technique used widely in North America, are designed to break down resistance through isolation, lengthy questioning, presentation of (sometimes fabricated) evidence, and minimisation of the moral seriousness of the offence coupled with implied leniency. Juveniles, people with intellectual disabilities or mental illness, and fatigued or intoxicated suspects are at elevated risk. A related hazard is contamination: interrogators may feed details of the crime to the suspect, after which the confession appears to contain "inside knowledge" that only the perpetrator could know, making it highly persuasive to juries.

Confession evidence is exceptionally powerful. Experiments show that mock jurors convict far more often when a confession is presented, even when told it was coerced and even when they claim to have disregarded it. Once a confession is obtained, it can also corrupt other evidence: witnesses become more confident, and forensic analysts interpreting ambiguous results may be influenced by knowing a suspect has confessed.

Notable cases

Documented examples include the Central Park Five, teenagers whose coerced confessions to a 1989 New York assault were vacated after DNA and a serial offender's confession identified the actual attacker; the Norfolk Four, sailors who confessed under interrogation to a murder later attributed by DNA to a lone perpetrator; and Brendan Dassey, whose confession featured in the documentary Making a Murderer and generated litigation over the interrogation of juveniles. In Iceland, the Guðmundur and Geirfinnur case saw six people convicted largely on confessions obtained in prolonged solitary confinement; they were acquitted in 2018, decades later.

Reforms

Proposed and adopted safeguards include mandatory electronic recording of custodial interrogations, time limits on questioning, special protections for juveniles and vulnerable suspects, expert testimony on false-confession research, and the PEACE model of investigative interviewing used in the United Kingdom, which replaced accusatorial methods with information-gathering interviews after miscarriages of justice in cases such as the Guildford Four and Birmingham Six.
