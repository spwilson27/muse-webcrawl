Ionization energy


Symbol: Ei
Units: kJ/mol or eV per atom

In physics and chemistry, ionization energy (IE) is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from a neutral atom or molecule in its ground state in the gas phase: X(g) → X+(g) + e−. It is quantitatively expressed in kilojoules per mole or electronvolts per atom, and is always positive (endothermic). Successive ionization energies remove additional electrons and increase steeply once the valence shell is exhausted.

Trends

First ionization energy generally increases across a period, as nuclear charge rises and atomic radius shrinks, and decreases down a group, as the outermost electron is farther from the nucleus and better shielded. Noble gases have the highest values in each period (helium has the highest of all elements, about 24.6 eV), and alkali metals the lowest. Notable dips at group 13 and group 16 reflect subshell structure and electron-pair repulsion.

Determinants and measurement

Ionization energies are determined by atomic size, nuclear charge, shielding, and electron configuration stability (half-filled and filled subshells). They are measured spectroscopically and by electron-impact and photoelectron methods, and they underpin concepts such as electronegativity and the classification of elements as metals and nonmetals.
