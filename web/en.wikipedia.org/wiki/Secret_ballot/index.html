Secret ballot

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The secret ballot, also known as the Australian ballot, is a voting method in which a voter's identity in an election or a referendum is anonymous. This forestalls attempts to influence the voter by intimidation, blackmail and potential vote buying. Voters cast a uniform official ballot, privately marked in a polling booth, and deposited unsigned into a sealed box.

History

Ancient precedents include Roman ballot laws of the late Republic. The modern system was a Chartist demand in Britain (1838 People's Charter) and was first implemented in the Australian colonies (Victoria and South Australia, 1856), whence the name; Britain adopted it in the Ballot Act 1872, and US states adopted the "Australian ballot" in the 1880s–90s, displacing party-printed coloured tickets publicly deposited. The secret ballot is now prescribed by the Universal Declaration (Art. 21) and ICCPR (Art. 25) as a component of genuine elections.

Tensions

Secrecy conflicts with verifiability in remote voting: postal and internet voting move the ballot outside the controlled booth, inviting family coercion ("family voting") and vote-selling proofs (photographed ballots), mitigated by procedures and offences. Debates also recur over parliamentary voting (where publicity is the norm — representatives vote openly, in contrast to citizens) and over assisted voting for disabled electors.
