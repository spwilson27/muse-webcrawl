Polluter pays principle

The polluter pays principle is an environmental principle according to which whoever causes environmental damage or pollution should bear the costs of preventing, controlling, and remedying it. It is a cornerstone of environmental policy in many jurisdictions and in international environmental law. The principle aims to internalise the external costs of pollution — costs that would otherwise be imposed on society at large — into the price of the goods and services that cause pollution during their production or consumption.

History

The principle was first promoted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) in Council Recommendations of 1972 and 1974, originally as an economic principle to prevent distortions in international trade and investment: states should not subsidise the pollution-control costs of their industries. It was later incorporated into the Rio Declaration on Environment and Development of 1992 (Principle 16) and into the law of the European Union, where it is enshrined in Article 191(2) of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union.

Implementation

Instruments implementing the principle include pollution taxes and charges (such as carbon taxes), emissions trading systems, environmental liability regimes (such as the EU Environmental Liability Directive), extended producer responsibility schemes for waste, and deposit-refund systems. A related but distinct concept is the "user pays" principle, and both contrast with public subsidy of pollution abatement.
