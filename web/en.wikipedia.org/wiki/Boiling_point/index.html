Boiling point

The boiling point of a substance is the temperature at which the vapour pressure of a liquid equals the pressure surrounding the liquid and the liquid changes into a vapour. The boiling point of a liquid varies depending upon the surrounding environmental pressure. A liquid in a partial vacuum has a lower boiling point than when at atmospheric pressure; a liquid in a high-pressure environment has a higher boiling point. The normal boiling point is the boiling point at one atmosphere of pressure.

Dependence on pressure

Bubbles of vapour form within the liquid only when its vapour pressure can overcome the external pressure; hence at high altitude, where atmospheric pressure is lower, water boils at a lower temperature — about 95 °C in Denver and roughly 70 °C on the summit of Mount Everest — which lengthens cooking times. Pressure cookers exploit the reverse effect, raising the boiling point to around 120 °C. The relationship is described by the Clausius–Clapeyron equation.

Measurement and purity

Pure substances boil at a sharp, constant temperature; mixtures boil over a range, a fact exploited in distillation. Dissolved non-volatile solutes raise the boiling point (boiling-point elevation, a colligative property), while superheating can briefly push a still liquid above its boiling point until nucleation triggers sudden boiling.

Examples

Normal boiling points include helium at −268.9 °C, nitrogen at −195.8 °C, ethanol at 78.2 °C, mercury at 356.7 °C and tungsten — the element with the highest boiling point — at about 5,555 °C.
