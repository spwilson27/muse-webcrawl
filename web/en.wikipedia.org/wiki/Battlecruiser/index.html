Battlecruiser

The battlecruiser (also written battle cruiser or battle-cruiser) was a type of capital ship of the first half of the 20th century. These were similar in displacement, armament and cost to a battleship, but differed slightly in form and balance of offensive and defensive properties. Battlecruisers typically had thinner armour and a somewhat lighter main gun battery than contemporary battleships, installed on a longer hull with much higher engine power to attain high speeds, generally in the region of 25–30 knots (46–56 km/h).

Origins

The type was created under the direction of British Admiral Sir John ("Jacky") Fisher, who envisioned a fast ship with battleship-calibre guns that could hunt down enemy armoured cruisers and scout for the battle fleet, outrunning anything it could not outgun. The first battlecruisers, the Invincible class, entered service with the Royal Navy in 1908, and Germany, Japan, France, Russia, Italy, the United States and other navies all laid down battlecruiser designs in the ensuing dreadnought arms race.

Service history

Battlecruisers fought successfully against cruisers — notably at the Battle of the Falkland Islands in 1914 — but proved dangerously vulnerable when committed against battleships. At the Battle of Jutland in 1916, three British battlecruisers blew up under German fire with the loss of more than 3,000 men, prompting Admiral Beatty's remark that "there seems to be something wrong with our bloody ships today." Post-war designs, culminating in HMS Hood, carried heavier protection, blurring the line with the "fast battleship". Hood herself was sunk by the German battleship Bismarck in 1941 with the loss of all but three of her crew.

Legacy

Few battlecruisers survived the Second World War, and none remain afloat. The large Russian Kirov-class nuclear cruisers, sometimes informally called battlecruisers because of their size and armament, are the only modern warships to which the label is still loosely applied.
