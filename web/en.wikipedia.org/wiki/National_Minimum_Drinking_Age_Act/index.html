National Minimum Drinking Age Act

National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984
Enacted by98th United States Congress
Effective17 July 1984 (signed)
CitationsPublic Law 98-363; 23 U.S.C. § 158
Signed byPresident Ronald Reagan

The National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984 is a United States federal law that required states to prohibit persons under 21 years of age from purchasing or publicly possessing alcoholic beverages as a condition of receiving federal highway funds. States that failed to comply faced a reduction — initially 5 percent, rising to 10 percent — in their annual federal highway apportionment. By 1988, all fifty states had adopted a minimum purchase age of 21.

Background

After the voting age was lowered to 18 by the Twenty-sixth Amendment in 1971, many states reduced their drinking ages to 18, 19, or 20. Concern over drunk-driving fatalities among teenagers, pressed by groups such as Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD), led Congress to use its spending power to press for a uniform age of 21. President Ronald Reagan, though initially doubtful about the federal role, signed the Act on 17 July 1984.

Constitutionality and effects

South Dakota challenged the law, but in South Dakota v. Dole (1987) the Supreme Court upheld it as a valid exercise of Congress's spending power. Studies have associated the age-21 laws with reductions in alcohol-related traffic deaths among young drivers, though the size and persistence of the effect remain debated. The Act sets a floor for purchase and public possession only; it does not prohibit consumption by minors in all settings, and several states retain exceptions (for example, with parental consent or for religious purposes).
