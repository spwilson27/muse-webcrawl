York Minster, formally the Cathedral and Metropolitical Church of Saint Peter in York, is the cathedral of York, England, and the seat of the Archbishop of York, the second-highest office in the Church of England. One of the largest Gothic cathedrals in Northern Europe, it is renowned for its stained glass — including the Great West Window and the Five Sisters window in the north transept — and for the craftsmanship of its stonemasons and glaziers.

History

Christian worship on the site dates to 627, when a wooden church was built for the baptism of King Edwin of Northumbria. A stone church followed, and after the Norman Conquest Archbishop Thomas of Bayeux began a new cathedral around 1080. The present Gothic minster was begun under Archbishop Walter de Grey about 1230: the transepts came first, then the chapter house, the nave (finished in the 1330s, the widest Gothic nave in England), the choir, and the central tower, with the building declared complete in 1472. A disastrous fire in 1984, probably caused by lightning, destroyed the roof of the south transept; its restoration included new roof bosses designed from a children's competition.

Architecture and treasures

The minster preserves the largest expanse of medieval stained glass in England. Its Great East Window, created by John Thornton of Coventry between 1405 and 1408, depicts scenes from Genesis and the Apocalypse across an area the size of a tennis court. The King's Screen (quire screen) carries statues of English kings from William I to Henry VI, and the chapter house is celebrated for its carved canopies and grotesques. The cathedral's library and archives hold records dating back a millennium.

Today

York Minster remains a working cathedral with daily services, a major tourist destination, and the center of the Diocese of York. Ongoing conservation programs, including the Centre of Excellence for Heritage Craft Skills opened in the 2020s, train stonemasons and glaziers to maintain the building.
