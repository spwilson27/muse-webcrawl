The Sanctuary of Monte Sant'Angelo (Italian: Santuario di San Michele Arcangelo) is a Roman Catholic shrine dedicated to Saint Michael the Archangel on Mount Gargano in Apulia, Italy. It is the oldest shrine in Western Europe dedicated to the archangel, and has been a major pilgrimage site since the early Middle Ages.

The apparitions

According to tradition recorded in the Liber de apparitione Sancti Michaelis, the archangel appeared in 490 AD to the bishop of Siponto after a straying bull was found kneeling at the mouth of a cave. Further apparitions followed, including the "apparition of the battle" in 492 and the dedication apparition of 493, in which Michael declared the cave already consecrated and forbade the bishop to dedicate it himself — hence the sanctuary's unique status as a basilica not consecrated by human hands.

History

The shrine drew pilgrims from across Europe, including Lombards, who made Michael their patron and enriched the site; Norman, Byzantine and Angevin rulers added the octagonal bell tower and the great staircase descending into the grotto. Crusaders and kings stopped at Monte Sant'Angelo on their way to the Holy Land, and the site became a station on the Via Francigena's southern routes. Pilgrims' graffiti covering many centuries survive on its walls.

Architecture

Visitors descend a long vaulted staircase to the sacred grotto, a natural cave church containing the altar, a 12th-century episcopal throne and a statue of St Michael attributed to the school of Sansovino. The sanctuary was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2011 as part of the serial site "Longobards in Italy: Places of the Power (568–774 A.D.)".
