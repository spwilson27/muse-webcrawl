Music of Morocco

The music of Morocco ranges from the courtly Andalusian classical tradition to Berber (Amazigh) village music, Gnawa ritual music, Sufi brotherhood chanting and modern popular styles such as chaabi and Moroccan rap. Morocco's musical culture reflects Arab, Amazigh, sub-Saharan African, Jewish and Iberian influences layered over more than a millennium.

Andalusian music

Al-āla, the Moroccan form of Andalusian music, preserves the nuba suites of medieval Muslim Spain, transmitted by refugees who settled in Fes, Tetouan and other cities after the fall of Granada and the expulsions from Spain. It is performed by orchestras of oud, rebab, violin, qanun and percussion, singing classical Arabic poetry. Related urban genres include malhun, sung poetry in Moroccan dialect, and the gharnati tradition of the east.

Gnawa and Berber music

The Gnawa, descendants of West African slaves, perform all-night ritual ceremonies (lilas) of healing and spirit invocation, led by a maâlem playing the guembri (a three-stringed bass lute) and metal qraqeb castanets; their music, fusing African rhythms with Arabic and Berber elements, has strongly influenced jazz and world music, and anchors the annual Gnaoua World Music Festival in Essaouira. Amazigh traditions include the collective ahidus and ahidous dances of the Middle Atlas, the poetry-songs of travelling rways bards in the Souss, and the female wedding singers of the Rif.

Popular music

Chaabi, a popular music of weddings and festivals, was shaped by groups such as Nass El Ghiwane, Jil Jilala and Lemchaheb in the 1970s, blending folk instruments with protest lyrics. Rai music from neighbouring Algeria is also widely heard, and since the 2000s Moroccan rap and pop — including stars such as Saad Lamjarred — have become dominant youth genres. The Mawazine festival in Rabat is one of the largest music festivals in the world.
