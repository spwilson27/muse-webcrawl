The sociology of culture examines how meaning is produced, distributed and received in society.

The sociology of culture is a subfield of sociology concerned with the social production, distribution and consumption of culture — understood as the symbols, meanings, values, beliefs, norms and artistic products through which people make sense of their world. It asks how culture shapes social life and, conversely, how social structures, institutions and inequalities shape culture.

Definitions and scope

"Culture" is a notoriously capacious term. Early sociological usage, following Edward Burnett Tylor's 1871 definition, treated culture as the whole way of life of a people. By the mid-twentieth century, American sociology often narrowed the term to values and norms, as in Talcott Parsons's pattern variables. Contemporary cultural sociology, by contrast, treats culture as an autonomous analytic dimension: systems of meaning embodied in symbols, narratives, rituals and classifications that cannot be reduced to social structure alone.

History

Classical foundations

Karl Marx's account of ideology, Max Weber's sociology of religion and his analysis of status groups and lifestyle, and Georg Simmel's essays on fashion, money and metropolitan life all laid foundations for the field. Émile Durkheim's The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912) treated collective representations and ritual as the glue of social solidarity.

The cultural turn

From the 1960s onwards, the "cultural turn" reshaped sociology. The founding of the Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies at Birmingham in 1964, the work of Raymond Williams and Stuart Hall, and the influence of Clifford Geertz's interpretive anthropology all pushed sociologists to take meaning seriously. Pierre Bourdieu's Distinction (1979) demonstrated how taste and lifestyle reproduce class inequality through cultural capital and habitus.

Major approaches

  • Production of culture perspective — studies how the organisation of cultural industries (publishing, recording, film) shapes the content of cultural products (Richard Peterson, Paul Hirsch, Howard Becker's Art Worlds).
  • Cultural diamond — Wendy Griswold's model linking creators, cultural objects, receivers and the social world.
  • Strong program — Jeffrey Alexander and Philip Smith's cultural sociology, which grants culture analytic autonomy and studies the codes and narratives structuring public life.
  • Culture in action — Ann Swidler's "toolkit" model, in which culture provides repertoires of strategies of action rather than unified values.
  • Reception studies — examines how audiences interpret and appropriate cultural goods, including fan cultures and everyday media use.

Topics

Substantive research areas include the sociology of art, literature, music and film; popular and mass culture; media and digital culture; religion; science and knowledge; memory and commemoration; food and taste; fashion; and the cultural dimensions of class, race, gender and nationhood. Sociologists of culture also study classification and evaluation — how genres, canons, prizes and rankings create cultural value.
