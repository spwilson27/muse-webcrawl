Battle of France

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: German troops marching past the Arc de Triomphe in Paris, June 1940]
German troops marching past the Arc de Triomphe in Paris, June 1940

The Battle of France (10 May – 25 June 1940), also known as the Fall of France, was the successful German invasion of France and the Low Countries during the Second World War. In just over six weeks, German forces — using concentrated armoured thrusts through the Ardennes — defeated the Allied armies, forced the British evacuation at Dunkirk and compelled France to sign an armistice, splitting the country between German occupation and the Vichy regime.

Plans and breakthrough

The original German plan mirrored 1914's sweep through Belgium; the final Sichelschnitt ('sickle cut'), associated with Erich von Manstein, instead feinted into the Low Countries to draw the best Allied forces north, while Army Group A's seven panzer divisions crossed the supposedly impassable Ardennes. On 13 May German forces crossed the Meuse at Sedan, shattering French reserves; by 20 May panzers reached the Channel at Abbeville, encircling the northern Allied armies.

Dunkirk and collapse

The halt order before Dunkirk allowed the evacuation of some 338,000 Allied troops (26 May – 4 June) — a deliverance that saved Britain's army but abandoned its equipment and France. The second German phase (Fall Rot) from 5 June broke the Weygand Line; Paris fell on 14 June; the government of Paul Reynaud gave way to Philippe Pétain, who sought the armistice signed at Compiègne on 22 June — in the same railway carriage used in 1918.

Consequences

France lost some 90,000–120,000 soldiers dead; over a million were taken prisoner. The defeat shocked the world — France had been rated Europe's strongest land power — and inaugurated four years of occupation, Resistance and Vichy collaboration, against which Charles de Gaulle's Free French fought from London. Historians debate the weight of doctrine, morale, intelligence failures and air inferiority in the collapse.
