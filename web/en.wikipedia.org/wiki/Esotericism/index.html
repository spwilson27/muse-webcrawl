Esotericism is a term for a broad range of loosely related ideas and movements that emphasize knowledge claimed to be reserved for a small group of initiates, or otherwise hidden from the general public. The adjective "esoteric" derives from the Greek esōterikos (ἐσωτερικός), meaning "inner" or "belonging to an inner circle," contrasted with "exoteric" knowledge intended for outsiders.

Scholars of Western esotericism, notably Antoine Faivre, have identified recurring characteristics in esoteric currents, including ideas of universal correspondences, a living nature, the role of imagination and mediation, and experiences of transmutation. Wouter Hanegraaff and others have framed the field as the study of "rejected knowledge" in Western culture — bodies of thought marginalized by mainstream religion and Enlightenment science.

History

Esotericism in the West has roots in late antiquity, in currents such as Hermeticism, Gnosticism, and Neoplatonism. During the Renaissance, figures such as Marsilio Ficino and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola revived Hermetic and Kabbalistic learning, blending it with Christian theology. The early modern period saw the emergence of Rosicrucianism, Paracelsian medicine, and the alchemical tradition.

In the nineteenth century, esotericism was reshaped by movements such as Spiritualism and the Theosophical Society, founded in 1875 by Helena Blavatsky and others, which popularized Eastern religious concepts in the West. The twentieth century brought ceremonial-magic orders such as the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the occultism of Aleister Crowley, Carl Jung's engagement with alchemy and symbolism, and the Traditionalist school of René Guénon and Julius Evola. Contemporary forms range from academic study to the diffuse spirituality of the New Age movement.
