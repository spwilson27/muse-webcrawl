Hierakonpolis (Ancient Greek: Ἱεράκων πόλις, "City of the Hawks"; Egyptian Nekhen) was the religious and political capital of Upper Egypt at the end of the Predynastic period (c. 4000–3100 BC) and probably also during the Early Dynastic period (c. 3100–2686 BC). It was one of the largest urban centres of its age in the Nile Valley and is among the most important archaeological sites for understanding the origins of the Egyptian state.

History

Nekhen lay on the west bank of the Nile opposite its secular counterpart, Nekheb (modern el-Kab). Its patron deity was the falcon god Horus of Nekhen, one of the earliest state gods of Egypt. The earliest settlement dates to the Naqada I period, and by Naqada II the town covered a large area with evidence of craft specialisation, including what is probably Egypt's earliest brewery and pottery kilns.

The site is famous for the Main Deposit, a cache of ceremonial objects found in the temple and excavated by James Quibell and F. W. Green in 1897–98. It contained the Narmer Palette, the Narmer and Scorpion maceheads, and numerous ivory and stone objects that document the unification of Egypt around 3100 BC.

Archaeology

Key features include the Predynastic cemeteries (notably HK6, with elite burials and evidence of early animal menageries), the unique painted tomb (Tomb 100, the "Decorated Tomb"), and Egypt's oldest known free-standing mudbrick temple enclosure, later overbuilt by New Kingdom structures. Excavations have continued for over a century and remain ongoing.
