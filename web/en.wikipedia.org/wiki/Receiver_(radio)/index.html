Receiver (radio)

In radio communications, a radio receiver, also known as a receiver, a wireless, or simply a radio, is an electronic device that receives radio waves and converts the information carried by them to a usable form. It is used with an antenna. The antenna intercepts radio waves (electromagnetic waves of radio frequency) and converts them to tiny alternating currents which are applied to the receiver, and the receiver extracts the desired information. The receiver uses electronic filters to separate the desired radio frequency signal from all the other signals picked up by the antenna, an electronic amplifier to increase the power of the signal for further processing, and finally recovers the desired information through demodulation.

Radio receivers are essential components of all systems that use radio: television, radio broadcasting, cell phones, wireless networks, radar, radio navigation, remote control, radio astronomy and many others. The most familiar type is the broadcast receiver which reproduces sound — historically at first by earphones, then by loudspeaker.

History

The first practical receivers, used for wireless telegraphy in the 1890s, employed a coherer to detect Morse code signals. Crystal receivers, using a galena crystal detector, were the first widely used type and required no external power. Reginald Fessenden's work on continuous-wave transmission and heterodyne detection, Edwin Armstrong's regenerative (1912) and superheterodyne (1918) circuits, and the development of vacuum-tube amplification transformed receiver performance. The superheterodyne architecture — converting the incoming signal to a fixed intermediate frequency for filtering and amplification — remains dominant. Transistors after 1947 miniaturised receivers, and since the 1990s digital signal processing and software-defined radio have shifted most receiver functions into software.

Design

Basic functions

  • Selectivity — the ability to separate the desired station from others on nearby frequencies, achieved by bandpass filtering.
  • Sensitivity — the minimum signal power usable, limited by noise generated in the antenna and the receiver's first stages.
  • Demodulation (detection) — recovering the modulating signal: envelope detection for AM, frequency discrimination or phase-locked loops for FM, and product detection for SSB.
  • Automatic gain control (AGC) — keeping output level roughly constant despite fading.

Architectures

The simplest architecture is the tuned radio frequency (TRF) receiver, amplifying the signal at its original frequency through several tuned stages. The superheterodyne receiver mixes the input with a local oscillator to a fixed intermediate frequency (455 kHz for AM broadcast, 10.7 MHz for FM), where sharp fixed filters give consistent selectivity. Direct-conversion (zero-IF) receivers mix straight to baseband and are common in integrated and software-defined designs.

Applications

Beyond broadcast sets, dedicated receivers serve aviation (airband), marine, amateur radio, weather satellites, GNSS navigation, garage-door and keyless-entry systems, and deep-space communication, where cryogenically cooled low-noise amplifiers recover signals billions of times weaker than broadcast radio.
