Electroporation

Electroporation, or electropermeabilization, is a physical method that uses short, intense electric pulses to create transient pores in the cell membrane, greatly increasing its permeability. Molecules that normally cannot cross the membrane — including DNA, RNA, proteins and drugs — can then enter the cell. When the pulses are brief and moderate, the pores reseal and the cell survives (reversible electroporation); stronger treatment destroys the membrane irreversibly.

Mechanism

An external electric field raises the transmembrane potential of a cell. Above a threshold (roughly 0.5–1 volt across the membrane), the lipid bilayer reorganises into aqueous pores. The induced potential is largest for big cells and depends on cell shape and orientation, which is why protocols must be tuned for each cell type. Pulse lengths range from microseconds to milliseconds, with field strengths from hundreds of volts per centimetre for large mammalian cells to many kilovolts per centimetre for bacteria.

Applications

Electroporation is a standard laboratory method for transforming bacteria and yeast and for transfecting mammalian cells — often more efficient than chemical methods for hard-to-transfect cells such as primary neurons and stem cells. In medicine it underlies electrochemotherapy, in which pulses dramatically increase tumour uptake of drugs such as bleomycin and cisplatin; irreversible electroporation (e.g. NanoKnife) ablates tumours non-thermally, sparing nearby vessels and ducts; and it is used to enhance DNA vaccine delivery and gene transfer in vivo (in vivo electroporation). A related variant, nucleofection, adapts the technique for delivering cargo directly to the nucleus.

History

Reversible membrane breakdown by electric pulses was described in the 1960s and 1970s; in 1982 Neumann and colleagues demonstrated gene transfer into mouse cells by electroporation, establishing it as a general transformation method. Clinical electrochemotherapy was developed through the 1990s and standardised by the European Standard Operating Procedures of Electrochemotherapy (ESOPE) study in 2006.
