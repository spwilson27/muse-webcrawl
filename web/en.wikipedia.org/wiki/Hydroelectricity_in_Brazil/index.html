Hydroelectricity in Brazil

The Itaipu Dam, shared with Paraguay, is one of the largest power stations in the world.

Brazil relies heavily on hydroelectricity to meet the country's energy needs. Hydroelectricity in Brazil accounts for the majority of the nation's electricity generation — historically around 60–70 percent of installed capacity — thanks to the country's abundant rivers, including the Amazon, Paraná, São Francisco and Tocantins basins. Brazil has the third largest hydroelectric potential in the world, after China and Russia.

Major plants

The Itaipu Dam on the Paraná River, a binational project with Paraguay completed in 1984, has an installed capacity of 14,000 MW and for decades was the world's largest power station by generation. The Belo Monte Dam on the Xingu River in Pará, commissioned in the 2010s, has a capacity of about 11,233 MW, though its run-of-river design produces far less on average. Other giants include Tucuruí (8,370 MW) on the Tocantins, and the Santo Antônio and Jirau plants on the Madeira River.

Grid and management

Most large plants are interconnected through the National Interconnected System (SIN), coordinated by the National Electric System Operator (ONS), which moves power between regions as rainfall varies. The state-controlled company Eletrobras historically owned much of the fleet before its privatisation in 2022.

Challenges

Dependence on rainfall makes the system vulnerable to drought; severe dry spells in 2001 forced electricity rationing, and the 2014–2015 and 2021 droughts pushed reservoir levels to critical lows, increasing the use of costly thermal backup. Large Amazonian dams also face criticism for flooding rainforest, displacing indigenous communities and releasing methane from submerged vegetation. Since the 2010s, rapid growth in wind and solar generation has diversified the mix and reduced hydro's share.
