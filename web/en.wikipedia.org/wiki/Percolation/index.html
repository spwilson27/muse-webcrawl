Percolation

In physics, chemistry and materials science, percolation (from Latin percolare, "to filter" or "trickle through") refers to the movement and filtering of fluids through porous materials. In earth science, percolation is the process by which water on the ground surface enters the soil; the percolation rate is the speed at which water moves through soil.

Description

During percolation, the water infiltrates through the soil, filling the pore spaces. Factors affecting the rate include soil texture, structure, vegetation cover and the initial moisture content. In hydrology, the water that percolates below the root zone may recharge groundwater and aquifers, and it plays a central role in the water cycle.

Percolation theory

Percolation theory is a branch of statistical physics and mathematics that describes the behaviour of a network when nodes or links are added. The central concept is the percolation threshold: when the fraction of occupied sites or bonds passes a critical value, a connected cluster first spans the whole system, allowing flow or conduction from one side to the other. The theory was introduced by Broadbent and Hammersley in 1957 and applies to problems ranging from coffee brewing and porous rock to the spread of disease and forest fires.

Applications

  • Coffee percolation: hot water passes through ground coffee, extracting soluble compounds; a percolator recirculates the brewing coffee.
  • Site percolation tests assess soil drainage for septic systems.
  • Materials science: conductive composites conduct once filler content exceeds the percolation threshold.
