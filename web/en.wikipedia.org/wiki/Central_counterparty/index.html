Central counterparty

A central counterparty (CCP), also known as a central clearing counterparty, is a financial institution that interposes itself between counterparties to contracts traded in one or more financial markets, becoming the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. By novating the original contracts, a CCP concentrates and mutualises counterparty credit risk.

How it works

Once a trade is accepted for clearing, the CCP replaces the bilateral contract with two contracts, one with each trading party. It manages risk through margin requirements — initial margin posted at the outset and variation margin exchanged daily as prices move — and through a default fund contributed by clearing members, applied in a defined "waterfall" if a member defaults.

Importance and regulation

After the 2008 financial crisis, the G20 agreed at Pittsburgh in 2009 that standardised over-the-counter derivatives should be cleared through CCPs. Major CCPs include LCH, CME Clearing, ICE Clear, and Eurex Clearing. Because CCPs concentrate risk, they are subject to intensive prudential regulation and recovery and resolution planning.
