Slack-key guitar

Slack-key guitar (Hawaiian: kī hō‘alu, meaning "loosen the [tuning] key") is a fingerstyle genre of guitar music that originated in Hawaii. In slack-key, some or all of the strings of the guitar are loosened — "slackened" — from standard tuning to form a chord when strummed open, most commonly a major chord. The style typically features an alternating bass pattern played by the thumb while the fingers pick out the melody on the higher strings.

Origins

Guitars arrived in Hawaii in the 1830s, most famously with Mexican and Spanish vaqueros (cowboys, paniolo) brought to work on cattle ranches under Kamehameha III. When the cowboys departed, they left guitars behind, and Native Hawaiian musicians developed their own tunings and techniques. For generations the tunings were guarded family secrets, passed down within ‘ohana (families), and the music was played mainly at home gatherings rather than commercially.

Characteristics

Dozens of traditional tunings exist. The best known is "Taro Patch" (open G: D–G–D–G–B–D). Other families of tunings include Wahine ("woman") tunings, which contain a major seventh note, and Mauna Loa tunings, in which the top two strings are tuned a fifth apart. The music is often gentle and rolling, in keeping with its nickname from the Hawaiian word nahenahe — soft and sweet. Slack-key may accompany hula, chant and song, or stand alone as solo instrumental music.

Revival and recognition

The style was largely hidden from the public until the mid-20th century. Gabby "Pops" Pahinui's recordings from the 1940s onward brought slack-key to a wider audience, and players such as Leonard Kwan, Sonny Chillingworth, Atta Isaacs Sr., Raymond Kāne and later Keola Beamer, Ledward Kaapana, Cyril Pahinui and Makana carried the tradition forward. The Dancing Cat Records "Hawaiian Slack Key Guitar Masters" series and the annual Grammy Award for Best Hawaiian Music Album (2005–2011) raised its international profile.
