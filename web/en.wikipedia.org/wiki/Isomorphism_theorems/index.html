In mathematics, specifically abstract algebra, the isomorphism theorems (also known as Noether's isomorphism theorems) are theorems that describe the relationships among quotients, homomorphisms and subobjects. Versions of the theorems exist for groups, rings, vector spaces, modules, Lie algebras and various other algebraic structures. In universal algebra the theorems can be generalised to arbitrary algebras and congruences.

History

The isomorphism theorems were articulated in general form by Emmy Noether in her 1927 paper Abstrakter Aufbau der Idealtheorie in algebraischen Zahl- und Funktionskörpern, building on work by Dedekind and on Noether's own earlier lectures. Bartel van der Waerden's Moderne Algebra (1930) popularised her formulation.

Groups

First isomorphism theorem

If φ : G → H is a group homomorphism, then the kernel of φ is a normal subgroup of G, the image of φ is a subgroup of H, and the image is isomorphic to the quotient group G / ker φ.

Second isomorphism theorem

If H is a subgroup of G and N is a normal subgroup of G, then HN is a subgroup of G, H ∩ N is normal in H, and H / (H ∩ N) ≅ HN / N.

Third isomorphism theorem

If N and K are normal subgroups of G with K ⊆ N, then N / K is normal in G / K and (G / K) / (N / K) ≅ G / N.

Rings and modules

Analogous statements hold for rings with ideals in place of normal subgroups, and for modules with submodules; for vector spaces the first isomorphism theorem reduces to the rank–nullity relation between a linear map and its image and kernel. Some authors count a "fourth isomorphism theorem", the correspondence (lattice) theorem, which gives an order-preserving bijection between subgroups (or submodules) containing the kernel and subgroups of the quotient.
