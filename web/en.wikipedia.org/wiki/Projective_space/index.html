Projective space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

FieldProjective geometry, algebraic geometry
NotationP(V), Pⁿ(K), KPⁿ
OriginPerspective in Renaissance art; formalised 19th century

In mathematics, the concept of a projective space originated from the visual effect of perspective, where parallel lines seem to meet at infinity. A projective space may be defined as the set of lines through the origin of a vector space, or equivalently as a quotient space of the vector space minus the origin by scalar multiplication.

Given a field K and a vector space V over K, the projective space P(V) is the set of one-dimensional subspaces of V. When V = Kⁿ⁺¹, the space is denoted Pⁿ(K) and its points are given by homogeneous coordinates:

[x₀ : x₁ : … : xₙ], not all zero, with [x] = [λx] for λ ≠ 0

Examples

The real projective line P¹(R) is a circle; the real projective plane P²(R) is a non-orientable surface obtained from a disc by identifying antipodal boundary points. Complex projective space CPⁿ is fundamental in algebraic geometry, and the Fano plane is the finite projective plane with seven points.

Properties and uses

Projective spaces complete affine space by adding points at infinity, so that (in dimension at least 2) any two distinct lines meet in exactly one point. Projective transformations are induced by linear maps and preserve cross-ratios. In algebraic geometry, projective varieties are studied via homogeneous polynomial equations, and Hilbert's Nullstellensatz and the theory of schemes extend the framework. Projective space is also used in computer vision and graphics, where homogeneous coordinates represent points and transformations uniformly.
