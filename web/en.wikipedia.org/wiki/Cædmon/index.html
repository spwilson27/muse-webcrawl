Cædmon

Cædmon (fl. c. 657–684) is the earliest English poet whose name is known. An Anglo-Saxon herdsman attached to the double monastery of Streonæshalch (Whitby Abbey) during the abbacy of St Hilda (657–680), Cædmon was, according to the Venerable Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People, an illiterate layman who received the gift of song in a dream and went on to compose Christian verse in Old English on biblical subjects.

Bede's account

Bede relates that Cædmon, ashamed that he could not sing at feasts when the harp passed around, left the hall one night and slept in the stable. In a dream a man commanded him to sing "the beginning of created things". On waking he composed verses in praise of God the Creator, which he had never heard before. Abbess Hilda, recognising a divine gift, had him instructed in sacred history and admitted him to the monastic community, where he turned biblical narratives into "most sweet" verse.

Cædmon's Hymn

Only the nine-line Hymn survives as plausibly his own work; it is preserved in manuscripts of Bede's History, in Northumbrian and West Saxon versions, and is among the oldest attested texts in English:

"Nu scylun hergan hefaenricaes uard, metudæs maecti end his modgidanc..." — "Now we must praise the guardian of the heavenly kingdom, the might of the Creator and the thought of his mind..."

A group of biblical narrative poems in the "Cædmon manuscript" (Junius 11, c. 1000) — Genesis, Exodus, Daniel and Christ and Satan — was once attributed to him but is now regarded as the work of several later poets.
