Opposition effect

Opposition effect FieldAstronomy, optics, photometry Also known asOpposition surge; Seeliger effect Observed onMoon, asteroids, planetary rings, rough natural surfaces MechanismsShadow hiding; coherent backscattering
Opposition surge visible as a bright halo around the antisolar point on dew-covered grass (related to heiligenschein).

The opposition effect (sometimes known as the opposition surge or Seeliger effect) is the sharp brightening of a rough surface, or of an object with many particles, when it is illuminated directly from behind the observer — that is, when the phase angle approaches zero and the surface is seen at opposition. The term is most commonly used in astronomy, where a planet, the Moon or an asteroid brightens nonlinearly by a fraction of a magnitude as it nears opposition.

Mechanisms

Two mechanisms are chiefly responsible. In shadow hiding, when the Sun is directly behind the observer, the shadows cast by grains and rocks in a regolith are hidden behind the grains themselves, so the surface appears brighter than at oblique illumination, where many shadows are visible. In coherent backscattering, light waves travelling along reciprocal paths through a scattering medium interfere constructively in the exact backward direction, producing a narrow spike of brightness at very small phase angles.

Occurrences

The effect is prominent on the Moon, whose lack of atmosphere and powdery regolith produce a pronounced surge at full moon, and on Saturn's rings, which visibly brighten near opposition. A related terrestrial phenomenon is the heiligenschein, a bright glow around the shadow of the observer's head on dewy grass.
