Mesocarnivore

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mesocarnivore is an animal whose diet consists of 50–70% meat with the balance consisting of non-vertebrate foods which may include insects, fungi, fruits, other plant material, or other invertebrates. Mesocarnivores are a category within the spectrum of carnivory feeding strategies, alongside hypercarnivores (more than 70% meat) and hypocarnivores (less than 30% meat).

The term is also used in a second, community-ecology sense for mid-sized mammalian carnivores — as opposed to large apex predators — though the usage is debated, because body size and trophic role do not always coincide.

Examples

Typical mesocarnivores include many canids such as the red fox and coyote, mustelids such as martens and fishers, procyonids such as the raccoon, mephitids (skunks), herpestids (mongooses), viverrids, and some small felids such as the bobcat and ocelot. Many of these animals are omnivorous generalists that supplement vertebrate prey with fruit and invertebrates.

Mesopredator release

Mesocarnivores are central to the mesopredator release hypothesis: where apex predators such as wolves or large cats are removed, mesocarnivore populations increase ("release"), often with cascading negative effects on small prey species, including songbirds and rodents. Restoration of apex predators can suppress mesocarnivores either by killing them or through fear effects, a trophic cascade described in the literature since the 1990s (for example in studies of coyote–fox–songbird systems in fragmented Californian habitat).

Role in ecosystems

Because of their moderate size and generalist diets, mesocarnivores are often abundant and widespread, frequently living close to humans. They act as seed dispersers, scavengers, and regulators of rodent and invertebrate populations, and they are important hosts and vectors in the epidemiology of diseases such as rabies.
