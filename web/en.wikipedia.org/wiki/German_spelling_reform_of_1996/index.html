German spelling reform of 1996

The German spelling reform of 1996 (Reform der deutschen Rechtschreibung von 1996) was an international agreement signed in Vienna on 1 July 1996 by the governments of Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Liechtenstein and other German-speaking regions to simplify and regularise the spelling of the German language. The new rules took effect in schools and public administration in August 1998, with a transition period during which both old and new spellings were accepted running until 2005.

Main changes

  • ß and ss: the letter ß is used only after long vowels and diphthongs, while ss follows short vowels — hence Fluss ("river", formerly Fluß) but still Straße ("street").
  • Consonant doubling and triple letters: triple consonants in compounds are written in full, e.g. Schifffahrt ("shipping", formerly Schiffahrt).
  • Loanwords: foreign words were partially adapted to German patterns (e.g. Majonäse for mayonnaise was permitted alongside the original spelling).
  • Capitalisation and hyphenation: rules for capitalising nouns in fixed expressions were clarified, and syllable-based line division was relaxed.
  • Comma rules for infinitive and participial clauses were significantly simplified, making many commas optional.
  • Separate and compound writing (Getrennt- und Zusammenschreibung) was regularised toward more separate spellings.

Controversy and revisions

The reform provoked intense public debate in Germany. Prominent writers and newspapers, most famously the Frankfurter Allgemeine Zeitung, reverted to the old spelling, and opponents challenged the reform in court; the Federal Constitutional Court ruled in 1998 that the state could prescribe spelling in schools. Following continued criticism, an international Council for German Orthography (Rat für deutsche Rechtschreibung) was established in 2004, which introduced revised versions of the rules in 2004, 2006 and later years — restoring some traditional forms, especially in the areas of separate/compound writing and capitalisation. The reform nevertheless remains the basis of official German orthography as recorded in the Duden.
