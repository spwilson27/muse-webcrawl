Claustrophobia is a specific phobia characterized by an intense fear of enclosed or confined spaces. It is classified as an anxiety disorder and often results in panic attacks triggered by situations such as being in elevators, small rooms without windows, crowded trains, tunnels, or MRI scanners. The word derives from the Latin claustrum ("a shut-in place") and the Greek phóbos ("fear").

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include sweating, trembling, accelerated heartbeat, hyperventilation, nausea, dizziness, and an overwhelming urge to escape. Clinicians distinguish two fears underlying claustrophobia: fear of suffocation (restriction of breathing) and fear of restriction or confinement itself. Many sufferers avoid subways, airplanes, caves, and medical imaging procedures, which can interfere with daily life and health care.

Causes

Claustrophobia may develop after a traumatic confinement experience, particularly in childhood, or through learned association; it may also arise without an identifiable event. Research has linked it to individual differences in spatial perception — people who project a larger "near space" around themselves report more claustrophobic fear — and a possible genetic component associated with a single gene (GPM6A) in animal studies.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made clinically when the fear is excessive, persistent (typically six months or more), and causes significant distress or avoidance. The most effective treatment is exposure therapy, including gradual desensitization and virtual-reality exposure to enclosed spaces; cognitive behavioral therapy helps patients restructure catastrophic thoughts. Sedatives or anti-anxiety medication may be used for necessary procedures such as MRI scans.

Prevalence

Estimates suggest roughly 4% of the population experiences claustrophobia, with some studies reporting higher rates of milder fear of enclosed spaces; women are affected more often than men. Claustrophobic reactions are a common cause of incomplete MRI examinations.
