An auk or alcid is a bird of the family Alcidae in the order Charadriiformes. The alcid family includes the murres, guillemots, auklets, puffins, and murrelets. The family contains 25 extant or recently extinct species divided into 11 genera. Auks are found throughout the Northern Hemisphere, breeding on northern coasts and feeding at sea.

Description and behaviour

Auks are superficially similar to penguins — black-and-white countershaded plumage, upright posture on land, and wings used for "flying" underwater — but they are not closely related; the resemblance is an example of convergent evolution. Unlike most penguins, nearly all auks can fly, though they are clumsy in the air and on land. They are expert pursuit-divers, propelling themselves with their wings to catch fish, krill, and other marine invertebrates.

Species

Well-known auks include the Atlantic puffin, the common murre (guillemot), the razorbill, the little auk (dovekie), and the tufted puffin. The largest modern species is the thick-billed murre. The flightless great auk of the North Atlantic, the largest alcid, was hunted to extinction by the mid-19th century; the last two confirmed individuals were killed on Eldey, Iceland, in 1852.

Evolution and distribution

Alcids are a Northern Hemisphere family, most diverse in the North Pacific. Fossils indicate the family originated in the Pacific basin and spread to the Atlantic. They breed colonially on cliffs and islands, most species laying a single egg on bare rock or in burrows.

Conservation

Many auk populations are threatened by introduced predators such as rats and foxes on breeding islands, oil spills, gillnet bycatch, and changes in prey abundance driven by climate change and overfishing.
