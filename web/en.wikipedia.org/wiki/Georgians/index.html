Georgians

The Georgians (Georgian: ქართველები, Kartvelebi) are a nation and indigenous Caucasian ethnic group native to Georgia and the South Caucasus. They are the largest ethnic group in the Republic of Georgia, where they form roughly 86 per cent of the population of about 3.7 million, and there are substantial historical and diaspora communities in Russia, Turkey, Iran, Ukraine, the European Union, and the United States. Georgians speak the Georgian language, written in the unique Georgian scripts, and belong overwhelmingly to the Georgian Orthodox Church.

Origins and history

The Georgian nation coalesced in the early Middle Ages from the tribal groups of the ancient kingdoms of Colchis and Iberia. Georgians are linguistically distinct from their neighbours, speaking Kartvelian languages unrelated to Indo-European, Turkic, or Semitic families. The kingdoms of Lazica and Kartli were Christianised in the 4th century AD — by tradition in 337, through the mission of Saint Nino. The medieval Kingdom of Georgia reached its zenith under Queen Tamar (r. 1184–1213), spanning much of the Caucasus, before fragmentation and invasions by Mongols, Persians, and Ottomans. In 1801 the Georgian kingdom of Kartli-Kakheti was annexed by the Russian Empire; after a brief independence in 1918–21, Georgia was incorporated into the Soviet Union, regaining independence in 1991.

Subgroups and culture

The Georgian nation comprises regional and ethnographic groups, including the Megrelians and Svans (who speak related Kartvelian languages), and the Laz of the Black Sea coast. Georgian culture is noted for its polyphonic singing, the supra feast led by a tamada (toastmaster), an ancient winemaking tradition in qvevri clay vessels dating back some 8,000 years, and the martial Khinkali and Khachapuri cuisines. The Georgian Orthodox Church, autocephalous since antiquity, is a central pillar of national identity.
