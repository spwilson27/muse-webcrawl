Fair value

FieldAccounting, finance, economics
Key standardsIFRS 13; FASB ASC 820 (formerly SFAS 157)
HierarchyLevel 1, Level 2, Level 3 inputs

In accounting and economics, fair value is a rational and unbiased estimate of the potential market price of a good, service, or asset. In accounting standards — notably IFRS 13 and US GAAP's ASC 820 — fair value is defined as the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date. It is sometimes called mark-to-market accounting when applied to assets traded in active markets.

The fair value hierarchy prioritises observable market inputs over unobservable model assumptions.

Fair value hierarchy

Both IFRS and US GAAP rank the inputs used in valuation into three levels:

  • Level 1: quoted prices in active markets for identical assets or liabilities (for example, a share price on a stock exchange).
  • Level 2: inputs other than quoted prices that are observable, directly or indirectly — quoted prices for similar assets, interest rates, yield curves, and other market-corroborated data.
  • Level 3: unobservable inputs based on the entity's own assumptions, typically valuation models (such as discounted cash flow) using estimated data. Level 3 measurements involve the most judgement and disclosure.

Fair value versus historical cost

Traditional accounting records many assets at historical cost — what was paid for them. Fair value accounting instead updates carrying values to current market-based estimates, which supporters say gives investors more relevant and timely information. Critics argue that fair value can be unreliable for illiquid assets, procyclical in crises (forced write-downs depressing prices further), and a source of earnings volatility. These debates were prominent during the 2007–2008 financial crisis, when banks' holdings of mortgage-related securities had to be marked to distressed or non-existent markets.

Uses

Fair value measurement is required or permitted for financial instruments, investment property (under IFRS), biological assets and agricultural produce, share-based payments, assets acquired in business combinations, and impairment testing. In economics and investing, the term is also used loosely for an asset's estimated intrinsic worth, as in the "fair value" of a stock index futures contract relative to the cash index.
