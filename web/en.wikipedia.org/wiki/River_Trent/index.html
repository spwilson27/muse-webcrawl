The River Trent is the third-longest river in the United Kingdom, flowing 298 km (185 mi) through the English Midlands. It rises in Staffordshire, passes Stoke-on-Trent, Burton upon Trent, Nottingham and Newark-on-Trent, and meets the Yorkshire Ouse at Trent Falls to form the Humber Estuary. Unusually for a British river of its size, it flows northwards for much of its course.

Course

The Trent rises on Biddulph Moor on the Staffordshire–Cheshire border and descends through the Potteries conurbation of Stoke-on-Trent. It then turns east and north-east, joined by the Sow, Tame (near Alrewas), Dove (near Newton Solney, forming the county border with Derbyshire), Derwent (near Shardlow) and Soar (near Ratcliffe-on-Soar, its largest tributary). It passes the brewing town of Burton, the power stations of the lower valley at Ratcliffe and Cottam, and the city of Nottingham, where it flows under the multi-arched Trent Bridge at West Bridgford beside the cricket ground of the same name. Below Newark the river becomes tidal, and its tidal bore is known as the Trent Aegir. At West Stockwith and Gainsborough the valley opens into flat carr lands, and at Trent Falls the Trent joins the Ouse to form the Humber.

Navigation and trade

The Trent was navigable to Burton from medieval times and was improved by the Trent Navigation Company from 1783; the Trent and Mersey Canal (1777) paralleled its upper course and connected it with the Potteries and the Irish Sea. Even today gravel, aggregates and some freight move by barge to inland wharves. Nottingham's lace and hosiery, Burton's beer — brewed with gypsum-rich Trent valley water — and Stoke's pottery all depended on the river corridor.

Flooding

The wide floodplain at Nottingham, Newark and the Trent villages has flooded repeatedly, notably in 1795, 1947, 2000, 2019 and January 2024; extensive embankments, washlands and relief channels now protect the valley towns.
