The Iron Bridge is a cast iron arch bridge that crosses the River Severn in Shropshire, England. Opened in 1781, it was the first major bridge in the world to be made of cast iron, and it gave its name to the village of Ironbridge and the surrounding gorge, a cradle of the Industrial Revolution. Its success influenced the widespread use of cast iron as a structural material, and its survival and striking appearance made it an icon of the industrial age.

Background and construction

By the 1770s the ferry crossing at the Severn Gorge, between the ironmaking centres of Coalbrookdale and Broseley, was inadequate for growing industrial traffic. Architect Thomas Farnolls Pritchard proposed an iron bridge; ironmaster Abraham Darby III cast the components at his Coalbrookdale furnaces. The bridge comprises some 384 tonnes of iron in over 800 castings, assembled using carpentry joints — mortise and tenon, dovetails and wedges — rather than rivets or bolts, and erected in the summer of 1779. It was formally opened on New Year's Day 1781.

Later history

The bridge carried road traffic until 1934, when it was closed to vehicles and scheduled as an ancient monument; tolls for pedestrians continued until 1950. Movement of the gorge sides strained the arch, leading to major conservation work, including a reinforced-concrete inverted arch in the riverbed in the 1970s. In 1986 the Ironbridge Gorge became a UNESCO World Heritage Site. English Heritage carried out a £3.6 million conservation project in 2017–2018, repainting the bridge in its original dark red-brown colour.

Design and influence

The single semicircular arch spans 100 feet 6 inches (30.6 m). Decorative rings and ogee braces in the spandrels lighten the structure. The bridge demonstrated that cast iron could be used for large spans, and iron bridges proliferated in the following decades, though the material's brittleness in tension later led engineers to prefer wrought iron and steel.
