Hydrogen ion

A hydrogen ion is created when a hydrogen atom loses or gains an electron. The positively charged hydrogen ion (cation), H+, is just a proton when isolated from the most abundant isotope, protium; the negatively charged ion (anion) is the hydride ion, H−. Hydrogen ions are central to acid–base chemistry and to the definition of pH.

In aqueous solution

A bare proton is far too reactive to exist freely in water; it attaches to a water molecule to form the hydronium (oxonium) ion, H3O+, which is itself further solvated in clusters such as the Eigen cation (H9O4+) and the Zundel cation (H5O2+). Proton transport through water occurs largely by the Grotthuss mechanism, in which bonds rearrange along a hydrogen-bonded chain rather than a single ion diffusing. Acids are defined (in the Brønsted–Lowry theory) as donors of hydrogen ions, and the pH scale measures hydrogen ion activity; pure water self-ionises to give equal hydronium and hydroxide concentrations of 10−7 mol/L at 25 °C.

Biological importance

Gradients of hydrogen ions across membranes (the proton-motive force) drive ATP synthesis in respiration and photosynthesis, and hydrogen ion pumps regulate the acidity of blood, stomach contents and cellular organelles such as lysosomes.
