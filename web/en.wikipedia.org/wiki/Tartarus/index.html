Tartarus (Τάρταρος)

In Greek mythology, Tartarus (Greek: Τάρταρος) is the deep abyss that lies far beneath Hades, used as a dungeon of torment and suffering for the wicked and as the prison for the Titans. It is also, in the earliest sources, a primordial deity — one of the first beings to emerge, alongside Chaos and Gaia.

In early sources

In Hesiod's Theogony, Tartarus is so deep that a bronze anvil dropped from heaven would fall nine days to earth, and another nine days from earth to Tartarus. Around it runs a bronze fence with gates guarded by the hundred-handed Hecatoncheires, posted there after Zeus defeated the Titans and hurled them into the abyss. From Tartarus, by Gaia, sprang the monster Typhon, the last challenger of Zeus's rule.

Place of punishment

In later tradition — notably in Plato's myths of the afterlife and in Virgil's Aeneid — Tartarus became specifically the place where the wicked dead were punished: Sisyphus eternally rolling his boulder, Tantalus tormented by receding food and water, Ixion bound to a fiery wheel, and the Danaids carrying water in sieves. Plato's Gorgias and Phaedo describe judges of the dead assigning souls to Tartarus or the Isles of the Blessed. In the New Testament, the verb tartaroō ("cast into Tartarus") appears once, in 2 Peter, for the imprisonment of fallen angels.
