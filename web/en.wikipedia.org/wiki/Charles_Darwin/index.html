Charles Robert Darwin (1809–1882) was an English naturalist, geologist and biologist, widely known for his contributions to evolutionary biology. His proposition that all species of life have descended from a common ancestor is now generally accepted and considered a fundamental concept in science. In a joint presentation with Alfred Russel Wallace, he introduced his scientific theory that this branching pattern of evolution resulted from natural selection, in which organisms better adapted to their environment tend to survive and produce more offspring.

The Beagle voyage

Aged 22, Darwin joined HMS Beagle's second survey expedition (1831–36) as a gentleman naturalist. Fossils of extinct giant mammals in South America, and the distinct mockingbirds and finches of the Galápagos Islands, set him thinking about species change. Back home, reading Malthus on population in 1838 gave him the selection mechanism; he then spent twenty years amassing evidence — pigeon breeding, barnacles, seed dispersal — before Wallace's similar essay prompted joint publication in 1858.

On the Origin of Species and later work

On the Origin of Species (1859) sold out immediately and reshaped biology, geology's timescale assumptions and wider thought; Darwin carefully avoided discussing human origins there, addressing them in The Descent of Man (1871), which also presented sexual selection. His other books covered orchid fertilisation, earthworms' role in soil formation, climbing plants and the expression of emotions. Chronic ill health confined him largely to Down House in Kent, where he worked in his study and garden.

Legacy

Darwin was buried in Westminster Abbey near Isaac Newton. His theory, later fused with genetics in the modern synthesis and confirmed molecularly through DNA, is the unifying theory of the life sciences, taught and extended by universities and museums worldwide — including the Natural History Museum in London (nhm.ac.uk), which holds his collections.
