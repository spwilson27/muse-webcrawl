The cingulate cortex is a part of the brain situated in the medial aspect of the cerebral cortex. It forms an arching band (Latin cingulum, "belt") above the corpus callosum and is a central component of the limbic system, receiving inputs from the thalamus and neocortex and projecting widely, including to the hippocampal system.

Divisions and functions

The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is involved in error detection and conflict monitoring, attention, anticipation of rewards, decision-making, empathy, emotion and the affective dimension of pain. The posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) is a highly connected hub of the default mode network, implicated in internally directed thought, autobiographical memory and arousal; it is one of the first regions to show metabolic decline in Alzheimer's disease. The retrosplenial cortex contributes to navigation and memory.

Clinical significance

Abnormalities of cingulate structure and activity are reported in depression, schizophrenia, obsessive–compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder and chronic pain; surgical cingulotomy has historically been used for intractable pain and severe obsessive–compulsive disorder.
