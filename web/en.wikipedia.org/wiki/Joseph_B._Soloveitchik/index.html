Joseph Ber Soloveitchik (February 27, 1903 – April 9, 1993) was an American Orthodox rabbi, Talmudist and modern Jewish philosopher. Known to his students simply as "the Rav," he was the preeminent leader of Modern Orthodoxy in the United States for half a century and the rosh yeshiva of the Rabbi Isaac Elchanan Theological Seminary at Yeshiva University in New York.

Early life and education

Soloveitchik was born in Pruzhany (now Belarus) into the famed Soloveitchik rabbinic dynasty, scions of the Brisker method of Talmudic analysis developed by his grandfather, Rabbi Chaim Soloveitchik. He studied under his father, Rabbi Moshe Soloveichik, and earned a doctorate in philosophy from the University of Berlin in 1932 with a dissertation on Hermann Cohen. That year he immigrated to the United States and became rabbi of the Orthodox community of Boston.

Career and thought

In 1941 he succeeded his father as rosh yeshiva at Yeshiva University, where he ordained more than 2,000 rabbis over four decades and delivered celebrated annual lectures. His philosophical works, written in a phenomenological idiom, include Halakhic Man (1944), The Lonely Man of Faith (1965) and the essay collection Fate and Destiny. His public lectures and Talmud classes drew thousands, and he commuted weekly between Boston and New York into his eighties.

Soloveitchik shaped the ideology of Modern Orthodoxy, insisting on rigorous halakhic observance combined with full engagement in secular learning and society, and he declined the offer to become Chief Rabbi of Israel.
