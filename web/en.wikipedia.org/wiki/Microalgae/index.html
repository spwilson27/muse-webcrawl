Microalgae

Microalgae are microscopic algae, typically found in freshwater and marine systems, that are invisible to the naked eye. They are unicellular or simple multicellular photosynthetic organisms, ranging in size from a few micrometres to a few hundred micrometres. Together with other phytoplankton, microalgae form the base of most aquatic food webs and are estimated to produce a large fraction of the Earth's atmospheric oxygen through photosynthesis.

Diversity

Major groups of microalgae include the diatoms, with ornate silica cell walls; the dinoflagellates, many of which swim with two flagella; green algae (chlorophytes); golden algae; euglenoids; and the cyanobacteria, photosynthetic bacteria sometimes called blue-green algae. Tens of thousands of species are estimated to exist, of which only a fraction have been described.

Ecology

Microalgae are primary producers: they fix carbon dioxide into organic matter using sunlight, supporting zooplankton, krill, shellfish, and ultimately fish and marine mammals. Some species form harmful algal blooms, including "red tides", which can produce toxins that accumulate in shellfish. Others live symbiotically inside corals, where their photosynthesis supplies much of the coral's energy.

Uses

Microalgae are cultivated commercially in raceway ponds and photobioreactors. Species such as Spirulina (Arthrospira) and Chlorella are sold as nutritional supplements rich in protein and vitamins. Dunaliella salina is grown for beta-carotene, and Haematococcus pluvialis for the pigment astaxanthin. Microalgae are also used in aquaculture as feed for larval fish and shellfish, in wastewater treatment, and have been widely researched as a source of biofuels such as biodiesel, though large-scale fuel production remains economically challenging.
