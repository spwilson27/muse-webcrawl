Ultimogeniture

Ultimogeniture, also known as postremogeniture or junior right, is the tradition of inheritance by the youngest child or youngest son. It contrasts with primogeniture, inheritance by the eldest. In medieval England the custom, associated especially with Borough English tenure, meant that the youngest son inherited the father's land or burgage tenement.

Under ultimogeniture, the youngest son—often the one still living at home—succeeded to the family holding.

Rationale

Various explanations have been offered for the custom. The youngest son was the one most likely to still be living in the parental household and caring for aging parents when the father died, since elder sons had typically left to establish their own households or seek land elsewhere. Some legal historians have also connected the custom to the youngest child's longer period of dependency and to the idea that the elder children had already received their portions through apprenticeships or marriage settlements.

Distribution

Ultimogeniture is documented in parts of medieval England (notably in boroughs and in Kent's neighbouring regions), in some Germanic and Scandinavian customs, and among various peoples elsewhere, including some Mongolian and Central Asian pastoral societies, where the youngest son inherited the father's hearth and remaining herds while elder sons received portions as they married and moved away. The English custom of Borough English survived in a few places until the property-law reforms of 1925 abolished it.
