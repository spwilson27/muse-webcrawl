The Swahili Coast is the coastal region of East Africa fronting the Indian Ocean, stretching roughly from Mogadishu in the north to Sofala (in modern Mozambique) in the south, together with offshore islands including Zanzibar, Lamu, Kilwa and the Comoros. From about the 8th century onward it was home to a chain of prosperous, largely independent Muslim city-states whose merchants linked the African interior to Arabia, Persia, India and even China through the monsoon trade.

History

Coastal settlements grew as Bantu-speaking farming and fishing communities adopted Islam and long-distance trade from roughly the 8th–10th centuries, developing the Swahili language — a Bantu language enriched with Arabic vocabulary — and a distinctive stone-built urban culture. Kilwa, controlling the gold trade from Great Zimbabwe via Sofala, was described by Ibn Battuta (who visited in 1331) as one of the most beautiful cities in the world; the Great Mosque of Kilwa and the palace of Husuni Kubwa attest to its wealth. Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1498 opened two centuries of Portuguese domination and disruption, including the building of Fort Jesus at Mombasa (1593). In the 18th–19th centuries Omani Arabs, especially under Seyyid Said, who moved his capital to Zanzibar in 1840, dominated the coast and its clove plantations and caravan trades in ivory and enslaved people, before Britain and Germany partitioned the region in the colonial era.

Trade and culture

The monsoon winds structured commerce: dhows sailed south with the northeast monsoon and home with the southwest monsoon. Excavations find Chinese celadon and porcelain, Persian and Arabian pottery, glass beads and coins in Swahili towns — evidence summarised in the Swahili chronicles and in the great coral-stone houses, carved doors and mosques that survive in Lamu and Zanzibar's Stone Town, both UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
