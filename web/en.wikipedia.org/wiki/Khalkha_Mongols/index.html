The Khalkha Mongols (Mongolian: Халх, romanized: Khalkh) are the largest subgroup of the Mongol people in modern Mongolia. They have continuously been the largest group of Mongols in that region since at least the 15th century, and their dialect, Khalkha Mongolian, is the basis of the standard written and spoken language of the independent state of Mongolia.

History

The Khalkha emerged in the 15th and 16th centuries from Mongol groups settled in the region around the Khangai Mountains and the Orkhon and Tuul river valleys. Their territory, known as Khalkha, was divided among the descendants of Dayan Khan, whose grandson Gersenz was the progenitor of the Khalkha princes. Khalkha was organized into aimags ruled by hereditary khans: the Tüsheet Khan, Zasagt Khan, Setsen Khan, and later Sain Noyan Khan aimags.

Under pressure from the rising Manchu Qing dynasty and the Oirat Dzungars, the Khalkha khans submitted to the Qing at the convention of Dolon Nor in 1691. The Khalkha formed the core of Outer Mongolia, which declared independence in 1911 and became the Mongolian People's Republic in 1924.

Culture

The Khalkha traditionally practice Tibetan Buddhism, introduced through the influence of figures such as Zanabazar, the first Jebtsundamba Khutughtu, himself of Khalkha royal descent. Traditional livelihoods centered on nomadic pastoralism with the "five snouts" — sheep, goats, cattle (including yaks), horses, and camels.
