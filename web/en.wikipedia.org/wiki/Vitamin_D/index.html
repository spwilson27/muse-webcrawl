Vitamin D

Vitamin D
ClassFat-soluble secosteroids
Main formsCholecalciferol (D3), ergocalciferol (D2)
SourcesSunlight (UVB) on skin, fatty fish, fortified foods, supplements
DeficiencyRickets (children), osteomalacia (adults)
Active formCalcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D)

Vitamin D is a group of fat-soluble secosteroids responsible for increasing intestinal absorption of calcium, magnesium, and phosphate, and for many other biological effects. In humans, the most important compounds are vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) and vitamin D2 (ergocalciferol). The body can synthesise vitamin D in the skin when exposed to ultraviolet B radiation from sunlight, which is the major natural source for most people.

Vitamin D is produced in the skin from cholesterol under UVB sunlight and converted in the liver and kidneys to its active hormone form, calcitriol.

Biology and metabolism

Cholecalciferol made in the skin or ingested is converted in the liver to calcifediol (25-hydroxyvitamin D), the main circulating form measured in blood tests. The kidneys then convert calcifediol to calcitriol, the biologically active hormone, which binds the vitamin D receptor and regulates calcium and phosphate balance, bone mineralisation, and numerous genes. Vitamin D deficiency impairs bone mineralisation, causing rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, and contributes to osteoporosis.

Sources and recommendations

Few foods naturally contain much vitamin D; oily fish such as salmon and mackerel are notable sources, and milk and other foods are fortified in many countries. Recommended intakes vary by authority, commonly around 600–800 IU (15–20 µg) per day for adults, with a tolerable upper intake level of 4,000 IU per day set by the U.S. National Academy of Medicine. Excessive supplementation can cause hypercalcemia.

History

Vitamin D was identified in the early 20th century during the search for the dietary factor preventing rickets; Elmer McCollum named it the fourth vitamin in 1922. The chemical structures were worked out in the 1930s by Adolf Windaus and others, and food fortification largely eliminated rickets as a public-health problem in industrialised countries.
