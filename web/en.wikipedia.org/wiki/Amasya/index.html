Amasya (ancient Greek: Ἀμάσεια, Amaseia) is a city in northern Turkey, capital of Amasya Province, set in a narrow valley of the Yeşilırmak River between steep limestone mountains. Its dramatic setting — Ottoman wooden houses along the river beneath the rock-cut tombs of the Pontic kings — makes it one of Turkey's most picturesque historic cities. It is also the birthplace of the geographer Strabo and famed throughout Turkey for its apples.

History

Amaseia was a fortified capital of the Kingdom of Pontus; the five great rock tombs carved high in the cliff above the river are attributed to the Pontic kings of the 3rd–2nd centuries BCE. Strabo, born here around 64 BCE, described his home city in detail in his Geography. The city passed through Roman, Byzantine, Danishmendid, Seljuk, and Ilkhanid hands, and under the Ottomans became a princely governorship: several sultans, including Mehmed the Conqueror for a time, were raised or served as governors here, and the city was a notable centre of calligraphy and learning. Şeyh Hamdullah, the master calligrapher, was born in Amasya.

In the Turkish War of Independence, Amasya hosted the June 1919 meeting at which Mustafa Kemal and his colleagues issued the Amasya Circular, declaring that the nation's independence must be saved by the nation's own determination — a foundational document of the Turkish Republic. The Amasya Protocol followed in October 1919.

Sights

  • Tombs of the Pontic Kings — monumental rock-cut chambers in the cliff face, floodlit at night.
  • Yalıboyu houses — restored Ottoman mansions lining the river.
  • Amasya Castle on Harşena Mountain above the city.
  • Sultan Bayezid II complex (1486) — mosque, madrasa, and soup kitchen.
  • Ferhat water canal and the Ferhat ile Şirin legend, commemorated in a local museum and park.
  • Amasya Museum, holding the famous medieval mummies of Ilkhanid notables.

Economy and culture

Agriculture, especially the prized Amasya apple (a small, fragrant misket variety), sugar beet, and cherries, anchors the local economy, supplemented by growing heritage tourism. The city's June cherry and culture festivals celebrate its Ottoman past.
