Homicide Act 1957

Citation5 & 6 Eliz. 2. c. 11
ExtentGreat Britain (principally England and Wales; separate provision for Scotland)
Royal assent21 March 1957

The Homicide Act 1957 (5 & 6 Eliz. 2. c. 11) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that substantially reformed the law of homicide in England and Wales (and made related provision for Scotland). It restricted the death penalty to certain categories of "capital murder", abolished constructive malice, and introduced the partial defences of diminished responsibility and suicide pact, which reduce murder to manslaughter.

Background

The Act followed the report of the Royal Commission on Capital Punishment (1949–1953) and parliamentary debates in which abolition of hanging was narrowly rejected. Sydney Silverman's abolition bill having passed the Commons but been defeated in the Lords, the government introduced the Homicide Bill as a compromise: capital punishment would be retained, but only for murders considered especially grave.

Provisions

Section 1 abolished constructive malice, so that a killing in the course of another offence is murder only if done with the ordinary mens rea of murder. Section 2 created the defence of diminished responsibility — an abnormality of mind (later "abnormality of mental functioning") substantially impairing responsibility — which had been familiar in Scots law but was new to England. Section 3 codified and widened the common law of provocation. Section 4 made killing in pursuance of a suicide pact manslaughter rather than murder. Sections 5 and 6 confined the death penalty to capital murders: murder in the course of theft, murder by shooting or explosion, murder to resist arrest or escape, murder of a police or prison officer, and a second murder. All other murders were punishable by life imprisonment.

Subsequent history

The distinction between capital and non-capital murder proved difficult in practice, and the death penalty for murder was suspended by the Murder (Abolition of Death Penalty) Act 1965 and abolished permanently shortly afterwards. The diminished responsibility and provocation provisions proved lasting: provocation was replaced by the partial defence of loss of control under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009, which also recast diminished responsibility, while section 4 (suicide pacts) and the abolition of constructive malice remain foundational to modern English homicide law.
