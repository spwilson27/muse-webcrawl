Hypercalcemia

Hypercalcemia, also spelled hypercalcaemia, is a high calcium (Ca2+) level in the blood serum. The normal range is 2.1–2.6 mmol/L, with levels above 2.6 mmol/L defined as hypercalcemia. Those with a mild increase that has developed slowly typically have no symptoms; higher levels may cause abdominal pain, bone pain, confusion, depression, weakness, kidney stones and abnormal heart rhythm including cardiac arrest.

Causes

Most cases are due to primary hyperparathyroidism or cancer. Other causes include sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, excessive vitamin D intake, familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia, certain medications such as lithium and thiazide diuretics, and prolonged immobilisation. In malignancy, hypercalcemia most often results from secretion of parathyroid hormone-related protein or from bone metastases.

Signs and symptoms

The classic mnemonic for symptoms is "stones, bones, abdominal groans, thrones and psychiatric overtones": kidney stones, bone pain and fractures, abdominal symptoms such as constipation and nausea, polyuria and dehydration, and neuropsychiatric effects including depression and confusion. On an electrocardiogram, hypercalcemia characteristically shortens the QT interval.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the severity and the underlying cause. It generally includes intravenous fluids, and for higher levels calcitonin, bisphosphonates such as pamidronate, or denosumab; corticosteroids are useful in vitamin D-mediated cases. Treating the underlying cause, for example parathyroid surgery or cancer therapy, is definitive.
