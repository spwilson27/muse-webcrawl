Boiling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A pot of water at a rolling boil on a stove]

Boiling is the method of cooking food in boiling water or other water-based liquids such as stock or milk. Water boils at 100 °C (212 °F) at standard atmospheric pressure, forming vigorous bubbles of vapour; at higher altitude the boiling point falls, so foods take longer to cook. Boiling is one of the oldest cooking techniques and underlies dishes from boiled eggs and pasta to soups, stews and the British boiled dinner.

Related gentler techniques are distinguished by temperature: simmering keeps liquid just below boiling with occasional bubbles, while poaching uses still cooler water for delicate foods such as eggs and fish. A "rolling boil" cannot be stirred down — stirring does not lower the temperature of boiling water.

Physics

Boiling is a phase transition in which liquid converts to vapour throughout the liquid, not only at the surface (as in evaporation). Dissolved salt raises the boiling point only slightly at culinary concentrations; the salt added to pasta water is mainly for seasoning. In a pressure cooker, raised pressure lifts the boiling point to about 120 °C, sharply cutting cooking times.

In cooking

Vegetables may be boiled briefly and refreshed in cold water — see blanching. Boiling tough cuts of meat in stock produces flavourful broth and fork-tender meat, and boiling is essential for pulses, grains and dumplings. Because water caps the temperature at 100 °C, boiling gives gentle, even cooking but no browning.

Safety and other uses

Beyond cooking, boiling disinfects drinking water and sterilises equipment; a full rolling boil of about one minute kills most pathogens (longer at altitude). It is also used in jam-making, brewing and the preparation of tea and coffee.
