Semantic externalism

Semantic externalism (also called content externalism) is the view in philosophy of language and philosophy of mind that the meanings of words, and the contents of mental states such as beliefs, are not determined solely by what is inside a person's head, but depend partly on the person's physical and social environment.

Twin Earth

Hilary Putnam argued for externalism with the Twin Earth thought experiment (1975). Oscar on Earth and his molecule-for-molecule duplicate on Twin Earth both use the word "water", but on Twin Earth the colourless liquid in lakes is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ. Putnam concluded that the two Oscars mean different things by "water" despite being internally identical — "meanings just ain't in the head".

The social dimension

Tyler Burge extended the argument to social environment: a patient who believes he has arthritis in his thigh has that (false) belief partly because of how "arthritis" is used in his linguistic community; in a community where the word covered thigh ailments, the same internal state would express a true belief. This supports anti-individualism about mental content.

Implications and debate

Externalism challenges the Cartesian picture of privileged self-knowledge and interacts with debates about narrow versus wide content, mental causation and the extended mind. Internalists reply that content relevant to explaining behaviour must supervene on internal states, prompting two-factor and hybrid theories.
