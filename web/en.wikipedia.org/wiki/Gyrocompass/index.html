A gyrocompass is a type of non-magnetic compass which is based on a fast-spinning disc and the rotation of the Earth to find geographical direction automatically. A gyrocompass makes use of one of the seven fundamental ways to determine the heading of a vehicle and is widely used on ships. Unlike a magnetic compass, its reading is not affected by ferromagnetic metal in a ship's hull, and it points to true north rather than magnetic north.

Principle of operation

The heart of the gyrocompass is a rapidly spinning rotor mounted so that its axle is free to turn in azimuth but constrained by gravity, typically by a pendulous weight or a mercury ballistic system. Because of the Earth's rotation, a gyroscope whose spin axis is not aligned with the meridian experiences a torque (through gyroscopic precession) that drives the axis toward alignment with the Earth's axis of rotation. With suitable damping, the compass settles so that its axis points along the local meridian — toward true north — and oscillates only slightly around that position.

The main elements of a classical marine gyrocompass are the master compass, containing the gyroscope in a gimballed sphere floated in supporting fluid, and a set of repeater compasses driven electrically from the master unit and installed at steering positions, on the bridge wings and at other stations. Modern gyrocompasses use the same physical principle with refined construction, while fibre-optic gyroscopes and ring laser gyroscopes provide solid-state alternatives that find north by sensing the Earth's rotation directly.

History

The underlying phenomenon was demonstrated by Léon Foucault in 1852, but practical development had to wait for reliable electric motors. The German Hermann Anschütz-Kaempfe, motivated by a wish to navigate under the polar ice in a submarine, patented a working gyrocompass in the first decade of the twentieth century and founded the company Anschütz & Co. in Kiel. In the United States, Elmer Ambrose Sperry developed his own design, first installed on the battleship USS Delaware in 1911; Sperry's gyrocompass proved transformative for naval gunnery and navigation. Protracted patent litigation between the two inventors followed, in which Albert Einstein served as an expert witness for the German patent office.

Advantages and errors

Gyrocompasses offer two great advantages over magnetic compasses: they indicate true north, simplifying chart work, and they are unaffected by the ship's magnetic field, so they can be placed anywhere and their output repeated electrically throughout the vessel, fed into autopilots, radar and ECDIS. Their principal errors are the speed-and-latitude (course) error, which varies with the ship's speed, course and latitude and is corrected by a built-in corrector; ballistic errors caused by changes of speed or course; and a settling time that can range from under an hour to several hours after start-up. Gyrocompasses become less accurate at very high latitudes, where the horizontal component of the Earth's rotation weakens.
