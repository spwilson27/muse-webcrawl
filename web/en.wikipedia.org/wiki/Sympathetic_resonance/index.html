Sympathetic resonance

Sympathetic resonance or sympathetic vibration is a harmonic phenomenon wherein a passive string, body, or system responds to external vibrations to which it has a harmonic likeness. A classic example occurs between two tuning forks of the same frequency: when one is struck, the other begins to ring without being touched, because sound waves from the first excite the second.

Description

The phenomenon is a form of resonance in which a passive system is set in motion by an active one of the same frequency. A violinist, for example, can make an untouched string sound by playing the corresponding note on an adjacent string. The effect depends on the strength of the driving vibrations and on how precisely the natural frequencies of the two systems match.

History

Sympathetic vibration was known in antiquity and was described in connection with vibrating strings and bells. The Chinese se, a zither-like instrument, was noted for strings that sounded in sympathy with others. In Europe, the phenomenon was exploited in the viola d'amore, whose sympathetic strings beneath the fingerboard give the instrument its characteristic silvery resonance.

Applications

Sympathetic resonance has practical uses in musical acoustics, in the design of resonators in radio engineering, and in studies of structural vibration, where unwanted sympathetic vibration in bridges and buildings must be avoided.
