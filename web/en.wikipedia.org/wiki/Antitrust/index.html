Antitrust

Antitrust law, known in most jurisdictions as competition law, is a body of law that regulates the conduct and organization of businesses in order to promote competition and prevent monopolies and other restraints of trade. The term derives from the nineteenth-century American practice of using business "trusts" to consolidate control of industries.

Principles

Antitrust law addresses three broad categories of conduct: agreements between competitors that restrain trade, such as cartels and price fixing; monopolization or abuse of a dominant position by a single firm; and mergers and acquisitions that may substantially lessen competition. Enforcement combines public action by competition authorities with private lawsuits by injured parties.

History

The United States enacted the foundational Sherman Antitrust Act in 1890, followed by the Clayton Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act in 1914. Landmark cases include the dissolution of Standard Oil (1911), the breakup of AT&T (1982), and the Microsoft litigation of the 1990s. The European Union developed its own competition regime under Articles 101 and 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, administered by the European Commission, and more than 130 countries now maintain competition laws. Modern debates focus on digital platforms, vertical integration, and whether the traditional consumer-welfare standard adequately addresses the power of large technology firms.
