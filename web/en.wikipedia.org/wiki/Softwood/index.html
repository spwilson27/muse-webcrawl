Softwood

Scots pine, a typical softwood, showing its grain

Softwood is wood from gymnosperm trees — principally conifers such as pines, spruces, firs, larches, and cedars. The term is botanical rather than literal: softwoods are not necessarily softer than hardwoods (which come from angiosperm trees); balsa, a hardwood, is far softer than most softwoods, while yew is an exceptionally hard softwood.

Characteristics

Softwood has a simpler cellular structure than hardwood. It consists mostly of tracheids, which provide both conduction and support, and lacks the vessel elements typical of hardwoods; resin canals are present in many species. Softwood trees are usually evergreen, fast-growing, and produce straight trunks, which makes them cheap and easy to work.

Uses

Softwoods account for the great majority of the world's timber production — about 80% of all lumber. They are used for construction framing, joinery, furniture, flooring, paper pulp, and manufactured boards such as plywood, MDF, and chipboard. Important commercial species include Scots pine, spruce, Douglas fir, southern yellow pines, and Western red cedar, valued for decay resistance in outdoor use.
