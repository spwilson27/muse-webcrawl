Dietary fiber

TypeIndigestible carbohydrate fraction of plant foods
Main classesSoluble fiber; insoluble fiber; resistant starch
ExamplesCellulose, pectin, beta-glucan, inulin, lignin
Recommended intakeRoughly 25–38 g/day for adults (varies by authority)

Dietary fiber (spelled fibre in Commonwealth English) is the portion of plant-derived food that cannot be completely broken down by human digestive enzymes. It consists largely of non-starch polysaccharides such as cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin, together with lignin and resistant starch, and passes into the large intestine largely intact, where much of it is fermented by the gut microbiota.

Classification

Fiber is traditionally divided by solubility. Soluble fiber (pectins, beta-glucans, many gums) dissolves in water to form viscous gels that slow gastric emptying and blunt postprandial glucose rises; it is readily fermented, producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate. Insoluble fiber (cellulose, lignin, some hemicelluloses) adds bulk to stool and speeds intestinal transit. Resistant starch, found in cooked-and-cooled starches and legumes, behaves physiologically like soluble fiber.

Health effects

Higher fiber intakes are consistently associated in cohort studies with lower risks of coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer and all-cause mortality. Viscous fibers modestly lower LDL cholesterol by binding bile acids. Fermentation products nourish colonocytes and influence immune and metabolic function. Health authorities generally recommend adults consume at least about 25 grams per day, from foods such as whole grains, legumes, vegetables, fruit, nuts and seeds; typical intakes in Western countries fall well short.

History

The term was coined in 1953 by Eben Hipsley, and the "fiber hypothesis" was popularised in the 1970s by Denis Burkitt and Hugh Trowell, who linked low-fiber diets with the chronic diseases common in industrialised countries. Regulatory definitions differ: the Codex Alimentarius definition includes carbohydrate polymers with ten or more monomeric units not digested in the small intestine, with some jurisdictions also counting shorter oligosaccharides and isolated or synthetic fibers that show a physiological benefit.
