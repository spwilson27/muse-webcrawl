Bengal fire

Bengal fire is a term used in pyrotechnics for a steady, brightly coloured flame produced by a slow-burning composition, and by extension for handheld flares and theatrical effects producing such flames. Bengal fires were historically used for signalling, illumination of buildings and celebrations, and are named after Bengal, from where early accounts of brilliant pyrotechnic displays reached Europe.

Composition

A classic Bengal fire composition combines an oxidiser such as potassium nitrate or a chlorate, a fuel such as sulphur or shellac, and a metal salt that colours the flame. Red Bengal fires traditionally use strontium nitrate; green uses barium nitrate or chlorate; blue compositions, the most difficult to produce with purity, use copper compounds. The mixture is packed loosely into a paper or cardboard tube, or spread in open pans for architectural illumination.

History and uses

Bengal lights were popular in the 18th and 19th centuries for illuminating public buildings during royal celebrations and were standard equipment in stage theatrics for simulating fires and supernatural effects. In maritime signalling, coloured Bengal flames served as night signals before electric signalling lamps. In modern times the term survives especially in European football culture, where handheld red flares — called Bengalos in German — are used in supporter choreography, frequently in contravention of stadium regulations.

Safety

Bengal fires burn at temperatures exceeding 1,000 °C and cannot be extinguished with water. Chlorate-based mixtures can be friction-sensitive. Their use is regulated in many jurisdictions, and unlicensed possession at sporting events is an offence in several countries.
