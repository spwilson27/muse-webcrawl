Distribution of the Uralic languages

The Uralic languages form a language family of about 38 languages spoken by approximately 25 million people, predominantly in Northern Eurasia. The family includes the three most widely spoken national languages Hungarian, Finnish and Estonian, together with the Mari, Udmurt, Komi, Mordvinic and Sami languages of Russia and northern Scandinavia, and the Samoyedic languages of Siberia such as Nenets, Enets, Nganasan and Selkup.

The name "Uralic" derives from the family's presumed homeland (Urheimat), which scholars have generally located in the vicinity of the Ural Mountains. The family's proto-language, Proto-Uralic, is thought to have been spoken somewhere between roughly 7000 and 2000 BCE; estimates vary considerably.

Classification

The traditional internal classification divides Uralic into two main branches: Finno-Ugric and Samoyedic. Finno-Ugric itself splits into Finnic (Finnish, Estonian, Karelian, Livonian, Veps, Võro), Ugric (Hungarian and the Ob-Ugric languages Khanty and Mansi), Sami, Mari, Mordvinic (Erzya and Moksha), and Permic (Komi and Udmurt). Many Uralic languages are endangered, and several have become extinct in recent centuries.

Typological features

Typical features of Uralic languages include agglutinative morphology, extensive case systems (Finnish has about 15 cases), vowel harmony in many branches, negative verbs, the absence of grammatical gender, and a lack of the verb "to have" — possession being expressed through locative constructions and possessive suffixes. Word order tends toward subject–object–verb in the more conservative branches, though contact with Indo-European languages has shifted several languages toward SVO order.

History of research

The relationship between Finnish and Hungarian was first convincingly demonstrated in the late 18th century by János Sajnovics and Sámuel Gyarmathi. In the 19th century scholars such as Matthias Alexander Castrén undertook expeditions across Siberia to document the Samoyedic and other Uralic languages, laying the foundations of Uralic studies (Finno-Ugric studies) as an academic discipline.
