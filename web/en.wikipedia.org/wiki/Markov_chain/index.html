Markov chain

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A state diagram with circles for states and arrows labelled with transition probabilities]

A Markov chain is a stochastic process describing a sequence of possible events in which the probability of each event depends only on the state attained in the previous event — the Markov property, or 'memorylessness'. It is named after the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov (1856–1922), who introduced them in 1906.

A Markov chain is specified by a set of states and a transition matrix giving the probabilities of moving between states. Long-run behaviour is described by stationary distributions; under mild conditions (irreducibility and aperiodicity) the chain converges to a unique stationary distribution regardless of its start.

Types and theory

Chains may be discrete- or continuous-time, and states may be absorbing, transient, or recurrent. The theory connects with linear algebra: the stationary distribution is a left eigenvector of the transition matrix with eigenvalue 1.

Hidden Markov models extend the idea to states that are not directly observable; they became central to speech recognition and bioinformatics.

Applications

Monte Carlo methods using Markov chains (MCMC — Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs sampling) revolutionised Bayesian computation. Google's PageRank modelled a web surfer as a Markov chain. Chains also model queueing, genetics, board games, and text generation.
