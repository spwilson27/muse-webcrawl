Welser

The Welser family was a German patrician family of merchants and bankers from Augsburg, prominent in European commerce and finance during the 15th and 16th centuries. Alongside the Fugger family, with whom they were frequently compared and sometimes allied by marriage, the Welsers were among the most important financiers of the Holy Roman Empire at the dawn of the early modern era.

Rise

The family firm, the Welser Company, traded in textiles, spices, sugar and metals, operating factories and trading posts from Antwerp and Lyon to the Canary Islands and the Americas. Bartolomeus V. Welser (Bartholomew Welser, c. 1484–1561) became the firm's dominant figure, lending vast sums to Emperor Charles V and financing imperial elections and wars.

Venezuela venture

In 1528 Charles V granted the Welsers a concession to colonise and exploit the province of Venezuela, known in German as Klein-Venedig ("Little Venice"), as security for imperial debts. Welser agents such as Ambrosius Ehinger, Nikolaus Federmann and Philipp von Hutten explored the interior in search of El Dorado, founding settlements including Coro and Maracaibo. The venture proved brutal and ultimately unprofitable; after years of litigation and the deaths of its leaders, the concession was revoked in 1556.

Decline

Imperial bankruptcies, particularly the Spanish crown defaults of the later 16th century, eroded the firm's capital. The Welser company declined through the early 17th century and effectively collapsed amid the turmoil of the Thirty Years' War. Branches of the family, ennobled as Barons Welser von Zinnenburg, persisted in German lands.
