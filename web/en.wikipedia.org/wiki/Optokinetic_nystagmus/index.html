Optokinetic nystagmus

Optokinetic nystagmus (OKN) is a physiological eye movement that occurs when the visual environment moves relative to the observer — for example, when watching the landscape pass from a moving train. The eyes smoothly follow the moving scene (slow phase) and then snap back with a fast saccade to fixate a new part of the scene (fast phase), producing the rhythmic oscillation known as nystagmus.

Mechanism

OKN is driven by retinal image slip detected by direction-selective retinal ganglion cells, including the ON direction-selective cells that project to the accessory optic system. It works together with the vestibulo-ocular reflex to stabilise images on the retina during self-motion: the vestibular system dominates for rapid head movements, while the optokinetic system sustains gaze stabilisation during continuous rotation.

Clinical use

Clinicians elicit OKN with a rotating striped drum or moving striped tape. The response is used to test visual acuity in infants and non-verbal patients (an intact response implies a functioning visual pathway), to assess patients suspected of feigning blindness, and in neuro-ophthalmology, where asymmetric OKN can indicate parietal lobe lesions. Optokinetic stimulation is also used in research on motion perception and virtual-reality sickness, and as a therapy stimulus in vestibular rehabilitation.
