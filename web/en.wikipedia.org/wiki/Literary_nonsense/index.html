Literary nonsense (or nonsense literature) is a broad categorization of literature that balances elements that make sense with elements that do not, often using formal literary devices to create a sense of play, paradox and deliberate absurdity. Unlike mere gibberish, literary nonsense typically follows grammatical and narrative conventions while subverting logic and meaning; it "makes sense" of nonsense, in the phrase of scholar Elizabeth Sewell, by giving it its own internal rules.

History

Nonsense elements appear in folk tales, nursery rhymes and Elizabethan "nonsense verses," but the genre flowered in Victorian Britain. Edward Lear popularised the limerick in A Book of Nonsense (1846) and wrote poems such as "The Owl and the Pussycat" (1871). Lewis Carroll brought nonsense to its height in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865) and Through the Looking-Glass (1871), whose poem "Jabberwocky" packs invented words into perfectly regular syntax, and in the mock-epic The Hunting of the Snark (1876).

Characteristics

Common traits include neologisms and portmanteau words (Carroll's "chortle"), inversions of cause and effect, literal-minded responses to figurative language, parodies of didactic verse, and an equilibrium between sense and nonsense: as Wim Tigges argues, nonsense literature is characterised precisely by this balance rather than by the absence of meaning. Scholars such as Susan Stewart and Jean-Jacques Lecercle have studied how nonsense interacts with philosophy of language and common sense.

Later nonsense

The tradition continued in the work of Edward Gorey, Spike Milligan, Dr. Seuss, Shel Silverstein, Mervyn Peake and the songs and drawings of figures such as Ivor Cutler; nonsense techniques also influenced Dada, surrealism and absurdist drama.
