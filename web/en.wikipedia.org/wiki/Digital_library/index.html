A digital library (also called an online library or digital repository) is a collection of digital objects — texts, images, audio, video and datasets — stored electronically and made accessible through computer networks, together with services for organising, searching, retrieving and preserving them. Unlike a simple website of files, a digital library provides systematic metadata, persistent identifiers and a commitment to long-term stewardship.

History and examples

Precursors include Project Gutenberg (founded 1971), which distributes plain-text e-books, and early networked repositories of the 1990s. Large-scale digitisation of print collections accelerated in the 2000s: Google Books (2004) scanned tens of millions of volumes from partner libraries; the HathiTrust consortium (2008) preserves library scans; Europeana (2008) aggregates European cultural heritage; and the Internet Archive lends digitised books and archives the web. National libraries run major digital libraries, such as Gallica (Bibliothèque nationale de France) and the Digital Public Library of America (2013).

Issues

Digital libraries contend with copyright — much of the 20th-century record remains in copyright and accessible only in limited ways (search and snippet view, or controlled digital lending, itself legally contested) — and with the technical demands of digital preservation, metadata quality, and sustainable funding. They also enable new scholarship, from full-text search across millions of books to computational analysis in the digital humanities.
