A Persian carpet (Persian: فرش ایرانی, farš-e Irāni), also known as a Persian rug, is a heavy textile made in Iran for home use, local sale, and export. Carpet weaving is an essential part of Persian culture and art, and Persian carpets are among the most famous of all oriental rugs, prized for their intricate designs, rich colours, and fine workmanship.

History

The oldest surviving pile carpet, the Pazyryk carpet (c. 400 BC), found frozen in a Scythian tomb in Siberia, is thought by many scholars to be of Persian workmanship. Carpet weaving flourished under the Safavid dynasty (1501–1736), whose court workshops in Isfahan, Kashan, Tabriz, and Kerman produced masterpieces such as the Ardabil Carpet, now in the Victoria and Albert Museum in London and often called the world's oldest dated Islamic carpet.

Design and production

Persian carpets are woven on looms with a warp and weft typically of cotton (or wool in tribal pieces), with a pile of wool or silk knotted using the asymmetric (Persian or Senneh) knot, allowing very fine work of several hundred knots per square inch. Designs are organised around a central medallion, corner spandrels, and multiple borders. Motifs include the shah abbasi palmette, herati pattern, boteh (paisley), and the gul of tribal weavings. Regional types — Tabriz, Kashan, Isfahan, Heriz, Qom, Nain, Kerman, and tribal rugs of the Qashqai, Bakhtiari, and Baluch — each have characteristic palettes and patterns. Traditional dyes come from madder, indigo, and other natural sources, though synthetic dyes became common from the late nineteenth century.

Cultural significance

In 2010, the "traditional skills of carpet weaving" in Fars and Kashan were inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Iran remains one of the world's largest producers and exporters of hand-woven carpets, despite competition from machine-made imitations.
