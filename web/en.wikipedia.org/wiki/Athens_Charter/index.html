Athens Charter

The Athens Charter (French: Charte d'Athènes) was a 1933 urbanism manifesto produced by the fourth session of the Congrès internationaux d'architecture moderne (CIAM) and published by Le Corbusier in 1943. The Charter, which adopted a functionalist view of the city, proposed that urban problems could be solved by strict functional segregation and the distribution of the population among widely spaced, high-rise towers standing in parkland.

Content

The congress, held partly aboard the ship Patris II sailing from Marseille to Athens, analyzed 33 cities and distilled findings into prescriptions organized around four functions: dwelling, work, recreation and transportation. It called for housing in tall blocks with abundant light and air, separation of industry from residences, and hierarchically organized road networks.

Influence and criticism

The Charter profoundly influenced post-war reconstruction and planning worldwide, from Chandigarh and Brasília to European housing estates. By the 1960s and 1970s critics such as Jane Jacobs attacked its prescriptions as destructive of street life and mixed-use urban fabric, and later CIAM members themselves repudiated much of it.
