Dinner theater

Dinner theater (sometimes called dinner and a show) is a form of entertainment that combines a restaurant meal with a staged play or musical. Dinner theater typically relies on light fare — farces, comedies, murder mysteries and popular musicals — performed in the same room in which the audience dines, either before the performance or between courses. The format flourished in the United States from the 1950s to the 1980s, especially in suburban venues that packaged an evening out for audiences outside the major theatrical centres.

History

The concept has precedents in medieval court entertainment and the supper clubs of the early 20th century, but the commercial American dinner theater is usually dated to 1950s venues such as the Barksdale Theatre in Virginia (1953), which served meals in actors' dressing rooms between acts, and Howard Johnson's-backed theatres in Washington, D.C. By the early 1970s there were more than 150 professional dinner theaters in the U.S., employing thousands of actors, and performers between film and television work toured the circuit. Alvina Krause's Northwestern-based model at the Eagles Mere Playhouse also influenced the form. Declining attendance, rising costs and competition from home entertainment closed most venues during the 1980s and 1990s, though survivors and modern descendants — murder-mystery dinners, Medieval Times-style jousting shows, and theatrical restaurants — keep the format alive.

Characteristics

Venues seat audiences at tables of six to ten; servers frequently double as performers, and the show is chosen for broad appeal. Professional dinner theaters historically operated under a special Actors' Equity "Dinner Theatre" contract. Critics long treated the circuit condescendingly ("the mashed-potato circuit"), though it provided steady work and regional access to theatre.
