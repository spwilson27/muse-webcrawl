Beluga (sturgeon)

Scientific nameHuso huso
FamilyAcipenseridae (sturgeons)
RangeCaspian, Black, Azov, and Adriatic basins
StatusCritically Endangered (IUCN)

The beluga (Huso huso), also known as the beluga sturgeon or great sturgeon, is a species of anadromous fish in the sturgeon family Acipenseridae. It is found primarily in the Caspian and Black Sea basins and formerly in the Adriatic Sea. One of the largest freshwater fish in the world, it is famous as the source of beluga caviar, the most expensive of all caviars.

The beluga is among the largest freshwater fish on Earth.

Description

The beluga grows very large: historically, specimens over 7 metres long and weighing more than 1,500 kilograms were reported, though such giants are no longer found. Females grow larger than males and may live for more than a century, not reaching sexual maturity until 16–22 years of age. Like other sturgeons it has a largely cartilaginous skeleton, a heterocercal tail, and rows of bony scutes instead of scales.

Life cycle

Belugas are anadromous: adults live in the sea and ascend rivers such as the Volga, Danube, and Ural to spawn over gravel beds in spring and autumn runs. A large female may carry many millions of eggs, which give the species enormous reproductive potential where spawning grounds remain accessible.

Caviar and conservation

Beluga caviar, made from the roe of the female, became a luxury delicacy in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Intense fishing, poaching, and the damming of spawning rivers caused catastrophic declines, and the species is now listed as Critically Endangered. International trade is regulated under CITES, the United States banned beluga caviar imports in 2005, and hatchery restocking and aquaculture are increasingly used to relieve pressure on wild stocks.
