Logicism is one of the schools of thought in the philosophy of mathematics, putting forth the theory that mathematics is an extension of logic and therefore some or all mathematics is reducible to logic. Bertrand Russell and Alfred North Whitehead championed this programme, created by the German philosopher and mathematician Gottlob Frege. Frege's path to this project began with his work on arithmetic, and much of the subject matter of his Begriffsschrift (1879) was later absorbed into his Grundlagen der Arithmetik (1884) and Grundgesetze der Arithmetik (1893–1903).

Origins

The logicist project originated in Frege's attempt to show that arithmetic is analytic — that the truths of arithmetic can be derived from purely logical laws together with definitions. Earlier, Richard Dedekind had pursued a related reduction of the real numbers in his work on continuity and irrational numbers. The term "logicism" itself was introduced later; Rudolf Carnap used it in his 1931 writings, though the doctrine is usually traced to Frege, Dedekind, and Russell.

Principia Mathematica

Russell's discovery of the paradox that bears his name in Frege's system (1902) — the set of all sets that are not members of themselves — posed a serious problem for the naive logicist programme. Russell and Whitehead responded with the theory of types, expounded in the three volumes of Principia Mathematica (1910–1913), which attempted to derive all of mathematics from a small set of axioms using a ramified type hierarchy to avoid the paradoxes. The system was famously laborious: the proposition that 1 + 1 = 2 is only proved hundreds of pages into the work.

Criticism and decline

The logicist programme faced criticism on several fronts. The axioms of infinity and reducibility required by Principia Mathematica were not obviously purely logical. More decisively, Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that any consistent formal system strong enough to contain arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency and cannot be complete, undermining the hope of a single logical system capturing all mathematical truth. Nevertheless, logicism profoundly influenced analytic philosophy and the development of mathematical logic, and its legacy persists in neo-logicism, notably in the work of Crispin Wright, which seeks to revive Frege's programme using Hume's principle.
