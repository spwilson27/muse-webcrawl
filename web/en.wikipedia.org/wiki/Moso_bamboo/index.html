Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis, syn. Phyllostachys heterocycla var. pubescens), known in Chinese as máo zhú (毛竹, "hairy bamboo") and in Japanese as mōsōchiku, is a temperate species of giant timber bamboo native to China. It is the most important bamboo in Chinese forestry and the principal commercial bamboo species of Asia.

Description

Moso is a running (leptomorph rhizome) evergreen bamboo that can reach heights of 15–28 m (50–92 ft), with culms up to about 20 cm in diameter. Young culms are covered in a fine velvety pubescence (hence "hairy bamboo") and a white powder, turning glossy green with age. New shoots emerge in spring and can grow extraordinarily fast — up to about a metre a day under ideal conditions — reaching full height within two months. The leaves are relatively small and narrow.

Cultivation and uses

Moso occupies several million hectares in China, where it is managed both for culms and for edible shoots. The winter and spring shoots are a major delicacy. Mature culms supply construction timber, flooring, plywood ("strand woven bamboo"), scaffolding, furniture, paper pulp, and charcoal. As a fast-growing, annually harvestable crop that sequesters carbon, moso attracts interest as a sustainable material, and its forests are also planted for erosion control. Groves of moso — such as those at Arashiyama in Kyoto — are celebrated landscapes in Japan, where the species was introduced centuries ago.

Invasiveness

Outside its native range, moso's running rhizomes can spread aggressively into neighbouring land and woodland, and it is regarded as invasive in parts of the south-eastern United States and other warm-temperate regions. Containment with rhizome barriers is recommended where it is planted ornamentally.
