Orbit insertion

Orbit insertion is the spaceflight operation of adjusting a spacecraft's trajectory so that it enters a stable orbit around a planet, moon, or other celestial body. It is one of the most demanding manoeuvres in interplanetary flight: the spacecraft must slow down by precisely the right amount at precisely the right moment, or it will either fly past its target or enter an unusable orbit.

Description

A spacecraft approaching a planet on a hyperbolic trajectory has too much energy to be captured by the planet's gravity alone. An insertion burn — usually performed by the spacecraft's main engine near periapsis, the closest point of approach — reduces its velocity so that its trajectory closes into an elliptical orbit. Because of the Oberth effect, performing the burn at periapsis, where the spacecraft is moving fastest, makes the most efficient use of propellant.

Types

For Earth satellites launched from the surface, insertion is the final burn of the launch vehicle that places the payload into its initial orbit. Interplanetary orbiters, such as Mars Global Surveyor or Cassini at Saturn, perform a long main-engine burn after months or years of cruising. Some missions use aerobraking afterwards, dipping into the upper atmosphere over many orbits to shrink and circularise the orbit while saving fuel.

Risks

Insertion is a single-point failure for many missions. The loss of NASA's Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999, caused by a units error in navigation, occurred at Mars orbit insertion. Earlier, Mars Observer was lost shortly before its planned insertion in 1993.
