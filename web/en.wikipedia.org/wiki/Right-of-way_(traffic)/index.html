Right-of-way (traffic)

Right-of-way
DomainTraffic law
Applies toDrivers, cyclists, pedestrians, vessels, aircraft
Key instrumentsVienna Convention on Road Traffic (1968); national highway codes
Common rulesPriority to the right; pedestrians at crossings; emergency vehicles

The right-of-way is the legal right of a pedestrian, vehicle or vessel to proceed with precedence over other road users at a particular place or in a particular situation. Right-of-way rules determine who must yield when the paths of road users conflict — at intersections, pedestrian crossings, merges and roundabouts — and are a central element of traffic law in every jurisdiction. Importantly, right-of-way is something granted by law to be yielded by others; all users retain a duty to avoid collisions even when they nominally have priority.

Common rules

At uncontrolled intersections many countries apply priority to the right: a driver must yield to traffic approaching from the right. Stop signs and yield (give way) signs impose an explicit duty to yield, and traffic signals allocate right-of-way in time. Pedestrians generally have right-of-way at marked and, in many places, unmarked crosswalks, and vehicles must yield to emergency vehicles displaying lights and sirens. Rules differ between left- and right-hand traffic countries and in detail between jurisdictions — for example the four-way stop convention in North America, where the first vehicle to stop is the first to proceed.

Other modes

Analogous rules exist at sea under the COLREGs (a vessel on the starboard side has right-of-way in crossing situations, and power-driven vessels give way to sailing vessels in most circumstances), and in aviation, where the aircraft on the right has priority and balloons, gliders and airships have right-of-way over powered aircraft in a defined hierarchy.
