Kennewick Man, referred to by Native American tribes as the Ancient One, is the name given to the nearly complete skeletal remains of a prehistoric Paleoamerican man found on the bank of the Columbia River near Kennewick, Washington, on July 28, 1996. Radiocarbon dating showed the bones to be about 8,400 to 8,700 years old, making the specimen one of the most complete ancient skeletons found in North America.

Discovery

Two spectators at the annual hydroplane races, Will Thomas and David Deacy, stumbled on a human skull in shallow water at Columbia Park. Police recovered a nearly complete skeleton. Anthropologist James Chatters, called in to examine the remains, found a stone projectile point embedded in the pelvis and initially suggested the skull showed features he described as uncharacteristic of modern Native Americans, a description that led to wide speculation — later corrected by genomic evidence — about the man's ancestry.

Legal dispute and DNA study

The discovery triggered a lengthy legal battle between scientists seeking to study the remains and a coalition of Columbia Plateau tribes, who claimed the Ancient One as an ancestor under the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) and sought reburial. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, which controls the land, initially sided with the tribes; in 2004 the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled for the scientists. A genomic study published in Nature in 2015 showed Kennewick Man is more closely related to present-day Native Americans — particularly the Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation, who had provided comparative DNA — than to any other population worldwide. In 2016 Congress passed legislation transferring the remains, and they were reburied by the tribes in February 2017.
