The Gaze

In critical theory, philosophy and psychoanalysis, the gaze (French: le regard) is the awareness of an individual (or group) perceiving how they are perceived by others, and the power relations embedded in the act of looking. The concept has been developed by thinkers including Jean-Paul Sartre, Jacques Lacan, Michel Foucault and Laura Mulvey, and applied across film studies, feminism, postcolonial theory and surveillance studies.

Origins

Sartre, in Being and Nothingness (1943), analysed the Look of the Other as that which fixes a person as an object and provokes shame or pride. Lacan developed the gaze as an object of the scopic drive — the point from which the subject feels observed, distinct from the eye that sees. Foucault connected looking with disciplinary power in his accounts of the clinic and of Bentham's Panopticon.

The male gaze

In her 1975 essay "Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema," Laura Mulvey coined "the male gaze" for the way mainstream cinema constructs women as objects of heterosexual male desire, organising camera, characters and spectators around that perspective. The term became central to feminist film theory and everyday cultural criticism. Later theorists proposed counterparts and critiques — the female gaze, the oppositional gaze of bell hooks, and the imperial or colonial gaze in postcolonial studies.
