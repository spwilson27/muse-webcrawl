One-way speed of light

The one-way speed of light is the speed at which light travels from one point to another. Unlike the two-way (round-trip) speed, which can be measured with a single clock and a mirror, measuring the one-way speed requires two spatially separated clocks, and the result depends on how those clocks are synchronized. Because synchronizing them conventionally assumes the very speed being measured, the one-way speed is entangled with the conventionality of simultaneity.

Discussion

In special relativity the two-way speed of light is invariant and measurable, and Einstein synchronization defines distant simultaneity by assuming light travels at the same speed in both directions. Philosophers and physicists such as Reichenbach and Grünbaum argued that alternative synchronizations (with parameter ε ≠ ½) are empirically equivalent, making the isotropic one-way speed partly a matter of convention rather than pure measurement. Proposed experiments — slow clock transport, rotating platforms, and astronomical timing — have been analyzed extensively; the mainstream view is that no experiment can determine the one-way speed independently of a synchronization convention, though Edwards–Winnie test theories and the Mansouri–Sexl framework parametrize possible anisotropies and experiments tightly constrain them.
