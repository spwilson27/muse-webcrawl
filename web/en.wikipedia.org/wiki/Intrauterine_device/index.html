Intrauterine device

Type: Long-acting reversible contraception
Failure rate: <1% per year
Duration: 3–12 years depending on type

An intrauterine device (IUD), also known as an intrauterine contraceptive device or coil, is a small, often T-shaped birth control device that is inserted into the uterus to prevent pregnancy. IUDs are a form of long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) and are among the most effective reversible methods available, with failure rates below 1% per year.

Types

Hormonal IUDs release the progestin levonorgestrel, thickening cervical mucus, thinning the endometrial lining, and sometimes suppressing ovulation; they typically reduce menstrual bleeding and last three to eight years. Copper IUDs contain no hormones; copper acts as a spermicide and provokes a local inflammatory response hostile to sperm and implantation, and devices can remain effective for ten to twelve years. A copper IUD can also serve as emergency contraception when inserted within five days of unprotected intercourse.

History

Precursors date to the early 20th century, including Ernst Gräfenberg's ring in the 1920s. Modern plastic devices spread in the 1960s, but the Dalkon Shield scandal of the 1970s — involving severe infections and deaths — damaged confidence in IUDs, particularly in the United States, before modern designs re-established their safety record.

Risks

Risks include expulsion, uterine perforation at insertion (rare), and a slightly increased relative risk of ectopic pregnancy if pregnancy occurs. IUDs do not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
