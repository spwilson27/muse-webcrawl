Firebreak

A firebreak or double track (British English) is a gap in vegetation or other combustible material that acts as a barrier to slow or stop the progress of a bushfire or wildfire. A firebreak may occur naturally where there is a lack of vegetation or "fuel", such as a river, lake or canyon. Firebreaks may also be man-made, and many of these also serve as roads, such as a logging road, four-wheel drive trail, secondary road, or a highway.

Construction and use

Firebreaks are created by removing vegetation down to mineral soil, by ploughing or bulldozing a strip, or by applying fire retardant. During an active wildfire, crews build "hand lines" with axes, pulaskis and chainsaws, or use bulldozers to cut wider dozer lines. Firefighters often anchor a backburn or prescribed burn to a firebreak, deliberately burning the fuel between the break and the advancing fire so the wildfire reaches the cleared strip and dies out.

The effectiveness of a firebreak depends on its width relative to flame length, wind speed and spotting — the lofting of embers that can carry fire across a break. Under extreme fire weather, even wide breaks and major roads can be breached. Pre-constructed "shaded fuel breaks" thin trees and underbrush while retaining some canopy to suppress undergrowth regrowth.

Green firebreaks

In some regions, notably in China, strips of less-flammable evergreen trees and shrubs are planted as "green firebreaks" to segment forests and farmland. Research has also promoted fire-resistant landscaping around homes in the wildland–urban interface as a form of defensible space.
