Honing steel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A honing steel, also known as a sharpening steel, butcher's steel or sharpening rod, is a rod of steel, ceramic or diamond-coated material used to maintain the edge of a knife. Despite the common name "sharpening steel", a honing steel does not primarily sharpen: it hones the blade by realigning the microscopic edge, which bends and folds over with use, back into a straight line.

Function

As a knife is used, its very thin cutting edge rolls over or moves out of alignment, making the knife feel dull even though little metal has been lost. Drawing the blade along the steel at a consistent angle (typically about 15–20 degrees) straightens the edge and restores cutting performance. Honing does not remove significant material, so it can be done frequently — professional butchers steel their knives many times a day — but it cannot repair a genuinely worn or chipped edge, which requires sharpening on a whetstone or other abrasive to grind a new edge.

Types

Steels are usually 23–36 cm (9–14 in) long and may be round or oval in cross-section, with a handle and a guard. Traditional steel rods have fine longitudinal ridges; ceramic rods are smoother and harder, suitable for harder Japanese-style blades; diamond-coated rods are abrasive enough to remove metal and blur the line between honing and sharpening. Some modern steels are magnetised to collect the metal particles removed in use.

Use

The steel may be held vertically with its tip resting on a board, or held in the air as commonly done by chefs: the knife is drawn down and across the rod in alternating strokes on each side of the blade, heel to tip, with light pressure.
