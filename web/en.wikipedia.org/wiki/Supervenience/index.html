Supervenience

Supervenience
Supervenient properties (top) fixed by subvenient properties (bottom)
FieldPhilosophy of mind, metaphysics, ethics
Key figuresR. M. Hare, Donald Davidson, Jaegwon Kim, Terence Horgan
Core ideaNo difference at the higher level without a difference at the lower level

In philosophy, supervenience refers to a relation between sets of properties or facts. A set of properties A is said to supervene on a set of properties B if and only if no two things can differ in their A-properties without also differing in their B-properties. In slogan form: "there can be no difference at the higher level without a difference at the lower level", or "the A-facts are determined by the B-facts". Supervenience is not itself an explanatory relation — it states a pattern of determination without explaining it — but it is widely used to articulate non-reductive forms of dependence, especially in the philosophy of mind.

History and forms

The term entered philosophy through moral philosophy: R. M. Hare used it for the idea that moral properties supervene on natural properties — two situations identical in all natural respects cannot differ morally. Donald Davidson later invoked supervenience for his anomalous monism, the claim that mental properties supervene on physical properties even though there are no strict laws linking them. Philosophers distinguish weak, strong and global supervenience, depending on whether the comparison is made within a single possible world, across worlds, or between whole worlds. Jaegwon Kim's work systematised the concept and used it both to clarify physicalism and to press the "causal exclusion problem" for non-reductive physicalism.

Examples

Mental properties are widely (though not universally) held to supervene on physical properties of the brain and body: a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of a person would duplicate their mind. Biological properties supervene on chemical ones; the aesthetic properties of a painting on its physical arrangement of pigment; the truth of a computer's output on its hardware states. In ethics, moral supervenience remains a minimal constraint accepted by most theories.

Criticism

Critics note that bare supervenience claims are compatible with many rival theories — dualism can accept psychophysical supervenience as a brute fact — so supervenience alone does not establish physicalism or reduction. Others question whether certain domains, such as semantics (where content may depend on history and environment, as in Twin Earth thought experiments) supervene on intrinsic physical properties at all.
