The Notting Hill Carnival is an annual Caribbean street festival held in the Notting Hill area of London over the August bank holiday weekend. Led by members of the British Caribbean community, it is one of the world's largest street festivals and the largest in Europe, attracting around two million visitors to its parades of costumed bands, steelpan orchestras, calypso and soca sound systems, and street food stalls.

Origins

The carnival grew out of two streams. In January 1959, Trinidadian activist and journalist Claudia Jones organised an indoor "Caribbean Carnival" at St Pancras Town Hall in response to the racist Notting Hill riots of 1958 and the murder of Kelso Cochrane. Separately, community activist Rhaune Laslett organised a street fayre for local children in 1964; when the Russ Henderson Steel Band joined the procession, the street carnival was born, rooted in the traditions of Trinidad Carnival.

The event

Sunday is traditionally "children's day" with a shorter parade route, while Monday is the main adult parade. Dozens of mas (masquerade) bands in elaborate costumes follow floats through the streets, judged at points along the route. Static sound systems — a tradition developed in London, distinct from Trinidad — play reggae, dub, soca and other music, and stalls sell Caribbean food such as jerk chicken and curried goat. The roots lie in the five art forms of carnival: calypso, soca, steelpan, masquerade and sound systems.

History and challenges

The carnival has periodically faced conflict with police, most seriously riots in 1976, and recurring debates over safety, noise and crowd size; it was cancelled in 2020 and 2021 because of the COVID-19 pandemic and held as smaller events before returning to the streets in 2022. It remains a major symbol of multicultural London and an expression of Caribbean identity and resistance.
