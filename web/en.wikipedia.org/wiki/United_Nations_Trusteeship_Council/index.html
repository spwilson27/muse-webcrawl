United Nations Trusteeship Council

The United Nations Trusteeship Council is one of the six principal organs of the United Nations. It was established under Chapters XII and XIII of the UN Charter to supervise the administration of Trust Territories — former League of Nations mandates and colonies detached from enemy states after the Second World War — and to promote their advancement toward self-government or independence.

Trust territories

Eleven territories were placed under the International Trusteeship System: in Africa, British and French Togoland, British and French Cameroons, Tanganyika, Ruanda-Urundi and Italian Somaliland; in the Pacific, Nauru, New Guinea, Western Samoa and the Trust Territory of the Pacific Islands (administered by the United States as a "strategic" trust). Administering powers submitted annual reports, and the Council examined them, dispatched visiting missions and received petitions from inhabitants.

Completion of the mandate

One by one the territories attained independence or free association, from Ghana (incorporating British Togoland) in 1957 to Palau, the last component of the Pacific trust, in 1994. Having fulfilled its mission, the Council amended its rules in 1994 to meet only as occasion requires; it now consists of the five permanent members of the Security Council. The Trusteeship Council chamber in New York, designed by Danish architect Finn Juhl, remains in use for meetings.

Proposed new roles

With the trusteeship system complete, various proposals have suggested repurposing the Council — for example to hold the global commons (the high seas, atmosphere and outer space) in trust for future generations, as proposed by Malta and others — but no amendment to the Charter has been adopted. The Council continues to elect officers and convene briefly each year.
