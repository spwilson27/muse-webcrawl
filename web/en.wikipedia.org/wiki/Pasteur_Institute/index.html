The Pasteur Institute (French: Institut Pasteur) is a French non-profit private foundation dedicated to the study of biology, micro-organisms, diseases, and vaccines. It is named after Louis Pasteur, who first developed a vaccine against rabies and made some of his most famous discoveries there. Founded in 1887 and inaugurated on 14 November 1888, the institute has been at the forefront of the battle against infectious disease for over a century.

History

Pasteur funded the institute with donations raised internationally after the success of his rabies treatment in 1885. Its scientists made landmark discoveries: the identification of the plague bacillus Yersinia pestis (Alexandre Yersin, 1894), early vaccines, the discovery of sulfonamide antibiotics (Prontosil's active metabolite, in the 1930s), and, in 1983, the isolation of HIV by a team including Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Luc Montagnier, who received the 2008 Nobel Prize for the discovery.

Ten scientists associated with the institute have received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, including Ilya Mechnikov (1908), Charles Nicolle (1928), and François Jacob, André Lwoff, and Jacques Monod (1965) for work on gene regulation.

International network

The institute heads the Pasteur Network, an association of more than 30 institutes on five continents — the first, in Saigon (now Ho Chi Minh City), was founded in 1891 — which cooperate on infectious-disease surveillance and research worldwide.
