Soma (biology)

In biology, the soma (from Greek σῶμα, "body"; plural somata) has two related meanings. In cytology it is the cell body of a neuron, containing the nucleus and surrounding cytoplasm, from which the dendrites and axon project. In broader biological theory, the soma refers to the cells of an organism other than the reproductive (germline) cells — the "body" as opposed to the germ plasm.

Neuron cell body

The soma of a neuron, also called the perikaryon, houses the nucleus and most organelles, including the Nissl substance (rough endoplasmic reticulum) that supports the neuron's high rate of protein synthesis. Somas range from about 4 to over 100 micrometres in diameter. They are concentrated in the grey matter of the central nervous system and in ganglia of the peripheral nervous system. The soma integrates incoming signals from the dendrites, and the axon hillock at its junction with the axon is where action potentials are typically initiated.

Soma versus germline

August Weismann's germ plasm theory distinguished the immortal germline — cells whose descendants become gametes — from the soma, the mortal body cells that develop from them and die with the individual. Somatic mutations are not inherited by offspring, whereas germline mutations can be. The soma/germline distinction underlies concepts such as somatic cells in genetics, somatic cell nuclear transfer in cloning, and the disposable soma theory of ageing, which proposes that organisms allocate limited resources between maintaining the soma and reproduction.
