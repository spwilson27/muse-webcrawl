Ballestas Islands


A natural rock arch on the Ballestas Islands.

The Ballestas Islands (Spanish: Islas Ballestas) are a group of rocky islands lying off the Pacific coast of Peru, near the town of Paracas in the Ica Region, about 260 kilometres south of Lima. They form part of the Paracas National Reserve and are renowned for their abundant marine wildlife, which has earned them the nickname "the poor man's Galápagos".

Wildlife

The islands are home to large colonies of seabirds and marine mammals. Notable species include:

  • Humboldt penguins, a vulnerable species that breeds in burrows and crevices on the islands.
  • South American sea lions, which haul out in large numbers on the rocky shores.
  • Guanay cormorants, Peruvian boobies, and Peruvian pelicans, whose droppings — guano — accumulate on the islands.
  • Inca terns, Humboldt squid in surrounding waters, and occasional dolphins.

Guano from the islands was a valuable fertiliser exported in large quantities during the nineteenth-century guano boom; it is still harvested periodically under government management, with harvesting suspended during breeding seasons.

Tourism

Visitors reach the islands by boat tours departing from the Paracas pier, usually in the morning when the sea is calmer. Landing on the islands is prohibited to protect the wildlife; tours circle the islands for close views of the animals and the sea-carved arches and caves. On the way, boats pass the Paracas Candelabra (El Candelabro), a large geoglyph etched into the hillside of the Paracas Peninsula, whose age and purpose remain debated.

Conservation

The islands lie within the Paracas National Reserve, established in 1975 and covering some 335,000 hectares of desert coast and marine area. The cold Humboldt Current sustains the rich ecosystem, though El Niño events periodically reduce food availability and cause die-offs among penguins and sea lions.
