Cultural appropriation

Cultural appropriation
The use of one culture's symbols by members of another is at the centre of the debate
FieldCultural studies, sociology, anthropology
Contrasted withCultural exchange, acculturation, assimilation
Common domainsFashion, music, art, religion, cuisine

Cultural appropriation is the adoption of elements of one culture by members of another culture, in a way that is regarded as inappropriate, exploitative or disrespectful. The concept is most often applied where a dominant group adopts the practices, dress, symbols or artistic forms of a marginalised group — historically, a colonised or minority culture — without understanding, crediting or respecting their original meaning, and frequently for profit or fashion.

Definition and distinctions

Scholars distinguish appropriation from ordinary cultural exchange, diffusion and assimilation, which are normal features of human societies. Appropriation is said to become harmful when it involves a power imbalance, strips sacred or significant items of their meaning, perpetuates stereotypes, or allows the borrowing group to profit while the source community remains disadvantaged. Examples frequently discussed include the wearing of Native American headdresses as festival fashion, the commercial use of Māori tā moko designs by non-Māori, and sacred Hindu or Buddhist iconography used as decoration.

History of the concept

The term entered academic discourse in the 1980s and 1990s in postcolonial and critical theory, drawing on earlier critiques of colonial collecting and primitivism in modern art. It spread into public debate in the 2010s through controversies over Halloween costumes, fashion collections, music videos, restaurant branding and literary fiction, including debates about who may write stories from another group's perspective.

Criticism of the concept

Critics argue that cultures have always borrowed from one another, that drawing boundaries around cultural ownership is impractical and can essentialise identities, and that some celebrated art and cuisine arose precisely through mixing. Others warn that the label can be applied too broadly, discouraging cross-cultural curiosity. Defenders respond that the concept does not forbid exchange but asks for context, consent, credit and reciprocity — distinguishing appreciation from appropriation.
