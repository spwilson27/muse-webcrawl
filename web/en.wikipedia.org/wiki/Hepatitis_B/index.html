Hepatitis B

Hepatitis B is an infectious disease caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV), a partially double-stranded DNA virus of the family Hepadnaviridae. It is transmitted through exposure to infectious blood or body fluids, including perinatal transmission from mother to child, sexual contact, and shared needles.

Course of disease

Acute infection may cause fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, dark urine, and jaundice. The risk of progression to chronic infection is highest in infants (around 90%) and much lower in healthy adults (under 5%). Chronic hepatitis B, affecting an estimated 250–300 million people worldwide, can lead over decades to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.

Prevention and treatment

A safe and effective recombinant vaccine — the first vaccine against a human cancer — has been available since the 1980s and is given at birth in many countries. Chronic infection is managed with antivirals such as tenofovir and entecavir, which suppress viral replication, and pegylated interferon in selected patients.

History

The viral surface antigen ("Australia antigen") was identified by Baruch Blumberg in the 1960s, work that led to both the vaccine and a Nobel Prize in 1976.
