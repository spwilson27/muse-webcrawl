Jean-Baptiste Joseph Delambre

Portrait of Jean-Baptiste Joseph Delambre

Jean-Baptiste Joseph Delambre (19 September 1749 – 19 August 1822) was a French mathematician and astronomer. He is best remembered for directing, with Pierre Méchain, the measurement of the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona that defined the metre.

Life

Born in Amiens, Delambre lost his father young and nearly lost his sight to smallpox at age twenty, but developed a formidable memory and mastered Latin, Greek, and several modern languages. Turning to astronomy under Jérôme Lalande, he produced celebrated tables of the motions of Jupiter, Saturn, and Uranus — whose orbit he calculated shortly after its discovery — winning prizes from the Academy of Sciences.

The meridian survey

In 1792 the Academy appointed Delambre and Méchain to measure the meridian arc through France in order to define the metre as one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator. Delambre surveyed the northern portion from Dunkirk to Rodez while Méchain took the south. Working through the turmoil of the French Revolution, often arrested as suspected spies because of their instruments, they completed the survey in 1798. Delambre published the full account, Base du système métrique décimal (1806–1810), candidly documenting errors including Méchain's concealed discrepancies.

Later work

Delambre became perpetual secretary of the Academy's mathematical section, a professor at the Collège de France, and author of the monumental Histoire de l'astronomie (1817–1827). The lunar crater Delambre is named for him, and his name appears among the 72 inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.
