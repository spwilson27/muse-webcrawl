Indo-European ablaut

Indo-European ablaut
Gradese-grade, o-grade, zero grade (plus lengthened grades)
Survives inGermanic strong verbs, Greek, Sanskrit, others

In linguistics, the Indo-European ablaut (from German Ablaut, "sound mutation") is a system of apophony in the Proto-Indo-European language (PIE). An example of ablaut in English is the strong verb sing, sang, sung and its related noun song, reflecting vowel alternations inherited from Proto-Indo-European.

The grades

PIE roots could appear in several vowel "grades": the normal e-grade, the o-grade, and the zero grade, in which the vowel disappeared entirely, along with lengthened e- and o-grades in certain formations. The choice of grade depended on the position of the accent and on the morphological function of the form: roughly, an accented syllable took e-grade, an unaccented syllable might lose its vowel entirely, and o-grade appeared in particular derivations and perfect formations. For example, the root *ph₂tér- "father" shows e-grade in the nominative and zero grade in forms where the accent shifted to the ending.

Descendants

Ablaut is preserved most visibly in the strong verbs of Germanic languages (English drive/drove/driven, begin/began/begun), in Greek verb alternations, and in the Sanskrit grammatical tradition, where the corresponding grades are known as guṇa and vṛddhi. Comparative linguists reconstruct ablaut patterns to recover PIE morphology, and modern theories link the zero grade to the placement of the PIE mobile accent.
