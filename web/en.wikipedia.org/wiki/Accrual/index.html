Accrual

In accounting, an accrual is an amount recognised in the financial statements for revenue earned or an expense incurred in a period, even though the related cash has not yet been received or paid. Accruals are the foundation of the accrual basis of accounting, under which transactions are recorded when they occur rather than when cash changes hands, as required by both IFRS and US GAAP for most businesses.

[Image placeholder: an accounting ledger]

Types

Accrued expenses are liabilities for costs already incurred — such as wages earned by employees but not yet paid, or interest accumulated on a loan. Accrued revenues are assets for income earned but not yet billed or received, such as interest receivable. Accruals differ from deferrals, which involve cash received or paid in advance of the related revenue or expense (for example, prepaid rent or unearned subscription income).

Example

If a company receives electricity in December but pays the bill in January, accrual accounting records the expense and a corresponding liability in December, matching the cost to the period in which the electricity was used. This matching principle gives a more faithful picture of performance and financial position than cash accounting, at the cost of greater estimation and complexity — a trade-off exploited in earnings management, where discretionary accruals can be used to smooth or inflate reported profits.
