Janowska was a German Nazi concentration, forced-labour and extermination camp established in September 1941 on the outskirts of Lwów (now Lviv, Ukraine), in German-occupied Poland. Named after the nearby Janowska Street (present-day Shevchenka Street), the facility combined a forced-labour camp serving the Deutsche Ausrüstungswerke armaments works with a transit and killing site for the Jews of Eastern Galicia. It was liquidated in November 1943.

History

Following the German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941, Lwów's large Jewish population was subjected to pogroms and mass shootings. In the autumn of 1941 the SS established the camp around a requisitioned factory complex. Prisoners were selected from the Lwów ghetto; those deemed unfit for work were commonly taken to the ravine behind the camp known as the "Sands" (Piaski) and shot, or deported to the Bełżec extermination camp.

The camp's SS commandant was Friedrich Warzok. Survivors and postwar testimony described extreme brutality, arbitrary killings and public executions, as well as a camp orchestra forced to play during prisoner assemblies and executions — episodes later documented in testimony at trials and in survivor memoirs.

The Valley of Death and Aktion 1005

In June 1943, as part of Sonderaktion 1005, Jewish prisoners were forced to exhume and burn the corpses of mass-grave victims to conceal Nazi crimes. On 19 November 1943, during the liquidation of the camp, the remaining inmates of the Sonderkommando rose in revolt; most were killed, though a small number escaped and later testified about the camp.

Victims and legacy

Historians estimate that between roughly 35,000 and more than 100,000 people, overwhelmingly Jews, passed through or were murdered at Janowska and its associated killing sites. After the war the site was used by the Soviet authorities for other purposes, and memorialisation was limited for decades. In recent years, researchers, including work associated with the "tango of death" documentation and Ukrainian-Jewish heritage projects, have sought to map the mass graves and preserve the site's memory.
