Literati painting

Literati painting (Chinese: 文人畫, wénrén huà) is a style of Chinese painting produced by scholar-officials — the literati — rather than by professional court or commercial painters. Rooted in ideals articulated during the Song dynasty and codified by the Ming-dynasty theorist Dong Qichang (1555–1636), literati painting values personal expression, calligraphic brushwork, learning and moral character over technical virtuosity or faithful depiction.

Origins and theory

The Northern Song scholar Su Shi (1037–1101) is often credited with the founding idea: he wrote that anyone who judges painting merely by formal likeness "sees with the eyes of a child", and praised the poet-painter Wang Wei as one whose poems contained paintings and whose paintings contained poems. Literati ideals held that painting, poetry and calligraphy — the "Three Perfections" — were unified arts, and that a painting revealed the mind and cultivation of its maker.

Dong Qichang later systematised these ideas into his theory of the Northern and Southern Schools of painting, tracing a lineage of scholar-amateur masters and exalting spontaneity, expressive brushwork and monochrome ink over the polished colour work of professionals.

Characteristics

  • Predominantly monochrome ink (or light colour) on paper or silk.
  • Calligraphic brushstrokes used as the structural basis of forms.
  • Subjects favouring landscape, bamboo, plum blossom, orchid and chrysanthemum (the "Four Gentlemen").
  • Poems, inscriptions and seals integrated into the composition.
  • Deliberate amateurism: expressive awkwardness preferred to slick professional finish.

Notable practitioners

Key figures include Zhao Mengfu, the Four Masters of the Yuan dynasty (Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan and Wang Meng), Shen Zhou and Wen Zhengming of the Wu School in the Ming dynasty, and the "individualists" of the early Qing, such as Shitao and Bada Shanren. Ni Zan's sparse, desolate landscapes — a river, empty banks, a bare pavilion — are among the most celebrated expressions of the literati ideal.

Influence

Literati ideals spread to Korea, where scholar painting (muninhwa) flourished, and to Japan, where it developed into the bunjinga or Nanga school in the Edo period. The literati framework dominated Chinese writing on painting for centuries and continues to shape how Chinese art is understood and judged.
