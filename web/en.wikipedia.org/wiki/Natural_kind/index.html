Natural kind

Field: Philosophy of science, metaphysics
Paradigm examples: Chemical elements, fundamental particles, biological species (debated)
Key figures: John Stuart Mill, Hilary Putnam, Saul Kripke, Richard Boyd

In the philosophy of science and metaphysics, a natural kind is a category that reflects the actual structure of the natural world rather than human interests or conventions — in Plato's image, a classification that "carves nature at its joints". Chemical elements are the standard example: gold is a natural kind because all samples share an underlying essence, atomic number 79, which explains their common properties and supports scientific induction and laws.

Essentialism and the causal theory

In the 1970s Saul Kripke and Hilary Putnam argued that natural kind terms like "water" and "gold" are rigid designators whose reference is fixed by the underlying nature of samples — water is H₂O in every possible world — rather than by superficial descriptions. This "scientific essentialism" revived the Aristotelian idea of essences within analytic philosophy.

Debates

Biological species are contested: because species evolve and lack fixed essences, some philosophers treat them as individuals or as "homeostatic property clusters" (Richard Boyd) — kinds held together by causal mechanisms without strict essences. Others question whether categories in the social sciences, or even some chemical and physical categories, are natural kinds at all, or whether kindhood is partly pragmatic.
