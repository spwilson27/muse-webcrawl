The Council of Basel was an ecumenical council of the Roman Catholic Church convened by Pope Martin V in 1431 under the decree of the Council of Constance requiring regular general councils. It became the high-water mark — and the crisis — of conciliarism, the theory that a general council outranks the pope: the assembly at Basel resisted papal dissolution, deposed Pope Eugene IV and elected an antipope, while the pope's rival council at Ferrara and Florence achieved a short-lived union with the Byzantine Church.

Course of the council

Meeting in Basel from 1431, the council asserted its superiority, reissued the Constance decree Haec sancta, and negotiated with the Hussites of Bohemia, granting them communion under both kinds in the Compactata of Basel (1436) — a notable diplomatic success where crusades had failed. Eugene IV, who had tried to dissolve the council, was forced to recognise it in 1433, but in 1437 he transferred it to Ferrara to negotiate union with the Greeks; a minority refused to move and stayed in Basel, suspending and then deposing Eugene in 1439 and electing Duke Amadeus VIII of Savoy as (anti)pope Felix V.

Ferrara–Florence and the unions

The papal council, attended by the Byzantine emperor John VIII and Patriarch Joseph II, proclaimed union between the Latin and Greek churches in the decree Laetentur Caeli on 6 July 1439 in Florence Cathedral, and later unions with Armenians and Copts — all repudiated in the East. The rump at Basel dwindled, moved to Lausanne, and dissolved in 1449 after Felix V abdicated, ending the last serious conciliar challenge to papal monarchy.

Significance

The council issued reform decrees on clerical discipline and annates, and its failure discredited conciliarism while spurring Gallicanism (the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges, 1438, adopted many of its reforms in France).
