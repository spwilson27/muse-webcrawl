Vietnam War in film

The Vietnam War has been depicted in cinema during the conflict and extensively afterwards, most prominently in American films of the late 1970s and 1980s that reassessed the war's meaning, as well as in Vietnamese and other national cinemas presenting different perspectives.

During the war

Hollywood largely avoided the subject while the war was ongoing; the notable exception was The Green Berets (1968), starring John Wayne, a pro-war film made with U.S. military cooperation. Documentary coverage, including television news and films such as Hearts and Minds (1974), played a larger role in shaping public perception during the conflict itself.

The post-war wave

After 1975, a major cycle of films confronted the war directly: The Deer Hunter (1978) and Francis Ford Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979) became defining works; the 1980s brought Platoon (1986), directed by veteran Oliver Stone and winner of the Academy Award for Best Picture, Stanley Kubrick's Full Metal Jacket (1987), Hamburger Hill (1987), Good Morning, Vietnam (1987) and Born on the Fourth of July (1989). Related films addressed the prisoner-of-war theme (Rambo: First Blood Part II, 1985) and later works such as We Were Soldiers (2002) adopted a more traditional combat-film approach.

Other perspectives

Vietnamese cinema has treated the war (known in Vietnam as the American War) from the standpoint of its soldiers and civilians, in films emphasising sacrifice and reunification. Australian and other allied perspectives appear in works such as The Odd Angry Shot (1979) and Danger Close (2019). Scholars continue to debate how the American films frame Vietnamese characters and the war's politics.
