Shutter speed

Shutter speed or exposure time is the length of time a camera's shutter remains open, exposing the film or image sensor to light. Together with the lens aperture (f-number) and the sensitivity of the recording medium (ISO), shutter speed determines the total exposure of a photograph. It also controls the rendering of motion: faster speeds freeze movement, while slower speeds blur it.

Measurement and scale

Shutter speeds are quoted in seconds and fractions of seconds, conventionally in a doubling scale: 1 s, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/15, 1/30, 1/60, 1/125, 1/250, 1/500, 1/1000, and faster. Each step halves the light admitted and is called one stop. Mechanical focal-plane shutters commonly reach 1/4000 or 1/8000 of a second; electronic shutters in modern cameras can be far faster. Long exposures of seconds, minutes, or even hours are used in night and astrophotography, often with the shutter held open in "bulb" mode.

Motion effects

A shutter speed of roughly 1/250 s or faster will freeze walking people; sports and wildlife photographers often use 1/1000 s and above. Slower speeds produce deliberate motion blur: waterfalls take on a silky appearance, car lights draw trails, and the technique of panning — tracking a moving subject — keeps the subject sharp against a streaked background. Below a threshold related to the lens's focal length, camera shake from the photographer's hands blurs the whole image; the reciprocal rule suggests hand-holding no slower than the inverse of the effective focal length (for example, 1/50 s with a 50 mm lens), though modern image stabilization extends this by several stops.

Relation to exposure

Shutter speed forms one side of the exposure triangle. Under the law of reciprocity, halving the shutter time can be compensated by opening the aperture one stop or doubling the ISO, keeping total exposure constant while changing the creative rendering of motion and depth of field.
