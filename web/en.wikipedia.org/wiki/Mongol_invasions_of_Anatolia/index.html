Mongol invasions of Anatolia

Date1241–1243 (initial conquest); Mongol suzerainty until 1335
LocationAnatolia (present-day Turkey)
ResultMongol victory; Sultanate of Rum becomes an Ilkhanate vassal

The Mongol invasions of Anatolia occurred in the 13th century, when Mongol armies under commanders such as Baiju invaded the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum and reduced it to a vassal state, first of the Mongol Empire and then of the Ilkhanate. The invasions reshaped the political map of Anatolia, weakening Seljuk central authority and setting the stage for the rise of the Anatolian beyliks, among them the Ottoman beylik.

Background

Following their conquests of Persia and the Caucasus, Mongol forces under Chormaqan and then Baiju pressed westward. The Sultanate of Rum, weakened by the Babai revolt of 1240 and by court factionalism, faced the Mongols with a divided command under Sultan Kaykhusraw II.

The campaign of 1242–1243

In the winter of 1242–43, Baiju's army besieged and captured Erzurum. Advancing into central Anatolia, the Mongols met the Seljuk army and its Christian and Frankish mercenaries at the Battle of Köse Dağ on 26 June 1243. The Seljuk vanguard charged prematurely and was destroyed; the sultan fled, and the Mongols won a decisive victory. Sivas and Kayseri were taken and plundered, and although Konya was spared through negotiation, Kaykhusraw sued for peace, agreeing to pay a heavy annual tribute.

Mongol suzerainty

Anatolia remained under Mongol oversight, with resident commissioners and garrisons; after 1256 it fell within the sphere of Hülegü's Ilkhanate. Mongol interference in Seljuk succession, punitive expeditions in the 1270s — including the Battle of Elbistan (1277) against the Mamluks — and heavy taxation progressively hollowed out the sultanate. Seljuk sultans continued to reign as clients until the early 14th century, while effective power shifted to Mongol governors and then to semi-independent Turkish beyliks.

Consequences

The invasions accelerated Turkmen migration into western Anatolia, reoriented trade toward Tabriz and the Black Sea ports, and fragmented the region politically. From this fragmentation emerged the frontier principalities from which the Ottoman Empire would later grow.
