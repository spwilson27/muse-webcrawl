An outliner (or outline processor) is a specialised text editor that organises text in a hierarchy — a tree of headings and sub-entries that can be expanded, collapsed, rearranged, and restructured. Outliners are used for note-taking, writing and planning documents, task and project management, brainstorming, and organising any information that benefits from hierarchical structure.

Features

The defining operations of an outliner operate on the tree as a whole rather than on characters: promoting and demoting entries between levels, moving subtrees up and down, "hoisting" a branch so it fills the window for focused work, and collapsing branches to show only the top-level structure of a large document. Many outliners add attachments and notes to headings, multiple columns (making them into simple databases), inline calculations, and cloning, in which one node appears in several places at once.

History

The outliner emerged from 1960s work on hypertext and augmentation: Douglas Engelbart's NLS system included outline views, demonstrated in the 1968 "Mother of All Demos". The first commercial outliner for personal computers, ThinkTank (1983) by Dave Winer's Living Videotext, ran on the Apple II and IBM PC; its successors ThinkTank for Macintosh and MORE (1986) added presentation generation from outlines. GrandView, Ecco Pro, and the outliner modes of word processors carried the concept through the 1990s, and Winer also created the OPML file format that became the standard interchange format for outlines and feed subscription lists.

Modern outliners

Contemporary examples range from dedicated applications — OmniOutliner on Apple platforms, Emacs Org-mode, and web-based tools such as Workflowy and Dynalist, whose bullet-based interfaces blur into personal knowledge management — to the outline and navigation panes embedded in most word processors, and to "outliner" panels in note-taking apps. The folding editors used by programmers are a related variant operating on source code structure.
