Electroosmotic flow


Electroosmotic flow in a charged capillary

Electroosmotic flow (or electro-osmotic flow, abbreviated EOF; synonymous with electroosmosis or electroendosmosis) is the motion of liquid induced by an applied potential across a porous material, capillary tube, membrane, microchannel, or other fluid conduit. Because electroosmotic flow velocities are independent of conduit size, as long as the electrical double layer is much smaller than the characteristic length scale of the channel, it becomes increasingly important in microfluidic systems with small channels.

Mechanism

Most surfaces, such as the silica walls of a capillary, carry a fixed charge when in contact with an aqueous solution. Counter-ions accumulate near the wall in an electrical double layer. When an axial electric field is applied, the mobile net charge in the diffuse part of the double layer experiences a force and drags the bulk liquid with it. The resulting velocity profile is nearly a flat "plug" profile, in contrast to the parabolic profile of pressure-driven flow — an advantage in capillary electrophoresis, where plug flow reduces band broadening.

Description

The electroosmotic velocity is proportional to the applied field and to the zeta potential of the wall, and inversely proportional to the viscosity of the solution, as described by the Helmholtz–Smoluchowski relation. Flow direction depends on the sign of the wall charge: with negatively charged walls, flow moves toward the cathode.

Applications

EOF underlies capillary electrophoresis and many lab-on-a-chip devices, electroosmotic pumps that have no moving parts, soil remediation techniques, and dewatering of clays and sludges.
