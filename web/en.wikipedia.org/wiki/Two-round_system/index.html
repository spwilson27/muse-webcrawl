Two-round system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The two-round system (TRS), also called runoff voting, second ballot or ballotage, is a voting method used to elect a single winner, in which the top two candidates from a first round advance to a second round if no candidate achieves a specified share (usually an absolute majority) in the first; voters who supported eliminated candidates may re-choose. It is the world's most common method for presidential elections and for legislative elections in France and many francophone and Latin American states.

Behaviour

Runoffs change political strategy: the first round licenses expressive voting and candidate proliferation ("vote with your heart, then with your head", as the French saying has it), while the inter-round period becomes an arena of endorsements, withdrawals and coalition-building (in French legislative elections, triangular three-way runoffs persist where a third candidate clears the qualification threshold). The system guarantees the winner a second-round majority of votes cast (though not of first-round preferences), protects against plurality winners with low support, and can dramatically reverse expectations (outsiders surging or collapsing between rounds). Critics note cost, turnout decline in round two, and the arbitrariness of top-two cutoffs in close multi-candidate fields — the same squeeze instant-runoff voting tries to compress into a single ranked ballot.
