Codetermination

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Codetermination (German: Mitbestimmung) is the practice of workers having a formal role in the management of companies — in Germany, through works councils at establishment level (Works Constitution Act) and employee representatives on supervisory boards: parity representation in coal and steel since 1951, and one-third to (quasi-)parity board representation under the Codetermination Act 1976 for companies above 2,000 employees (2,000+ : half the supervisory board seats, with a shareholder-side chair holding a casting vote — hence “quasi-parity”). The Constitutional Court upheld the 1976 Act in 1979.

Codetermination is the institutional centrepiece of the German model of capitalism (coordinated market economy): it is credited by supporters with long-termism, training investment, industrial peace and information flow to boards, and criticised by some economists and shareholders for slowing restructuring and diluting accountability. Weaker board-representation variants exist across much of the EU (a majority of member states have some form), and the European Company (SE) statute protects participation rights on conversion. Anglo-American systems, built on shareholder primacy, repeatedly debate importing works councils or board seats — proposals that surface in UK and US politics without (so far) adoption at German scale.
