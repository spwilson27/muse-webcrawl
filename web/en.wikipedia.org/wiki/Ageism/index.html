Ageism (also spelled agism) is stereotyping, prejudice, or discrimination against individuals or groups on the basis of their age. The term was coined in 1969 by the American gerontologist Robert Neil Butler, who modelled it on "sexism" and "racism". Although ageism can be directed at any age group — adolescents and young adults are sometimes dismissed as inexperienced — it most commonly describes bias against older people, who may be stereotyped as frail, rigid or burdensome.

Forms and effects

Ageism operates at three levels: institutional (mandatory retirement ages, age limits in policies), interpersonal (patronising speech, exclusion), and internalised, when older people absorb negative stereotypes and limit themselves — a process research links to worse health, slower recovery and even shorter life expectancy. The World Health Organization's 2021 Global Report on Ageism estimated that one in two people worldwide hold ageist attitudes, and that ageism costs societies billions through poorer health outcomes and lost employment.

Law and policy

Many jurisdictions prohibit age discrimination: the US Age Discrimination in Employment Act (1967) protects workers aged 40 and over, and the UK's Equality Act 2010 covers age across employment and services. Compulsory retirement ages have been abolished in several countries, though debates continue over age-based rules in driving, medicine and politics. Campaigns such as the WHO Decade of Healthy Ageing aim to combat ageism through education, intergenerational contact and legal reform.
