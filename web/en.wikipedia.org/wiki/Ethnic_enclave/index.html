Ethnic enclave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ethnic enclave is a geographic area with a high concentration of an ethnic group, together with culturally or ethnically distinctive businesses, institutions and social networks. Classic examples include Chinatowns, Little Italys and Koreatowns in North American cities, though the concept is used in urban sociology and geography worldwide.

Formation

Enclaves typically form through chain migration: earlier migrants provide newcomers with housing, jobs, credit and information, lowering the costs of settlement. Spatial concentration supports ethnic businesses serving co-ethnic customers in a familiar language, religious institutions, media and mutual aid associations, and can protect residents from discrimination in the wider labour and housing markets.

Enclave economy debate

Sociologists Alejandro Portes and others developed the enclave economy hypothesis: working within a prosperous enclave economy can give immigrants returns to their human capital — wages comparable to the primary labour market — and paths to self-employment unavailable to them in the secondary labour market. Critics respond that enclave jobs may instead confine workers to low-wage, exploitative niches dependent on co-ethnic solidarity. Related concepts include the ethnoburb, a multiethnic suburban cluster of ethnic residences and businesses, and the distinction between enclaves chosen as a springboard and ghettos imposed by exclusion and segregation.
