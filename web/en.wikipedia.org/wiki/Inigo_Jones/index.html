Inigo Jones (15 July 1573 – 21 June 1652) was the first significant architect in England in the early modern period and the first to apply the classical rules of proportion and symmetry to British buildings. As the most notable architect in England, Jones was the first person to introduce the classical architecture of Rome and the Italian Renaissance to Britain. He also made major contributions to stage design in his role producing masques for the Stuart court, most famously in collaboration — and eventual rivalry — with playwright Ben Jonson.

Career

The son of a London clothworker, Jones travelled in Italy around 1598–1603 and again in 1613–14, studying the buildings of Palladio and Scamozzi and collecting drawings that deeply influenced his work. Appointed Surveyor of the King's Works in 1615, he produced the first fully classical buildings in England: the Queen's House at Greenwich (begun 1616) and the Banqueting House in Whitehall (1619–1622), with its ceiling painted by Rubens. He laid out Covent Garden (1630s), London's first formal square, with St Paul's Church, and designed the Queen's Chapel at St James's Palace and Wilton House's celebrated state rooms.

Stage design and masques

For more than two decades Jones designed the scenery and costumes for court masques written by Ben Jonson, Samuel Daniel, and others. He introduced changeable perspective scenery and proscenium staging to England, transforming the English stage; disputes with Jonson over whether poetry or spectacle mattered more ended their partnership in 1631.

Later life and influence

Jones's career ended with the English Civil War; his office was abolished in 1643 and his property sequestered. He died in 1652. A century later, the Palladian revival led by Lord Burlington and Colen Campbell treated Jones as its founding father, and his reputation — inflated by the attribution of buildings such as the "Jones" west front of old St Paul's — shaped English classical architecture for generations.
