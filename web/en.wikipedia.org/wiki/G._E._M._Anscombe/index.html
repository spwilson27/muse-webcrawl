G. E. M. Anscombe

Gertrude Elizabeth Margaret Anscombe
Born: 18 March 1919, Limerick, Ireland
Died: 5 January 2001 (aged 81), Cambridge, England
Era: 20th-century philosophy
Main interests: philosophy of mind, philosophy of action, ethics, philosophy of language

Gertrude Elizabeth Margaret Anscombe (18 March 1919 – 5 January 2001), usually cited as G. E. M. Anscombe or Elizabeth Anscombe, was a British analytic philosopher. She wrote on the philosophy of mind, philosophy of action, philosophical logic, philosophy of language, and ethics. Her 1958 article "Modern Moral Philosophy" introduced the term consequentialism into the language of analytic philosophy and had a seminal influence on contemporary virtue ethics. Her monograph Intention (1957) was described by Donald Davidson as "the best treatment of action since Aristotle."

Life

Anscombe studied at St Hugh's College, Oxford, graduating in 1941. She converted to Catholicism as a teenager and remained a devout Catholic throughout her life. At Oxford and later at Cambridge she was a student — and later literary executor — of Ludwig Wittgenstein, whose Philosophical Investigations she translated into English. In 1970 she became Professor of Philosophy at the University of Cambridge, a chair she held until 1986. She was married to the philosopher Peter Geach.

Work

Anscombe's Intention (1957) is a foundational text in the philosophy of action, distinguishing intentional actions by the applicability of a certain sense of the question "Why?" In "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) she argued that the moral "ought" had survived the demise of the divine-law framework that gave it sense, and she urged philosophers to return to an ethics grounded in human flourishing, anticipating the revival of virtue ethics. She also wrote extensively on causation, perception, and the first person, arguing in "The First Person" (1975) that "I" is not a referring expression.

Controversy

Anscombe publicly opposed Oxford's awarding of an honorary degree to Harry S. Truman in 1956, denouncing the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki as murder. Her pamphlet Mr Truman's Degree defended absolute prohibitions on intentionally killing the innocent.
