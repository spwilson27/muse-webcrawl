Code sharing

[Image placeholder: Departures board showing two flight numbers for one flight]

Code sharing is an aviation business arrangement, originating in 1967, where two or more airlines publish and market the same flight under their own airline designator and flight number ("airline code") as part of their published timetable or schedule. Typically, a flight is operated by one airline (technically called an "administrating carrier" or "operating carrier") while seats are sold for the flight by all cooperating airlines using their own designator and flight number.

Reasons for code sharing

Code sharing allows airlines to offer destinations they do not serve themselves, increase flight frequency on a route, and feed passengers into their hub networks. It is a cornerstone of the three global airline alliances — Star Alliance, SkyTeam and Oneworld — and of many bilateral partnerships.

Types

Arrangements include block space agreements, in which a partner buys a fixed block of seats, and free-flow agreements, in which seats are sold from a shared inventory. A related practice is wet leasing, where an aircraft and crew are provided by another carrier.

Criticism

Consumer advocates have criticised code sharing for potentially confusing passengers about which airline actually operates their flight, which is why regulations in many countries require the operating carrier to be disclosed at booking.
