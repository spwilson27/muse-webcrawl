Counter word

[Image: Counter word]

A counter word, also called a count word or numeral classifier, is a word or morpheme used in combination with a numeral to indicate the quantity of a noun. Many languages, including Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Malay and numerous indigenous languages of the Americas, require a specific counter to be used when counting objects; the choice of counter depends on the shape, animacy or category of the thing counted.

In Japanese

Japanese has hundreds of counters, though only a subset is in everyday use. Examples include hon for long cylindrical objects, mai for flat thin objects such as sheets of paper, hiki for small animals, nin for people, dai for machines and vehicles, and satsu for bound volumes. The pronunciation of the numeral and counter often changes when combined, as in ippon (one long object) from ichi + hon.

In Chinese

In Mandarin Chinese, measure words (liàngcí) are obligatory between a numeral or demonstrative and a noun. Common examples are gè (generic), běn for books, zhāng for flat objects, and tiáo for long flexible things. Some measure words also function as units of measurement for uncountable quantities.

Other languages

Classifier systems differ in size and in how grammaticalised they are. Some languages have only a handful of counters; others, like certain Austronesian and Amazonian languages, have elaborate systems. English retains vestiges in words such as "head" of cattle and "sheets" of paper.
