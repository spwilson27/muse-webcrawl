Biscayne Bay is a lagoon with characteristics of an estuary located on the Atlantic coast of South Florida, United States. The northern end of the lagoon is surrounded by the densely developed Miami metropolitan area, while the southern end is largely undeveloped, with a large portion of the lagoon included in Biscayne National Park. The bay is about 35 miles (56 km) long and up to 8 miles (13 km) wide, and is separated from the Atlantic Ocean by a chain of barrier islands and keys including Miami Beach and Key Biscayne.

Geography

The bay is divided into North Bay, Central Bay, and South Bay. Causeways cross the northern bay to Miami Beach, and the Rickenbacker Causeway links Miami to Key Biscayne, where Bill Baggs Cape Florida State Park and the historic Cape Florida Lighthouse stand. The bay is fringed in places by mangroves and supports extensive seagrass meadows, hardbottom communities, and the northernmost extent of the Florida Reef Tract.

History

The Tequesta people inhabited the bay's shores for centuries; their principal town stood at the mouth of the Miami River. Juan Ponce de León visited in 1513, and the bay's name derives from early Spanish references to a sailor from the Bay of Biscay. In the 19th century the bay area was a frontier of wreckers, Bahamian settlers, and pineapple plantations; Miami was incorporated in 1896 following the arrival of Henry Flagler's railroad, and dredging created the Port of Miami and numerous artificial islands.

Ecology and environmental issues

Biscayne Bay is habitat for manatees, sea turtles, bottlenose dolphins, and many fish and wading bird species; stiltsville structures — wooden houses on stilts — stand in the central bay. The bay suffers from nutrient pollution, sewage spills, and stormwater runoff, which have caused seagrass die-offs and fish kills, notably a major die-off beginning in 2020 linked to eutrophication.
