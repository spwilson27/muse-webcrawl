Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine is a wine with significant levels of dissolved carbon dioxide, making it fizzy. The carbon dioxide results from natural fermentation, either in the bottle (the traditional method, as in Champagne) or in a large tank (the Charmat method), or from direct injection of carbon dioxide. Sparkling wines are usually white or rosé, though red examples exist, and range from very dry (brut nature) to sweet.

History

The earliest recorded sparkling wine was Blanquette de Limoux, produced by Benedictine monks in southern France from 1531. In England, Christopher Merret presented a paper to the Royal Society in 1662 describing how sugar added to wine produced sparkle — decades before the monk Dom Pérignon, later mythologized as Champagne's inventor, was cellar master at Hautvillers Abbey. Champagne itself was systematized in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries by houses such as Veuve Clicquot, which pioneered riddling and disgorgement.

Production methods

In the traditional method (méthode champenoise), a still base wine undergoes a second fermentation in the bottle after yeast and sugar (liqueur de tirage) are added; the wine ages on its lees, the bottles are riddled to gather sediment in the neck, and the sediment is disgorged before a dosage of wine and sugar sets the final sweetness. In the Charmat (tank) method, the second fermentation occurs in pressurized tanks, preserving fresh fruit character — typical of Prosecco. The ancestral method (pétillant naturel) bottles wine before its first fermentation finishes.

Major styles

Champagne, from the Champagne region of France, is made chiefly from Chardonnay, Pinot noir and Meunier and must meet strict appellation rules. Prosecco comes from Italy's Veneto and Friuli regions and is made mainly from Glera grapes. Cava, from Spain (chiefly Catalonia), uses the traditional method with Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada. Other notable sparkling wines include Crémant from various French regions, German Sekt, Franciacorta from Lombardy, and sparkling wines from England, California, Australia and South Africa.

Sweetness levels

Residual sugar is expressed in terms from driest to sweetest: brut nature (no added sugar), extra brut, brut, extra dry, sec, demi-sec and doux. Despite its name, "extra dry" is sweeter than brut.
