Waltzing Matilda

"Waltzing Matilda" is Australia's best-known bush ballad and an unofficial national anthem. The song was written by the poet Banjo Paterson in 1895, with music adapted by Christina Macpherson, and tells of an itinerant worker (a swagman) who camps by a billabong, steals a sheep, and drowns himself rather than be captured by the police.

Origins

Paterson wrote the lyrics at Dagworth Station near Winton, Queensland, in January 1895, drawing on stories he heard during a visit there — including the recent death of a striking shearer, Samuel Hoffmeister, during the 1894 shearers' strike, and Macpherson's recollection of a tune she had heard played by a band at the Warrnambool races, commonly associated with "The Craigielee March". The phrase "waltzing Matilda" derives from the slang for carrying one's swag (bedroll) — the "Matilda" — while walking from place to place in search of work.

Lyrics

The song opens: "Once a jolly swagman camped by a billabong, / Under the shade of a coolibah tree". Catching a stray sheep ("jumbuck") and stuffing it in his tucker bag, the swagman is confronted by the squatter and three troopers; he leaps into the billabong, declaring "You'll never take me alive", and his ghost is said to haunt the spot. The chorus — "Waltzing Matilda, waltzing Matilda, / You'll come a-waltzing Matilda with me" — addresses his swag as a travelling companion.

Cultural status

First published as sheet music in 1903, the song became a soldiers' favourite in both world wars and an emblem of the egalitarian, anti-authoritarian strain in Australian identity. It was one of four songs in the 1977 plebiscite on a national anthem (won by "Advance Australia Fair") and is performed at sporting events and commemorations. The Waltzing Matilda Centre in Winton, opened in 1998, is devoted to the song; the original manuscript is held by the National Library of Australia.
