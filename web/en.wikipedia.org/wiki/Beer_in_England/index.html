Beer in England

Beer in England has a long and distinctive history. Ale has been brewed in England since at least the Iron Age, and the country developed characteristic styles — bitter, pale ale, porter, stout and India pale ale — as well as the tradition of cask-conditioned "real ale" served in public houses. England is one of the world's major beer cultures and the birthplace of several globally influential styles.


Cask ale hand pumps in an English pub

History

Brewing in England was traditionally a domestic and monastic activity; ale, flavoured with herbs rather than hops, was a staple drink. Hops arrived from the Low Countries around the 15th–16th centuries, and the hopped drink gradually displaced unhopped ale. The Industrial Revolution transformed brewing in London and Burton upon Trent: London brewers developed porter and stout, while Burton's sulphate-rich water proved ideal for pale ales, including the heavily hopped India pale ale shipped to India.

The 20th century saw consolidation into large national brewers, the spread of pressurised keg beer, and declining pub numbers. In response, the Campaign for Real Ale (CAMRA), founded in 1971, championed traditional cask-conditioned ale; its efforts helped preserve the style and later encouraged today's large population of small independent breweries — over two thousand breweries now operate in the United Kingdom.

Styles

Traditional English styles include bitter and best bitter, golden ale, mild, old ale and barley wine, brown ale, porter and stout, and pale ales ranging from English IPA to session-strength interpretations. Cask ale is conditioned and served without extraneous carbon dioxide, typically at cellar temperature (around 11–13 °C), dispensed by hand pump or directly from the cask.

Pub culture

The public house remains central to English beer culture, and beer is also closely tied to events such as CAMRA's Great British Beer Festival. Measurement by the pint (568 ml) is protected in law, a surviving imperial measure.
