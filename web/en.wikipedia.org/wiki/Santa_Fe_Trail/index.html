The Santa Fe Trail was a 19th-century route through central North America that connected Franklin, Missouri (and later Independence) with Santa Fe, New Mexico. Pioneered in 1821 by William Becknell, it served primarily as a commercial highway used by Mexican and American traders, plus as a route for emigrants and the U.S. Army, until the introduction of the railroad to Santa Fe in 1880.

History

William Becknell departed Franklin, Missouri, in September 1821 after Mexico won independence from Spain and opened trade with the United States. His party reached Santa Fe that November and returned with substantial profits, advertising the trail's potential. In 1822 Becknell pioneered the use of wagons on the route, and in 1825 the trail was surveyed under the direction of George Sibley. The Council Grove treaty with the Osage secured safe passage through their lands.

Trade grew rapidly: by the 1840s wagon caravans carried several million dollars' worth of merchandise annually, mostly manufactured goods exchanged for silver, furs, and mules. During the Mexican–American War in 1846, Stephen W. Kearny's Army of the West followed the trail to occupy Santa Fe. After the war the trail became a national freight road, with Fort Larned, Fort Dodge, and Bent's Fort protecting traffic.

Route

The trail crossed Missouri, Kansas, southeastern Colorado, and the Oklahoma Panhandle into New Mexico. Near present-day Great Bend, Kansas, it split into the wetter but longer Mountain Branch, which followed the Arkansas River to Bent's Fort and turned south over Raton Pass, and the drier Cimarron Cutoff through the Cimarron Desert, a shortcut notorious for its scarcity of water. Both branches rejoined near Fort Union before descending to Santa Fe.

Decline and legacy

The Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railway reached Santa Fe in 1880, ending large-scale wagon traffic almost overnight. Today, preserved ruts, forts, and landmarks dot the route, and the Santa Fe National Historic Trail, administered by the National Park Service, commemorates the corridor and its role in westward expansion and U.S.–Mexican commerce.
