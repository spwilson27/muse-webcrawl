Melamine

Melamine is an organic compound with the formula C3H6N6. This white solid is a trimer of cyanamide, with a 1,3,5-triazine skeleton. Like cyanamide, it contains 66% nitrogen by mass, and when combined with resins it acquires fire-retardant properties through the release of nitrogen gas. Melamine is a metabolite of the pesticide cyromazine and is produced on a large industrial scale, principally for use in melamine-formaldehyde resins.

Uses

Combined with formaldehyde and other agents, melamine produces melamine resin, a hard, durable thermosetting plastic used in high-pressure laminates (such as Formica), kitchenware, plates, and whiteboards. Melamine foam is used as an insulating and soundproofing material and as a cleaning abrasive (marketed, for example, as a "magic eraser"). Melamine is also used in concrete plasticizers, in flame retardants, and in some fertilizers.

Toxicity and contamination scandals

Melamine itself has low acute toxicity, but ingestion in combination with cyanuric acid can lead to the formation of insoluble crystals and kidney stones, renal failure, and death. In 2007, melamine adulteration of pet food ingredients exported from China caused widespread pet deaths in North America. In the 2008 Chinese milk scandal, melamine was added to diluted milk to falsely inflate apparent protein content in nitrogen-based tests; around 300,000 infants were affected, with at least six deaths, leading to executions, long prison terms, and a global overhaul of food-safety testing.

Production

Melamine was first synthesized by Justus von Liebig in 1834. Modern industrial production most commonly starts from urea, which decomposes to cyanic acid and ammonia and then polymerizes to melamine at high temperature, either in low-pressure catalysed gas-phase processes or high-pressure processes.
