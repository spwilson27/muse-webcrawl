A cable railway is a railway in which the vehicles are hauled by a cable rather than by a locomotive or onboard power. A stationary engine drives a continuously moving cable, and the cars grip the cable to move and release it (or brake) to stop. Cable haulage allows trains to climb grades far steeper than adhesion railways can manage.

San Francisco's cable cars are the best-known surviving cable railway system.

Types

In the classic urban cable car system, pioneered by Andrew Smith Hallidie in San Francisco in 1873, an endless cable runs in a conduit beneath the street and cars attach to it with a grip operated by the gripman. Cable haulage was also used on steep main-line inclines, on industrial and mine railways, and historically to haul trains through early tunnels. The funicular is a related system in which two counterbalanced cars are linked by a cable passing over a pulley at the top of the slope.

History

Rope-worked inclines date to the earliest railways; by the late 19th century dozens of cities in the United States, Britain, Australia, and New Zealand operated street cable railways, and Chicago and Melbourne built some of the largest networks. Electric traction, cheaper and more flexible, displaced almost all of them in the early 20th century; San Francisco's lines survive as a National Historic Landmark and a working part of the city's transit system.
