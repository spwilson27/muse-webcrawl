Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane, commonly known as DDT, is a colorless, tasteless, and almost odorless crystalline chemical compound, an organochloride. Originally developed as an insecticide, it became infamous for its environmental impacts. DDT was first synthesized in 1874 by the Austrian chemist Othmar Zeidler; its insecticidal action was discovered by the Swiss chemist Paul Hermann Müller in 1939, who received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1948.

DDT was used extensively in the second half of World War II by Allied forces to control insect vectors of malaria and typhus, and after the war as an agricultural insecticide. Rachel Carson's 1962 book Silent Spring documented its adverse environmental effects, helping launch the modern environmental movement. Most agricultural use was banned worldwide under the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (2001), though limited use for disease-vector control continues.

History

After Müller's discovery at Geigy, DDT was deployed to protect troops and civilians from typhus — famously halting an epidemic in Naples in 1943–44 — and to suppress malaria around military installations. Postwar, it was sprayed on crops across the world; global agricultural use peaked around 1963. Eradication campaigns driven by the World Health Organization eliminated malaria from many regions, though resistance in mosquitoes grew steadily.

Mechanism of action

DDT acts on the nervous system of insects by opening sodium ion channels in neurons, causing spasms and death. Its breakdown products, principally DDE and DDD, are persistent in soil and sediment and accumulate in fatty tissues, biomagnifying up food chains.

Environmental impact

DDT is a persistent organic pollutant with a soil half-life of years to decades. DDE caused eggshell thinning in predatory birds — including the bald eagle, brown pelican, and peregrine falcon — driving population crashes in the 1950s–60s. The United States banned agricultural DDT in 1972; bird populations subsequently recovered, a landmark success of environmental regulation.

Effects on wildlife and humans

DDT is moderately acutely toxic to mammals and is classified as a probable human carcinogen; it is also an endocrine disruptor. Epidemiology links exposure to various reproductive and developmental outcomes, though the magnitude of risks at low environmental doses remains studied.

Malaria control debate

Because indoor residual spraying with DDT remains one of the cheapest ways to kill malaria mosquitoes, the WHO endorsed its continued use for vector control in 2006. The debate pits malaria mortality in the tropics against the documented ecological harms, with most programs now relying on integrated vector management and alternative insecticides where feasible.
