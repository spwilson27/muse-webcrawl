Air sac

Air sacs are spaces within an organism where air is continuously circulated. In birds, a system of typically nine thin-walled air sacs connected to the lungs enables a unidirectional flow of air through the gas-exchange surfaces, making avian respiration far more efficient than the tidal breathing of mammals. Air sacs or homologous pneumatic structures also occur in other archosaurs, including saurischian dinosaurs, where they invaded and lightened the bones (postcranial skeletal pneumaticity).

Function in birds

The air sacs themselves take little part in gas exchange; they act as bellows. During inhalation, fresh air passes partly into the posterior sacs and partly through the parabronchial lungs; on exhalation, air stored in the posterior sacs is pushed through the lungs while air already used exits via the anterior sacs. A given parcel of air therefore requires two full breath cycles to pass through the system, and flow through the paleopulmonic parabronchi is always in the same direction, supporting a cross-current exchange with the blood that sustains the high metabolic rates flight demands. Air sacs also pneumatize bones, reducing weight, and help with cooling.

In other animals

Beyond birds, air sacs occur as diverticula of the trachea in some insects (aiding ventilation and sometimes buoyancy or sound production) and as lung diverticula in certain other vertebrates. The term is also used for the alveoli of mammalian lungs in older literature, though these are structurally different.
