AES-GCM

GCM combines counter-mode encryption with a polynomial hash (GHASH) for authentication.

AES-GCM is the use of the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) block cipher in Galois/Counter Mode (GCM), an authenticated encryption mode of operation that provides both confidentiality and data integrity. GCM was designed by John Viega and David A. McGrew and was standardized by NIST in Special Publication 800-38D (2007). Because it is fast in hardware and can be parallelized, AES-GCM became the most widely deployed authenticated cipher in protocols such as TLS 1.2 and 1.3, IPsec, and SSH.

Operation

GCM encrypts plaintext using AES in counter mode: a nonce (typically 96 bits) combined with a counter generates a keystream that is XORed with the plaintext. Authentication is provided by GHASH, a polynomial evaluation over the finite field GF(2128) defined by the polynomial x128 + x7 + x2 + x + 1. The hash is computed over the additional authenticated data (AAD) and the ciphertext, then masked with an encrypted counter block to produce the authentication tag, typically 128 bits.

Security considerations

The security of GCM depends critically on never reusing a nonce with the same key: nonce reuse leaks the GHASH key and allows both keystream recovery and universal forgery. Random 96-bit nonces carry a birthday-bound risk of collision after roughly 232 messages. Derivatives such as AES-GCM-SIV (RFC 8452) were designed to be nonce-misuse resistant. GCM also has an upper bound of about 64 GiB of data per invocation.

Adoption

AES-GCM is mandatory in TLS 1.3 cipher suites such as TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256 and TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384, is widely supported by the AES-NI instruction set on x86 processors and ARMv8 cryptography extensions, and is used in standards including IEEE 802.11ad, MACsec, and the Web Cryptography API.
