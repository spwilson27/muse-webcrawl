Endoscopy

Endoscopy is a procedure used in medicine to look inside the body. The endoscopy procedure uses an endoscope — a long, thin, flexible (or sometimes rigid) tube with a light and a camera at one end — to examine the interior of a hollow organ or cavity of the body. Unlike many other medical imaging techniques, endoscopes are inserted directly into the organ, usually through a natural opening such as the mouth or anus, or through a small surgical incision.

Types

Endoscopic procedures are named for the organ or region examined. They include gastroscopy (stomach and upper digestive tract), colonoscopy (large intestine), bronchoscopy (airways), cystoscopy (bladder), arthroscopy (joints) and laparoscopy (abdomen). Capsule endoscopy, in which the patient swallows a small camera, allows examination of the small intestine.

Uses

Endoscopy is used to investigate symptoms such as gastrointestinal bleeding, persistent abdominal pain, difficulty swallowing and unexplained weight loss; to confirm diagnoses by taking biopsy samples; and to perform treatments, including removing polyps, stopping bleeding, dilating narrowed passages and removing foreign objects. It is also a key tool in screening for colorectal cancer.

Risks

Endoscopy is generally safe, but carries small risks including adverse reactions to sedation, bleeding, infection and perforation of the organ being examined.

History

Early endoscopes date to the nineteenth century; the German physician Philipp Bozzini developed a light-conducting instrument in 1806. The flexible fibre-optic gastroscope introduced by Basil Hirschowitz in the 1950s transformed the field, and video endoscopes followed in the 1980s.
