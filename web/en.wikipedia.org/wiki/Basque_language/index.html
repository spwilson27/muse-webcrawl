Basque (euskara) is a language spoken by the Basques in the western Pyrenees, straddling the border between Spain and France. It is a language isolate — not demonstrably related to any other living language — and is widely considered the only surviving pre-Indo-European language of Western Europe, descending from or closely related to the Aquitanian language attested in Roman-era inscriptions.

History

Basque survived Romanisation, the medieval expansion of Castilian and French, and the suppression of the Franco era, when its public use was banned. Since Spain's 1978 constitution it has been co-official in the Basque Autonomous Community and parts of Navarre, and its numbers have grown through education in ikastolak (Basque-medium schools) and adult learning. A unified standard, Euskara Batua, was established by the Royal Academy of the Basque Language (Euskaltzaindia) from 1968, alongside the traditional dialects (Biscayan, Gipuzkoan, Upper Navarrese, Lapurdian, Zuberoan, and others).

Linguistic features

Basque is an ergative–absolutive, highly agglutinative language: verbs agree with subject, object and indirect object, and nouns take case suffixes from a system of about a dozen cases. It has a vigesimal (base-20) numeral system, no grammatical gender, and a distinctive phonology with a contrast between apico-alveolar and lamino-alveolar sibilants. Its vocabulary includes borrowings from Latin, Spanish and French, but its core lexicon and grammar remain unique.

Status

About 750,000–800,000 people speak Basque, roughly a third of the population of the greater Basque Country, with bilingualism increasing among younger generations in Spain; in France it has no official status and is classified by UNESCO as endangered, though revival efforts continue.
