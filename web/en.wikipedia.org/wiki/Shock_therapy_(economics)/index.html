Shock therapy (economics)

Shock therapy is an economic policy approach in which the transition from a controlled economy to a market economy — or the correction of a macroeconomic crisis — is carried out abruptly and comprehensively: prices are liberalised at once, state subsidies are cut, trade is opened, and state assets are privatised rapidly, rather than through gradual reform.

History and examples

The term is associated with post-war West Germany's 1948 currency reform and price decontrol under Ludwig Erhard, with Bolivia's 1985 stabilisation under advice from Jeffrey Sachs, and above all with the post-communist transitions after 1989. Poland's Balcerowicz Plan (1990) is usually classed as a relatively successful shock therapy; Russia's rapid reforms after 1992, overseen by Yegor Gaidar, coincided with output collapse, hyperinflation and oligarchic privatisation and remain deeply controversial. China's gradualist, dual-track transition is the standard contrast case.

Criticism

Supporters argue that gradualism allows vested interests to block reform and prolongs distortion; critics, including Joseph Stiglitz and Naomi Klein (in The Shock Doctrine), argue that shock therapy ignores institutions, imposes severe social costs, and can entrench corruption. Empirical assessments differ by country and remain contested among economists.
