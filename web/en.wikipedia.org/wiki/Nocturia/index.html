Nocturia


Labelled placeholder: nocturia illustration

Nocturia (derived from Latin nox, night, and Greek [τα] ούρα, urine) is the complaint that the individual has to wake at night one or more times for voiding (i.e., to urinate). The term is derived from Latin and Greek. Factors that contribute to nocturia include excessive fluid intake late in the evening, global polyuria, nocturnal polyuria, reduced bladder storage capacity, sleep disorders, and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH).

Twenty-four-hour urine production determines the division into global and nocturnal polyuria. Nocturia may lead to sleep deprivation, and has been linked to increased accidents and falls, lower quality of life and mortality. It is often associated with aging and is one of the most common causes of disturbed sleep in older adults.

Causes and risk factors

Among people with nocturia, 76% produce a disproportionate amount of urine overnight (nocturnal polyuria), and 24% have reduced bladder storage capacity (functional nocturnal bladder capacity reduced). Major causes of nocturnal polyuria include congenital heart failure, obstructive or central sleep apnea, circadian defect in secretion of arginine vasopressin (AVP), and excessive fluid intake in the evening. Causes of reduced bladder storage capacity include increased post-void residual urine, detrusor overactivity, urogenital aging, bladder obstruction, bladder pain syndrome/interstitial cystitis, and secondary anxiety disorders.

Prevalence

Nocturia is very common: over 50% of people over the age of 50 are thought to suffer from the condition. It has been shown to affect men and women equally. The number of people affected increases with age; more than 50% of men and women over the age of 60 have nocturia in many populations.

Mechanism

The kidney normally produces urine at a slower rate during the night, allowing uninterrupted sleep. In nocturia due to nocturnal polyuria, urine production at night exceeds roughly 20–33% of total 24-hour production, with the threshold rising with age. Global polyuria — excessive production throughout the day and night — may result from diabetes mellitus, diabetes insipidus, or primary polydipsia.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis commonly involves a frequency-volume chart (bladder diary) recorded over several days, measurement of post-void residual volume, blood tests including serum electrolytes and glucose, and screening for sleep apnea where indicated.

Treatment

Treatment targets the underlying cause. Lifestyle measures include reducing evening fluid intake, limiting caffeine and alcohol, leg elevation and compression stockings for patients with peripheral edema, and treating sleep apnea. Medications may include desmopressin for nocturnal polyuria, alpha-blockers or other drugs for benign prostatic obstruction, and agents for overactive bladder. Hyponatremia is a risk of desmopressin, particularly in older patients.
