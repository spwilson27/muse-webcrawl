René Laennec

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Laennec and an early stethoscope

René-Théophile-Hyacinthe Laennec (17 February 1781 – 13 August 1826) was a French physician and musician. His skill at carving his own wooden flutes led him to invent the stethoscope in 1816, while he was at the Hôpital Necker. He pioneered its use in diagnosing various chest conditions.

He also coined the term "stethoscope", and terms such as pectoriloquy, egophony and rales, and he was the first to describe bronchiectasis and to give the first full description of cirrhosis of the liver. He became a lecturer at the Collège de France in 1822 and professor of medicine in 1823.

Invention of the stethoscope

In 1816, Laennec, feeling embarrassed to place his ear directly on the chest of a young female patient, recalled that sound travels through solids and rolled a sheet of paper into a tube, placing one end on the patient's chest and the other to his ear. He was surprised to find he could hear the heart sounds more clearly. He then developed the instrument in wood — a single-earpiece hollow cylinder — and coined its name from the Greek stethos (chest) and skopos (examination).

Traité de l'auscultation médiate

In 1819 Laennec published De l'auscultation médiate, a landmark work that systematically correlated the sounds heard through the stethoscope (mediate auscultation) with lesions found at autopsy — in pneumonia, bronchiectasis, emphysema, pleurisy and pulmonary tuberculosis, among others. His clinical-pathological method helped found modern physical diagnosis. Ironically, Laennec himself died of tuberculosis in 1826, diagnosed, it is said, with his own instrument.
