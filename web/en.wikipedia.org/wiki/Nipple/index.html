Nipple

The nipple is a raised region of tissue on the surface of the breast from which, in females, milk leaves the mammary gland through the lactiferous ducts to feed an infant. The nipple is surrounded by the areola, a circular area of pigmented skin. Nipples are present in both sexes of mammals, though in males they do not normally serve a lactating function.

Anatomy

The nipple contains the openings of 15 to 20 lactiferous ducts arranged cylindrically around its tip, embedded in smooth muscle fibres that allow it to become erect in response to cold, touch, or arousal. Its blood supply and innervation derive principally from the intercostal vessels and the fourth intercostal nerve. In humans the nipples are located around the level of the fourth intercostal space, though position varies considerably.

Development

Nipples develop in the embryo before sexual differentiation, which is why both males and females possess them. Additional nipples along the embryonic "milk lines" (polythelia) occur in a small percentage of people and are usually harmless.

Clinical significance

Changes in the nipple—such as inversion, discharge, scaling, or a new lump—can be signs of breast conditions including infection, benign duct disorders, or cancer, including Paget's disease of the breast, and warrant medical assessment. Nipple pain and cracking are common in early breastfeeding and are usually addressed by correcting the infant's latch.
