Large-scale structure of the universe

Large-scale structure

The large-scale structure of the universe describes the distribution of matter and galaxies on the largest observable scales. Rather than being spread uniformly, galaxies are organised into a vast network — the cosmic web — of clusters, superclusters, filaments and sheets surrounding enormous, nearly empty voids.

Components

Galaxies gather in groups and clusters of up to thousands of members. Clusters chain together into filaments and walls hundreds of millions of light-years long — such as the CfA2 Great Wall (1989), the Sloan Great Wall (~1.4 billion light-years), and the claimed Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall (~10 billion light-years) — separated by voids typically 100–300 million light-years across containing very few galaxies. Above supercluster scales, surveys are consistent with the cosmological principle: the universe becomes homogeneous and isotropic, the so-called "End of Greatness" around a few hundred million light-years.

Formation

In the ΛCDM model, structure grows by gravitational instability from tiny density fluctuations imprinted in the cosmic microwave background. Dark matter collapses first into halos and filaments; gas falls into these wells to form galaxies. Massive computer simulations such as the Millennium and Illustris runs reproduce the observed web in detail. Redshift surveys — CfA, 2dF, and the Sloan Digital Sky Survey — mapped this structure, and baryon acoustic oscillations frozen into the galaxy distribution serve as a standard ruler for measuring cosmic expansion.
