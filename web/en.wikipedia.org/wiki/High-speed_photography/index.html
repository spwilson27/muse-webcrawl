High-speed photography

High-speed photography
DefinitionImages exposed in 1 millisecond or less, or sequences at 250 frames per second and above
PioneersEadweard Muybridge, Étienne-Jules Marey, Harold Edgerton
Key techniqueElectronic flash (stroboscope), rotating-mirror and image-converter cameras

High-speed photography is the science and practice of making photographs with extremely short exposures or extremely rapid frame rates in order to record events too fast for the human eye to perceive, such as a bullet in flight, a bursting balloon, or the splash formed by a falling droplet.

History

The field began with the motion studies of the 1870s and 1880s: Eadweard Muybridge used banks of cameras with fast shutters to settle the question of whether a galloping horse lifts all four hooves from the ground, and Étienne-Jules Marey developed chronophotography to analyse movement. In the 1930s Harold Edgerton at MIT perfected the electronic stroboscopic flash, making microsecond exposures practical; his images of milk drops and hummingbirds became iconic. During the Second World War, high-speed cameras were developed for ballistics and weapons testing, including work connected with the Manhattan Project, where rotating-mirror cameras reached millions of frames per second.

Techniques

Short exposures freeze motion: a flash duration of a microsecond or less can stop most macroscopic events. For continuous recording, mechanical rotating-prism and rotating-mirror cameras, image-converter tubes, and modern high-speed CMOS sensors provide frame rates from thousands to millions of frames per second, usually at reduced resolution and for very short sequences. Kerr and Faraday cells and, more recently, compressed ultrafast photography techniques have pushed effective frame rates into the trillions of frames per second, fast enough to watch pulses of light propagate. Illumination is often the limiting factor, and synchronisation with the event—by sound, light or contact triggers—is essential.

Applications

High-speed photography is used in engineering (crash testing, combustion and fluid-flow analysis), ballistics, biomechanics and sports science, manufacturing troubleshooting, natural-history filmmaking, and advertising. Its scientific cousin, schlieren photography, visualises density gradients such as shock waves.
