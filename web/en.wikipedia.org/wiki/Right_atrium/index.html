The right atrium is one of the four chambers of the human heart. It receives deoxygenated blood from the systemic circulation via the superior and inferior venae cavae and the coronary sinus, and pumps it into the right ventricle through the tricuspid valve. The right atrium forms the right border of the heart and sits anterior to the left atrium.

Structure

The interior of the right atrium is divided by the crista terminalis into a smooth-walled posterior part, the sinus venarum (derived embryologically from the sinus venosus, into which the great veins open), and a rough-walled anterior part lined by pectinate muscles, which extends into the right auricle (atrial appendage). The interatrial septum, separating the right from the left atrium, bears the fossa ovalis, the remnant of the fetal foramen ovale. The openings of the inferior vena cava and coronary sinus are guarded by rudimentary valves (the Eustachian and Thebesian valves). The sinoatrial node, the heart's natural pacemaker, lies in the wall near the opening of the superior vena cava, and the atrioventricular node sits near the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve, within the triangle of Koch.

Function

During ventricular diastole, blood flows passively from the right atrium into the right ventricle; atrial contraction ("atrial kick") completes ventricular filling, contributing roughly a quarter of the filling volume in a healthy heart at rest. Right atrial pressure reflects central venous pressure and right heart filling, and is clinically measured via central venous catheters. Distension receptors in the atrial walls respond to increased volume by triggering the release of atrial natriuretic peptide and reflex adjustments in heart rate (the Bainbridge reflex).

Clinical significance

Enlargement of the right atrium occurs with pulmonary hypertension, tricuspid valve disease, and congenital conditions such as Ebstein's anomaly. The chamber is a common site of origin for atrial fibrillation and typical atrial flutter, the latter circulating around the tricuspid annulus through the cavotricuspid isthmus — an important target for catheter ablation. In fetal life, the foramen ovale allows blood to bypass the unventilated lungs; its failure to close leaves a patent foramen ovale, present in roughly a quarter of adults.
