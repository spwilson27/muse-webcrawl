Shareware

Shareware is a type of proprietary software that is initially shared by the owner for trial use at little or no cost. Often the software has limited functionality or incomplete documentation, but sometimes it has more functionality than "retail" software. Shareware is often offered as a download from a website. Shareware differs from freeware, which is fully functional software distributed without fee, and free and open-source software, in that the source code is typically not made available.

Shareware was widely distributed on floppy disks and CD-ROMs in the 1980s and 1990s.

History

The term was coined in the early 1980s. In 1982 Andrew Fluegelman created PC-Talk, a telecommunications program for the IBM PC, and distributed it under a scheme he called "freeware," asking satisfied users to send $25. Around the same time Jim Knopf distributed PC-File under the name "user-supported software," and Bob Wallace coined "shareware" for his word processor PC-Write. The Association of Shareware Professionals was founded in 1987 to set standards for the industry.

Shareware distribution flourished through bulletin board systems (BBSes), online services such as CompuServe, and later the World Wide Web. Companies such as Apogee Software and id Software popularized the episodic shareware model for games: the first episode of a game such as Doom (1993) was distributed free, with further episodes available for purchase.

Types

  • Trialware / demoware — full or partial programs distributed for a limited trial period, after which they may stop working ("crippleware" when features are disabled).
  • Nagware — software that persistently reminds the user to register or pay, often with a delay screen at startup.
  • Adware — free to use but supported by advertising.
  • Donationware — fully functional, with a requested donation to the author or a charity.
  • Postcardware — distributed in exchange for the user sending the author a postcard.

Decline

With the rise of the web, app stores, freemium business models and software as a service, the classic shareware model declined in prominence during the 2000s, though trial and "try before you buy" distribution remains common in modified forms.
