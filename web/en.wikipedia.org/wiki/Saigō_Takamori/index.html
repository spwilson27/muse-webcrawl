Saigō Takamori

Saigō Takamori (Japanese: 西郷 隆盛; 23 January 1828 – 24 September 1877) was a Japanese samurai of the Satsuma Domain and one of the most influential figures of the Meiji Restoration. Regarded as one of the "Three Great Nobles of the Restoration", he helped overthrow the Tokugawa shogunate, yet later led the Satsuma Rebellion against the very imperial government he had helped to create, dying in its final battle. He is often romanticised as "the last true samurai".

Early life

Saigō was born in Kagoshima, the castle town of Satsuma, into a low-ranking samurai family. He came to the attention of the reforming daimyō Shimazu Nariakira and rose in the domain's service, but after Nariakira's death he twice suffered exile to the remote Amami and Okinoerabu islands. Recalled in 1864, he played a leading role in forging the Satchō Alliance between Satsuma and Chōshū.

Restoration and government

During the Boshin War (1868–69) Saigō commanded imperial forces and negotiated the bloodless surrender of Edo Castle with Katsu Kaishū. In the new Meiji government he became a central figure, serving as a councillor and commander of the Imperial Guard, and he supported the abolition of the feudal domains. In 1873, however, he broke with colleagues such as Ōkubo Toshimichi over the Seikanron debate — his proposal to lead a mission to Korea — and resigned, returning to Kagoshima, where he founded private military academies.

Satsuma Rebellion and death

In 1877 his followers, alarmed by government moves to disarm them, rose in the Satsuma Rebellion. Saigō, though reluctant, agreed to lead the revolt. After initial successes, the rebels were worn down by the government's conscript army. Wounded at the final Battle of Shiroyama on 24 September 1877, Saigō died there — by most accounts killed by a follower at his own request to prevent capture. The government pardoned him posthumously in 1889 and later enshrined him as a hero; his statue, unveiled in Ueno Park in 1898, became an enduring symbol of loyal, selfless service.
