Panama Canal locks

The Panama Canal locks are a system of locks that lift ships up to the level of Gatun Lake, 26 metres (85 feet) above sea level, and lower them again on the other side of the Isthmus of Panama, allowing vessels to cross between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans without rounding South America. The canal, opened in 1914, originally had three sets of locks — Gatun, Pedro Miguel and Miraflores — each with twin lanes. A third, larger set of locks was opened in 2016 as part of the Panama Canal expansion project.

Original locks

The original lock chambers are 33.5 metres (110 feet) wide and 304.8 metres (1,000 feet) long, which defined the maximum size of ships that could transit, known as Panamax: about 294 metres long and 32.3 metres in beam. Gatun Locks, on the Atlantic side, raise ships in three steps directly from the Caribbean to Gatun Lake. On the Pacific side, the single step at Pedro Miguel and the two steps at Miraflores lower ships back to the ocean.

The locks do not use pumps: chambers are filled and emptied by gravity, through culverts in the lock walls, using water from Gatun Lake. The steel mitre gates, some over 20 metres tall, are moved by electric motors. Electric locomotives known as "mules" run on tracks along the lock walls and keep ships centred in the chambers during transit.

New locks

The expansion, approved by referendum in 2006 and inaugurated on 26 June 2016, added a third lane with two new lock complexes — Agua Clara on the Atlantic side and Cocoli on the Pacific side. These chambers are 427 metres long and 55 metres wide, accommodating "Neopanamax" vessels of up to roughly 366 metres in length and 49 metres in beam. Unlike the original locks, the new locks use rolling gates and water-saving basins that reuse about 60 per cent of the water in each transit.
