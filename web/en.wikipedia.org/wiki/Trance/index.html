Trance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This article is about the altered state of consciousness. For the music genre, see Trance music.

A trance is a state of semi-consciousness or altered awareness in which a person is not fully aware of their surroundings, is less responsive to external stimuli, and may experience heightened focus on an internal train of thought, sensation, or vision. Trance states occur across human cultures and have been reported in religious ritual, hypnosis, meditation, music and dance, and certain neurological and psychiatric conditions.

Characteristics

Common features attributed to trance include narrowed or intensely focused attention, reduced awareness of peripheral stimuli, diminished voluntary control or a sense that actions happen spontaneously, altered perception of time, and, in many religious settings, the experience of being possessed by or in contact with a spirit. The depth and character of trance vary widely: the same word covers light absorption in music, the mediumistic trance of a séance, and the ecstatic dancing of ritual possession.

Religious and cultural use

Trance is a central element of shamanic practice, in which the practitioner is believed to journey to a spirit world to heal or divine. Possession trance, in which a deity or ancestor is understood to speak and act through the entranced person, appears in traditions as diverse as Haitian Vodou, the zar cults of north-east Africa and the Middle East, Pentecostal worship, and the oracle traditions of ancient Greece — the Pythia at Delphi delivered prophecies in an altered state. Rhythmic drumming, chanting, dancing, fasting, sleep deprivation, and psychoactive substances are all used cross-culturally to induce trance.

Hypnosis

In clinical and experimental settings the term is closely associated with hypnosis: a hypnotic "trance" is induced by focused relaxation and suggestion, after which the subject may be unusually responsive to further suggestions. Researchers dispute whether hypnosis is a distinct altered state or a product of expectation and role-playing, but hypnotic procedures are used in managing pain and some psychological symptoms.

Medical perspectives

Trance-like episodes can also occur involuntarily: in dissociative disorders, in some forms of epilepsy, and in catatonia. Psychiatric classification distinguishes culturally sanctioned religious trance from pathological dissociative trance, in which involuntary episodes cause distress or impairment. Neuroscientific studies of trance and related states report altered patterns of activity in attention and self-referential brain networks, though findings differ across the very different practices grouped under the term.
