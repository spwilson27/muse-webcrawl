Export processing zone

An export processing zone (EPZ) is a defined area within a country in which imported materials and components can be brought in, processed or assembled, and re-exported with exemption from customs duties and with simplified administrative procedures, often combined with tax holidays and dedicated infrastructure. EPZs are a form of free trade zone intended to attract foreign direct investment, create employment, and promote export-oriented industrialisation.

History

One of the first modern EPZs was established at Shannon Airport in Ireland in 1959. The model spread widely in the 1970s and 1980s — to Taiwan's Kaohsiung zone (1966), South Korea's Masan zone, the Bataan zone in the Philippines, Malaysia, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh (Chittagong, 1983), and the maquiladora programme along Mexico's northern border. China's special economic zones, beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, adapted the concept on a much larger scale. By the 2000s the International Labour Organization estimated there were more than 3,500 zones in some 130 countries, employing tens of millions of workers, a large share of them women in garment and electronics factories.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters credit EPZs with jobs, foreign-exchange earnings, skills, and technology transfer. Critics cite low wages, weak labour rights and restrictions on union organisation, generous tax concessions that limit fiscal benefits, "footloose" firms that relocate when incentives expire, and limited linkages to the domestic economy (the "enclave" problem). Evaluations suggest outcomes depend heavily on a country's broader policies, infrastructure, and ability to move up the value chain.
