Self-awareness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An orangutan gazing at its own reflection in a mirror
An orangutan gazing at its own reflection in a mirror

Self-awareness is the capacity for introspection and the ability to recognise oneself as an individual separate from the environment and other individuals. It is studied through the Mirror test in animals (Gordon Gallup, 1970), through developmental milestones in children (mirror recognition at 15–24 months, the 'terrible twos' self-assertion), and in philosophy and neuroscience as a core component — though not a synonym — of consciousness.

2. Animals and machines

Forms and development

Psychologists distinguish bodily self-awareness (agency, ownership), social self-awareness (oneself as seen by others) and conceptual self-knowledge (the autobiographical self). Rochat's five levels map its emergence; disorders mark its fragility — depersonalisation, anosognosia, the mirror delusions of dementia. Objective self-awareness theory (Duval and Wicklund) showed that self-focus (mirrors, audiences) drives self-evaluation, for better and worse.

Animals and machines

The pass-list debates (apes, dolphins, elephants, magpies) define the empirical frontier, while philosophy asks whether recognition equals awareness at all. In AI, 'self-awareness' claims for language models (which can describe themselves without, most researchers hold, experiencing anything) sharpen the question of what the test of a self could be.
