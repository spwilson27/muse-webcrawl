The outer and middle ear, where conductive hearing loss originates

Conductive hearing loss (CHL) occurs when there is a problem transferring sound waves anywhere along the pathway through the outer ear, tympanic membrane (eardrum), or middle ear (ossicles). If a conductive hearing loss occurs in conjunction with a sensorineural hearing loss, it is referred to as a mixed hearing loss. Depending upon the severity and nature of the conductive loss, this type of hearing impairment can often be treated with surgical intervention or pharmaceuticals to partially or, in some cases, fully restore hearing acuity to within normal range. However, cases of permanent or chronic conductive hearing loss may require other treatment modalities such as hearing aid devices to improve detection of sound and speech perception.

Causes

Common causes of conductive hearing loss include:

  • External ear: impacted cerumen (earwax), otitis externa ("swimmer's ear"), foreign bodies, exostoses, and congenital atresia of the ear canal.
  • Tympanic membrane: perforation from trauma or infection, retraction, and tympanosclerosis.
  • Middle ear: otitis media with effusion ("glue ear"), acute otitis media, cholesteatoma, ossicular chain discontinuity or fixation, and otosclerosis — abnormal bone growth around the stapes, one of the commonest causes in adults.

Diagnosis

Pure-tone audiometry shows reduced air-conduction thresholds with preserved bone-conduction thresholds, producing an air–bone gap. Tuning-fork tests support the diagnosis: in the Rinne test, bone conduction is heard as louder than air conduction in the affected ear, and in the Weber test sound lateralises to the worse-hearing ear. Tympanometry assesses middle-ear compliance and may reveal effusion or ossicular problems. Imaging, usually CT of the temporal bone, is used when surgery is contemplated or cholesteatoma is suspected.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause: removal of earwax, antibiotics for infection, ventilation (tympanostomy) tubes for persistent effusion, tympanoplasty to repair a perforated eardrum, and stapedectomy or stapedotomy for otosclerosis. Where medical or surgical correction is not possible, amplification with conventional hearing aids or bone-conduction devices can bypass the defective conductive pathway.
