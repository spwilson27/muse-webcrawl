Cetus is a constellation, sometimes called "the whale", in the northern sky. Cetus was among the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd century astronomer Ptolemy, and it remains one of the 88 modern constellations. Its name is Latin for "large sea creature" and refers to a sea monster in Greek mythology, which the hero Perseus slew in order to save the princess Andromeda from being sacrificed to it. Cetus is located in the region of the sky known as the Water, along with other watery constellations such as Aquarius, Pisces, and Eridanus.

Notable stars

The brightest star in Cetus is Beta Ceti, also called Deneb Kaitos ("tail of the sea monster"), an orange giant of magnitude 2.02 about 96 light-years away; it occasionally outshines the constellation's traditional lucida, Alpha Ceti (Menkar), a red giant of magnitude 2.54 that marks the monster's head. Mira (Omicron Ceti, "the Wonderful") is the prototype of the Mira variable stars — a pulsating red giant that varies between magnitudes 2 and 10 over a period of about 332 days, discovered to be variable by David Fabricius in 1596. Tau Ceti, only 11.9 light-years away, is a Sun-like star long of interest in the search for extraterrestrial life and known to host a system of planets.

Deep-sky objects

Although Cetus lies well away from the Milky Way's plane and contains few bright nebulae, its position facing the galactic pole region makes it rich in external galaxies. The most famous is Messier 77, a barred spiral galaxy and one of the nearest and brightest Seyfert galaxies, about 47 million light-years away. The sculptor-like spiral NGC 247 and the large planetary nebula NGC 246, the "Skull Nebula", are also located in Cetus.

Mythology

In Greek myth, Cetus was the sea monster sent by Poseidon to ravage the coast of Aethiopia as punishment for Queen Cassiopeia's boasting that her daughter Andromeda was more beautiful than the Nereids. Andromeda was chained to a rock as a sacrifice, but was rescued by Perseus, who slew the monster — in some versions using the head of Medusa to turn it to stone. Cetus lies near the other constellations of the Perseus legend: Andromeda, Perseus, Cassiopeia, Cepheus, and Pegasus.
