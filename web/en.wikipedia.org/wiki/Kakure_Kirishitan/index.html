Kakure Kirishitan (Japanese: 隠れキリシタン, "Hidden Christians") is a modern term for the Japanese Catholic Christians who preserved their faith in secret during the more than two centuries when Christianity was outlawed in Japan, from the early seventeenth century until 1873. Driven underground by the Tokugawa shogunate's persecution, these communities developed distinctive prayers, rituals and devotional objects disguised as Buddhism or Shinto.

Background and persecution

Christianity arrived in Japan with Francis Xavier in 1549 and won perhaps 300,000 converts. From 1587, Toyotomi Hideyoshi began restricting the mission; the Tokugawa shogunate expelled missionaries in 1614 and enforced a total prohibition, most notoriously after the Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38. Suspected Christians were forced to trample on Christian images (fumi-e) to prove apostasy, and every household had to register at a Buddhist temple. In remote fishing villages around Nagasaki — on Ikitsuki Island, in the Gotō Islands, at Sotome and in the Urakami valley — believers instead went underground.

Hidden practice

Without priests for some 250 years, the hidden Christians centred their community on elected lay officers: the mizukata, who performed baptisms; the chōkata, who secretly kept the liturgical calendar; and elders who transmitted prayers called orasho (from Latin oratio), Latin and Portuguese chants whose words became garbled over generations. The Virgin Mary was venerated in the form of "Maria-Kannon" statues — the bodhisattva of mercy cradling a child. Crosses were concealed in lanterns and household shrines, feast days were keyed to the agricultural cycle, and a narrative scripture, the Tenchi Hajimari no Koto ("Account of the Beginning of Heaven and Earth"), retold Genesis and the life of Christ in a syncretic folk idiom.

Rediscovery and after

In March 1865, villagers from Urakami approached the French priest Bernard Petitjean at the newly built Ōura Church in Nagasaki and revealed themselves — the "discovery of the Christians" that showed Rome the faith had survived. Even then, thousands of Urakami Christians were exiled in a final persecution before the ban was lifted in 1873. On legalisation the hidden Christians divided: many rejoined the Catholic Church, while others — the Hanare Kirishitan ("separated Christians") — continued their ancestral practices, which survive in attenuated form on Ikitsuki and the Gotōs. In 2018 UNESCO inscribed the "Hidden Christian Sites in the Nagasaki Region" as a World Heritage Site.
