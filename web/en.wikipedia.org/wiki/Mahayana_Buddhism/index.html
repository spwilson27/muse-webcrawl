Foundedc. 1st century BCE–1st century CE, India
RegionEast and Northern Asia: China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Tibet, Mongolia

Mahayana (Sanskrit: महायान, romanized: Mahāyāna, lit. 'Great Vehicle') is a term for a broad group of Buddhist traditions, texts, philosophies, and practices developed in ancient India (c. 1st century BCE onwards). It is considered one of two main existing branches of Buddhism, the other being Theravada. Mahayana accepts the main scriptures and teachings of early Buddhism, but also recognises various additional doctrines and texts, such as the Mahayana sutras, which are not accepted as authoritative by Theravada. Adherents number in the hundreds of millions, concentrated in China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and the Himalayan world.

History

Mahayana emerged gradually within Indian Buddhism, drawing on movements that emphasized the universal aspiration to Buddhahood. Its earliest texts, the Prajñāpāramitā ("Perfection of Wisdom") sutras, began to appear around the turn of the Common Era. Philosophers such as Nāgārjuna (founder of the Madhyamaka school, teaching emptiness, śūnyatā) and Asaṅga and Vasubandhu (of the Yogācāra school) systematized its doctrines. Transmitted along the Silk Road, Mahayana took distinctive forms in China — Chan, Pure Land, Tiantai — and from there passed to Korea, Japan, and Vietnam, while its later, tantric-influenced forms (Vajrayana) became dominant in Tibet.

Doctrine

The defining ideal of Mahayana is the bodhisattva: a being who vows to attain full Buddhahood for the benefit of all sentient beings rather than seeking private liberation. Associated doctrines include emptiness (the lack of intrinsic existence in all phenomena), Buddha-nature (the innate potential for awakening present in all beings), the trikāya doctrine of the Buddha's three bodies, and upāya, the "skillful means" by which awakened beings adapt teachings to their audiences. Devotional practice directed toward celestial Buddhas and bodhisattvas — Amitābha, Avalokiteśvara, Maitreya — is central to much East Asian Mahayana.
