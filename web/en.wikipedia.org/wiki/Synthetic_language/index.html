Synthetic language

Synthetic language
TypologyMorphological typology
SubtypesFusional, agglutinative, polysynthetic
ExamplesLatin, Russian, Turkish, Finnish, Mohawk
OppositeAnalytic language

A synthetic language is a language that uses inflection or agglutination to express syntactic relationships within a sentence. Inflection is the addition of morphemes to a root word to assign it a grammatical property, whereas agglutination combines morphemes that each retain a single clear meaning. Synthetic languages stand in contrast to analytic languages, which convey relationships mainly through word order and separate helper words.

Degrees and types

The degree of synthesis is measured by the morpheme-to-word ratio: the higher the ratio, the more synthetic the language. Fusional languages (e.g., Latin, Russian, German) pack several grammatical categories into single endings that are not easily segmented; agglutinative languages (e.g., Turkish, Finnish, Japanese) string together transparent affixes; polysynthetic languages (e.g., Mohawk, Inuktitut, many languages of the Americas and Siberia) build whole sentences into single long words incorporating nouns into verbs.

Examples

In Latin, amō "I love" encodes person, number, tense, mood and voice in one word, where English requires separate pronouns and auxiliaries. Most Indo-European languages were historically more synthetic than their modern descendants; English and French have become relatively analytic over time, while Lithuanian and Icelandic retain rich inflection.
