Ḥasan Ibn al-Haytham (Latinized as Alhazen; c. 965 – c. 1040) was an Arab mathematician, astronomer, and physicist of the Islamic Golden Age. He is often described as the "father of optics" and a pioneer of the scientific method, and he made significant contributions to the principles of vision, optics, astronomy, mathematics, and experimental physics.

Book of Optics

His seven-volume Book of Optics (Kitāb al-Manāẓir, 1011–1021) revolutionised the understanding of vision and light. Ibn al-Haytham demonstrated experimentally that vision occurs when light enters the eye — refuting both Euclid's and Ptolemy's emission theory and the Platonic intromission model. He studied the camera obscura, refraction, reflection from spherical and parabolic mirrors, and the anatomy of the eye. Translated into Latin as De aspectibus, the work shaped Western optics through Roger Bacon, Witelo, and Kepler.

Scientific method and other work

Ibn al-Haytham insisted that hypotheses be tested by experiment and mathematics, a stance often cited as an early articulation of the modern scientific method. In mathematics he worked on number theory and geometry — including "Alhazen's problem" of reflection from a spherical mirror — and in astronomy he critiqued Ptolemy's models in his Doubts Concerning Ptolemy. According to legend, he feigned madness to escape the Fatimid caliph al-Ḥākim's demand that he regulate the flooding of the Nile, and lived under house arrest in Cairo writing for the rest of his life.
