Helminth

Helminths are a group of parasitic worms, not a single taxonomic clade, that infect humans and other animals. The name derives from the Greek helmins (worm). The medically important groups are the roundworms (phylum Nematoda), the flukes (class Trematoda) and the tapeworms (class Cestoda), the last two both flatworms (phylum Platyhelminthes). Acanthocephalans (thorny-headed worms) are also sometimes included. Helminths are macroparasites—large, multicellular organisms visible to the naked eye as adults—which typically live in the intestine, blood, liver, lungs or other tissues of their hosts.

Classification and life cycles

  • Nematodes: intestinal roundworms such as Ascaris lumbricoides, hookworms (Necator, Ancylostoma), whipworm and pinworm; tissue forms include the filarial worms causing lymphatic filariasis and onchocerciasis, and Trichinella.
  • Trematodes: blood flukes (Schistosoma), liver flukes (Fasciola, Clonorchis) and lung flukes (Paragonimus), almost all using snails as intermediate hosts.
  • Cestodes: tapeworms such as Taenia species and Echinococcus, whose larval cysts cause hydatid disease.

Disease burden

Helminthiases are among the most common infections worldwide; soil-transmitted helminths alone infect well over a billion people, predominantly children in tropical and subtropical regions with poor sanitation, and schistosomiasis affects over 200 million. Most infections are chronic and relatively low-grade, but heavy burdens cause anaemia, malnutrition, impaired growth and cognition, organ damage and, for some species (e.g., Clonorchis, Schistosoma haematobium), cancer. Treatment uses anthelmintic drugs such as albendazole, mebendazole, praziquantel and ivermectin, often delivered in preventive mass drug administration campaigns. Because helminths strongly modulate host immunity, exposures have also been studied in relation to allergy and autoimmune disease (the "hygiene hypothesis") and even in experimental helminthic therapy.
