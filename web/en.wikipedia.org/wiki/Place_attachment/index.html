Place attachment

Place attachment
FieldEnvironmental psychology, human geography
Key componentsPlace identity, place dependence
Influential worksTuan (1974) Topophilia; Proshansky et al. on place identity; Scannell & Gifford (2010) tripartite model

Place attachment is the emotional bond between a person and a place, studied in environmental psychology and human geography. It describes the positive affective connection people form with places that are meaningful to them — homes, neighbourhoods, landscapes, and sacred sites — and which contributes to their sense of identity, security, and belonging.

Concepts and dimensions

Two dimensions are most commonly distinguished. Place identity is the extent to which a place becomes part of a person's self-concept, formed through memories, experiences, and personal milestones associated with it. Place dependence is a functional attachment: the degree to which a place serves a person's goals and activities well compared with alternatives — a climbing crag for a climber, or a harbour for a fisher. Scannell and Gifford's tripartite framework organises the literature around three Ps: the person (individual or group attachment), the psychological process (affect, cognition, behaviour), and the place itself (its social and physical characteristics).

Development and consequences

Attachment grows with length of residence, home ownership, social ties in a community, and repeated positive experiences, and can arise at scales from a childhood bedroom to a nation. Strong place attachment is associated with greater civic engagement, pro-environmental behaviour toward the place, and willingness to protect it, but also with distress when the place is threatened or lost — the grief for a changing home environment has been termed solastalgia — and with resistance to beneficial change. Displacement by disaster, development, or war can sever attachments with lasting psychological effects.

Related ideas

The geographer Yi-Fu Tuan coined the term topophilia for the affective bond between people and place, and Edward Relph explored insideness and the threat of "placelessness". Place attachment overlaps with sense of place, place identity (in urban planning), and rootedness, and informs fields from heritage conservation and urban design to climate adaptation and resettlement policy.
