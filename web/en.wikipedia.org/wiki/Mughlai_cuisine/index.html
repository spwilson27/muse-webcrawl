Mughlai cuisine

Mughlai cuisine consists of dishes developed in the imperial kitchens of the Mughal Empire and the royal kitchens of regional successor courts such as Awadh and Hyderabad. It represents a synthesis of Central Asian and Persian culinary traditions with the ingredients and techniques of the Indian subcontinent, and it profoundly influenced the cooking of North India, Pakistan and Bangladesh — the rich, aromatic, restaurant-style "Mughlai" food of Delhi, Lucknow, Lahore and Dhaka descends from it.

History

The Mughals, a Turco-Mongol dynasty ruling much of India from 1526, brought with them the Persianate court culture of kebabs, pilafs and dried-fruit garnishes. Under Akbar and his successors the imperial kitchen (matbakh) became a vast institution blending Persian, Central Asian and Indian cooks; dishes were documented in the Ain-i-Akbari and later cookbooks such as the Nuskha-i-Shahjahani. Regional courts refined the tradition: Lucknow's Awadhi cooks perfected the slow sealed-pot dum pukht method and galouti kebabs, while Hyderabad under the Nizams produced its own biryani and haleem.

Characteristics and dishes

Mughlai food is characterised by rich gravies based on butter, cream, ground nuts and yoghurt; the use of aromatic whole and ground spices — saffron, cardamom, mace, nutmeg, cinnamon, cloves — rather than fiery heat; tandoor and skewer roasting; and garnishes of almonds, pistachios and silver leaf (varq). Signature dishes include biryani and pulao; kebabs (seekh, shami, galouti, kakori); korma and pasanda curries; chicken tikka masala's Mughlai ancestors; the egg-and-mince keema; rich breads such as sheermal and bakarkhani; and desserts including shahi tukra, kulfi, falooda and phirni. The samosa and jalebi, both of Middle Eastern origin transmitted through this milieu, became universal South Asian foods.

Legacy

Mughlai cuisine spread worldwide with the South Asian diaspora and the curry-house restaurant trade; in Britain and North America, dishes labeled "Mughlai" typically denote mild, creamy, nut-thickened curries in the imperial style. In India the tradition survives most vigorously in Old Delhi, Lucknow, Hyderabad and Kolkata.
