Lead time bias

Lead time bias
FieldEpidemiology; screening
Related biasesLength time bias; overdiagnosis

Lead time in healthcare is the length of time between the detection of a disease (usually by new, experimental criteria) and its usual clinical presentation and diagnosis (based on traditional criteria). Lead time bias is the systematic error that occurs when comparing survival times between screened and unscreened groups: earlier detection appears to increase survival, even when the course of the disease and the time of death are unchanged.

For example, if a cancer is detected by screening at age 60, would have presented clinically at 65, and causes death at 70 either way, the screened patient appears to survive 10 years after diagnosis versus 5 years for the unscreened patient — yet nothing about the outcome has improved. The "extra" five years are simply the lead time.

Consequences

Lead time bias can make screening programmes look effective in observational comparisons even when they do not reduce mortality. The appropriate evaluation of screening is therefore by randomised controlled trials using disease-specific or all-cause mortality as the endpoint, rather than survival from diagnosis. Related pitfalls include length time bias (screening preferentially detects slow-growing disease) and overdiagnosis (detection of disease that would never have caused symptoms).

Correction

Where randomisation is impossible, analysts may estimate the lead time distribution (often modelled from the preclinical detectable phase, or sojourn time) and subtract it from observed survival, or compare mortality rates instead of survival after diagnosis.
