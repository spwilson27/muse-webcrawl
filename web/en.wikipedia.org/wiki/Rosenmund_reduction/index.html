Rosenmund reduction

The Rosenmund reduction is a hydrogenation process in organic chemistry in which an acyl chloride is selectively reduced to an aldehyde over a poisoned palladium catalyst. It is named after the German chemist Karl Wilhelm Rosenmund, who reported it in 1918.

Reaction

The substrate is treated with hydrogen gas in the presence of palladium on barium sulfate (Pd/BaSO4), often with an added catalyst poison such as sulfur compounds, quinoline-S, or thiourea. The poison moderates the catalyst's activity so that reduction stops at the aldehyde stage instead of proceeding to the primary alcohol or hydrocarbon. Dry conditions are required, since water would hydrolyse the acyl chloride to the carboxylic acid.

Significance

Before modern hydride reagents, the Rosenmund reduction was one of the few practical routes from carboxylic acid derivatives to aldehydes, making it valuable in synthesis. It remains a textbook example of catalyst poisoning used constructively. Diisobutylaluminium hydride (DIBAL-H) reduction of esters and nitriles is often preferred today, but the Rosenmund method is still used industrially for certain substrates.
