Pseudoword

A pseudoword is a unit of speech or text that appears to be an actual word in a certain language, while in fact it has no meaning in the lexicon. It is a kind of non-word: specifically, a string of letters that obeys the phonotactic and orthographic rules of a language — and so can be pronounced like a real word — without being one. Examples in English include "bleg", "tove" and "wug".

Use in research

Pseudowords are widely used in psycholinguistics and reading research. In lexical-decision tasks, participants must distinguish real words from pseudowords, and response times reveal how words are recognised and stored. Because pseudowords cannot be read from memory, reading them aloud tests decoding (grapheme–phoneme conversion), which makes pseudoword reading a sensitive measure in the diagnosis of dyslexia. Jean Berko Gleason's famous 1958 "wug test" used pseudowords to show that children learn morphological rules rather than merely memorising forms.

Construction

Researchers generate pseudowords by altering one or a few letters of real words, recombining morphemes, or using computational generators constrained by letter patterns. Care is taken that the item is not accidentally a real word, a proper name, or homophonous with an existing word (such items are sometimes called pseudohomophones, e.g. "brane").
