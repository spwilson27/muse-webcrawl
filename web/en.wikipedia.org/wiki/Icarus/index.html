In Greek mythology, Icarus (Ancient Greek: Ἴκαρος) is the son of the master craftsman Daedalus, the architect of the labyrinth of Crete. Icarus and Daedalus were imprisoned in Crete by King Minos; to escape, Daedalus fashioned wings of feathers and wax. Ignoring his father's warning not to fly too close to the sun, Icarus soared high, the wax melted, and he fell into the sea and drowned. The myth is a famous warning against hubris.

Myth

Daedalus crafted two pairs of wings from feathers held together with wax, and warned Icarus not to fly too low (lest the sea's moisture clog the feathers) nor too high (lest the sun melt the wax). Icarus, overcome by the thrill of flight, soared too high; the wax melted, the feathers came loose, and he plunged into the sea near an island, later called Icaria, in the sea named the Icarian Sea after him.

The tale is told by several ancient authors, most fully by the Roman poet Ovid in the Metamorphoses, and it appears in Apollodorus and Hyginus as well.

In art and culture

The fall of Icarus has been a favourite subject in art: a famous Landscape with the Fall of Icarus, long attributed to Pieter Bruegel the Elder, shows the world going about its business indifferent to the tragedy — a theme taken up in W. H. Auden's poem "Musée des Beaux Arts" (1938). The myth has become an enduring emblem of overambition and of the perils of both disobedience and excessive daring.
