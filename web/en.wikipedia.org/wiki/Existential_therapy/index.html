Existential therapy explores freedom, responsibility, and the search for meaning.

Existential therapy is a form of psychotherapy based on the model of human nature and experience developed by the existential tradition of philosophy and psychology. It holds that exploring the realities of existence — mortality, freedom and responsibility, isolation, and the search for meaning — is central to psychological health, and that much human distress arises from avoiding these "givens" of existence.

History

The approach grew out of the work of European psychiatrists Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss, who applied Heidegger's philosophy clinically, and Viktor Frankl, whose logotherapy (developed before and after his survival of the Holocaust and set out in Man's Search for Meaning, 1946) centred therapy on the "will to meaning". In the United States, Rollo May popularised existential psychology, and Irvin D. Yalom's textbook Existential Psychotherapy (1980) organised the field around four ultimate concerns: death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness. In Britain, Emmy van Deurzen developed a philosophical counselling model and founded training programmes in existential therapy.

Practice

Existential therapists generally do not treat the client as "ill" nor focus on symptom removal or diagnostic labels; instead, therapy is a philosophical investigation of the client's way of being in the world, conducted in an authentic, present-centred therapeutic relationship. Clients are encouraged to take responsibility for their choices, confront anxiety as a signal of unlived possibilities, and construct meaning in the face of life's uncertainties. The approach overlaps with, but is distinct from, humanistic therapies such as person-centred therapy and Gestalt therapy.

Criticism

Critics note the relative scarcity of controlled outcome research compared with cognitive behavioural therapy and question the approach's sometimes abstract philosophical language; supporters respond that it addresses dimensions of suffering — grief, life transitions, terminal illness, loss of meaning — that symptom-focused models may neglect.
