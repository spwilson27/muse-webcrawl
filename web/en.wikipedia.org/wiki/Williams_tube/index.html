Williams tube

TypeComputer memory
InventorsFreddie Williams, Tom Kilburn
Developed1946–1947, University of Manchester
First usedManchester Baby (1948)

The Williams tube, or Williams–Kilburn tube, was a cathode-ray tube device used as random-access memory in some of the earliest stored-program electronic computers. Developed by Freddie Williams and Tom Kilburn at the University of Manchester in 1946–47, it was the first practical high-speed electronic storage device for digital data.

Operation

The tube stored bits as patterns of electrostatic charge deposited on the phosphor screen of a standard cathode-ray tube by its electron beam. A dot or a short dash at each grid position represented a binary value; reading a position produced a characteristic voltage pulse on a metal pickup plate mounted against the screen. Because the charge leaked away within a fraction of a second, and because reading a bit disturbed its neighbours, each stored "page" had to be continually scanned and regenerated — an early form of memory refresh, conceptually similar to modern DRAM.

Use

A single tube typically stored 512 to 2,048 bits. The Williams tube was the main memory of the Manchester Baby, which ran the world's first stored program in June 1948, and of its successor the Manchester Mark 1. The technology was also adopted in the United States on machines such as the IAS machine at Princeton and the IBM 701. Though delicate and sensitive to electrical noise, the Williams tube remained in use until magnetic core memory displaced it in the mid-1950s.
