Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan

Abd al-Malik ibn Marwan ibn al-Hakam (Arabic: عبد الملك ابن مروان; c. 644/647 – 9 October 705) was the fifth Umayyad caliph, ruling from 685 until his death. Succeeding his father Marwan I amid the Second Muslim Civil War (the Second Fitna), he reunified the caliphate by defeating his rivals — the Zubayrids in the Hejaz and Iraq, the Alid loyalists around Mukhtar al-Thaqafi, and the Kharijites — by 692. He is often called the "second founder" of the Umayyad Caliphate.

Reforms

Abd al-Malik's reign transformed the Islamic state. He replaced Byzantine and Sasanian coinage in circulation with a distinctly Islamic, epigraphic gold dinar (from 696–97) and silver dirham bearing Qur'anic phrases and the caliph's authority, removing figural imagery. He made Arabic the language of administration (diwan) throughout the empire, displacing Greek and Persian bureaucratic practice, and centralised tax and military administration. He reorganised the postal service (barid) and the standing armies of Syria.

Building works

His best-known monument is the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, completed in 691–692 — the oldest surviving monument of Islamic architecture, with inscriptions proclaiming Islamic doctrine. Building and renovation works are also associated with the al-Aqsa Mosque precinct and with road works marked by the "caliph's milestones".

Death and succession

Abd al-Malik died in 705 and was succeeded by his son al-Walid I, under whom the empire reached its greatest extent. Of his sons, four — al-Walid I, Sulayman, Yazid II and Hisham — eventually ruled as caliphs, an unusual concentration of succession within one family.
