Luxury goods

In economics, a luxury good (or upmarket good) is a good for which demand increases more than proportionally as income rises, so that expenditure on the good becomes a greater proportion of overall spending. Luxury goods stand in contrast to necessity goods, whose demand rises less than proportionally with income.

Characteristics

Luxury goods typically combine high quality, durability and aesthetic appeal with scarcity, exclusivity and a prestigious brand image; they command premium prices well above what their functional qualities alone would justify. Some function as Veblen goods, where a higher price itself increases desirability as a status signal. Typical categories include haute couture and accessories, fine jewellery and watches, perfumes and cosmetics, premium automobiles, yachts, wines and spirits, and high-end travel and hospitality.

Market

The modern luxury market is dominated by conglomerates such as LVMH, Kering and Richemont, which assemble portfolios of heritage brands. Global personal luxury goods sales amount to several hundred billion euros annually, with growth driven in recent decades by consumers in China and other emerging markets, by younger buyers, and by the expansion of "accessible luxury" lines. Counterfeiting and grey-market resale are persistent concerns for luxury brands.
