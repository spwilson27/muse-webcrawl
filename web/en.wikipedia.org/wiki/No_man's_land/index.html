No man's land

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


No man's land on the Western Front

No man's land is land that is unoccupied or under dispute between parties who leave it unoccupied out of fear or uncertainty. The term was first used in medieval England (recorded in the Domesday Book era as nonesmanneslond) for ground outside London's walls used for executions, and later for any unclaimed or contested territory. It became indelibly associated with the First World War, where it described the devastated strip of ground between opposing trench systems.

First World War

On the Western Front, no man's land varied in width from a few metres to several hundred. It was typically cratered by shellfire, tangled with barbed wire, strewn with debris and the bodies of the dead, and swept by machine-gun and artillery fire. Crossing it in a frontal assault was the central tactical problem of trench warfare; soldiers also ventured into it at night for patrols, raids, listening posts, and wiring parties. The Christmas truce of 1914 famously saw British and German troops meet in no man's land to exchange gifts and play football.

Other uses

The term has been applied to disputed or unclaimed territories such as Bir Tawil between Egypt and Sudan, to the demilitarised zones between North and South Korea, and metaphorically to any ambiguous or contested area of law, science, or social life. In the Cold War, the mined and fortified strip along the inner German border was similarly described.
