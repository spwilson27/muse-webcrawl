Polychloroprene

Polychloroprene
Repeat unit: (C4H5Cl)n
Best known trade name: Neoprene
Type: synthetic rubber (elastomer)

Polychloroprene is a family of synthetic rubbers produced by polymerization of chloroprene (2-chloro-1,3-butadiene). It is sold under the trade name Neoprene, developed by DuPont scientists — from work by Wallace Carothers' group, with the key discovery credited to Arnold Collins in 1930 — and marketed from 1931 as one of the first successful synthetic rubbers.

Properties

Polychloroprene exhibits good chemical stability, resistance to oils, ozone, weathering, and flame, and maintains flexibility over a wide temperature range. It crystallizes on stretching, giving it good tensile strength. It can be vulcanized using metal oxides (typically zinc oxide and magnesium oxide) rather than sulfur alone, and can be foamed with gas to produce closed-cell foam rubber.

Applications

  • Wetsuits and drysuits (foamed neoprene)
  • Gaskets, hoses, belts, and vibration-damping mounts
  • Laptop sleeves, orthopedic braces, and gloves
  • Electrical insulation and cable jackets
  • Adhesives and coated fabrics
  • Racket sports grips and mouse pads

Production and safety

Chloroprene is produced industrially from butadiene via chlorination and dehydrochlorination, or historically from acetylene. Chloroprene is classified as a probable human carcinogen, and emissions from polychloroprene manufacturing plants — notably the Denka facility in Louisiana's "Cancer Alley" — have been the subject of environmental regulation and community litigation.
