Promissory note

A promissory note, sometimes called a note payable, is a legal instrument in which one party (the maker or issuer) makes a written, unconditional promise to pay a definite sum of money to another party (the payee), either on demand or at a specified future date, on stated terms. It is a form of negotiable instrument, akin to a bill of exchange but simpler: a note is a two-party promise to pay, whereas a bill of exchange is a three-party order to pay.

Legal requirements

Under instruments such as the UK Bills of Exchange Act 1882 and Article 3 of the US Uniform Commercial Code, a valid note generally requires: a promise in writing, signed by the maker; an unconditional undertaking to pay a sum certain in money; payment to, or to the order of, a specified person or to bearer; and a maturity on demand or at a fixed or determinable future time. Notes may bear interest, provide for instalments, and be secured (for example by a mortgage or deed of trust) or unsecured.

Uses

Promissory notes are widely used in private and commercial lending: mortgage notes accompanying home loans, student loans, business and inter-company loans, and loans between individuals. In corporate finance, large short-term unsecured notes are issued as commercial paper. Because they are negotiable, notes can be endorsed and transferred to third parties, who may become holders in due course with rights free of certain defences.

History

Circulating credit instruments resembling promissory notes were used in Tang-dynasty China ("flying money"), in the medieval Islamic world, and by European bankers from the Italian city-states onward; banknotes themselves began as promissory notes issued by banks payable to bearer on demand.
