Kurds

Kurds
Total population: 30–45 million
Regions: Turkey, Iran, Iraq, Syria
Languages: Kurdish (Kurmanji, Sorani), Zaza, Gorani
Religion: Predominantly Sunni Islam, also Alevi, Yazidi, Christianity

The Kurds are an Iranian ethnic group native to Kurdistan, a mountainous region spanning southeastern Turkey, western Iran, northern Iraq and northern Syria. They form one of the largest stateless ethnic groups in the world.

History

Kurdish identity coalesced in the medieval period, though peoples such as the Medes are sometimes cited as ancestors. Under the Ottoman and Safavid empires, Kurdish principalities enjoyed varying autonomy. After World War I, the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) envisaged a Kurdish state, but the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) divided Kurdish lands among Turkey, Iraq, Syria and Iran.

In Iraq, Kurds suffered the Anfal campaign and the Halabja chemical attack in 1988, but gained an autonomous region after 1991 and constitutional recognition in 2005. In Syria, Kurdish-led forces established the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria during the civil war. In Turkey, a long conflict between the state and the PKK began in 1984.

Language and culture

Kurdish languages belong to the northwestern Iranian branch of Indo-European. Kurmanji is written in Latin script, Sorani in an adapted Arabic script. Newroz, celebrated on 21 March, is the central Kurdish festival. See also Kurdistan and Mustafa Barzani.

Diaspora

Large diaspora communities live in Germany, France, Sweden and elsewhere in Europe, formed by labour migration and refugee movements.
