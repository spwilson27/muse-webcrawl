Samudragupta (reigned c. 335–375 CE) was the second ruler of the Gupta Empire of ancient India and is regarded as one of its greatest military commanders. Through campaigns across the subcontinent he transformed a regional kingdom into an empire, earning from the historian Vincent Smith the label "the Napoleon of India".

Sources

The main source for his reign is the Prayaga Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar inscription), a panegyric composed by his court poet Harisena and inscribed on an Ashokan pillar at Prayaga. His gold coins also provide information about his conquests and interests.

Conquests

According to the inscription, Samudragupta uprooted numerous kings of Aryavarta (northern India) and annexed their territories, while the kings of Dakshinapatha (the Deccan and south) were defeated, captured and then released as tributaries. Frontier kingdoms and tribal republics accepted his suzerainty, and distant powers including the Kushan-Shahs, the Shakas and the ruler of Simhala (Sri Lanka) sent embassies and gifts.

Administration and culture

Samudragupta performed the ashvamedha horse sacrifice to proclaim his imperial status and issued an extensive gold coinage. His coins depicting him playing the veena reflect his reputation as a musician and patron of the arts; the inscription calls him a poet as well as a warrior. A Vaishnavite himself, he was tolerant of other religions. He was succeeded, most likely, by his son Chandragupta II.
