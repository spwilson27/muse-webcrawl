Ferroconcrete

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ferroconcrete is a building material in which steel reinforcement — bars, mesh, or cables — is embedded in concrete so that the two materials act together to resist forces. The modern term is reinforced concrete, and "ferroconcrete" survives mainly in older literature and in some regional usage. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension (about a tenth of its compressive strength); steel is strong in tension, and because the two materials bond well and expand at nearly the same rate with temperature, embedding steel where tensile stresses occur produces a composite of great versatility. Reinforced concrete, together with its development prestressed concrete, is the most widely used structural material in the world, employed in buildings, bridges, dams, tunnels, and roads.

History

The composite was invented in the mid-19th century. French gardener Joseph Monier patented flowerpots and then panels of concrete reinforced with iron mesh from 1867, extending his patents to bridges and beams. In England, William Wilkinson had embedded wire ropes in concrete floors as early as 1854; in France, François Coignet built reinforced structures and Joseph-Louis Lambot made a celebrated ferro-cement boat (1848). The German firm of Gustav Adolf Wayss bought Monier's patents in the 1880s and developed systematic engineering design methods, while in the United States Ernest L. Ransome pioneered twisted-bar reinforcement and the reinforced-concrete skyscraper frame (the Ingalls Building, Cincinnati, 1903). Eugène Freyssinet later developed prestressing, and engineers such as Pier Luigi Nervi and Robert Maillart showed that ferroconcrete could achieve thin, sculptural forms impossible in masonry or steel alone.

Behaviour and design

In a reinforced-concrete beam, concrete resists compression on one face while steel bars near the opposite face resist tension; cracks in the concrete on the tension side are expected and are controlled by distributing the reinforcement. Shear is carried by stirrups and bent-up bars. Durability hinges on protecting the steel from corrosion: the alkaline concrete normally passivates the steel surface, but carbonation and chloride ingress (from de-icing salt or seawater) can break down passivation, causing rust that expands and spalls the concrete cover — the principal degradation mechanism of older ferroconcrete structures. Design codes specify minimum concrete cover, crack widths, and bar details, and modern practice supplements steel with fibres, stainless or galvanized reinforcement, cathodic protection, or prestressing in aggressive environments.

Uses

Ferroconcrete is used wherever a mouldable, fire-resistant, heavy material is advantageous: foundations and frames of buildings, bridges and flyovers, dams, cooling towers, silos, chimneys, tunnels, pavements, marine structures, and precast elements from railway sleepers to pipes. Its raw ingredients — cement, aggregates, water, and steel — are available nearly everywhere, making it the default structural material of global construction.
