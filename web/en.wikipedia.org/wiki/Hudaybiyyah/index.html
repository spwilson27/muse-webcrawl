Hudaybiyyah

Al-Hudaybiyyah, on the outskirts of Mecca.

Al-Hudaybiyyah (Arabic: ٱلْحُدَيْبِيَّة) is a locality on the western outskirts of Mecca, in present-day Saudi Arabia, famous as the site of the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah concluded in 628 CE (6 AH) between the Prophet Muhammad and the Quraysh of Mecca.

The treaty

In 628 Muhammad led some 1,400 followers towards Mecca intending to perform the pilgrimage (umrah), unarmed and in pilgrim dress. The Quraysh blocked their advance at al-Hudaybiyyah, just outside the sacred territory. After negotiations — during which Muhammad's envoy Uthman was rumoured murdered, prompting the Pledge of the Tree (Bay'at al-Ridwan) from Muhammad's companions — the two sides agreed a treaty with the Quraysh envoy Suhayl ibn Amr.

Its terms included a ten-year truce; the Muslims would return to Medina that year but could come for a three-day pilgrimage the following year; tribes could ally freely with either side; and any Meccan who came to Muhammad without his guardian's permission would be returned, while the reverse would not apply. Many companions found the terms humiliating, but Muhammad presented the outcome as a victory — the Qur'an's chapter 48, Al-Fath ("Victory"), is traditionally associated with the event.

Aftermath

The truce let Islam spread peacefully; within two years the Muslim community grew greatly. In 630, after allies of the Quraysh attacked allies of Muhammad in violation of the treaty, Muhammad marched on Mecca and took the city almost without bloodshed. Hudaybiyyah remains a landmark in Islamic history and diplomacy, and the site, where a modern mosque stands, lies on the old Jeddah–Mecca road.
