Gyōza (ギョーザ or 餃子) are Japanese dumplings derived from the Chinese jiaozi, consisting of a thin wheat wrapper filled with ground meat and vegetables, crimped at the edges. They became widespread in Japan after the Second World War, popularised in part by soldiers returning from Manchuria, and are now a staple of ramen shops, izakaya and home cooking.

Preparation

The standard filling combines ground pork with finely chopped cabbage, garlic chives (nira), garlic and ginger, seasoned with soy sauce, sake and sesame oil. Japanese gyōza wrappers are thinner than those of Chinese jiaozi, and garlic is used more assertively. The most popular style is yaki-gyōza: the dumplings are fried in a pan until their bottoms crisp, then steamed by adding water and covering the pan, and finally crisped again — combining fried, steamed and crisp textures. Other styles are sui-gyōza (boiled) and age-gyōza (deep-fried). They are typically dipped in a mixture of soy sauce and rice vinegar with chilli oil.

Regional culture

The cities of Utsunomiya (Tochigi) and Hamamatsu (Shizuoka) are famous rivals for the title of Japan's gyōza capital, competing in annual per-household consumption statistics; Utsunomiya maintains gyōza-themed tourism including a statue and dedicated restaurant streets. Gyōza are a classic accompaniment to ramen and beer, and a standard side dish in Japanese Chinese-style (chūka) restaurants.
