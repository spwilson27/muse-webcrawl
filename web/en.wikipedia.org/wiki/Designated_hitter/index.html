Designated hitter

The designated hitter (DH) is a role in baseball that allows a team to have a player bat in place of another player, most commonly the pitcher, without that player being required to play a defensive position. The rule was adopted by Major League Baseball's American League in 1973 and by the National League in 2022, and is used in most professional and many amateur leagues around the world, though some leagues — notably Japan's Pacific League uses it while the Central League does not — still differ.

Major League Baseball

The American League adopted the designated hitter beginning with the 1973 season, in order to increase offence and attendance; Ron Blomberg of the New York Yankees became the first DH to bat in a regular-season game on 6 April 1973. For decades the rule was a defining difference between the two leagues, applying in World Series and All-Star games according to the home team's league until 2022, when the National League adopted the DH permanently as part of a new collective bargaining agreement, creating a "universal DH".

Under MLB rules the DH is optional: a team may decline to use one and let its pitcher bat. If a DH is used, he bats for the pitcher throughout the game; if the DH later takes a defensive position, the team forfeits the DH for the remainder of the game. A rule adopted in 2022 and commonly called the "Ohtani rule" allows a starting pitcher who is also in the batting order as DH to remain as DH after being removed as pitcher.

Notable designated hitters

Several players have built celebrated careers primarily as designated hitters, including Edgar Martínez — for whom the annual award for the best DH is named — David Ortiz, Frank Thomas, Harold Baines and Shohei Ohtani. Ortiz and Martínez are among the DHs inducted into the National Baseball Hall of Fame, an honour once considered unlikely for players who rarely played the field.

Debate

The DH has been one of baseball's most debated rules. Supporters argue it produces more offence, extends the careers of ageing sluggers and removes the near-automatic out of the pitcher's batting spot; critics contend it removes strategic elements such as the sacrifice bunt, the double switch and decisions about when to remove a pitcher for a pinch hitter.
