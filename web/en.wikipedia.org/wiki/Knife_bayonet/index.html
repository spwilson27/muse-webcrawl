Knife bayonet

A knife bayonet doubles as a general-purpose fighting and utility knife when detached.

A knife bayonet is a type of bayonet equipped with a full knife handle and a blade suitable for use as a fighting knife or utility tool when detached from the rifle. It contrasts with earlier spike bayonets and sword bayonets, which were dedicated thrusting weapons of little use in the hand. Knife bayonets became the dominant pattern during the twentieth century as infantry tactics changed and soldiers required a practical camp and combat knife.

Development

Early bayonets were plug, socket, or sword types. By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, armies began issuing shorter "knife" patterns, such as the German Seitengewehr and the British Pattern 1907 (a sword bayonet in early form). In the Second World War, shorter blades proved handier, and designs such as the American M4 bayonet — derived from the M3 fighting knife — set the pattern: a knife first, a bayonet second. The M7 and M9 bayonets continued the concept, the M9 adding wire-cutting and saw features as part of a multi-purpose tool.

Design

A typical knife bayonet has a blade of roughly 15–20 cm (6–8 in), a crossguard incorporating a muzzle ring, a handle of wood, plastic, or composite with a pommel latch that locks onto the rifle's bayonet lug, and a scabbard often designed to combine with the blade as wire cutters. When fixed, it serves for close combat; detached, it serves for cutting, digging-in tasks, opening ration tins, and survival uses. Examples include the AK bayonets of the Kalashnikov family, the British L3A1, and the German KCB-77.

Modern usage

Although bayonet charges are rare in modern warfare, knife bayonets remain standard issue in most armies because of their utility role. Training with the bayonet is retained in many forces for aggression and morale, and the weapon remains in use for ceremonial purposes and riot control in some contexts.
