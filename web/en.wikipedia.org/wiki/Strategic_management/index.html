Strategic management

Strategic management is the formulation and implementation of the major goals and initiatives taken by an organization's managers on behalf of stakeholders, based on consideration of resources and an assessment of the internal and external environments in which the organization operates. Strategic management provides overall direction to an enterprise and involves specifying the organization's objectives, developing policies and plans to achieve those objectives, and then allocating resources to implement the plans.

Overview

Strategic management involves the related concepts of strategic planning and strategic thinking. Strategic planning is analytical in nature and refers to formalized procedures to produce the data and analyses used as inputs for strategic thinking, which synthesizes the data to produce the strategy. Strategic management may also refer to the activities of those within an organization who formulate and execute strategy.

Academics and practicing managers have developed numerous models and frameworks to assist in strategic decision-making in the context of complex environments and competitive dynamics. Strategic management is not static in nature; the models can include a feedback loop to monitor execution and to inform the next round of planning.

History

The discipline has its roots in the 1950s and 1960s. Among the early contributors, Alfred Chandler is a key figure. Chandler recognized the importance of coordinating management activity under an all-encompassing strategy. He argued that a long-term strategy was necessary to give a company structure, direction and focus, famously stating that "structure follows strategy." Igor Ansoff built on Chandler's ideas, adding concepts such as the product-market growth matrix and the idea that strategy requires explicit strategic planning processes.

Formulation

Strategy formulation involves analysing the environment in which the organization operates, then making a series of strategic decisions about how the organization will compete. Widely used tools include SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats), PEST analysis of the macro-environment, and Michael Porter's five forces framework for analysing industry attractiveness and competitive position. Porter's generic strategies — cost leadership, differentiation, and focus — remain canonical choices at the business level, complemented by corporate-level decisions about diversification, vertical integration, and international expansion.

Implementation and evaluation

Strategy implementation translates plans into action: aligning organizational structure, culture, leadership, and control systems with the chosen strategy. Evaluation measures performance against objectives and feeds back into a new round of analysis. Henry Mintzberg distinguished intended (deliberate) strategy from emergent strategy that arises from patterns of decisions over time, arguing that real-world strategy is usually a blend of the two.
