Lysippos (Greek: Λύσιππος; c. 390 – c. 300 BC) was a Greek sculptor of the 4th century BC, regarded with Scopas and Praxiteles as one of the three great sculptors of the Classical Greek age. He was the personal sculptor of Alexander the Great and revolutionised the canon of human proportions, producing some 1,500 bronze statues according to tradition, though none survives in the original bronze.

Life

Lysippos was born at Sicyon in the Peloponnese and, according to Pliny, began as a bronze-worker's apprentice, teaching himself by studying the Doryphoros of Polykleitos and declaring that nature, not any artist, was his teacher. He became the favoured portraitist of Alexander, who is said to have decreed that only Lysippos should sculpt him, as only Apelles should paint him. His workshop was enormous and continued under his sons and pupils, including Chares of Lindos, creator of the Colossus of Rhodes.

Style and canon

Lysippos replaced Polykleitos's 1:7 head-to-body canon with a slimmer 1:8 ratio, making figures appear taller and more elegant. He rendered the body in the round — most famously in the Apoxyomenos, an athlete scraping oil from his skin with a strigil, whose outstretched arm breaks into the viewer's space — and paid meticulous attention to surface details such as hair and toenails. Pliny summed up his contribution as the depiction of men "as they appeared to be" rather than "as they were."

Famous works

Known works, surviving in Roman marble copies, include the Apoxyomenos (Vatican Museums; a nearly complete bronze version was recovered from the sea off Croatia in 1996), the weary Herakles known as the Farnese Hercules — signed copies of a Lysippan original — the Hermes of Atalante, and the Agias of Pharsalos from the Daochos monument at Delphi. His bronze Alexander portraits, characterised by the upturned gaze, the leonine hair (anastole) and the slight tilt of the neck, set the template for Hellenistic ruler imagery for centuries.

Legacy

Lysippos bridged Classical and Hellenistic art: his naturalism, multi-viewpoint compositions and heroic portraiture shaped the art of the successor kingdoms and, through Roman copies, the Renaissance masters who studied them.
