Forever wild

"Forever wild"
OriginNew York State Constitution, Article XIV (1894)
Applies toNew York Forest Preserve (Adirondack and Catskill parks)
EffectState forest lands kept "forever wild"; timber may not be sold, removed or destroyed

"Forever wild" is the popular name for the conservation clause of the New York State Constitution — Article XIV, Section 1 — which protects the state's Forest Preserve lands in the Adirondack and Catskill parks. Adopted by constitutional convention in 1894 and approved by voters that year, it provides that the lands "shall be forever kept as wild forest lands" and that the timber thereon may not be "sold, removed or destroyed".

Background

The clause arose from 19th-century alarm over logging, fires and watershed damage in the Adirondacks, where extensive cutting threatened the headwaters of the Hudson River and the Erie Canal's water supply. New York had created the Forest Preserve in 1885 and the Adirondack Park in 1892; the 1894 clause gave the protection constitutional permanence, beyond the reach of ordinary legislation.

Interpretation and amendments

The clause is among the strongest land-protection provisions in any American constitution. Courts and state agencies have read it to prohibit substantial cutting and many forms of development on Forest Preserve land, while permitting trails, lean-tos and similar low-impact facilities. Because the text is constitutional, even modest projects — ski centres, roads, reservoirs, utility lines — have repeatedly required specific constitutional amendments approved by statewide referendum; dozens have been sought over the decades, with mixed results, including amendments for the Whiteface and Gore Mountain ski areas and a 2017 land-account amendment for local infrastructure.

Legacy

The "forever wild" principle made the Adirondack Park a landmark in American conservation history and a model for wilderness protection, influencing later state and federal policy, including debates leading to the federal Wilderness Act of 1964.
