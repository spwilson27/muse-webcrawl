Honky-tonk

A honky-tonk (also called honkatonk, honkey-tonk, or tonk) is a bar that provides country music for the entertainment of its patrons. Bars of this kind are common in the South and Southwest United States. Many eminent country music artists, such as Jimmie Rodgers, Ernest Tubb, Lefty Frizzell, Hank Williams, Patsy Cline, Johnny Horton and George Jones, began in such bars. The term also denotes a style of country music and a type of piano playing.

Etymology and origin

The origin of the term is uncertain. The first known use in print was in 1894, when The Daily Ardmoreite in Oklahoma reported of a "honk-a-tonk" performance. Honky-tonks were rough establishments serving working-class customers in frontier and oil-boom towns, offering alcohol, dancing, and sometimes gambling and prostitution. They were closely related to the barrelhouse and juke joint traditions of Black American music.

Music

Honky-tonk music developed in the 1930s–1950s around the bars of Texas and Oklahoma. Its sound — full band with fiddle, steel guitar, electric guitar and a driving rhythm — was built to be heard over barroom noise, and its lyrics took up drinking, infidelity and lost love. Ernest Tubb's "Walking the Floor Over You" (1941) is often cited as the genre's first hit; Hank Williams, Lefty Frizzell, Webb Pierce and George Jones became its defining stars. The "honky-tonk piano" style, derived from ragtime and adapted to out-of-tune uprights with missing keys, also flourished in these venues.

Decline and revival

As Nashville country polished its commercial sound in the 1960s, classic honky-tonk receded, but outlaw country artists and later revivalists such as Dwight Yoakam kept the style alive. Famous surviving venues include the Lower Broadway honky-tonks of Nashville, where stars like Loretta Lynn and Willie Nelson were discovered, and Billy Bob's Texas in Fort Worth, billed as the world's largest honky-tonk.
