A radio altimeter (or radar altimeter) is an airborne electronic device capable of measuring the height of an aircraft above terrain immediately below it. It is distinguished from a barometric altimeter, which measures altitude relative to a pressure datum such as mean sea level.

Radio altimeters transmit a signal toward the ground and measure the time taken for the reflection to return, or use frequency-modulated continuous wave (FMCW) techniques to determine the distance. They operate in the C band between 4.2 and 4.4 GHz, a band reserved worldwide for this safety-critical function.

Operation

Two antennas are mounted on the underside of the aircraft, one for transmission and one for reception. In an FMCW system, the transmitted frequency is swept linearly; the difference between the transmitted and received frequencies is proportional to the round-trip travel time and hence to the height above the surface. The measurement is most accurate at low altitudes and is used primarily during approach and landing.

Applications

Radio altimeter readings feed several aircraft systems: the ground proximity warning system (GPWS), autoland systems, thrust reverser logic, and cockpit callouts during landing. In military aircraft and missiles, radar altimeters assist terrain-following flight. They are also used on spacecraft during landing phases, including lunar and planetary landers.

5G interference debate

In the early 2020s, the rollout of 5G cellular services in the adjacent 3.7–3.98 GHz band in the United States raised concerns that older radio altimeters, whose receivers may lack adequate filtering, could pick up spurious emissions. The issue led to temporary restrictions around certain airports and an accelerated program of altimeter retrofits and retrofits of protective filters.
