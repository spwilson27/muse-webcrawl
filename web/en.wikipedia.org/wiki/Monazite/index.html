Monazite is a reddish-brown phosphate mineral containing rare-earth elements. Due to variations in composition, the mineral is considered a group with several species, the most common being monazite-(Ce), with the formula (Ce,La,Nd,Th)PO4. It occurs in small isolated grains as an accessory mineral in igneous and metamorphic rocks and in placer deposits concentrated by weathering and transport.

Uses

Monazite is an important ore of thorium and lanthanides such as cerium and lanthanum, and was historically the principal commercial source of rare-earth elements. It typically contains 4–12% thorium oxide, which makes monazite sands mildly radioactive and subject to regulation; the mineral was also once a source of thorium for gas mantles. Major deposits occur in India, Brazil, Australia, South Africa and the United States.

Geochronology

Because monazite incorporates uranium and thorium and largely excludes lead during crystallization, it is widely used for U–Th–Pb radiometric dating of metamorphic rocks, where individual growth zones within a single crystal can record successive metamorphic events.

History

The mineral's name derives from the Greek monazein, "to be solitary," referring to its isolated crystals. In the late nineteenth century, monazite sands became commercially valuable with the invention of the incandescent gas mantle by Carl Auer von Welsbach.
