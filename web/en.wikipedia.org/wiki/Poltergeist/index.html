Poltergeist

In German folklore and ghostlore, a poltergeist (from German poltern "to make sound, to rumble" and Geist "ghost, spirit") is a type of ghost or spirit responsible for physical disturbances, such as loud noises and objects thrown around. Usually, poltergeists are depicted as troublesome spirits who haunt a particular place, object or person.

Most claims about the existence of poltergeists are unproven; many recorded incidents have later been explained by trickery, misperception, children's pranks, or natural phenomena. Parapsychologists have long studied poltergeist cases, often hypothesising "recurrent spontaneous psychokinesis" (RSPK) centred on a living "agent", frequently an adolescent — a model popularised by researcher William G. Roll. Sceptics argue the evidence is consistent with fraud and psychological factors.

Famous cases

Famous alleged cases include the Rosenheim poltergeist in Germany (1967), investigated by parapsychologist Hans Bender; the Enfield poltergeist in London (1977), centred on the Hodgson family and investigated by Maurice Grosse and Guy Lyon Playfair; the Amherst mystery in Nova Scotia (1878–79) involving Esther Cox; and the Thornton Heath case of 1938. The Enfield case inspired the film The Conjuring 2 (2016).

In popular culture

The concept gave its title to the 1982 horror film Poltergeist, produced by Steven Spielberg and directed by Tobe Hooper, in which a suburban family is terrorised by malevolent spirits — summed up by the line "They're here." Poltergeists recur throughout horror literature, film, and games, generally distinguished from traditional ghosts by their noisy physical activity rather than apparitions.

Explanations

Suggested natural explanations include air currents and drafts, underground water movements, seismic activity, static electricity, and infrasound. Psychological explanations include attention-seeking behaviour, hoaxing, and delusion; sceptical investigator Joe Nickell and others have documented confessions and evidence of fraud in prominent cases.
