Hex sign

Hex signs are a form of Pennsylvania Dutch folk art, colorful circular designs painted on barns and other buildings, most famously in Pennsylvania Dutch Country in the eastern United States. Typical motifs include geometric stars, rosettes, tulips, hearts, birds (distaff and distelfink), and borders of scallops or waves.

Origins

The tradition derives from the fraktur and painted decoration of German-speaking immigrants who settled in Pennsylvania in the 17th and 18th centuries. Small painted barn signs appeared by the early 19th century; the large, standardized circular signs spread widely in the early 20th century, helped by commercial painters and pattern books. Similar star and rosette motifs appear on Alpine and European barns.

Meaning

The popular name links the signs to "hex" (from German Hexe, witch), and folklore holds that they ward off evil or bring luck, fertility, and good harvests — an interpretation reinforced by their association with pow-wowing folk magic. Many scholars and painters, however, argue the signs were primarily decorative, expressing ethnic identity and pride, with meanings assigned to colors and symbols largely a later, touristic elaboration.

Modern use

Hex signs remain a regional emblem, sold as souvenirs and painted by folk artists in counties such as Berks, Lancaster, and Lehigh. Artists like Johnny and Eric Claypoole have kept the hand-painted tradition alive, while printed aluminum signs are mass-produced for the tourist trade.
