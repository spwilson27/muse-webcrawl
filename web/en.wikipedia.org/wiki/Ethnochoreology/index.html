Ethnochoreology

Ethnochoreology is the study of dance in its cultural context — the branch of anthropology and dance studies concerned with traditional, folk and social dance forms around the world. The term derives from the Greek ethnos (people) and choreia (dance). Related terms include dance ethnology and, historically, choreology.

Scope and methods

Ethnochoreologists study dance not as an isolated movement vocabulary but as social behaviour embedded in ritual, celebration, identity and politics. Fieldworkers record dances on film and video, transcribe movement using notation systems such as Labanotation and Kinetography Laban, and interview dancers and communities about meaning, transmission and change. The discipline studies how dances migrate and transform, how states use folk-dance ensembles to project national identity, and how traditional forms adapt to stage performance, tourism and diaspora communities.

History

The field took shape in Europe in the early 20th century alongside folk-dance collecting, and in the United States through figures such as Gertrude Prokosch Kurath, who in the 1950s argued for "dance ethnology" as a rigorous anthropological pursuit, studying Native American and Mexican dance. The ICTM (International Council for Traditional Music) Study Group on Ethnochoreology, founded in the early 1960s, became the discipline's principal international forum, with regular symposia and its own yearbook. University programmes, notably in Norway (NTNU), Ireland (University of Limerick) and Hungary, train specialists in the field.
