A truss bridge is a bridge whose load-bearing superstructure is composed of a truss: a structure of connected elements, usually forming triangular units, joined at nodes. In a truss, members primarily carry axial forces—tension or compression—which makes very efficient use of material and allows long spans with relatively little weight. Truss bridges were among the most common bridge types from the 1870s to the 1930s and remain ubiquitous in railway and rural road crossings.

How it works

The top and bottom horizontal members (chords) resist bending by acting as a couple—one in compression, one in tension—while the vertical and diagonal web members transfer shear. In a through truss, traffic passes between two trusses braced overhead; in a deck truss, the roadway sits on top; in a pony truss, the trusses are lower and not connected above. Because loads are axial, truss analysis is comparatively simple (method of joints or sections), a great advantage in the pre-computer era.

Common designs

• Pratt truss (1844): verticals in compression, diagonals (except end posts) in tension—the classic American railway truss.
• Howe truss (1840): diagonals in compression, verticals in tension; suited to timber with iron rods.
• Warren truss: equilateral triangles, sometimes with added verticals.
• Bowstring, Baltimore, Pennsylvania, K-truss and others: later refinements for longer spans.
• Cantilever truss: e.g. the Forth Bridge in Scotland and the Quebec Bridge, among the longest spans of their kind.

History

Timber truss designs such as Theodore Burr's arch-truss and Stephen Long's X-braced truss were patented in the early United States republic and used in thousands of covered bridges. Iron and then steel trusses fuelled the railroad age, and standardised Pratt trusses were mass-produced by bridge companies. Notable failures, such as the Quebec Bridge collapse of 1907 during construction, spurred advances in structural engineering. Few large trusses are built today, but many historic examples are preserved as landmarks.
