Landau theory

Landau theory (also known as Landau–Ginzburg theory) in physics is a theory that Lev Landau introduced in an attempt to formulate a general theory of continuous (second-order) phase transitions. The free energy is expanded as a power series in an order parameter that measures the departure of the system from its symmetric state.

Formulation

For a system near a critical temperature Tc, the free energy density is written as an analytic expansion in the order parameter ψ, with terms constrained by the symmetry of the high-temperature phase. Minimising the free energy gives equilibrium values of ψ and predicts how quantities such as heat capacity and susceptibility vary near the transition. In its simplest form the expansion F = F0 + a(T−Tc)ψ² + bψ⁴ yields mean-field critical exponents.

Extensions

Vitaly Ginzburg and Landau extended the theory by adding gradient terms, producing the Ginzburg–Landau theory used to describe superconductivity and inhomogeneous order parameters. The Landau paradigm, relating phase transitions to symmetry breaking, underlies modern classifications of phases of matter, although fluctuations near the critical point require renormalisation-group methods for quantitative accuracy.
