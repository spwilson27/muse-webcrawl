Precarious work
DefinitionEmployment that is uncertain, unpredictable, low-paid, and lacking in legal and social protections
FormsTemporary and fixed-term contracts, agency work, zero-hours contracts, involuntary part-time work, dependent self-employment, gig/platform work
Related conceptsPrecariat, gig economy, informal economy, standard employment relationship

Precarious work is employment that lacks the security, stability, and protections associated with the standard employment relationship — the full-time, open-ended job with a single employer that anchored postwar labour markets in industrialised countries. Precarious jobs are characterised by some combination of short duration or uncertain hours, low and unpredictable income, absence of social insurance and benefits, weak legal protection against dismissal, and little or no collective representation. The growth of such work since the 1970s — through temporary agency labour, fixed-term and zero-hours contracts, franchising and subcontracting, and, most recently, digital platform ("gig") work — is a central theme of contemporary labour sociology and policy debate.

Dimensions and measurement

Researchers typically treat precariousness as multidimensional: employment insecurity (risk of job loss, short tenure, variable hours), income inadequacy, lack of rights and protection (pensions, sick pay, unemployment insurance, safety coverage), and powerlessness at work. The International Labour Organization documents that informal and non-standard work together encompass the majority of the world's workers — informal employment alone accounts for some 60% of global employment — while in OECD countries roughly one worker in six is in temporary employment and significant minorities work very short or on-call hours. Precarious work is unevenly distributed: women, young people, migrants, and racialised minorities are disproportionately represented, and sectors such as hospitality, retail, care, agriculture, construction, and delivery platforms concentrate it.

Causes and debates

Explanations emphasise employer strategies to shift risk and cost onto workers under competitive pressure, globalisation and supply-chain fissuring (lead firms outsourcing employment to contractors), technological change culminating in algorithmic platform management, and the weakening of unions and labour regulation since the 1980s. The British economist Guy Standing popularised the term precariat — a would-be class of the insecure — in The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class (2011), linking precarity to political volatility. Other scholars caution that non-standard work is not uniformly bad: some workers value flexibility, some temporary jobs are stepping stones, and the "golden age" of standard employment was itself historically brief and largely confined to men in rich countries.

Policy responses

Responses include extending social protection to non-standard workers (portable benefits), tightening the rules on abusive contract forms (as in EU directives on transparent working conditions and platform work, and Spain's 2021 "Riders' Law" presuming delivery riders to be employees), reclassifying gig workers (California's AB5 and Proposition 22 battle; the UK Supreme Court's 2021 ruling that Uber drivers are "workers"), minimum-wage extension, and union organising among precarious workers. The COVID-19 pandemic, which exposed the absence of sick pay and unemployment cover among essential front-line workers, intensified these debates worldwide.
