Lost, mislaid, and abandoned property

Property law

Area: Common law property

Key doctrine: Finders' rights depend on classification of the property

Lost, mislaid, and abandoned property are categories of the common law of property which deal with personal property or land which has left the possession of its rightful owner without having directly entered the possession of another person. Property can be considered lost, mislaid or abandoned depending on the circumstances under which it is found by the next party who obtains its possession.

Definitions

Lost property is property the owner has involuntarily parted with and does not know where to find it — for example, a wallet that falls out of a pocket in the street. Mislaid property is property the owner intentionally placed somewhere and then forgot — such as a wallet deliberately set on a shop counter. Abandoned property is property the owner has intentionally relinquished all rights to, with no intention of reclaiming it.

Rights of finders

The classification matters. A finder of lost property generally acquires rights good against everyone except the true owner. Mislaid property is usually held by the owner of the premises where it was found, in trust for the true owner, on the theory that the owner will retrace their steps. Abandoned property belongs to the first person who takes possession of it with intent to own it. Classic cases include Armory v Delamirie (1722), where a chimney sweep's boy who found a jewel was held to have a right to it against all but the true owner.

Treasure trove and statutes

Some jurisdictions retain the old doctrine of treasure trove, under which hidden gold or silver belonged to the Crown; many modern states have replaced the common-law rules with unclaimed-property (escheat) statutes requiring finders and businesses to report and remit unclaimed property to the state after a period of time.
