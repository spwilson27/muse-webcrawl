Count Alessandro Giuseppe Antonio Anastasio Volta (18 February 1745 – 5 March 1827) was an Italian physicist and chemist who invented the voltaic pile in 1800 — the first practical source of continuous electric current and the ancestor of the modern battery. The SI unit of electric potential, the volt, is named in his honour.

Early work

Born in Como, Volta became professor of experimental physics at the University of Pavia in 1779, holding the chair for nearly forty years. Earlier inventions included the electrophorus (1775), a device for generating static electricity by induction. In 1776–78, studying marsh gas, he isolated and described methane.

The voltaic pile

Volta disputed Luigi Galvani's claim that electric currents in frog legs arose from "animal electricity," arguing instead that the metals in contact produced the current. To prove his contact theory he built, in 1799–1800, a stack of alternating zinc and silver (or copper) discs separated by brine-soaked cloth — the voltaic pile — which produced a steady electric current for the first time. Announced to the Royal Society in a letter of March 1800, the invention instantly transformed electrical science, enabling electrolysis and Humphry Davy's isolation of new elements within a few years.

Later life and legacy

Napoleon summoned Volta to Paris in 1801 to demonstrate the pile, made him a count and senator of the Kingdom of Italy, and awarded him pensions. Volta retired to Como in 1819 and died there in 1827. The Tempio Voltiano museum in Como preserves his instruments.
