The gastropods, commonly known as snails and slugs, are a large taxonomic class of invertebrates within the phylum Mollusca. With an estimated 65,000 to 80,000 living species, Gastropoda is the most diverse class of molluscs and the second most diverse class of animals overall, after the insects. The name derives from the Greek words for "stomach" and "foot", referring to the position of the foot below the gut.

Anatomy

Gastropods are characterised by torsion, a developmental process in which the visceral mass rotates 180 degrees, bringing the mantle cavity and anus to a position above the head, and often by a single, usually spirally coiled shell secreted by the mantle. Slugs are gastropods in which the shell is reduced or absent. Respiration is by gills in most aquatic species and by a vascularised mantle cavity functioning as a lung in land snails and slugs. The mouth bears a radula, a ribbon of chitinous teeth used for feeding.

Diversity and habitats

Gastropods occupy marine, freshwater and terrestrial habitats on every continent. They include limpets, abalone, whelks, conches, nudibranchs, pond snails and the familiar garden snails and slugs. Diets range from grazing on algae and detritus to active predation; cone snails hunt fish with venomous harpoon-like teeth.

Evolution

Gastropods appear in the Cambrian fossil record and diversified greatly through the Palaeozoic and Mesozoic. Modern classification, based increasingly on molecular phylogenetics, recognises major clades including Patellogastropoda, Vetigastropoda, Neritimorpha, Caenogastropoda and Heterobranchia, the last containing the land snails and slugs (Stylommatophora) and the sea slugs.
