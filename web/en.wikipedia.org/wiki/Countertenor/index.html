Countertenor

[Image: a countertenor performing in concert dress]
Voice typeAdult male
RangeRoughly G3–C6 (comparable to contralto/mezzo-soprano)
TechniquePredominantly falsetto (head voice)

A countertenor is a type of classical male singing voice whose vocal range is equivalent to that of the female contralto or mezzo-soprano voice types, generally extending from around G3 to C6. Countertenors sing mainly in falsetto or head voice, using only part of the vocal folds, which gives their sound its distinctive, ethereal quality.

History

High male voices were prized in medieval and Renaissance polyphony, where the contratenor was originally a part written "against the tenor". In England, male altos became a fixture of cathedral choirs, a tradition that survives in Anglican choral music. On the Baroque operatic stage, heroic male roles were written for castrati; as that practice died out, the parts languished until the 20th-century early-music revival. Alfred Deller (1912–1979) is credited with reviving the countertenor voice: Benjamin Britten wrote Oberon in A Midsummer Night's Dream (1960) for him.

Modern countertenors

Since Deller, countertenors have become standard in Baroque opera (Handel, Gluck, Monteverdi) and in contemporary works. Prominent singers include James Bowman, Michael Chance, David Daniels, Andreas Scholl, Philippe Jaroussky, Iestyn Davies and Jakub Józef Orliński. Some countertenors sing in a natural, non-falsetto production (sometimes called "sopranists" at the highest range).

Terminology

The countertenor voice should not be confused with the castrato voice of earlier centuries. Not all male altos are countertenors: the term usually denotes a soloistic falsetto voice, whereas "male alto" often refers to choral singers, including boys whose voices have not yet broken.
