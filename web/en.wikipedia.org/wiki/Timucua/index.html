Timucua

The Timucua were a Native American people who lived in Northeast and North Central Florida and southeast Georgia at the time of European contact. They spoke the Timucua language, a language isolate. The Timucua were organized into a number of distinct chiefdoms, numbering perhaps some 200,000 people in the early 16th century. They lived in villages of circular houses and practiced agriculture supplemented by hunting, fishing, and gathering.

Territory and chiefdoms

Timucua territory extended from the Altamaha River in Georgia south to central Florida and from the Gulf of Mexico to the Atlantic. Western Timucua groups, such as the Potano and Northern Utina, occupied the north-central peninsula, while eastern groups included the Saturiwa and Mocama along the coast and the St. Johns River. The French artist Jacques le Moyne de Morgues recorded the earliest European images of the Timucua during the 1560s colony of Fort Caroline.

Contact and decline

Spanish colonization brought missions, forced labor, and catastrophic epidemics. By the mid-17th century the Timucua population had collapsed, and raids by English-allied peoples destroyed the mission provinces. The last known Timucua speakers were evacuated to Cuba with the Spanish in 1763, where the remaining community disappeared from record.

Language and culture

Timucua is one of the best-documented indigenous languages of the southeastern United States thanks to the Franciscan friar Francisco Pareja, who published a grammar in 1614 and recorded confessions and catechisms in the language. Its written corpus is unusually large for the region. See also Timucua language and Spanish Florida.
