Infimum and supremum

The supremum of a set is its least upper bound; it need not belong to the set itself.

In mathematics, the infimum (abbreviated inf; plural infima) of a subset S of a partially ordered set P is the greatest element in P that is less than or equal to each element of S, if such an element exists. If the infimum exists, it is unique. The term greatest lower bound is also commonly used. Similarly, the supremum (abbreviated sup; plural suprema) of S is the least element in P that is greater than or equal to each element of S — the least upper bound.

Examples

The supremum of the open interval (0, 1) is 1, even though 1 is not an element of the interval; the infimum is 0, likewise not an element. For the set of rational numbers whose square is less than 2, the supremum in the rationals does not exist, but in the real numbers it is √2. This illustrates why the completeness of the real numbers — the property that every non-empty set bounded above has a real supremum — is a foundational axiom of real analysis, distinguishing the reals from the rationals.

Properties

If a set has a maximum element, that maximum is its supremum; if it has a minimum, that minimum is its infimum. The converse fails when the supremum lies outside the set, as in the open-interval example. Suprema and infima generalise to joins and meets in lattice theory and order theory, where sup S is written ⋁S and inf S as ⋀S. In analysis they underpin definitions of limits superior and inferior, the supremum norm in functional analysis, and essential suprema in measure theory.
