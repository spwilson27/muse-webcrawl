Quinine

Formula: C20H24N2O2
Molar mass: 324.45 g/mol
Source: Bark of the cinchona tree
Uses: Antimalarial; flavouring in tonic water

Quinine is a medication used to treat malaria and babesiosis. It is an alkaloid obtained from the bark of the cinchona tree, native to South America. Quinine was the mainstay of malaria treatment from the seventeenth century until the widespread adoption of synthetic antimalarials and, more recently, artemisinin-based therapies. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. The bitter compound is also the flavouring agent in tonic water.

History

Quechua peoples of Peru used cinchona bark against fevers and shivering; Jesuit missionaries brought the bark to Europe in the 1630s, where it became known as "Jesuit's bark" or "Peruvian bark". Legend links its introduction to the Countess of Chinchón, wife of the Viceroy of Peru. The active alkaloid was isolated in 1820 by French chemists Pierre Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Caventou, who named it quinine after the Quechua word quina (bark). British and Dutch colonial powers smuggled cinchona seeds to plantations in India and Java; by the early twentieth century Java supplied most of the world's quinine. A famous 1940s-era total synthesis by Robert Burns Woodward and William von Eggers Doering (1944) capped centuries of chemical interest.

Medical use

Quinine kills the malaria parasite in red blood cells, probably by interfering with the parasite's detoxification of haem. It remains useful for severe malaria when artesunate is unavailable and for chloroquine-resistant Plasmodium falciparum, though treatment requires several days and side effects are common. Side effects include cinchonism—tinnitus, headache, nausea, and disturbed vision—as well as hypoglycaemia and, rarely, serious blood and cardiac effects. Quinine was long used for leg cramps, but regulators discourage this because of potentially fatal adverse reactions.

Tonic water and other uses

British officials in India mixed quinine with soda and sugar, creating tonic water, which was taken with gin partly as a palatable prophylactic—the origin of the gin and tonic. Modern tonic water contains far less quinine than medicinal doses and is regulated (in the United States, limited to 83 mg per litre). Quinine fluoresces strongly under ultraviolet light, a property famously demonstrated by its blue glow in tonic water.
