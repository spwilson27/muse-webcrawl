Ethiopian Jews in Israel

Total population~170,000 (2023)
RegionsNetanya, Ashdod, Beersheba, Kiryat Malakhi, and throughout Israel
LanguagesHebrew, Amharic, Tigrinya
ReligionJudaism (Haymanot and Rabbinic)

Ethiopian Jews in Israel are immigrants and descendants of the Beta Israel community of Ethiopia, most of whom arrived during several waves of aliyah (immigration) beginning in the late 1970s. By the early 2020s the community numbered roughly 170,000 people, about 2% of Israel's population.

Immigration

After Israel formally recognised the Beta Israel as Jews under the Law of Return in 1975 — following rulings by Sephardi Chief Rabbi Ovadia Yosef — immigration began in earnest. Thousands trekked to refugee camps in Sudan during the Ethiopian civil war, from which many were airlifted in Operation Moses (1984–85). In May 1991, as Addis Ababa was about to fall, Operation Solomon airlifted 14,325 Ethiopian Jews to Israel in about 36 hours, a record for passengers on a single aircraft. Later immigration focused on the Falash Mura, descendants of Beta Israel who had converted to Christianity, whose eligibility became a recurring political and religious controversy.

Integration

The community's absorption into Israeli society has been marked by both progress and persistent inequality. Ethiopian Israelis faced disputes over religious recognition of their clergy (the kessim), the 1996 revelation that donated Ethiopian blood had been routinely discarded, gaps in income and education, and allegations of police brutality. The death of Yehuda Biadga in 2019 and other incidents sparked mass protests by Ethiopian Israelis against racism and police violence. At the same time, Ethiopian Israelis have risen in the military, politics, academia and culture, and the Beta Israel festival of Sigd is an official Israeli holiday.

Culture

Ethiopian Jewish religious practice, known as Haymanot, preserves pre-Talmudic traditions, a Ge'ez liturgy and holidays such as Sigd. In Israel, Amharic-language media, Ethiopian restaurants and musicians blending Ethiopian and Israeli styles have made the community's culture increasingly visible nationwide.
