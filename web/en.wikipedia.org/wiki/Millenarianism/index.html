Millenarianism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The millennial kingdom — a transformed world — is the hope at the heart of millenarian movements.

Millenarianism (also called millenarism or millennialism in its religious form) is the belief in a coming transformation of society, after which all things will be changed for the better. Derived from the Latin mille ("thousand") and associated originally with the thousand-year reign of Christ described in the Book of Revelation, the term has broadened in scholarship to cover movements — religious or secular — that expect an imminent, total, and this-worldly collective salvation.

Characteristics

Anthropologists and sociologists commonly identify millenarian movements by the expectation of a salvation that is collective (for the faithful group), terrestrial (realised on earth), imminent (expected soon), total (transforming life completely), and miraculous (brought about by supernatural or superhuman agency). Such movements often arise among colonised, oppressed, or rapidly changing societies, and frequently gather around a charismatic prophet who announces the coming transformation.

Examples

Early Christianity contained strong millenarian currents, and medieval Europe produced movements such as the flagellants and the followers of Joachim of Fiore. The Anabaptist seizure of Münster in 1534–35, the English Fifth Monarchists of the seventeenth century, and the Millerite movement of the 1840s in the United States are classic cases. Colonial encounters generated movements such as the Ghost Dance among Native Americans, the Xhosa cattle-killing of 1856–57, cargo cults in Melanesia, and the Taiping movement in China. Scholars have also applied the term, by extension, to secular ideologies promising a radically transformed future.

Relationship to millennialism

The terms millenarianism and millennialism are often used interchangeably. In theological contexts, millennialism refers more narrowly to Christian doctrines of Christ's thousand-year reign (premillennialism, postmillennialism, and amillennialism), whereas millenarianism is the broader comparative category used by historians, anthropologists, and sociologists.
