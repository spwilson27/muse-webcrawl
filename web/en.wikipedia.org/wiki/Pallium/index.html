Pallium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pallium (Latin: pallium or palla, "woollen cloak") is an ecclesiastical vestment in the Catholic Church, originally worn only by the pope, and later bestowed by him on metropolitans and primates as a symbol of their participation in papal authority and of communion with the Holy See.

Description

The modern pallium is a narrow circular band of white wool, about five centimetres wide, worn around the shoulders over the chasuble, with two hanging pendants — one in front and one in back — weighted with lead pieces covered in black silk. It is decorated with six black silk crosses. It is traditionally woven from the wool of two lambs blessed each year on the feast of Saint Agnes (21 January) at the basilica of Sant'Agnese fuori le mura in Rome, and the finished pallia are stored in a niche beneath the high altar of St. Peter's Basilica, near the tomb of Saint Peter.

Use and conferral

The pope bestows the pallium on newly appointed metropolitan archbishops, signifying their jurisdiction over an ecclesiastical province and their bond with Rome. Since 2015, following a change by Pope Francis, the pallia are blessed by the pope on the Solemnity of Saints Peter and Paul (29 June) in the presence of the new archbishops, but formally imposed later in their home dioceses by the apostolic nuncio. A metropolitan may wear the pallium only within his own province and on specified occasions; it is customarily buried with him.

History

The origins of the pallium are obscure; it appears in Christian use by the fourth or fifth century, possibly derived from a late Roman garment of office. Grants of the pallium to favoured bishops — such as Augustine of Canterbury in 601 — became symbols of papal favour, and by the medieval period its conferral on metropolitans was regularized and closely tied to papal approval of their office. An Eastern counterpart, the omophorion, is worn by bishops in Byzantine-rite churches.
