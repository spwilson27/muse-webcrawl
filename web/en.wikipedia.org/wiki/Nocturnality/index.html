Nocturnality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nocturnality is a behaviour pattern in which an animal is active at night and sleeps during the day. It is widespread — most mammals are primarily nocturnal, a legacy of their origins under dinosaur dominance — and is supported by keen senses of smell and hearing, large light-gathering eyes, whiskers, or echolocation in bats and some other groups.

Advantages and adaptations

Night activity avoids daytime predators and heat, and opens resources with less competition; desert animals especially benefit. Adaptations include the tapetum lucidum (the reflective layer causing eyeshine), enlarged ears, and in owls, silent flight and asymmetric ears for locating prey by sound. Conversely, artificial light at night now disrupts nocturnal animals — confusing migrating birds, sea-turtle hatchlings and insects, and altering predator–prey balances.

Contrasts

Diurnal animals are active by day; crepuscular ones at dawn and dusk (rabbits, deer, many predators); cathemeral animals (some lemurs) spread activity across the 24 hours. Activity patterns can shift under human pressure, with many normally diurnal species becoming more nocturnal to avoid people.
