Dhikr (Arabic: ذِكْر, lit. "remembrance") is a form of Islamic devotion in which phrases, prayers or names of God are repeated, silently or aloud, in order to remember Allah and purify the heart. It is grounded in the Qur'an, which commands believers to "remember your Lord much" and promises that "verily, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Qur'an 13:28). Dhikr is practised by Muslims generally and is a central discipline of Sufism.

Forms

Common formulas include Subhan Allah ("Glory be to God"), Alhamdulillah ("Praise be to God"), Allahu Akbar ("God is greatest"), La ilaha illallah ("There is no god but God"), and recitation of the 99 names of God. Repetitions are frequently counted on a string of prayer beads called a misbaha or tasbih, often in sets of 33 or 99. Dhikr is especially performed after the five daily prayers, but may be done at any time.

In Sufism

Sufi orders (tariqas) developed elaborate dhikr rituals, performed individually or in gatherings, sometimes accompanied by controlled breathing, rhythmic movement, chanting or music, as in the whirling of the Mevlevi order founded by the followers of Rumi. Orders differ over whether dhikr should be silent (khafi) or vocal (jahri). For Sufis, dhikr is the principal means of progressing along the spiritual path toward annihilation of the ego in God (fana).
