Archibald Percival Wavell, 1st Earl Wavell (5 May 1883 – 24 May 1950) was a senior officer of the British Army. He served in the Second Boer War, the First World War, and the interwar period, and in the Second World War was Commander-in-Chief, Middle East, before becoming Commander-in-Chief, India, and finally Viceroy of India from 1943 until 1947.

Early career

Educated at Winchester College and the Royal Military College, Sandhurst, Wavell was commissioned into the Black Watch in 1901 and served in South Africa and on the North-West Frontier of India. In the First World War he lost an eye at the Second Battle of Ypres in 1915, won the Military Cross, and served under Allenby in the Palestine campaign. Between the wars he commanded the 6th Infantry Brigade, a division, and British forces in Palestine during the Arab revolt.

Second World War

Appointed Commander-in-Chief, Middle East in 1939, Wavell directed the spectacularly successful Operation Compass (December 1940 – February 1941), in which his outnumbered Western Desert Force destroyed the Italian Tenth Army and advanced across Cyrenaica. However, the diversion of troops to Greece, followed by Rommel's arrival in North Africa and the German conquest of Greece and Crete, reversed British fortunes. After the failure of Operation Battleaxe in June 1941, Churchill replaced Wavell with Auchinleck, exchanging commands with him in India.

As Commander-in-Chief, India, and briefly of the ill-fated ABDA Command, Wavell bore responsibility for the defeats in Malaya, Singapore, and Burma in 1942, though he lacked the resources to prevent them. He was promoted field marshal in January 1943.

Viceroy of India

In June 1943 Wavell was appointed Viceroy, succeeding Linlithgow. His tenure was dominated by the Bengal famine of 1943, for which he pressed the government in London for relief, and by the deadlocked constitutional negotiations with Congress and the Muslim League, including the failed Wavell Plan of 1945. Exhausted and at odds with the new Labour government, he was replaced by Mountbatten in February 1947, months before partition and independence.

Personal life and legacy

A reserved, scholarly soldier with a deep love of poetry, Wavell edited the anthology Other Men's Flowers (1944), which became a bestseller. He was created Viscount Wavell in 1943 and Earl Wavell in 1947. He died in London in May 1950 following an operation.
