Pashtunwali

Pashtunwali (Pashto: پښتونولي), also called Pukhtunwali, is the traditional lifestyle and unwritten honour code of the Pashtun people of Afghanistan and Pakistan. It is a pre-Islamic tradition, surviving since ancient times, that governs social behaviour, law, and disputes in Pashtun communities, often taking precedence over state law in tribal areas.

Principal tenets

  • Melmastia (hospitality): generous hospitality and protection owed to all guests, regardless of relationship.
  • Nanawatai (asylum): protection granted to a person who seeks refuge and repents, even an enemy.
  • Badal (revenge/justice): the obligation to avenge a wrong or insult, which can generate long-running feuds.
  • Nang (honour): defence of honour, particularly concerning zan, zar, zamin — women, treasure (gold), and land.
  • Sabat (loyalty) and Imandari (righteousness): steadfastness to kin and community, and just conduct.

Institutions

Disputes are settled by the jirga, an assembly of tribal elders whose decisions are enforced by communal sanction. The hujra (guest house) is the social centre where guests are received and councils meet. Sanctions such as nagha (fines) support enforcement.

Modern context

Pashtunwali remains influential in the tribal districts of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa and Balochistan and across Afghanistan, where it interacts — sometimes uneasily — with Islamic law, state courts, and modern human-rights norms, notably in practices criticised for their effects on women, such as swara (giving a girl in marriage to settle a feud), which is now illegal in Pakistan.
