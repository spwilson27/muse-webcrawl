Lucius Aelius Sejanus (c. 20 BC – 18 October AD 31) was a Roman soldier and confidant of the emperor Tiberius. A member of the equestrian class, he rose to become prefect of the Praetorian Guard and one of the most powerful men in Rome, before his sudden arrest and execution in AD 31.

Rise to power

Sejanus became praetorian prefect jointly with his father in AD 14 and sole prefect soon afterwards. He concentrated the previously scattered Praetorian cohorts in a single camp, the Castra Praetoria, greatly increasing the prefect's influence. When Tiberius withdrew from Rome to Capri in AD 26, Sejanus governed the capital in his name, using treason (maiestas) trials to eliminate rivals. Ancient sources — Tacitus, Suetonius and Cassius Dio — accuse him of engineering the death of Tiberius's son Drusus in AD 23 (through Drusus's wife Livilla, Sejanus's lover) and of persecuting the family of Germanicus, including Agrippina the Elder.

Fall

In AD 31 Sejanus shared the consulship with Tiberius and appeared poised to marry into the imperial family. A letter from the emperor's sister-in-law Antonia Minor reportedly warned Tiberius of Sejanus's ambitions. In a carefully staged coup, the emperor appointed Naevius Sutorius Macro to command the night watch, had Sejanus denounced in the Senate on 18 October 31, and Sejanus was arrested and strangled the same day; his children were also executed, and his memory was formally condemned (damnatio memoriae). A purge of his supporters followed.

Legacy

Sejanus became the archetype of the scheming court favourite. His career inspired Ben Jonson's play Sejanus His Fall (1603), and he appears as a principal character in Robert Graves's novel I, Claudius (1934).
