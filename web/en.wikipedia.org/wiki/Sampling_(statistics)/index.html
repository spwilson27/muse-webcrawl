Sampling (statistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A researcher selecting a small group of items from a large warehouse of boxes]

In statistics, sampling is the selection of a subset (a statistical sample) of individuals from within a statistical population, to estimate characteristics of the whole population. Good sampling makes inference affordable: surveying thousands can describe millions, if the sample is representative.

Probability sampling gives every member a known, non-zero chance of selection — simple random sampling, stratified sampling (deliberate proportions from subgroups), cluster and systematic sampling — enabling principled error estimates. Non-probability sampling (convenience, voluntary response) risks unquantifiable bias.

Lessons from history

The 1936 Literary Digest fiasco — 2.4 million returned ballots predicted Landon over Roosevelt, catastrophically wrong due to sampling-frame and response bias, while George Gallup's small quota sample was right — is the canonical cautionary tale. Modern polling fights falling response rates with weighting and likely-voter models; election misses (2016, 2020 in the US) show the battle continues.

In computing, sampling (random subsets, reservoir sampling, importance sampling) scales statistics and machine learning to big data; Monte Carlo methods are sampling by another name.
