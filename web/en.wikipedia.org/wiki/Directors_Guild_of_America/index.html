Directors Guild of America

The Directors Guild of America (DGA) is an entertainment guild that represents the interests of film and television directors in the United States motion picture industry and abroad. Founded in 1936 as the Screen Directors Guild, it merged in 1960 with the Radio and Television Directors Guild to form the modern Directors Guild of America. The guild has more than 19,000 members and negotiates collective bargaining agreements covering directors, assistant directors, unit production managers, associate directors and stage managers.

History

The guild was organised in 1936 by directors including King Vidor, John Ford, Frank Capra and Howard Hawks, at a time when directors had little contractual protection or recognition. Its founding struggles included securing the right to a director's cut and credit recognition — the origin of the "A film by" possessory credit convention. In 1978 the guild struck over the right of directors to be credited and other issues; the DGA has historically struck less often than the Writers Guild. In 2002 the guild's name appeared in litigation over edited-for-television films, and the DGA has long campaigned against unauthorised alteration of films.

Functions

The DGA negotiates the Basic Agreement with the Alliance of Motion Picture and Television Producers, setting minimum compensation, working conditions, creative rights and residuals for members, and administers pension and health plans. It enforces such protections as the director's right to assemble a first cut and to be present during editing. The guild also lobbies on copyright, piracy and tax issues affecting film production.

DGA Awards

Since 1948 the guild has presented the Directors Guild of America Awards, honouring outstanding directorial achievement in film, television and commercials. The DGA Award for Outstanding Directing – Feature Film is regarded as a strong predictor of the Academy Award for Best Director; the two have differed only rarely in the award's history.
