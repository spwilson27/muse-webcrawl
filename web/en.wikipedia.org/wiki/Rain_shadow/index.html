Rain shadow

Orographic lifting produces rain on the windward side and a dry rain shadow to leeward.

A rain shadow is a dry area on the leeward side of a mountain range. As moist air is forced up the windward slopes (orographic lift), it cools, condenses, and loses moisture as precipitation; the air then descends on the lee side, warming and drying, so the region beyond the mountains receives little rain.

Mechanism

Rising air cools at the moist adiabatic lapse rate once condensation begins, wringing out water vapour. Descending air is compressed and warms at the faster dry adiabatic rate, lowering its relative humidity and creating warm, dry downslope winds such as the foehn, chinook, and zonda.

Examples

Famous rain shadows include the Great Basin and Mojave Desert east of California's Sierra Nevada; the Patagonian Desert east of the Andes; the Atacama Desert, intensified by the Andes and the coastal Chilean range; the Tibetan Plateau north of the Himalayas; and Death Valley, which lies in the shadow of several successive ranges. Rain shadows strongly influence local climate, vegetation, and settlement. See also orographic lift and foehn wind.
