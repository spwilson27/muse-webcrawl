Cyrus Levinthal (May 2, 1922 – November 4, 1990) was an American molecular biologist and biophysicist, a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and later at Columbia University. He is remembered for formulating Levinthal's paradox of protein folding and for pioneering the computer graphics display of molecular structures.

Career

Born in Kyoto, Japan, to American parents, Levinthal trained in physics and worked on proteins and bacterial genetics during the early decades of molecular biology. At MIT he built one of the first interactive computer graphics systems for displaying and manipulating models of protein molecules, allowing scientists to visualise molecular structure in three dimensions — foundational work for modern computational structural biology.

Levinthal's paradox

In talks from 1968–1969, Levinthal pointed out that a protein of even 100 amino acids has an astronomically large number of possible conformations (roughly 3198 if each residue has a few states), so random search could never fold a protein in biological time — yet real proteins fold in milliseconds to seconds. The "paradox" framed the modern protein-folding problem and motivated theories of folding pathways, energy landscapes, and funnel-shaped folding funnels. Levinthal himself suggested that folding follows specific pathways rather than random sampling.
