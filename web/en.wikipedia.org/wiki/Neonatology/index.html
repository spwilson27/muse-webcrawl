Neonatology

Neonatology is a subspecialty of pediatrics concerned with the medical care of newborn infants, especially those who are ill or born prematurely. Neonatologists work mainly in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), managing conditions such as prematurity and low birth weight, respiratory distress, congenital malformations, sepsis, and birth asphyxia. The field developed in the mid-20th century as incubators, mechanical ventilation, and specialised nutrition transformed the survival prospects of preterm babies.

History

Physicians such as Pierre Budin in Paris pioneered the care of premature infants in the 1890s, and Martin Couney famously exhibited incubator babies at fairs in the early 1900s. The modern specialty emerged after the Second World War: Virginia Apgar introduced her scoring system in 1952; the first NICUs opened in the 1960s; and advances followed — CPAP and ventilators for respiratory distress syndrome, exogenous surfactant therapy in the 1980s–90s (following the work of Tetsurō Fujiwara), antenatal corticosteroids, and parenteral nutrition. Survival at ever lower gestational ages improved dramatically; in high-income settings, most infants born at 26 weeks now survive, and the limit of viability is around 22–24 weeks.

Common conditions

  • Respiratory distress syndrome and bronchopulmonary dysplasia
  • Patent ductus arteriosus
  • Necrotizing enterocolitis
  • Neonatal sepsis
  • Neonatal jaundice
  • Intraventricular hemorrhage and perinatal brain injury
  • Retinopathy of prematurity
  • Congenital anomalies requiring surgery or specialist care

Organisation of care

NICUs are graded by level, from Level I basic newborn care to Level IV units offering complex surgery and ECMO. Care is multidisciplinary, involving neonatologists, neonatal nurses, respiratory therapists, pharmacists, dietitians, and developmental specialists; modern practice emphasises family-integrated care, kangaroo (skin-to-skin) care, and developmental follow-up of graduates at risk of neurodevelopmental impairment.

Training

In the United States, neonatologists complete a pediatrics residency followed by a three-year neonatal–perinatal medicine fellowship; similar pathways exist in other countries. In many settings, neonatal nurse practitioners and advanced neonatal nurse practitioners provide much of the day-to-day care.
