Karl Theodor Wilhelm Weierstrass (31 October 1815 – 19 February 1897) was a German mathematician often called the "father of modern analysis". He placed the calculus on a rigorous logical footing, gave the epsilon–delta definition of continuity and limits, and trained a generation of leading mathematicians in Berlin.

Life

Weierstrass was born in Ostenfelde, Westphalia. Pressured by his father, he studied law and finance at Bonn — where he neglected his studies for fencing and beer — and left without a degree. He trained as a schoolteacher instead and spent fourteen years teaching at provincial schools in Deutsch Krone and Braunsberg, writing mathematics in obscurity. A breakthrough paper on Abelian functions (1854) won him sudden fame and an honorary doctorate; in 1856 he obtained a chair in Berlin, where his famously rigorous lecture courses drew students from across Europe.

Among his students was Sofia Kovalevskaya, whom he tutored privately because women were barred from the university, and whom he supported throughout her career.

Contributions

Weierstrass established the arithmetisation of analysis: his ε–δ definitions of limit and continuity underpin modern treatments. He shocked contemporaries with the Weierstrass function (1872) — continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere — showing that intuition about curves could mislead. Other results include the Stone–Weierstrass approximation theorem, the Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem, the Weierstrass product theorem, and a rigorous theory of elliptic functions.
