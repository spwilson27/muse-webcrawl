Joseph Schumpeter

Joseph Alois Schumpeter (February 8, 1883 – January 8, 1950) was an Austrian political economist. Among the most influential economists of the twentieth century, he popularised the term "creative destruction" and developed influential theories of entrepreneurship, business cycles and the evolution of capitalism.

Career

Born in Triesch, Moravia, Schumpeter studied law and economics in Vienna under Eugen Böhm von Bawerk. He taught at Czernowitz and Graz, briefly served as Austria's Minister of Finance in 1919, and ran the private Biedermann Bank before emigrating to the United States, where he was a professor at Harvard University from 1932 until his death.

Ideas

In The Theory of Economic Development (1911), Schumpeter placed the entrepreneur — the innovator who carries out "new combinations" of resources — at the centre of economic change. In Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy (1942) he argued that capitalism's very success would undermine its institutional foundations, describing innovation as a "perennial gale of creative destruction" in which new products and processes continually displace old ones. His Business Cycles (1939) proposed that overlapping long, medium and short cycles drive economic fluctuations, and his posthumous History of Economic Analysis (1954) is a landmark of the field.
