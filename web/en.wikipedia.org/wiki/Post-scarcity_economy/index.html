Post-scarcity economy

Field: Economics, futurology
Enablers: Automation, nanotechnology, cheap energy
Related: Abundance, universal basic income

A post-scarcity economy is a theoretical economic situation in which most goods can be produced in great abundance with minimal human labour, so that they become available to all very cheaply or even freely. Post-scarcity does not mean that all scarcity disappears — land, original artworks and status goods may remain scarce — but that basic needs and many wants could be supplied without the rationing role normally played by prices and wages.

Proposed mechanisms

Writers on the subject usually invoke some combination of advanced automation and robotics; molecular nanotechnology "assemblers" able to build goods from raw materials; very cheap energy from fusion or solar power; and digital goods, which can already be copied at near-zero marginal cost. Authors such as Murray Bookchin (Post-Scarcity Anarchism, 1971), Paul Mason (PostCapitalism, 2015) and Aaron Bastani (Fully Automated Luxury Communism, 2019) have explored the political consequences, while Iain M. Banks's Culture novels depict a fictional post-scarcity civilisation.

Criticism

Critics argue that scarcity of time, attention, land and positional goods cannot be automated away, that energy and material limits (including ecological constraints) bound production, and that institutions such as intellectual property artificially maintain scarcity for digital goods. Economists also debate whether automation-driven abundance would raise living standards broadly or concentrate wealth, a debate linked to proposals for universal basic income.
