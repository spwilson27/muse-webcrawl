Solder

Solder is a fusible metal alloy used to create a permanent bond between metal workpieces. In soldering, the solder is melted and drawn into the joint by capillary action; it solidifies on cooling to form a mechanical and, in electronics, electrical connection. Unlike welding, the workpieces themselves are not melted.

Composition

Traditional solder is an alloy of tin and lead, most commonly 60/40 or 63/37 tin–lead; the 63/37 composition is eutectic, melting sharply at 183 °C. Because of the toxicity of lead, electronics sold in the European Union and many other markets must use lead-free solders under the RoHS directive. Common lead-free alloys include SAC305 (96.5% tin, 3% silver, 0.5% copper), which melts around 217–220 °C. Other solders contain bismuth for low melting points, antimony for strength, or high lead content for high-temperature applications.

Flux

Most solder wire has a core of flux, which cleans oxides from the metal surfaces and promotes wetting. Rosin fluxes are traditional in electronics; water-soluble and no-clean fluxes are used in automated assembly.

Applications

Soldering is fundamental to electronics assembly, joining components to printed circuit boards by hand with a soldering iron or in bulk by wave soldering and reflow soldering. Soft solders are also used in plumbing (with lead-free alloys for potable water), stained glass, jewellery, and sheet-metal work. Higher-temperature joining with stronger filler alloys is called brazing.

Health and regulation

Lead-bearing solder is a source of lead exposure, and its use in plumbing for drinking water is banned in many jurisdictions. Lead-free substitutes generally melt at higher temperatures and can be more prone to defects such as tin whiskers, which are managed by alloy choice and conformal coatings.
