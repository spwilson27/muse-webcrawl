Callender-Hamilton bridge

A Callender-Hamilton bridge in service as a road crossing.

The Callender-Hamilton bridge is a modular, prefabricated through-truss bridge system designed by the New Zealand engineer A. M. Hamilton and manufactured by P. & W. MacLellan Ltd of Glasgow, Scotland. It was developed in the 1930s as a transportable bridge that could be shipped in components and erected quickly with unskilled labour, and it is often regarded as the civilian counterpart of the military Bailey bridge.

Design

Like the Bailey bridge, the Callender-Hamilton is built from standardised, interchangeable steel panels and transoms that bolt together, allowing spans to be assembled to the length and load capacity required. Unlike the Bailey, it was intended for permanent or semi-permanent civilian use, typically carrying a single lane of road traffic with provision for pedestrian walkways. Components were hot-dip galvanized for corrosion resistance, giving the bridges long service lives.

History and use

The design grew out of Hamilton's work on bridges for remote districts of New Zealand, where conventional construction was impractical. The system found wide use across the British Commonwealth and in developing countries, where Callender-Hamilton bridges were erected over rivers in Africa, Asia and the Pacific, often replacing ferries or fords on rural road networks. Many examples remain in service decades after erection, valued for their simplicity and ease of repair.
