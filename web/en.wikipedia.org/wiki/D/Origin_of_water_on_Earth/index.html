Origin of water on Earth

The origin of water on Earth is the subject of a body of research in planetary science, astronomy, and geochemistry. Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago from the solar nebula, at a distance from the young Sun where temperatures were generally too high for large quantities of water ice to condense directly. Explaining how the planet acquired its oceans — roughly 1.4 billion cubic kilometres of water — therefore requires either that water was delivered from elsewhere or that it was retained and released from Earth's own building blocks.

Hypotheses

Leading proposals include: (1) outgassing of water vapour from Earth's interior during accretion and volcanism, with hydrogen-bearing minerals having carried water into the planet from the start; (2) delivery by asteroids, particularly carbonaceous chondrites from the outer asteroid belt, whose hydrogen isotope (D/H) ratios closely match ocean water; and (3) delivery by comets, once favoured but now considered a minor contributor because most measured cometary D/H ratios are roughly twice that of Earth's oceans.

Evidence

The deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio is the key fingerprint used to test these hypotheses. Studies of ancient rocks, including 3.8-billion-year-old pillow lavas, suggest liquid water existed very early. Analyses of enstatite chondrites — meteorites with an isotopic composition similar to Earth's — indicate the planet's building blocks may have contained enough hydrogen to supply several oceans, reducing the need for late delivery. Most current models combine an indigenous water supply with some contribution from carbonaceous asteroids during the later stages of accretion.
