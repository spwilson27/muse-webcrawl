D/H ratio

The deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio (D/H ratio) is the ratio of the abundance of deuterium (hydrogen-2, the heavy isotope of hydrogen) to ordinary hydrogen (protium, hydrogen-1) in a sample of material. It is a key tracer in astrophysics, planetary science, and geochemistry, used to study the origin and evolution of water and volatiles in the Solar System.

Reference values

The protosolar D/H ratio, measured in Jupiter's atmosphere and in the solar wind, is about 2.5 × 10−5. Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW) has a D/H ratio of about 1.56 × 10−4, enriched relative to the protosolar value by fractionation processes. The local interstellar medium has a D/H of roughly 1.5 × 10−5.

Origin of Earth's water

Comparing D/H ratios is a central method for testing hypotheses about the source of Earth's water. Carbonaceous chondrite asteroids have D/H ratios close to that of Earth's oceans, supporting the idea that much of Earth's water was delivered by asteroid impacts. Many comets, such as Halley-family and Jupiter-family comets measured by the Giotto and Rosetta missions (67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko showed a D/H about three times the terrestrial value), have higher D/H, suggesting comets contributed less — although some comets, like 103P/Hartley, match Earth's value, indicating diversity among comets.

Other applications

D/H measurements in the atmospheres of Mars and Venus trace the loss of water to space over geological time: both planets show strongly enriched D/H, evidence that they once had much more water. D/H is also measured in interstellar molecules, lunar samples, Titan's methane, and exoplanet atmospheres, and is used in isotope hydrology to trace water sources and evaporation.
