Isotope geochemistry

Isotope geochemistry is an aspect of geology based upon the study of the relative and absolute concentrations of the elements and their isotopes in the Earth. It divides broadly into two branches: the study of stable isotopes, whose ratios vary through physical and chemical fractionation, and radiogenic isotopes, produced by radioactive decay and used to date rocks and trace their sources.

Stable isotope geochemistry

Stable isotope studies focus on light elements such as hydrogen, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, and sulfur. Variations in isotope ratios, expressed in delta (δ) notation relative to a standard, record processes such as evaporation and precipitation (in oxygen and hydrogen isotopes), photosynthesis (carbon), and microbial metabolism (sulfur and nitrogen). Ice cores, marine sediments, and speleothems preserve these signals, enabling reconstruction of past climates and environments.

Radiogenic isotope geochemistry

Radioactive decay systems — including uranium–lead, potassium–argon, rubidium–strontium, and samarium–neodymium — provide the principal tools of geochronology. Because parent isotopes decay at known rates, measuring parent/daughter ratios yields absolute ages of rocks and minerals. Radiogenic tracers also fingerprint the mantle and crustal reservoirs from which magmas were derived.

Methods

Measurements are performed by mass spectrometry, principally thermal ionization (TIMS) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS), together with isotope ratio mass spectrometry (IRMS) for light stable isotopes.
