D, or d, is the fourth letter of the Latin alphabet, used in the modern English alphabet, the alphabets of other western European languages and others worldwide. Its name in English is dee (pronounced /diː/), plural dees.

History

The letter D descends from the Phoenician letter dalet, thought to depict a fish or a door, through the Greek letter delta (Δ δ). The Etruscans and Romans adopted it, and its modern form — a vertical stroke with a curved bowl — was fixed in Roman capitals. In the Latin alphabet it usually represents the voiced alveolar plosive /d/, as in English dog.

Use in writing systems

In English, D is the seventh most common letter, occurring in about 4.3% of words. In several languages the digraph or modified forms derived from D have special values; D with stroke (Đ đ) is used in Vietnamese and South Slavic languages, and Eth (Ð ð) is used in Icelandic and Faroese.

Other uses

  • In Roman numerals, D represents 500.
  • In music, D is a note of the diatonic scale and the key of D major has two sharps.
  • In grading systems, D is a passing but below-average mark in many countries.
  • D is the chemical symbol formerly used for deuterium (now ²H), and vitamin D is an essential nutrient group.
  • In computing, D is a programming language, and hexadecimal digit D equals decimal 13.
