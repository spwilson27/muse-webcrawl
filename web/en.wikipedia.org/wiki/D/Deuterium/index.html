Deuterium

Deuterium (symbol D or 2H) is one of two stable isotopes of hydrogen, the other being protium (1H). The nucleus of a deuterium atom, called a deuteron, contains one proton and one neutron, whereas the far more common protium nucleus contains no neutrons. Deuterium has a natural abundance in Earth's oceans of about one atom in 6,420 of hydrogen (roughly 0.0156%).

Discovery

Deuterium was discovered and named by Harold Urey in 1931, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1934. Urey and his collaborators detected the heavy isotope spectroscopically in samples of liquid hydrogen concentrated by evaporation. Gilbert N. Lewis first isolated a pure sample of heavy water (D2O) in 1933.

Properties and applications

Because deuterium is twice as massive as protium, compounds containing it — such as heavy water — have measurably different physical and chemical properties, a phenomenon known as the kinetic isotope effect. Heavy water is used as a neutron moderator in certain nuclear reactors (notably the CANDU design) because deuterium absorbs far fewer neutrons than ordinary hydrogen. Deuterium is also a candidate fuel for nuclear fusion: deuterium–tritium and deuterium–deuterium reactions release large amounts of energy, and deuterium is readily extractable from seawater. In science, deuterated compounds serve as tracers in spectroscopy, NMR studies, and pharmaceutical research.

Antimatter counterpart

Antideuterium, consisting of an antiproton and an antineutron, has been produced in particle accelerators but not yet observed in nature.
