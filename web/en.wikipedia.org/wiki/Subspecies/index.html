Subspecies

In biological classification, a subspecies (abbreviated subsp. or ssp.) is a rank below species, used for populations that live in different areas and vary in size, shape, or other physical characteristics, but that can successfully interbreed. A subspecies cannot be recognized in isolation: a species will either be recognized as having no subspecies at all or at least two, including any extinct ones. The term is most common in zoology; in botany, infraspecific ranks also include variety and form.

Nomenclature

In zoology, a subspecies is named with a trinomen: the generic name, specific epithet, and a subspecific epithet — for example, the Bengal tiger is Panthera tigris tigris, which is also the nominotypical subspecies because it repeats the specific epithet. In botany, the subspecies epithet follows the abbreviation "subsp." or "ssp." A species divided into subspecies is called polytypic; a species with no recognized subdivisions is monotypic.

Criteria

Subspecies are typically geographically separated populations that differ diagnosably in morphology or other traits. Because they interbreed where their ranges meet, the boundary between species and subspecies is debated, and many former subspecies have been elevated to full species (especially in birds, following the phylogenetic species concept), while others have been synonymized. The concept is used far more for animals than for plants, and rarely for bacteria or fungi.

Examples

Domestic dogs are classified as Canis lupus familiaris, a subspecies of the wolf Canis lupus. The African bush elephant's forest relative is often treated as the subspecies Loxodonta africana cyclotis, though many authorities now treat it as a distinct species — illustrating the fluidity of the rank.
