Tulip mania (Dutch: tulpenmanie) was a period during the Dutch Golden Age when contract prices for some bulbs of the recently introduced and fashionable tulip reached extraordinarily high levels. The period generally is considered to have begun in 1634 and lasted until the dramatic collapse of the market in February 1637. It is generally considered to have been the first recorded speculative bubble or asset bubble in history, although modern scholarship has questioned both its scale and its economic impact.

Background

The tulip was introduced to Europe from the Ottoman Empire in the mid-16th century. The flowers' intense, saturated colours and the striped patterns produced by the tulip breaking virus made "broken" bulbs highly prized status symbols among wealthy Dutch merchants. By the 1630s a futures market had developed: bulbs still in the ground were bought and sold through contracts traded in taverns by so-called "colleges" of traders, often with only a small deposit paid.

The bubble

Prices for rare bulbs rose steeply during 1636. At the peak, a single bulb of the celebrated Semper Augustus variety was reportedly worth 6,000 florins — more than a grand Amsterdam canal house — while a Viceroy bulb was said to have been exchanged for goods worth 2,500 florins, including wheat, rye, oxen, silver and wine. In early February 1637, prices collapsed almost overnight; buyers refused to honour contracts and the market effectively disappeared.

Aftermath and legacy

The collapse bankrupted relatively few people, since most trades were never settled in cash; many contracts were annulled for a small percentage of their nominal value. Nonetheless, the episode became a byword for speculative excess, popularised by Charles Mackay's Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds (1841). Economists and historians continue to debate whether tulip mania was an irrational mania or a largely rational, if dramatic, market episode in a novelty luxury good.
