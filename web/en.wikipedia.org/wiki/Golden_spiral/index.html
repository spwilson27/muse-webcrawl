Golden spiral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geometry, a golden spiral is a logarithmic spiral whose growth factor is φ, the golden ratio. That is, a golden spiral gets wider (or farther from its origin) by a factor of φ for every quarter turn it makes.

Formula

The polar equation for a golden spiral is the same as for other logarithmic spirals, but with a special value of the growth factor: r = a ebθ, where the factor is chosen so that the radius grows by φ each quarter turn. This gives a growth factor per radian of φ2/π. The angle between the spiral and lines from its origin is a constant, approximately 73 degrees for the exact golden spiral.

Approximations

A common approximation is constructed inside a tiling of rectangles whose side lengths are Fibonacci numbers (the "Fibonacci spiral"): quarter-circles are drawn inside each square of the tiling. This produces a spiral visually close to, but not exactly, a golden spiral. Another true golden spiral can be approximated by drawing circular arcs within nested golden rectangles, whence the familiar picture of the spiral inscribed in a subdivided golden rectangle.

In nature and art

Spirals resembling the golden spiral are often claimed to occur in nautilus shells, hurricanes, and galaxies; most such claims are imprecise, since natural spirals are logarithmic-like but rarely have exactly the golden growth factor. The golden spiral nevertheless remains popular in art and design as a compositional device associated with the golden ratio.
