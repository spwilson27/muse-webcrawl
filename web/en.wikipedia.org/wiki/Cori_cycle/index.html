Cori cycle

The Cori cycle (also known as the lactic acid cycle), named after its discoverers, Carl Ferdinand Cori and Gerty Cori, is a metabolic pathway in which lactate, produced by anaerobic glycolysis in muscles, is transported to the liver and converted to glucose, which then returns to the muscles and is metabolized back to lactate.

Process

During vigorous exercise, oxygen supply cannot keep pace with demand, and muscle cells convert glucose to lactate, gaining a net 2 ATP per glucose molecule. Lactate enters the bloodstream and is taken up by the liver, where gluconeogenesis converts it back to glucose at a cost of 6 ATP. The glucose is released into the blood and returns to the muscle. The cycle thus shifts part of the metabolic burden from muscle to liver and prevents lactic acidosis, at a net cost of 4 ATP per cycle turn.

Significance

The Cori cycle is especially important during intense exercise and recovery. Its activity is increased in some pathological states, including cancer, where the related glucose–alanine cycle and tumour lactate production contribute to cachexia. Gerty and Carl Cori received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1947, in part for their work on carbohydrate metabolism.
