Antiretroviral drug

Antiretroviral drugs are medications used to treat infections by retroviruses, principally human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Different classes of antiretroviral drugs act at different stages of the HIV life cycle. When several drugs are combined, typically three from at least two classes, the regimen is known as antiretroviral therapy (ART), historically called highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART). Treatment suppresses viral replication to undetectable levels, restores immune function, prevents progression to AIDS, and blocks onward transmission.

Used forHIV infection; hepatitis B (some agents); pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis
First approvedZidovudine (AZT), 1987
Major classesNRTIs, NNRTIs, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors, entry inhibitors

Drug classes

Nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs)

NRTIs are analogues of natural nucleosides that terminate the DNA chain being built by the viral enzyme reverse transcriptase. Zidovudine (AZT), approved in 1987, was the first antiretroviral drug. Others include lamivudine, emtricitabine, abacavir, and the nucleotide analogue tenofovir. Two NRTIs usually form the "backbone" of a combination regimen.

Non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs)

NNRTIs such as efavirenz, nevirapine, rilpivirine, and doravirine bind directly to a pocket on reverse transcriptase and block its activity without being incorporated into DNA. They have a relatively low barrier to resistance.

Protease inhibitors

Protease inhibitors block the viral protease enzyme that cleaves new viral polyproteins into functional units. Early agents such as saquinavir (1995) transformed outcomes when combined with NRTIs. Modern agents like darunavir are "boosted" with ritonavir or cobicistat, which inhibit the drug's breakdown by the liver.

Integrase inhibitors

Integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs), beginning with raltegravir (2007) and including dolutegravir and bictegravir, prevent insertion of viral DNA into the host genome. Because of their potency, tolerability, and high resistance barrier, INSTI-based regimens are now preferred first-line therapy in WHO and other guidelines.

Entry and other inhibitors

Entry inhibitors block HIV's attachment to or entry into cells: the CCR5 antagonist maraviroc, the fusion inhibitor enfuvirtide, the attachment inhibitor fostemsavir, and the monoclonal antibody ibalizumab. The capsid inhibitor lenacapavir, approved in 2022, is given by injection every six months and interferes with several stages of the life cycle.

Development history

The rapid development of antiretrovirals after the identification of HIV in 1983–84 was driven by massive public and private investment and by activist pressure for faster drug approval. The arrival of combination therapy in 1996 sharply reduced AIDS mortality in countries with access, changing HIV from a usually fatal infection into a manageable chronic condition. Global access programmes — notably the U.S. PEPFAR initiative (2003) and the Global Fund — together with generic manufacture brought treatment to tens of millions in low- and middle-income countries; by the mid-2020s more than 30 million people were receiving ART worldwide.
