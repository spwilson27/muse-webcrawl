Cultural pluralism

Cultural pluralism is a term used when distinct cultural groups coexist within a society while maintaining their traditions and identities, as opposed to assimilation into a dominant culture or the "melting pot" model. Cultural pluralism assumes that diversity itself is valuable, that groups can participate fully in the larger society without surrendering their distinctiveness, and that mutual respect between groups is possible and desirable.

Origins

The concept is closely associated with the American philosopher Horace Kallen, who coined "cultural pluralism" in essays beginning in 1915 and in his book Culture and Democracy in the United States (1924). Writing against coercive Americanisation during the First World War, Kallen argued that democracy should resemble an orchestra in which each ethnic culture contributes its own timbre, rather than a melting pot that dissolves differences. Randolph Bourne's 1916 essay "Trans-National America" made a related cosmopolitan case.

Pluralism, multiculturalism, and assimilation

Cultural pluralism overlaps with, but is not identical to, multiculturalism, which usually denotes government policies actively recognising and supporting minority cultures (as officially adopted in Canada in 1971). It contrasts with assimilation, in which minorities gradually adopt the majority culture, and with segregation, in which groups coexist without equality or interaction. Critics of pluralism worry about relativism — whether respect for cultures can extend to practices that violate universal rights — and about whether group-based recognition freezes identities that are in fact fluid and contested from within, a point pressed by thinkers such as Will Kymlicka, Charles Taylor, and Amartya Sen.
