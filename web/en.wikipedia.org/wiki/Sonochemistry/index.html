Sonochemistry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sonochemistry is the area of chemistry concerned with the effects of ultrasound on chemical reactions and processes. Ultrasound (typically 20 kHz to several MHz) does not interact directly with molecules to break bonds; instead, the chemical effects arise from acoustic cavitation — the formation, growth, and violent implosive collapse of bubbles in a liquid.

Acoustic cavitation

When a bubble collapses, it generates a microscopic hot spot with estimated transient temperatures of about 5,000 K, pressures of hundreds to a thousand atmospheres, and heating and cooling rates above 10⁹ K per second. Within the bubble, vapour molecules are pyrolysed to free radicals — in water, hydroxyl and hydrogen radicals — which can initiate reactions in the surrounding liquid. Collapse near a solid surface produces microjets and shock waves that clean surfaces, erode particles, and drive mass transfer, while acoustic streaming emulsifies immiscible liquids.

Applications

Sonochemistry is used to accelerate and switch reaction pathways in organic synthesis, to prepare nanostructured materials such as amorphous metals and hollow nanospheres, to depolymerise polymers, and in environmental treatment to degrade pollutants in water. Common laboratory equipment includes ultrasonic cleaning baths (low intensity, good for heterogeneous reactions) and probe sonicators that deliver much higher power densities. The field grew rapidly from the 1980s, and a related discipline, sonoluminescence — the emission of light from collapsing bubbles — provided key evidence for the extreme conditions inside cavitation bubbles.
