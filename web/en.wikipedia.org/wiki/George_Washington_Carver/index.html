George Washington Carver

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: George Washington Carver in his Tuskegee laboratory surrounded by plant specimens]
George Washington Carver in his Tuskegee laboratory surrounded by plant specimens

George Washington Carver (c. 1864 – 5 January 1943) was an American agricultural scientist and inventor who promoted alternative crops to cotton — above all peanuts and sweet potatoes — to improve the lives of poor Southern farmers and the depleted soils of the Cotton Belt. Born into slavery in Missouri, he became the most prominent Black scientist of his era as head of agriculture at Tuskegee Institute under Booker T. Washington.

Life and education

Kidnapped as an infant with his mother by raiders, Carver was raised by his former enslavers, Moses and Susan Carver. Barred from local schools for his race, he educated himself across Kansas towns, eventually earning a master's in agricultural science from Iowa State University (1896), where he was its first Black student and later faculty member, before Washington recruited him to Tuskegee.

Work at Tuskegee

Carver's research station taught impoverished farmers crop rotation with legumes to restore nitrogen to cotton-exhausted soil, and his bulletins and 'Jesup wagon' mobile school carried practical agriculture to the countryside. To create markets for the new crops he publicised hundreds of uses for peanuts (some 300 products are attributed to him — most were compilations and variations rather than inventions; he received only three patents) and sweet potatoes. He testified before Congress in 1921 for the peanut tariff, a national sensation.

Legacy

Carver became an icon of education and self-help, admired across racial lines — and sometimes criticised for accommodationism — while his actual science centred on sustainability and the poor. He died at Tuskegee in 1943; the George Washington Carver National Monument in Missouri was the first US national monument honouring an African American.
