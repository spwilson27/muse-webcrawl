Forest gardening

Forest gardening is a low-maintenance, sustainable, plant-based food production and agroforestry system based on woodland ecosystems, incorporating fruit and nut trees, shrubs, herbs, vines and perennial vegetables which have yields directly useful to humans. Making use of companion planting, these can be intermixed to grow in a succession of layers to build a woodland habitat. Forest gardening is a prehistoric method of securing food in tropical areas. In the 1980s, Robert Hart coined the term "forest gardening" and adapted the principles and applied them to temperate climates.

History

Forest gardens are probably the world's oldest form of land use and most resilient agroecosystem. They originated in prehistoric times along jungle-clad river banks and in the wet foothills of monsoon regions. In the gradual process of families improving their immediate environment, useful tree and vine species were identified, protected and improved whilst undesirable species were eliminated. Eventually foreign species were also selected and incorporated into the gardens.

Forest gardening was once common in the tropical regions of Asia, Africa and the Americas. In Europe and North America, Robert Hart pioneered a temperate-climate forest garden at Wenlock Edge in Shropshire during the 1980s, growing apples, pears, other fruit and vegetables on a small plot modelled on the structure of a natural woodland.

The seven layers

Hart identified the layers of a natural woodland and adapted them to a productive garden:

  1. The canopy layer, consisting of the original mature fruit trees.
  2. The low tree layer of smaller nut and fruit trees on dwarfing root stocks.
  3. The shrub layer of fruit bushes such as currants and berries.
  4. The herbaceous layer of perennial vegetables and herbs.
  5. The rhizosphere or underground layer of plants grown for their roots and tubers.
  6. The ground cover layer of edible plants that spread horizontally.
  7. The vertical layer of vines and climbers.

Principles and practice

A forest garden is designed to be largely self-maintaining: perennial plants replace annual cropping, ground cover suppresses weeds, and nitrogen-fixing plants provide fertility. Forest gardening forms a core technique of permaculture. Once established, inputs are mainly limited to harvesting, monitoring and limited pruning, although yields per unit area may be lower than in intensive annual systems, the diversity of produce — fruit, nuts, herbs, perennial vegetables, fungi and firewood — is much greater.
