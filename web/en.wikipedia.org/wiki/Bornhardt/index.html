A bornhardt is a dome-shaped, steep-sided, bald rock landform, a type of inselberg typically composed of granite or gneiss. Bornhardts rise abruptly from otherwise gently sloping or flat erosion surfaces, often in tropical and subtropical regions. The term was introduced by the German geologist Wilhelm Bornhardt, who described such features in German East Africa around 1900.

Characteristics

Bornhardts are characterised by convex-upward slopes, a bare rock surface largely free of soil, and a sharp break of slope (piedmont angle) at their base. Many show sheet jointing — curved fractures parallel to the surface — from which thin rock slabs exfoliate. They range from small domes a few tens of metres high to massive features such as Uluru in Australia or Sugarloaf Mountain in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.

Formation

The origin of bornhardts has been debated for over a century. The currently favoured two-stage (etching) hypothesis holds that deep chemical weathering along fracture systems first produces an irregular weathering front beneath the surface; compartments of closely fractured rock weather to deep saprolite while massive, sparsely jointed compartments remain as solid rock. Subsequent stripping of the weathered material — by erosion during drier or tectonically uplifted phases — exposes the unweathered compartments as domes. Alternative explanations emphasise scarp retreat and pediplanation (championed by Lester Charles King) or structural control alone. Bornhardts are noted for their great longevity: some survive as relict landforms over tens of millions of years of landscape evolution.

Distribution

Classic bornhardt landscapes occur on the ancient shields and cratons of Africa, Brazil, India, Sri Lanka and Australia, as well as in the southwestern United States (e.g., Texas and Georgia's Stone Mountain).
