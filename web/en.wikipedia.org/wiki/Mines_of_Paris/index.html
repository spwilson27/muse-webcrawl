Mines of Paris

The mines of Paris (French: carrières de Paris, "quarries of Paris") are a network of underground tunnels and chambers beneath Paris, France, created by centuries of quarrying of limestone, gypsum, and chalk used to build the city above. The network extends for roughly 300 kilometres in total, mostly on the Left Bank of the Seine, though only a small fraction is accessible to the public.

History

Extraction began in Roman times in open pits south of Lutetia and moved underground as the city grew over the workings. By the eighteenth century, abandoned quarries beneath the streets caused a series of collapses, prompting Louis XVI to create the Inspection Générale des Carrières in 1777 to map, consolidate, and reinforce the voids. Beginning in 1786, part of the network was consecrated as a municipal ossuary — the famous Catacombs of Paris — into which the remains of millions of Parisians were transferred from overflowing cemeteries.

The network today

Access to the quarries outside the official Catacombs museum is prohibited, but the tunnels have long attracted clandestine urban explorers known as cataphiles, and the network has acquired a rich folklore. The tunnels are monitored by a dedicated police unit. Only about 1.7 kilometres of ossuary galleries, in the Denfert-Rochereau area, are open to visitors as the Catacombs museum.
