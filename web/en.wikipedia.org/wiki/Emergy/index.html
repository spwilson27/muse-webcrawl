Emergy

Emergy accounting traces all energy embodied in a product back to solar energy

Emergy is the amount of energy consumed in direct and indirect transformations to make a product or service. The concept and methodology were developed by the systems ecologist Howard T. Odum. Emergy is usually expressed in terms of a common baseline, most often solar energy, in units of solar emjoules (sej): it is the total solar energy, used directly and indirectly, required to make something.

Concepts

Emergy differs from energy because it accounts for the quality of different energy forms and for the convergence of many inputs into a single output through chains of transformations. The emergy per unit of available energy of a product is its transformity (sej/J); the further along a transformation chain a product lies — for example, electricity relative to the sunlight that grew the fuel — the higher its transformity. Emergy synthesis applies these ideas to evaluate the environmental support, sustainability and efficiency of ecosystems, technologies and whole economies.

Applications and criticism

Emergy analysis has been applied to agriculture, electricity generation, waste treatment, national economies and ecosystem valuation, providing a donor-side measure of value based on the work of nature required to produce goods and services. The approach has also attracted criticism: some researchers question the theoretical basis of transformities, the universality of the maximum power principle associated with Odum's work, and the practical reproducibility of emergy calculations.
