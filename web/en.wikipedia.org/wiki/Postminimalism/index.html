Postminimalism

Postminimalist works, such as Eva Hesse's latex and rope sculptures, let process and material shape the result

Postminimalism is an art movement that emerged in the late 1960s and 1970s, primarily in the United States, as a reaction against the rigid geometry and industrial fabrication of minimalism. The term was coined by the critic Robert Pincus-Witten around 1971 to describe work that retained minimalism's reduction of form while reintroducing process, the body, unconventional and perishable materials, and a sense of the handmade. Key early exhibitions included Lucy Lippard's "Eccentric Abstraction" (1966) and the Whitney Museum's "Anti-Illusion: Procedures/Materials" (1969).

Characteristics

Postminimalist artists often worked with materials such as latex, felt, rope, rubber, neon, wax, and scattered or poured substances, allowing gravity, sagging, and chance to determine the final form. Richard Serra's splash pieces and thrown lead, Robert Morris's felt works, Eva Hesse's latex and fibreglass sculptures, and Bruce Nauman's body casts and neon works exemplify the movement's emphasis on process art and the physical presence of materials. Unlike the minimalists' factory-finished objects, postminimalist works frequently appeared soft, fragile, erotic, or deliberately anti-monumental.

Artists and influence

Artists associated with postminimalism include Eva Hesse, Bruce Nauman, Richard Serra, Robert Morris, Louise Bourgeois, Lynda Benglis, Keith Sonnier, Alan Saret, and Jackie Winsor. The movement overlapped with and fed into land art, performance art, and conceptual art, and it strongly influenced later sculpture through its validation of impermanence, bodily reference, and material experimentation. Pincus-Witten later connected postminimalism's sensibility to the rise of earthworks and body art in his writings on postmodernism.
