Robinson–Schensted correspondence

Robinson–Schensted correspondence
Row-insertion (bumping) in a Young tableau
FieldAlgebraic combinatorics

MapsPermutations ↔ pairs of standard Young tableaux of the same shape

In mathematics, the Robinson–Schensted correspondence, also known as the RSK correspondence in its generalisation by Donald Knuth, is a bijective correspondence between permutations and pairs of standard Young tableaux of the same shape. It is a cornerstone of algebraic combinatorics, linking the combinatorics of permutations with the representation theory of the symmetric group and of the general linear groups.

The insertion algorithm

Given a permutation, the correspondence is constructed by Schensted insertion: the entries of the permutation are inserted one at a time into an initially empty Young tableau by row insertion. To insert a number x into a row, find the smallest entry in the row greater than x; if none exists, append x at the end of the row and stop; otherwise replace that entry with x and "bump" the displaced entry into the next row, repeating the process. The final insertion tableau is called P. A second tableau Q, the recording tableau, is built by writing the step number into the new cell created at each insertion. The construction is reversible, establishing a bijection between permutations of {1, …, n} and pairs (P, Q) of standard Young tableaux of the same shape with n cells.

Properties

Schensted's theorem states that the length of the first row of the shape equals the length of the longest increasing subsequence of the permutation, and the length of the first column equals the length of the longest decreasing subsequence. The inverse permutation corresponds to swapping P and Q. Summing the squares of the numbers of tableaux over all shapes recovers the identity n! = Σ (fλ)2, the combinatorial shadow of the decomposition of the regular representation of the symmetric group.

Generalisations

Knuth generalised the correspondence to matrices of non-negative integers, matched to pairs of semistandard tableaux (the RSK algorithm), yielding the Cauchy identity for Schur functions. The correspondence also underlies the hook-length formula's bijective proofs, the combinatorics of the plactic monoid, and — through the work of Greene — refined invariants tracking unions of increasing subsequences. Robinson's original 1938 construction arose in his work on the representation theory of the symmetric group; Schensted independently rediscovered it in 1961 while studying longest monotone subsequences.
