Echinoderms are marine invertebrates of the phylum Echinodermata (from Greek echinos, "hedgehog," and derma, "skin"), recognised by their (usually five-point) radial symmetry, a water vascular system, and an internal skeleton of calcareous ossicles often bearing spines. The phylum includes starfish (sea stars), brittle stars, sea urchins, sand dollars, sea cucumbers, and crinoids. Echinoderms are exclusively marine and are found at every ocean depth, from the intertidal zone to the abyss.

Anatomy and physiology

Adult echinoderms are radially symmetrical — typically pentaradial — although their larvae are bilaterally symmetrical, evidence that they evolved from bilateral ancestors; they are deuterostomes, closely related to chordates. A unique water vascular system, a network of fluid-filled canals derived from the coelom, powers hundreds of tube feet used in locomotion, feeding, and respiration. The body wall contains an endoskeleton of calcite plates (ossicles) that may form a rigid test (as in sea urchins) or remain flexible. Many echinoderms can regenerate lost arms and other body parts, and some reproduce by fission.

Ecology and importance

Echinoderms are ecologically important as grazers (sea urchins can denude kelp forests when their predators decline), bioturbators, and prey; starfish such as the crown-of-thorns can devastate coral reefs. Sea cucumbers and sea urchins are harvested commercially as food in Asia and elsewhere, and echinoderms — especially sea urchin embryos — have been classic model organisms in developmental biology since the nineteenth century.
