Relative and absolute verbal aspects

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, relative and absolute describe the deictic anchoring of tense–aspect categories. An absolute tense or aspect locates an event directly in relation to the moment of speech, whereas a relative tense or aspect locates it in relation to some other reference point, such as the time of a main-clause event.

Distinction

The distinction, associated with the typological work of Bernard Comrie, is illustrated by the English pluperfect: in "John had arrived", the arrival is not dated directly from the present moment but is presented as anterior to a past reference point — making the perfect a relative past. By contrast, the simple past in "John arrived" is absolute, being interpreted relative to the speech event itself. Relative tenses arise naturally in subordinate clauses and reported speech, where forms such as the future-in-the-past ("he said he would come") express anteriority or posteriority to a reference time other than now.

Grammatical aspect

Applied to aspect, the absolute/relative contrast concerns whether a viewpoint — for example, perfective or imperfective — is computed from the speech situation or from an internal reference interval. Many languages combine both systems, and analyses of aspect in narrative discourse frequently treat the foreground/background movement of a text as a chain of relative temporal relations.
