Retraction in academic publishing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In academic publishing, a retraction is a public statement, made by the authors of an article or by the editor of the journal that published it, that withdraws the article from the scientific literature, because its content is seriously flawed, its findings unreliable, or it was produced through research or publication misconduct. Retracted articles are typically retained in the journal's archive, marked as retracted, rather than removed, so that the scholarly record is preserved.

Reasons for retraction

  • Scientific misconduct — fabrication, falsification, or manipulation of data or images.
  • Plagiarism or redundant (duplicate) publication.
  • Honest error — mistakes in data, methods, or analysis that invalidate the conclusions; author-initiated retractions for genuine errors are sometimes called self-retractions.
  • Ethical violations — research conducted without required approvals or consent.
  • Compromised peer review, authorship disputes, undisclosed conflicts of interest, and papers produced by paper mills.

The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) publishes widely used guidelines recommending that editors retract articles when findings are unreliable, whether through misconduct or honest error, and that retraction notices be linked to the article and state clearly who is retracting it and why.

Scale and impact

The number of retractions has grown markedly: more than 10,000 papers were retracted in 2023 alone, a record driven in part by mass retractions of paper-mill articles from special issues. The database maintained by Retraction Watch tracks tens of thousands of retractions. Retracted papers may continue to be cited, sometimes uncritically, a phenomenon known as citation contamination. High-profile examples include Andrew Wakefield's 1998 Lancet paper fraudulently linking the MMR vaccine to autism (fully retracted in 2010) and the Schön scandals in condensed-matter physics.

Related notices include corrections or errata (for minor errors that do not invalidate the work), expressions of concern (flagging unresolved doubts), and removals (used rarely, for legal or safety reasons).
