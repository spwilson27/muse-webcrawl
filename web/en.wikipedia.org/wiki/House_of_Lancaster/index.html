The House of Lancaster was a cadet branch of the royal House of Plantagenet of England. It provided three kings — Henry IV, Henry V and Henry VI — between 1399 and 1461, and was one of the two rival houses (against the House of York) in the Wars of the Roses.

Origins

The Lancastrian line descends from Edmund "Crouchback", younger son of Henry III, who was made Earl of Lancaster in 1267. His descendant John of Gaunt, third son of Edward III, married the heiress Blanche of Lancaster and became Duke of Lancaster, the richest magnate in England. Gaunt's son Henry Bolingbroke deposed his cousin Richard II in 1399 and reigned as Henry IV.

Kings of England

Henry IV (r. 1399–1413) faced repeated rebellions, including Owain Glyndŵr's Welsh revolt and the Percy rebellion defeated at Shrewsbury. His son Henry V (r. 1413–1422) revived the English claim to France, won the Battle of Agincourt (1415), and was recognised as heir to the French throne by the Treaty of Troyes, but died young. Henry VI (r. 1422–1461, 1470–1471), who succeeded as an infant, lost nearly all English possessions in France and suffered bouts of mental illness.

Wars of the Roses and end

Henry VI's incapacity and unpopular government provoked the challenge of Richard, Duke of York, leading to the Wars of the Roses. Henry was deposed by Edward IV in 1461, briefly restored in 1470, and died in the Tower of London in 1471; his only son Edward of Westminster was killed at the Battle of Tewkesbury the same year, extinguishing the direct Lancastrian male line. The Lancastrian claim passed through the Beaufort line to Henry Tudor, who defeated Richard III at Bosworth in 1485 and founded the Tudor dynasty, marrying Elizabeth of York to unite the two houses.
