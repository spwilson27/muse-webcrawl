Southern elephant seal

The southern elephant seal (Mirounga leonina) is the largest living member of the order Carnivora and the largest pinniped. It inhabits subantarctic and Antarctic waters of the Southern Ocean, breeding on islands such as South Georgia, Kerguelen, Heard and Macquarie, and on the Valdés Peninsula in Argentina.

Description

The species is extremely sexually dimorphic: bulls may exceed 5 metres in length and 3,000–4,000 kilograms, several times the mass of females, and possess an inflatable proboscis used to amplify roars during the breeding season. Males fight violently for control of harems on breeding beaches, where a dominant "beachmaster" may mate with dozens of females.

Life at sea

Elephant seals spend most of the year foraging at sea, diving almost continuously to depths commonly of 400–600 metres, and recorded beyond 2,000 metres, for squid and fish. Satellite tracking has made them important samplers of Southern Ocean oceanography, their instrumented dives supplying temperature and salinity profiles from otherwise inaccessible regions.

Conservation

Hunted heavily in the 19th century for oil, the species recovered under protection; populations at some islands later declined, for reasons still debated, and the species is currently listed as Least Concern overall.
