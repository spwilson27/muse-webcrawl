Adhesive tape is a strip of material — paper, plastic film, cloth, foil or foam — coated on one or both sides with a pressure-sensitive adhesive that sticks to surfaces with light pressure and without heat, water or solvents. Adhesive tapes are used for sealing, masking, fastening, insulating, repairing and medical purposes, and have become among the most commonplace products of the twentieth century.

History

Surgical and sticking plasters date to the nineteenth century, but modern tape began with the work of Richard Gurley Drew at the Minnesota Mining and Manufacturing Company (3M). In 1925 Drew developed masking tape, a paper tape with a gentle adhesive that painters could remove without damaging two-tone car paintwork. In 1930 he introduced transparent cellophane tape, sold as Scotch tape, which became a household staple during the Great Depression for mending books, paper and packaging. Cloth-backed duct tape was developed during the Second World War for sealing ammunition cases.

Construction and adhesion

A typical single-sided tape comprises a backing, a primer, the adhesive layer (based on natural rubber, acrylics or silicones), and a release coating that lets the roll unwind. Pressure-sensitive adhesives are viscoelastic: they flow under light pressure to wet the surface and resist peeling through cohesive strength. Performance is described by tack, peel adhesion and shear resistance, and tapes may be reinforced, double-sided, foam-cored, or designed for high temperatures, electrical insulation or skin contact.

Common types

  • Masking tape — crepe-paper tape for painting and general use.
  • Duct tape — strong cloth-backed tape for repairs and sealing ducts.
  • Electrical tape — stretchy PVC tape for insulating wires.
  • Cellophane/clear tape — transparent film tape for office and packaging use.
  • Medical tape — skin-friendly tapes for dressings; athletic tape for support.
  • Gaffer tape — matte cloth tape used in film and stage work.
