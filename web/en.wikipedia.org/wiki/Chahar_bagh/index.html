Chahar bagh

Chahar bagh or charbagh (Persian: چهارباغ, "four gardens") is a Persian and Islamic quadrilateral garden layout based on the four gardens of Paradise described in the Quran. The rectangular garden is divided into four smaller parts by walkways or flowing water channels, often with a pavilion or pool at their intersection. The form travelled from Persia across the Islamic world, reaching its most famous expression in the Mughal gardens of India.

Origins

The concept is ancient: the royal garden at Pasargadae, laid out for Cyrus the Great in the sixth century BC, was irrigated by channels laid out in a regular fourfold geometry, and is often cited as the earliest chahar bagh. In Islamic interpretation the four channels came to represent the rivers of Paradise — of water, milk, honey and wine — flowing through the Garden (Jannah), and the four parts the elements or the four rivers of Eden.

Development

Safavid Isfahan gave its name to the Chaharbagh Avenue, a grand tree-lined boulevard flanked by gardens, laid out under Shah Abbas I. In Mughal India the form fused with tomb architecture: Humayun's Tomb in Delhi (c. 1570) set the emperor's mausoleum at the centre of a vast chahar bagh, while at the Taj Mahal the tomb stands at the end of the garden, with the four-part plan extending before it and the Yamuna river completing the scheme. Terraced variants, such as the Shalimar Gardens in Kashmir and Lahore, cascaded the fourfold layout down hillsides in a series of levels.

Design elements

Typical elements include enclosing walls, axial water channels (chadar cascades on slopes), fountains and pools for reflection and cooling, raised walkways, shade trees and fruit trees planted in the quarters, and a pavilion (baradari) at the crossing. The geometry imposes order and coolness on the garden's microclimate in arid regions.
