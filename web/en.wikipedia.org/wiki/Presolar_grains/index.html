Presolar grains

Presolar grains are tiny particles of solid material that formed in the outflows and explosions of stars that lived and died before the Sun was born. They became incorporated into the collapsing molecular cloud that produced the Solar System, survived — largely unaltered — inside primitive meteorites and cometary material, and can be identified in the laboratory because their isotopic compositions differ dramatically from anything formed within the Solar System. They are, literally, samples of stardust that can be analysed atom by atom.

Discovery

The existence of presolar material was first implied in the 1960s and 1970s by anomalies in the isotopic ratios of noble gases in meteorites, but the grains themselves were isolated in 1987, when Edward Anders and colleagues at the University of Chicago dissolved away most of a meteorite's mass with acids and found that the residue of nanodiamonds carried the anomalous xenon signature. Silicon carbide grains bearing unmistakable signatures of slow neutron-capture (s-process) nucleosynthesis were identified soon afterwards, clinching the case that solid stellar condensates survive in meteorites.

Types

Known presolar minerals include nanodiamond (the most abundant, though only a fraction are demonstrably presolar), silicon carbide, graphite, corundum and other aluminium oxides, spinel, hibonite, silicon nitride, and silicates. Presolar silicates, recognised only in the 2000s because they are destroyed by the acid treatments used to isolate other grains, are now known to be among the most abundant types in the most primitive chondrites and in interplanetary dust particles.

Stellar sources

Isotopic fingerprints let researchers assign many grains to particular kinds of stars. Mainstream silicon carbide grains match the predicted composition of the winds of low-mass asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars, where the s-process operates. Rare grain types record other environments: some graphite and silicon carbide grains carry signatures of Type II supernovae, including decay products of short-lived isotopes such as titanium-44, while others come from novae or from carbon-rich AGB stars. Because each grain condenses in a single star, the population samples many individual stars that predated the Sun.

Significance

Presolar grains provide ground truth for models of stellar nucleosynthesis and dust formation, reveal the mix of stellar sources that contributed material to the early Solar System, and show that the Sun's parent cloud contained material processed through several generations of stars. In 2020, dating of presolar silicon carbide from the Murchison meteorite identified grains around 7 billion years old — the oldest solid material ever dated, far older than the Solar System itself.
