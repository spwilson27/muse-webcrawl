Abyssopelagic zone

The abyssopelagic zone, or abyssal zone, is the layer of the deep ocean extending from about 4,000 metres (13,000 ft) to 6,000 metres (20,000 ft) below the surface. Its name comes from the Greek abyssos, "bottomless." It lies between the bathypelagic zone above and the hadalpelagic zone of the deep trenches below, and its lower boundary roughly coincides with the abyssal plains that cover more than half of the Earth's solid surface.

Conditions

No sunlight penetrates to abyssal depths; the water is uniformly cold (around 2–4 °C) and under immense pressure, roughly 400–600 atmospheres. Oxygen is nonetheless present throughout, supplied by deep thermohaline circulation from polar surface waters. With no photosynthesis, the ecosystem runs almost entirely on "marine snow" — a slow rain of dead organisms and detritus from the sunlit layers above — supplemented locally by chemosynthesis at hydrothermal vents and cold seeps, and occasionally by large food falls such as whale carcasses.

Life

Despite the harshness, the abyss supports a diverse fauna adapted to scarcity: slow-growing fishes such as rattails (grenadiers) and cusk-eels, giant isopods, sea cucumbers and other deposit-feeding invertebrates that dominate the soft-sediment plains, glass sponges, and cephalopods including the dumbo octopus. Animals tend to be small or gelatinous, with slow metabolisms, long lives and low population densities, though total diversity is high — a single survey of abyssal sediment may contain hundreds of species, most undescribed.

Human significance

The abyssal plains host polymetallic (manganese) nodules rich in nickel, cobalt and copper, which are the target of proposed deep-sea mining — a source of international regulatory debate given the fragility and slow recovery of abyssal ecosystems. The zone is explored with remotely operated and autonomous vehicles and landers, since few crewed submersibles (such as Alvin, rated to 6,500 m) can reach it.
