Alienation (philosophy)

Alienation (German: Entfremdung) is a concept in philosophy and social theory describing a condition in which individuals become estranged from themselves, from other people, from their work, or from society. It was developed by Hegel, reworked by Feuerbach, and given its most influential form in Karl Marx's theory of the alienation of labour under capitalism.

Marx's theory

In the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, Marx argued that under capitalism the worker is alienated in four ways: from the product of labour, which belongs to another; from the activity of production, which is coerced and external; from his or her species-being (Gattungswesen), the distinctively human capacity for free, conscious, creative activity; and from other workers, with whom relations are mediated by competition and the market.

Earlier and later developments

Hegel had treated alienation as a necessary stage in Spirit's self-realization, and Feuerbach applied it to religion: God, he argued, is humanity's own essence projected and estranged. In the twentieth century, existentialists and thinkers such as Erich Fromm, Herbert Marcuse, and the Frankfurt School extended the notion to consumer society and mass culture, while sociologists operationalised it in studies of powerlessness, meaninglessness, and anomie.
