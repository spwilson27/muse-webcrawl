Fischer–Speier esterification

Fischer–Speier esterification
TypeOrganic reaction
Named afterEmil Fischer, Arthur Speier
Reaction typeCondensation

Fischer esterification or Fischer–Speier esterification is a special type of esterification by refluxing a carboxylic acid and an alcohol in the presence of an acid catalyst. The reaction was first described by Emil Fischer and Arthur Speier in 1895. Most carboxylic acids are suitable for the reaction, but the alcohol should generally be a primary or secondary alkyl. Tertiary alcohols are prone to elimination, and phenols are usually too unreactive to give useful yields.

Mechanism

The mechanism involves protonation of the carbonyl oxygen of the carboxylic acid, nucleophilic attack by the alcohol on the carbonyl carbon, proton transfers within the tetrahedral intermediate, and loss of water to give the ester after deprotonation. The reaction is an equilibrium process: the classic Fischer esterification is thermodynamically controlled, and the position of equilibrium can be shifted toward the ester by using excess alcohol or by removing water as it forms, for example with a Dean–Stark trap or molecular sieves.

Applications

The reaction is widely used in the laboratory and in industry to prepare esters, which are important as solvents, flavourings, fragrances and intermediates. Common acid catalysts include sulfuric acid, p-toluenesulfonic acid and dry hydrogen chloride. Lewis acids such as scandium triflate can also catalyse the reaction.
