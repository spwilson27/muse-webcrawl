Kashubians

The Kashubians (Kashubian: Kaszëbi; Polish: Kaszubi) are a West Slavic ethnic group native to Kashubia, a region of Eastern Pomerania on the Baltic coast of north-central Poland, around the cities of Gdańsk, Gdynia and Kartuzy. They speak Kashubian, the only surviving language of the Pomeranian group of Lechitic languages, recognised in Poland since 2005 as a regional language.

History

The Kashubians descend from the Slavic Pomeranian tribes settled along the Baltic since the early Middle Ages. Their lands were ruled successively by the Duchy of Pomerania, the Teutonic Order, the Polish Crown, Prussia and the German Empire, and after the First World War most of Kashubia lay in the Polish "corridor" to the sea, while centuries of Germanisation pressure reduced Kashubian speech in the west. During the Second World War Kashubians suffered persecution, including massacres at Piaśnica, and many were conscripted into the Wehrmacht. After 1945 Kashubia remained in Poland, and Kashubian identity revived strongly after 1989.

Culture and language

The griffin — the black griffin on a golden field — is the Kashubian emblem, and the Kashubian anthem is Zemia rodnô. Folk traditions include distinctive embroidery (with motifs in seven colours), snuff-taking from ornate horn boxes, the legend of the Stolem giants, and a strong Catholic heritage centred on shrines such as Sianowo, the "Queen of Kashubia". In Poland's 2011 census, over 200,000 people declared Kashubian identity (often jointly with Polish identity), and roughly 100,000 reported speaking Kashubian at home. Kashubian is taught in schools, used in some local administration and media in Pomeranian Voivodeship, and has a growing literature whose founders include Florian Ceynowa, the 19th-century "awakener" of the Kashubians.
