Sensory substitution

Sensory substitution is the use of one sense to supply environmental information normally gathered by another sense. A sensory substitution device converts stimuli characteristic of one sensory modality into stimuli of another modality, allowing a person who lacks a sense — for example, a blind person — to perceive the world through an intact sense such as touch or hearing. The field was pioneered in the 1960s by the neuroscientist Paul Bach-y-Rita.

History

Bach-y-Rita's Tactile Vision Substitution System (1969) used a camera whose image was translated into a pattern of vibrations on a grid of stimulators placed on the user's back (later the abdomen and tongue). Blind subjects learned to recognise objects, judge distances and even catch rolling balls, reporting that the perception seemed located in external space rather than on the skin. Later devices include the BrainPort, which delivers camera images as electrotactile patterns on the tongue, and the vOICe software, which converts images into soundscapes.

Mechanism

Sensory substitution works because perception depends less on the sensory organ than on the structure of the information and the brain's ability to exploit it. Neuroimaging shows that in trained users, visual areas of the brain (such as the occipital cortex) are activated by the substitute input, evidence of cross-modal plasticity. Skilled users may describe the resulting experience in quasi-visual terms.

Applications

Devices have been developed to assist the blind and deaf, to augment the senses (for example, a belt that vibrates toward magnetic north, giving a continuous sense of direction), and for balance rehabilitation in patients with vestibular damage.
