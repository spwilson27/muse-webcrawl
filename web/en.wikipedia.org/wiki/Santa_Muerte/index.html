Santa Muerte (Spanish for "Saint Death" or "Holy Death"), formally Nuestra Señora de la Santa Muerte, is a Mexican folk saint personifying death, depicted as a female skeletal figure draped in robes and holding a scythe and a globe. Venerated for healing, protection and safe passage to the afterlife, her cult has grown rapidly since the 1990s–2000s in Mexico and among the Mexican diaspora, spread also to the United States and Central America, and is often described as the fastest-growing religious movement in the Americas.

Devotion

Devotees — who include many who also identify as Catholic, together with marginalised groups such as the poor, street vendors, taxi drivers, sex workers, LGBT communities, prisoners and, notoriously, members of drug cartels — pray to Santa Muerte for favours: health, work, love, money, protection from violence, and a painless death. She is regarded as impartial and non-judgemental, accepting petitions others might not bring to conventional saints.

Altars to her are decked with candles (whose colours encode the request: white for purity and consecration, red for love, gold for prosperity, black for protection against enemies, green for justice and legal matters), flowers, fruit, sweets, tequila or mezcal, cigarettes, and statues dressed in bridal, coloured or sequined robes. Public devotion took off after 2001, when Enriqueta Romero displayed a life-size statue outside her home in Tepito, Mexico City, creating the cult's most famous shrine. Rosary services, novenas and feast-day processions are held at shrines across Mexico; the largest modern temple sanctuary is in Tultitlán, State of Mexico.

Origins

Scholars trace Santa Muerte's roots to a syncretism of pre-Hispanic Aztec and Maya death deities (such as Mictecacihuatl, "Lady of the Dead") with the skeletal female Death figures of medieval Spanish Catholicism and European danse macabre imagery. Colonial-era records mention love-magic rituals invoking a "Santa Muerte", suggesting her folk cult survived in private for centuries before emerging into public view in the late 20th century.

Controversy

The Roman Catholic Church condemns the cult as blasphemous — Vatican officials have called it a degeneration of religion — and the Mexican army has destroyed roadside Santa Muerte shrines, particularly those associated with cartels. The association of some devotees with organised crime has stigmatised the cult as "narco-religiosity", though researchers stress that the great majority of devotees are law-abiding poor people seeking protection in dangerous circumstances.
