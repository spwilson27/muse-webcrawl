Safari park

A safari park, sometimes known as a wildlife park, is a zoo-like commercial drive-in tourist attraction in which visitors can drive their own vehicles or ride in vehicles provided by the facility to observe freely roaming animals. Safari parks often combine drive-through reserves with conventional walk-around zoo areas, amusement rides, and other attractions. The main attractions are large animals from sub-Saharan Africa such as giraffes, zebras, antelopes, white rhinoceroses, lions and cheetahs, kept in sizable paddocks intended to evoke an African savanna.

History

The concept reverses the traditional zoo: the animals range freely over large enclosures while the humans are confined in vehicles ("the people are in the cage"). An early precursor was the "African safari" park at Whipsnade in England (opened 1931 as a country zoo). The modern drive-through safari park was pioneered by Longleat in Wiltshire, England, which opened the "Lions of Longleat" reserve in 1966 on the Marquess of Bath's estate, in partnership with circus impresario Jimmy Chipperfield. The idea spread rapidly: Windsor Safari Park, Woburn Safari Park and Knowsley Safari Park followed in Britain, and in North America parks such as Lion Country Safari in Florida (1967) and later San Diego Zoo Safari Park (opened 1972 as the San Diego Wild Animal Park) adopted the model. Large safari parks were also established in Japan, China (e.g. Chimelong Safari Park in Guangzhou, among the world's largest) and elsewhere.

Operation and rules

Reserves are divided into sections, often separating herbivores from carnivores, with double gates between sections. Rules typically require visitors to remain in their vehicles with windows closed in predator sections; soft-top vehicles and motorcycles may be excluded from lion reserves. Many parks operate their own buses or monorails (San Diego's guided tours; the former monorail at Longleat) and offer feeding encounters, particularly with giraffes.

Conservation and criticism

Safari parks participate in managed breeding programmes for endangered species (including white rhinos, Grevy's zebras and scimitar-horned oryx) and argue that large enclosures provide better welfare than conventional cages. Criticisms include the keeping of animals in unsuitable climates, inbreeding in small populations, surplus-animal culling controversies in the wider zoo industry, and incidents in which animals have damaged vehicles or, rarely, injured visitors who broke the rules.
