Line (geometry)

In geometry, a straight line, usually shortened to line, is an infinitely long object with no width, depth, or curvature. In modern mathematics, given the multiplicity of geometries, the concept of a line is closely tied to the way the geometry is described. In Euclidean geometry, a line is taken as a primitive notion, defined by Euclid as "breadthless length" that "lies equally with respect to the points on itself"; two points determine a unique line passing through them.

Euclidean plane

In Cartesian coordinates, a (non-vertical) line in the plane is described by the slope-intercept equation

y = mx + b,

where m is the slope and b the y-intercept. Lines may be parallel (never meeting), perpendicular (meeting at a right angle), or intersecting. A finite portion of a line between two endpoints is a line segment, and a portion extending infinitely in one direction is a ray.

Generalizations

In three dimensions, lines are described by parametric equations with a direction vector. In projective geometry, lines are augmented by points at infinity, so that parallel lines meet — see line at infinity. In non-Euclidean geometries, the role of "straight lines" is played by geodesics: great circles on a sphere, for example. In differential geometry, more general curves take over as the paths of shortest distance.
