The Soviet famine of 1930–1933 was a man-made famine that swept the grain-growing regions of the Soviet Union in the early 1930s, killing several million people. It struck Ukraine (where it is known as the Holodomor), Kazakhstan (where a proportionally even greater share of the population died), the North Caucasus, the Volga region and parts of Russia proper.

Causes

The famine followed Joseph Stalin's forced collectivisation of agriculture, launched in 1929: peasants were driven into collective farms (kolkhozy), "kulaks" were deported or shot, and the state imposed grain procurement quotas that left many villages without enough food or seed. Livestock herds collapsed as peasants slaughtered animals rather than surrender them. When harvests fell, the regime continued to export grain, refused to release state reserves on an adequate scale, and in Ukraine and the Kuban applied "blacklisting" of villages that failed quotas, sealing them off from supplies. An internal passport system and directives blocking peasant flight from Ukraine and the North Caucasus (January 1933) prevented the starving from seeking food elsewhere.

Death toll and debates

Demographic reconstructions generally estimate between roughly 5.7 and 8.7 million excess deaths across the USSR in 1930–34, including about 3.5–5 million in Ukraine and around 1.5 million or more in Kazakhstan, where the Kazakh share of the population fell from about 60 percent to about 38 percent. Historians debate the relative roles of policy, ideology and weather, and whether the famine in Ukraine constituted genocide — a position adopted by Ukraine and recognised by many states, and disputed by others, including Russia. The famine was denied by the Soviet authorities at the time and covered up in much Western reporting, most notoriously by the New York Times correspondent Walter Duranty.
