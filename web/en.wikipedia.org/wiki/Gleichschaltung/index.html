Gleichschaltung

Gleichschaltung
Literal meaning"Coordination"; "switching onto the same track"
Period1933–1934 (continuing thereafter)
Key measuresReichstag Fire Decree; Enabling Act; civil-service purge; abolition of state governments and trade unions; Night of the Long Knives

Gleichschaltung (German: [ˈɡlaɪçʃaltʊŋ], "coordination" or "synchronisation") is the term for the process by which Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party successively established a system of totalitarian control and coordination over all aspects of German society and societies occupied by Nazi Germany — from the economy and trade associations to the media, culture and education — following their seizure of power in 1933. The word, borrowed from electrical engineering, where it means switching circuits onto the same current, was applied to bringing every organisation "into line" with Nazi ideology and leadership.

The process in 1933–34

Within weeks of Hitler becoming Chancellor on 30 January 1933, the Reichstag fire of 27 February provided the pretext for the Reichstag Fire Decree, suspending civil liberties and enabling mass arrests of Communists and other opponents. The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 allowed the cabinet to legislate without the Reichstag. There followed in rapid succession: the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service (7 April 1933), purging Jews and political opponents; the synchronisation laws subordinating the federal states (Länder) to Reich governors; the smashing of the trade unions on 2 May 1933 and their replacement by the German Labour Front; the banning or self-dissolution of all other political parties (the NSDAP was declared the only legal party on 14 July 1933); and the coordination of the press, radio, film, universities, churches and professional bodies under party control. Boy and girl scouts were absorbed into the Hitler Youth; even leisure was organised through Strength Through Joy.

Completion

The purge of the SA leadership in the Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934) removed the last rival centre of power within the movement, and on President Hindenburg's death on 2 August 1934 Hitler merged the offices of Chancellor and President as Führer und Reichskanzler, with the armed forces swearing a personal oath of loyalty to him. Within nineteen months the pluralist Weimar Republic had been converted into a one-party dictatorship — achieved, historians stress, through a mixture of coercion, legal forms, opportunism and enthusiastic self-coordination (Selbstgleichschaltung) by institutions eager to curry favour.

Usage

The term has entered general usage for any forced ideological conformity imposed on institutions, and remains central to debates about how democracies collapse — through legal increments as much as through violence.
