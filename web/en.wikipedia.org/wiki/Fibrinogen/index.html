Fibrinogen (coagulation factor I) is a soluble plasma glycoprotein, synthesised by the liver, that plays an essential role in blood clotting. During tissue and vascular injury it is converted enzymatically by thrombin to fibrin and then to a fibrin-based blood clot that helps stop bleeding. Fibrinogen also supports platelet aggregation via the glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor.

Structure

Fibrinogen is a 340 kDa hexameric glycoprotein composed of two sets of three different polypeptide chains: Aα, Bβ and γ, joined by disulfide bridges. The chains are encoded by three separate genes — FGA, FGB and FGG — clustered on human chromosome 4. The molecule is about 45 nm long, with a central E domain and two outer D domains connected by coiled-coil regions.

Function

In the final stage of the coagulation cascade, thrombin cleaves fibrinopeptides A and B from the Aα and Bβ chains of fibrinogen, producing fibrin monomers. These polymerise spontaneously into an insoluble fibrin mesh, which is then cross-linked and stabilised by factor XIIIa. Fibrinogen deficiency or dysfunction leads to bleeding disorders such as afibrinogenemia and dysfibrinogenemia, while elevated fibrinogen is an acute-phase reactant associated with inflammation and cardiovascular risk.

Clinical significance

The plasma fibrinogen level is measured in the laboratory, commonly by the Clauss method. Low levels occur in disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), severe liver disease and massive transfusion. Fibrinogen concentrate and cryoprecipitate are used therapeutically to treat bleeding in acquired or congenital deficiency.

Discovery

Fibrinogen was first described in the 19th century; the protein was isolated from horse plasma and its conversion to fibrin characterised by early biochemists. The term was introduced by Rudolf Virchow, and Alexander Schmidt clarified its enzymatic conversion by thrombin.
