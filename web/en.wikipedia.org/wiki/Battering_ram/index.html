Battering ram

A battering ram is a siege engine that originated in ancient times and was designed to break open the masonry walls of fortifications or splinter their wooden gates. In its simplest form, a battering ram is a large heavy log carried by several people and propelled with force against a target; sophisticated designs suspended the ram in a wheeled, covered frame to protect the operators from defenders' missiles.

History

Battering rams were used in the ancient Near East by the Assyrians, whose reliefs depict wheeled rams with iron-shod heads attacking city walls, and they remained standard siege equipment through Greek, Roman, and medieval warfare. Roman rams (aries) were housed in mobile sheds called tortoises. The ram's head was often capped with metal, sometimes cast in the shape of a ram's head, from which the name derives.

Countermeasures and modern use

Defenders countered rams with padded cushions lowered against the wall, hooks to seize the ram, and sally ports for attacks on its crew; moats, sloped glacis, and reinforced gates also reduced its effect. With the advent of gunpowder artillery, wall-breaching rams became obsolete, but the term survives in the small hand-held rams used by police and firefighters to force entry through doors.
