Digital twin

A digital twin is a digital representation of an intended or actual real-world physical product, system, or process (a physical twin) that serves as the effectively indistinguishable digital counterpart of it for practical purposes, such as simulation, integration, testing, monitoring, and maintenance. The concept is a central element of the fourth industrial revolution (Industry 4.0) and of the Internet of things, which supplies the sensor data that keeps the twin synchronised with its physical counterpart.

History

The idea has roots in NASA's practice of maintaining mirrored systems for spacecraft: during the Apollo 13 crisis of 1970, engineers used ground-based simulators and duplicates of onboard systems to devise the crew's rescue. The term "digital twin" was later popularised by Michael Grieves in connection with product lifecycle management in the early 2000s, and by NASA engineer John Vickers around 2010. Advances in sensors, connectivity, and cloud computing made continuous, data-driven twins practical across manufacturing, energy, healthcare, and urban planning.

Types and characteristics

Digital twins range from twins of individual components (such as a jet engine or a wind turbine) to twins of entire systems, processes, or cities. Descriptive twins present live operational data; predictive twins use models and machine learning to forecast failures and performance, enabling predictive maintenance; prescriptive twins go further, recommending or initiating corrective action. A widely used conceptual model comprises the physical entity, the virtual entity, and the data and information connections between them.

Applications

In manufacturing, twins optimise production lines and predict machine failures before breakdown. In healthcare, patient-specific models are explored for personalised treatment planning. City-scale twins, such as Virtual Singapore, support planning, traffic management, and emergency simulation. In construction, twins extend building information modelling into operation and maintenance. Analysts have forecast a market worth tens of billions of dollars, though definitions vary enough that estimates differ widely.
