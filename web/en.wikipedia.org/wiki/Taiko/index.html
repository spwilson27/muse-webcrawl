Taiko

Taiko (太鼓) are a broad range of Japanese percussion instruments. In Japanese, the term taiko refers to any kind of drum, but outside Japan it is used specifically to refer to the various Japanese drums called wadaiko (和太鼓, "Japanese drums") and to the form of ensemble drumming more specifically called kumi-daiko.

History

Drums have been used in Japan since ancient times in warfare, theatre, Shinto and Buddhist ritual, and festival music (matsuri-bayashi). Archaeological evidence of drums dates to the Kofun period, and large temple and shrine drums have been preserved for over a thousand years. The modern ensemble style, kumi-daiko, was created in 1951 by Daihachi Oguchi, a jazz drummer who arranged traditional rhythms and festival pieces for groups of drums of different sizes, and founded one of Japan's first taiko ensembles. Groups such as Ondekoza (founded 1969) and Kodō popularised the art internationally, turning drumming into a physically demanding theatrical performance.

Instruments

Common taiko include the large ōdaiko, the deep-toned nagadō-daiko (long-body drum) used in festivals, the smaller high-pitched shime-daiko tightened with ropes or bolts, the barrel-shaped okedō, and accompanying instruments such as the atarigane hand gong, bamboo flutes (shinobue) and the shamisen. Drums are played with thick wooden sticks called bachi, often with choreographed movement drawn from martial arts stances.

Taiko outside Japan

Taiko was introduced to North America by Japanese-American communities in the late 1960s; Seiichi Tanaka founded the San Francisco Taiko Dojo in 1968. Thousands of community groups now exist worldwide, and taiko has become a prominent expression of Japanese and Asian-American cultural identity as well as a popular performance art in its own right.
