Wayland the Smith

TypeLegendary master blacksmith
TraditionsNorse, Germanic, English folklore
Key textsVölundarkviða, Þiðrekssaga, Deor

Wayland the Smith (Old English: Wēland; Old Norse: Völundr) is a master blacksmith originating in Germanic heroic legend. In Old Norse sources he is the son of a king of the Sami, and in other traditions he is a supernatural elf or the son of a giant.

The legend

The Old Norse poem Völundarkviða tells how King Níðuðr captured Wayland, hamstrung him, and imprisoned him on an island to forge treasures. Wayland took revenge by killing the king's sons, fashioning goblets from their skulls and jewels from their eyes, then escaped by flying with wings or a feather suit he had made. Versions of the tale appear in the Þiðrekssaga and in Old English poems such as Deor and Waldere; Wayland is also depicted on the Franks Casket.

Places and legacy

In England, the Neolithic long barrow in Oxfordshire known as Wayland's Smithy is associated with the legend: folklore held that a traveller who left a horse and a coin there overnight would find the horse shod by morning. Wayland remains a stock figure of the superhuman smith in later literature and fantasy.
