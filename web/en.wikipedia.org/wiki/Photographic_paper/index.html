Photographic paper

Photographic paper is a paper coated with a light-sensitive chemical formula, like photographic film, used for making photographic prints. When photographic paper is exposed to light it captures a latent image that is then chemically developed to form a visible image; with most papers the image density from exposure can be sufficient to not require further development, but typically, exposure adds more to the final image density, even though in some cases it is not visible until the paper is chemically developed.

Rolls and sheets of photographic paper ready for darkroom printing.

Structure

The light-sensitive layer of photographic paper is normally an emulsion of silver halide crystals suspended in gelatin. The emulsion is coated onto a base, which for most papers is either a fibre base of baryta-coated paper or a resin-coated (RC) base in which the paper core is sealed between two polyethylene layers. The image-forming layer sits on top of a baryta (barium sulfate) layer that smooths the surface and provides a bright white background.

Types

Photographic papers are divided into several categories:

  • Graded papers, manufactured in a range of contrast grades, typically numbered 0 (soft) through 5 (hard).
  • Variable-contrast (multigrade) papers, whose contrast can be controlled during printing by filtering the enlarger light with magenta and yellow filters.
  • Fibre-based papers, prized for fine-art printing and archival stability.
  • Resin-coated papers, which process and dry quickly and are widely used for commercial and amateur printing.
  • Colour papers, such as the RA-4 chromogenic papers, carrying three emulsion layers sensitive to different parts of the spectrum.

History

The effect of light in darkening a prepared paper was discovered by Thomas Wedgwood around 1800. Early photographic prints were made on papers sensitized with silver salts, and the albumen print and gelatin silver print processes dominated the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Although digital printing has largely displaced darkroom paper for everyday use, photographic paper remains in production for fine-art, archival and alternative-process work.
