Martensite

Martensite is a very hard form of steel crystalline structure. It is named after Adolf Martens, a German metallographer who first described the microstructure in the 1890s. Martensite is formed in carbon steels by the rapid cooling (quenching) of the austenite phase of iron at such a high rate that carbon atoms do not have time to diffuse out of the crystal structure.

Formation and structure

When austenite is quenched, its face-centred cubic lattice transforms diffusionlessly — by a coordinated shear of atoms — into a highly strained body-centred tetragonal structure supersaturated with carbon. The transformation begins at the martensite start temperature (Ms) and proceeds as cooling continues. The resulting crystals are typically lath- or plate-shaped, appearing as needles under the microscope.

Properties

Martensite is extremely hard and strong but brittle; the higher the carbon content, the harder the martensite. Freshly quenched steel is usually tempered — reheated to a moderate temperature — to trade some hardness for toughness, as in blades, tools, springs, and gears. Martensitic stainless steels, containing chromium, are used for cutlery and surgical instruments.

The martensitic transformation also occurs in shape-memory alloys such as nitinol, where it is thermoelastic and reversible.
