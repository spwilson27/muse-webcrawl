The Salvation Army

Founded: 2 July 1865, London, England
Founders: William Booth, Catherine Booth
Type: Protestant church and charitable organization

The Salvation Army is an international Protestant Christian church and charitable organization founded in 1865 in London's East End by William Booth and his wife Catherine Booth. Organized along quasi-military lines — with officers, soldiers, uniforms, ranks, and brass bands — it combines evangelical preaching with extensive social welfare work.

History

Booth began as a Methodist preacher ministering to the poor of Whitechapel under the name the East London Christian Mission; in 1878 it adopted the name The Salvation Army and a military structure. Its "soup, soap, and salvation" approach and William Booth's 1890 social plan, set out in In Darkest England and the Way Out, established shelters, food depots, and labour programs.

Work today

The Salvation Army now operates in more than 130 countries, running homeless shelters, disaster relief, addiction rehabilitation, children's services, and charity shops. It is famous for Christmas red kettle collections and for disaster response. Its members in the United States number among the largest charitable organizations by donations received.

Beliefs and structure

The Army's theology is Wesleyan-Arminian; notably, it does not practise baptism or the Eucharist as sacraments. It is led by an elected General from its international headquarters in London; Evangeline Booth (1934) and Eva Burrows (1986) were among the women to hold the office.
