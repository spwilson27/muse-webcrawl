Theophilus of Adana (also Theophilus the Penitent) was a cleric in 6th-century Cilicia whose legend, recorded by Eutychianus and widely retold in the Middle Ages, is the earliest surviving story of a pact with the Devil — and a direct forerunner of the Faust legend. He is venerated as a saint, his story being one of the most popular Marian miracles of medieval devotion.

Legend

Theophilus was archdeacon of Adana, famed for his piety; after the bishop's death he humbly refused the bishopric, but the new bishop, jealous of his influence, deposed him. Desperate to regain his position, Theophilus consulted a sorcerer, who summoned the Devil. The Devil demanded a written contract, signed in Theophilus's own blood, renouncing Christ and the Virgin Mary; Theophilus signed, and his office was restored. Soon consumed by remorse, he fasted and prayed for forty days in a church, begging the Virgin's intercession. Mary appeared, rebuked and forgave him, and days later miraculously returned the contract from Hell, laying it on his chest as he slept. Theophilus publicly confessed, burned the pact, received communion, and died three days later in a state of grace.

Influence

The tale was versified in Latin by Hrotsvitha of Gandersheim, dramatized by Rutebeuf in Le Miracle de Théophile (13th century), and depicted on cathedral windows and sculpture across Europe. It established the template — pact, remorse, intercession — that the Faust tradition later inverted by removing the repentance.
