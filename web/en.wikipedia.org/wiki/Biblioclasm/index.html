Biblioclasm

Biblioclasm is the deliberate destruction of books or other written material. It is often motivated by politics, religion, or ideology, and is closely related to — but distinct from — book burning, in which fire is the method of destruction; biblioclasm can also include pulping, burying, or otherwise annihilating texts, as well as the destruction of libraries as institutions.

Historical examples

Famous cases include the destruction of the works of rival schools following the unification of China under Qin Shi Huang in 213 BCE; the repeated losses of the Library of Alexandria; the burning of Mayan codices by Spanish clergy in the 16th century; and the systematic Nazi book burnings of 1933 targeting works by Jewish, pacifist, and other authors deemed "un-German". In the wars of the 1990s, the shelling of the National and University Library of Bosnia and Herzegovina in Sarajevo in 1992 destroyed some 1.5 million volumes, an act often described as cultural genocide. The burning of the Jaffna Public Library in Sri Lanka in 1981 is another widely cited modern instance.

Motivations and analysis

Scholars such as Rebecca Knuth have analyzed biblioclasm as an instrument for erasing the cultural memory and identity of targeted groups, distinguishing it from incidental wartime losses. Biblioclasm is treated in international law contexts alongside other forms of cultural property destruction, protected in principle by the 1954 Hague Convention.
