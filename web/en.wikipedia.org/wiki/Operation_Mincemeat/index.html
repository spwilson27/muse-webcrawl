Operation Mincemeat was a successful British deception operation of the Second World War designed to disguise the Allied invasion of Sicily in 1943. It formed part of Operation Barclay, a wider deception plan. The operation is famous for its central device: a corpse, dressed as a Royal Marines officer and carrying faked secret documents, was released from a submarine off the Spanish coast so that German intelligence would believe the Allies intended to invade Greece and Sardinia rather than Sicily.

Background and planning

The idea was developed by Flight Lieutenant Charles Cholmondeley of MI5 and Lieutenant Commander Ewen Montagu of the Naval Intelligence Division, drawing on a 1939 memo by Rear Admiral John Godfrey (and his assistant Ian Fleming) listing deception schemes — the so-called "Trout memo". The planners obtained the body of Glyndwr Michael, a Welsh labourer who had died after ingesting rat poison, and created for him the identity of "Major William Martin" of the Royal Marines, complete with a fiancée, personal letters, theatre ticket stubs and other "pocket litter". A briefcase chained to the body contained letters between senior generals implying that Greece and Sardinia were the invasion targets and that Sicily was merely a feint.

Execution

On 30 April 1943 the submarine HMS Seraph released the body off Huelva, Spain, where the local tidal conditions would carry it ashore. Spanish authorities recovered it; as the planners had hoped, documents were copied by German agents operating in Spain and passed to Berlin before the originals were returned to Britain. Forensic examination of the returned letters confirmed they had been opened and read.

Outcome and legacy

German forces were redeployed to Greece, Sardinia and Corsica, and only two German divisions were in Sicily when the real invasion, Operation Husky, began on 9 July 1943. Montagu's 1953 book The Man Who Never Was popularised the story, and it has since been the subject of films (1956 and 2021), a stage musical, and Ben Macintyre's 2010 history, which identified the corpse as Glyndwr Michael; a memorial plaque naming Michael was added to the grave in Huelva.
