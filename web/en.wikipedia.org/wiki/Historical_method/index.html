Historical method

The historical method is the collection of techniques and guidelines historians use to research and write history from primary and secondary sources, evaluating evidence for authenticity, credibility, and context. It underpins historiography and the critical study of the past.

Historical method
Key steps: Source criticism, corroboration, contextualisation, synthesis
Foundational texts: Langlois & Seignobos (1898), Bernheim (1889)

Source criticism

External criticism asks whether a document is authentic — its provenance, authorship, date, and material form — using palaeography, diplomatics, archaeology and forgery detection (as Lorenzo Valla exposed the Donation of Constantine). Internal criticism asks whether its contents are credible: the author's competence, bias, distance from events, and intended audience.

Corroboration and synthesis

Claims gain strength when independent sources agree; singular testimony is treated cautiously. Historians place evidence in political, social, economic and cultural context, apply auxiliary sciences (chronology, numismatics, genealogy, statistics), and construct arguments acknowledging gaps and competing interpretations.

Development

Critical method developed from Renaissance humanism and 17th–18th century antiquarians and Benedictine scholars (Mabillon), and was professionalised in the 19th century by Leopold von Ranke's seminar methods and archival emphasis on primary sources. 20th-century schools — Annales, Marxist, social and oral history — added quantitative methods, oral testimony protocols, and reflexivity about narrative and power.

Criticisms

Postmodern critics such as Keith Jenkins argued all history is narratively constructed; most practising historians respond that method constrains interpretation even if complete objectivity is unattainable.
