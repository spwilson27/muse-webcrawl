CTD (instrument)

CTD
MeasuresConductivity, temperature, pressure (depth)
Derived quantitySalinity, density, sound speed
Used byOceanographers
PlatformsShips, Argo floats, gliders, moorings

A CTD (conductivity, temperature, depth) is an oceanography instrument used to measure the electrical conductivity, temperature, and pressure of seawater as a function of depth. From these three variables, salinity, density, and other properties of the water column are computed. The CTD is a standard package of instruments deployed on research vessels worldwide and is the modern successor to the bathythermograph and the Nansen bottle cast.

A CTD rosette, fitted with water-sampling bottles, is lowered into the ocean.

Operation

A shipboard CTD is typically mounted in a metal frame — a rosette — surrounded by water-sampling bottles that can be closed at chosen depths. The package is lowered on a conducting cable, transmitting data in real time, or records internally. Conductivity is measured with an electrode or inductive cell, temperature with a fast-response thermistor or platinum thermometer, and pressure with a precision strain-gauge sensor.

Applications

CTD profiles reveal the structure of the ocean — thermoclines, water masses, and fronts — and underpin studies of ocean circulation and climate. Miniaturized CTDs are carried by Argo profiling floats, underwater gliders, moored instruments, and even marine mammals fitted with sensor tags. Accuracy requirements are stringent: climate-quality measurements demand temperature accuracy of about 0.001 °C.
