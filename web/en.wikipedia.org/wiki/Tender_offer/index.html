Tender offer

A tender offer is a public offer made by a prospective acquirer directly to the shareholders of a publicly traded corporation, proposing to purchase their shares at a specified price — usually at a premium to the market price — during a specified period. The offer is typically contingent on shareholders tendering a minimum number of shares, often enough to give the bidder control. Tender offers are a principal mechanism for corporate takeovers, including hostile takeovers in which the bidder bypasses the target's board of directors and appeals straight to shareholders.

Regulation in the United States

In the United States, tender offers are regulated under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, as amended by the Williams Act of 1968. Key requirements include:

  • The offer must generally remain open for at least 20 business days.
  • The bidder must file a Schedule TO with the Securities and Exchange Commission disclosing its identity, financing, and plans for the target; the target board must state its position on Schedule 14D-9.
  • Under the "all holders" and "best price" rules, the offer must be open to all shareholders of the class, and the price paid to any shareholder must be the highest price paid to any other.
  • Shareholders may withdraw tendered shares while the offer remains open, and shares are accepted on a pro-rata basis if the offer is oversubscribed.

Types and defences

A two-tier tender offer offers a higher price for the first portion of shares (enough for control) and a lower price in a second-step merger for the remainder; such coercive structures were common in the 1980s and are now constrained by regulation and charter provisions. A bidder may also make a partial offer for less than all shares, or a self-tender (issuer tender offer) in which a company buys back its own shares.

Targets defend against unwanted tender offers with measures such as the poison pill (shareholder rights plan), staggered boards, golden parachutes, and the solicitation of a friendly "white knight" bidder. A board that decides a company is for sale may come under the so-called Revlon duties in Delaware law to obtain the best value reasonably available for shareholders.
