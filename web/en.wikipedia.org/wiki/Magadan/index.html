Magadan

Magadan (Russian: Магадан) is a port city and the administrative centre of Magadan Oblast in the Russian Far East. It lies on the Sea of Okhotsk in Nagayev Bay, on the isthmus of the Staritsky Peninsula. Founded in 1929 as a settlement serving the Soviet gold-mining trust Dalstroy, Magadan became notorious as the gateway to the Kolyma Gulag camps, to which prisoners were shipped and from which they were sent to mines across the region. Its population is roughly 90,000.

History

Magadan grew out of a cultural base established in Nagayev Bay in 1929 to support geological exploration of the Kolyma goldfields. From 1932 the town was administered by Dalstroy, the Far North construction trust, which ran a vast network of forced-labour camps. Prisoners arrived by sea from Vladivostok and built the city, its port and the Kolyma Highway — the "Road of Bones" — to the gold fields. After Stalin's death the camp system was dismantled, and Magadan developed as a conventional administrative and supply centre for the mining region.

Geography and economy

Magadan has a subarctic climate with long, very cold winters moderated somewhat by the sea. It is one of the most isolated regional capitals in Russia: there is no railway, and the R504 Kolyma Highway links it to Yakutsk over some 2,000 km of largely gravel road. The port is the region's main supply gateway, and the economy rests on fishing, mining services and administration. The city's best-known landmark is the Mask of Sorrow, a monumental concrete sculpture by Ernst Neizvestny, unveiled in 1996 on a hill above the city to commemorate the victims of the Gulag.
