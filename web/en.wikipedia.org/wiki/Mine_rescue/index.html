Mine rescue

Mine rescue is the specialised practice of rescuing miners trapped or endangered underground after fires, explosions, collapses, flooding or toxic-gas incidents. Trained rescue teams enter hazardous atmospheres using long-duration breathing apparatus to locate survivors, provide first aid, fight fires, ventilate workings and recover the injured and dead.

History

Organised mines rescue developed after disasters of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries; central rescue stations were established in Britain after the Coal Mines Act 1911, and in the United States the Bureau of Mines promoted rescue training after 1910. Self-contained rebreather apparatus, gas detection and refuge chambers became standard.

Notable rescues

Famous operations include the 2010 Copiapó mining accident in Chile, where 33 men were rescued after 69 days underground, and the Quecreek rescue in Pennsylvania in 2002. Modern practice emphasises prevention, emergency communication and rapid-response teams stationed at mines.
