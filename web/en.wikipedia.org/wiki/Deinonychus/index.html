Deinonychus

KingdomAnimalia
CladeDinosauria; Theropoda
FamilyDromaeosauridae
GenusDeinonychus Ostrom, 1969
SpeciesD. antirrhopus
LivedEarly Cretaceous, ~115–108 million years ago

Deinonychus (meaning "terrible claw") is a genus of dromaeosaurid theropod dinosaur with one described species, Deinonychus antirrhopus. This species, which could grow up to 3.4 metres (11 ft) long, lived during the early Cretaceous Period, about 115–108 million years ago (from the mid-Aptian to early Albian stages). Fossils have been recovered from the US states of Montana, Utah, Wyoming, and Oklahoma, in rocks of the Cloverly Formation and Antlers Formation, though teeth attributed to Deinonychus have also been found in Maryland.

Discovery and significance

Paleontologist John Ostrom described and named Deinonychus in 1969, based on fossils excavated in Montana, including material found near remains of the ornithopod Tenontosaurus. His description — of an agile, active, evidently warm-blooded predator — transformed scientific understanding of dinosaurs and ignited the "dinosaur renaissance", reversing the earlier image of dinosaurs as sluggish and cold-blooded. Ostrom also noted its close similarity to birds, reviving the theory that birds evolved from theropod dinosaurs.

Description

Deinonychus was a bipedal predator with a long, stiffened tail used as a counterbalance. Its most famous feature is the greatly enlarged, sickle-shaped claw — up to about 13 cm (5 in) long — on the second toe of each hind foot, held off the ground while walking. The arms bore large three-fingered hands with sharp claws, and the jaws were lined with serrated, blade-like teeth. Like other dromaeosaurids, it is now thought to have been covered in feathers, though no direct feather impressions are known for this genus; relatives such as Velociraptor bear quill knobs indicating large wing feathers. The enlarged claw was probably used to restrain or disembowel prey; biomechanical studies suggest it functioned more like a crampon for pinning prey (the "raptor prey restraint" model) than as a slashing weapon.

Paleobiology

Finds of several Deinonychus individuals around Tenontosaurus carcasses have been interpreted as evidence of pack hunting, though some researchers argue the pattern instead reflects mobbing or feeding aggregations, like those of modern Komodo dragons. Its powerful bite, estimated from tooth marks and jaw mechanics, and its relatively large brain for a dinosaur of its size have kept it central to debates about dinosaur behaviour and intelligence.

In popular culture

The "raptors" of the film Jurassic Park were modelled on Deinonychus — enlarged to the size of the later-discovered Utahraptor — although the films call them Velociraptor, following a then-current proposal (used by Gregory S. Paul) that classified Deinonychus within that genus.
