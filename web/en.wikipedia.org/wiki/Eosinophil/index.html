Eosinophil

Eosinophils are a variety of white blood cells (granulocytes) and one of the immune system components responsible for combating multicellular parasites and certain infections. Along with mast cells and basophils, they also control mechanisms associated with allergy and asthma.

Characteristics

Eosinophils are roughly 12–17 micrometres in diameter, with a bilobed nucleus and large cytoplasmic granules that stain bright red-orange with the acidic dye eosin — hence their name, given by Paul Ehrlich. Their granules contain major basic protein, eosinophil peroxidase and other cytotoxic substances released by degranulation.

Function and clinical significance

They develop in the bone marrow and circulate in blood before migrating into tissues, especially the gut, lungs and skin. Elevated counts (eosinophilia) occur in parasitic infestations, allergic disease, asthma, drug reactions and some malignancies; abnormally low counts may follow acute infection or corticosteroid treatment.
