Commitment device

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A commitment device is, in economics and game theory, a technique by which a person or organisation deliberately restricts their own future options in order to make a promise or threat credible, or to protect a long-term goal against short-term temptation. The concept is associated with the work of economist Thomas Schelling and with the study of time inconsistency in behavioural economics.

Rationale

People frequently exhibit present bias: they intend to save money, diet, or study, but when the moment of choice arrives they defect from their own plans. By binding their future selves — the classic example is Odysseus having himself tied to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without steering toward them — an agent changes the incentives that the future self will face.

Examples

  • Self-imposed deadlines and stakes: deposit contracts in which money is forfeited if a goal (such as quitting smoking) is not met.
  • Illiquid savings: commitment savings accounts, offered by banks in developing countries, that lock funds until a target date or amount is reached.
  • Strategic commitments: in business and war, burning bridges or publicly staking reputation to make a course of action credible — for example, Hernán Cortés scuttling his ships after landing in Mexico, as commonly retold.
  • Digital tools: website blockers and apps that restrict access to distractions for a set period.

In game theory

In strategic settings, a commitment device changes the order or payoffs of a game: a player who can irrevocably commit to a response can deter rivals, as in the logic of nuclear deterrence and mutually assured destruction. Credibility requires that the commitment be observable and genuinely costly or impossible to reverse.
