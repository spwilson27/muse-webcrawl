Animal painting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Animal painting is a genre of painting in which animals are the principal subject matter. It encompasses portraits of individual animals — especially horses and dogs — hunting and sporting scenes, farmyard subjects, and depictions of wild animals. The genre reached its height of popularity in Europe between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries.

Development

Animals appear in the earliest surviving art, from cave paintings of bison and horses to ancient Egyptian and Assyrian hunting reliefs. In early modern Europe, detailed studies by artists such as Albrecht Dürer and Leonardo da Vinci set new standards of observation. Flemish painters of the seventeenth century, notably Frans Snyders and Jan Fyt, produced dramatic hunts and still lifes of game, while Dutch masters painted serene cattle pieces.

Sporting and portrait traditions

In Britain, the growth of horse racing, fox hunting, and estate culture drove demand for sporting art. Painters depicted prized racehorses, hounds, and game birds with near-portrait accuracy, and individual animal portraiture — recording famous racehorses or beloved dogs — became a substantial trade. Edwin Landseer's narrative and anthropomorphic scenes achieved mass popularity in Victorian Britain through engravings.

Wildlife art

From the late nineteenth century, artists increasingly painted wild animals in their habitats, reflecting new interest in natural history and conservation. In the twentieth century, wildlife art developed as a distinct field, particularly in North America and Africa, with artists such as Bruno Liljefors in Sweden and Wilhelm Kuhnert in Germany setting early standards for the depiction of animals in the wild.
