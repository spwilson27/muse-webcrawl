Bioacoustics

Bioacoustics
DisciplineInterdisciplinary: biology, acoustics, ethology
SubjectsAnimal sound production, hearing, acoustic communication

Bioacoustics is a cross-disciplinary science that combines biology and acoustics. Usually it refers to the investigation of sound production, dispersion and reception in animals (including humans). This involves the neurophysiological and anatomical basis of sound production and detection, and the relation of acoustic signals to the medium through which they disperse. The findings provide clues about the evolution of acoustic mechanisms, and from that, the evolution of animals that employ them.

History

For most of human history, understanding animal sounds relied on direct observation. The invention of the phonograph in the late nineteenth century, and later of magnetic tape recording and the sound spectrograph, transformed the field by allowing sounds to be stored, visualised and analysed. The Slovenian biologist Ivan Regen is often called the father of bioacoustics for his early twentieth-century experiments on sound production and hearing in crickets and other insects. Donald Griffin's discovery of echolocation in bats in the 1940s opened a major new research area.

Methods

Bioacousticians record animal sounds in the field and laboratory using microphones and hydrophones, and analyse them as spectrograms — visual representations of frequency over time. Playback experiments, in which recorded calls are broadcast to animals, reveal the function of signals. Modern passive acoustic monitoring deploys automated recorders across habitats to track species presence, abundance and behaviour over long periods, and machine-learning tools increasingly automate the classification of calls.

Applications

Bioacoustic methods are used in taxonomy (many cryptic species are distinguishable chiefly by their calls), conservation monitoring, pest control, and the study of animal communication and cognition. Underwater bioacoustics studies whale song, fish choruses and the effects of human-generated ocean noise on marine life.
