Vehicle emissions control

Vehicle emissions control is the study and practice of reducing the emissions produced by motor vehicles, especially internal combustion engines. The pollutants of concern include nitrogen oxides (NOx), carbon monoxide (CO), unburned hydrocarbons, particulate matter, and volatile organic compounds, which contribute to smog, acid rain and adverse health effects.

History

Concern over photochemical smog in Los Angeles in the 1940s and 1950s, traced by Arie Haagen-Smit to automobile exhaust, led to the first controls: positive crankcase ventilation in the 1960s, then the US Clean Air Act amendments of 1970, which forced steep reductions and effectively required the catalytic converter on US cars from 1975. Unleaded petrol was introduced in part because lead poisons catalyst materials.

Control technologies

Exhaust aftertreatment

The three-way catalytic converter simultaneously reduces NOx and oxidises CO and hydrocarbons in petrol engines, requiring precise air–fuel ratio control via oxygen sensors and electronic fuel injection. Diesel engines use oxidation catalysts, diesel particulate filters (DPF) that trap soot, and selective catalytic reduction (SCR), which injects urea solution (AdBlue) to convert NOx to nitrogen and water.

Engine measures

Exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) lowers combustion temperatures and thus NOx formation. Evaporative emission controls capture fuel vapours from the tank in a charcoal canister. Engine management, variable valve timing and, more recently, gasoline particulate filters and hybridisation further reduce emissions.

Regulation and testing

Emission standards such as the European Euro series and US EPA tiers set limits measured over standardised driving cycles; real-driving emissions (RDE) testing was added in Europe after discrepancies between laboratory and road results. The 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal, in which "defeat devices" detected laboratory testing, led to tighter enforcement worldwide. Periodic inspection and maintenance programmes check in-service vehicles, and on-board diagnostics (OBD) monitor the emission control system.
