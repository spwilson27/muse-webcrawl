Devil in Islam

In Islam, the devil is known as Iblis (Arabic: إِبْلِيس), also associated with the title al-Shayṭān ("Satan"). According to the Quran, Iblis was a jinn created from smokeless fire who was raised among the angels but was cast out for refusing God's command to prostrate before Adam, arguing that he, made of fire, was superior to Adam, made of clay.

Quranic account

The story is told in several surahs (including Al-Baqarah, Al-A'raf, Al-Hijr and Sad). God grants Iblis respite until the Day of Resurrection, during which Iblis vows to lead humans astray, though he has no authority over God's sincere servants. Unlike in some Christian traditions, Iblis is not a fallen angel — angels in Islam cannot disobey God — but a jinn with free will.

Shayatin

The plural shayatin (devils) are evil jinn or demons who whisper temptations (waswas) to humans and jinn, encourage idolatry, and obstruct worship. Muslims seek refuge in God from Satan ("A'udhu billahi min ash-shaytan ir-rajim") before reciting the Quran.

In Islamic tradition

During Ramadan the devils are said to be chained. In the Hajj, pilgrims stone pillars at Mina (the Stoning of the Devil), commemorating Abraham's rejection of Satan's temptation. Sufi literature sometimes portrays Iblis more complexly, as a tragic monotheist who refused to bow to any but God.
