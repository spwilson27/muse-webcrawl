Carmichael number

Composite numbers that pass Fermat's test for every coprime base

In number theory, a Carmichael number is a composite number n which satisfies the modular arithmetic congruence relation bn ≡ b (mod n) for all integers b. Equivalently, bn−1 ≡ 1 (mod n) for every integer b that is coprime to n. They are named after the American mathematician Robert Carmichael, who defined them in 1910.

Carmichael numbers are important because they are counterexamples to the naive converse of Fermat's little theorem: a number that passes the Fermat primality test for every coprime base need not be prime. They are sometimes called absolute pseudoprimes, since they are Fermat pseudoprimes to every base coprime to them.

Examples

The smallest Carmichael number is 561 = 3 × 11 × 17. It satisfies 561 − 1 = 560 being divisible by each of 3 − 1 = 2, 11 − 1 = 10, and 17 − 1 = 16, in accordance with Korselt's criterion. The first few Carmichael numbers are 561, 1105, 1729, 2465, 2821, 6601, 8911, 10585, 15841, 29341, 41041, 46657, 52633, 62745, 63973, and 75361. The number 1729 is also known as the Hardy–Ramanujan number.

Korselt's criterion

An equivalent characterization was given by Alwin Korselt in 1899, before Carmichael found the first examples: a composite number n is a Carmichael number if and only if n is square-free and, for every prime divisor p of n, p − 1 divides n − 1. In particular, every Carmichael number has at least three distinct prime factors and is odd.

Distribution

For decades it was unknown whether infinitely many Carmichael numbers exist. This was proved in 1994 by W. R. Alford, Andrew Granville, and Carl Pomerance. They showed that the number of Carmichael numbers up to x eventually exceeds x2/7. Nevertheless, Carmichael numbers are rare: there are only 20,138 of them below 1018, a tiny fraction of the composite numbers in that range.

Generalizations

The concept generalizes to other algebraic settings, giving Carmichael numbers in number fields and in abstract algebra, where ideals satisfying an analogue of Korselt's criterion play the corresponding role. Related notions include Knödel numbers and Lucas–Carmichael numbers.
