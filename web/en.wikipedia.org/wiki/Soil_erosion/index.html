Soil erosion

Soil erosion is the denudation and transport of the upper layer of soil by water, wind, ice, or gravity. It is a natural process, but human activities — deforestation, overgrazing, intensive tillage, and construction — can accelerate it far beyond the rate of soil formation, degrading agricultural land and polluting waterways with sediment.

Processes

Water erosion includes splash erosion from raindrop impact, sheet erosion, rill erosion in small channels, and gully erosion in large channels. Wind erosion dominates in arid and semi-arid regions, lifting fine particles in suspension — as in the Dust Bowl of the 1930s in North America. Mass movement and tillage erosion also redistribute soil on slopes.

Impacts

On-site, erosion strips nutrient-rich topsoil and reduces yields; globally, tens of billions of tonnes of soil are lost from cropland each year. Off-site effects include siltation of reservoirs and harbours, eutrophication from nutrient runoff, flooding aggravated by sediment-filled channels, and dust storms affecting health and transport.

Control

Conservation measures include contour ploughing, terracing, cover crops, no-till farming, shelterbelts and windbreaks, grassed waterways, and re-vegetation of degraded land. Soil loss is commonly modelled with the Universal Soil Loss Equation (USLE) and its revised version (RUSLE).
