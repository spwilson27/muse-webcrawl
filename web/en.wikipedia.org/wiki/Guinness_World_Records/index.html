Guinness World Records, known from its inception in 1955 until 1999 as The Guinness Book of Records and in previous United States editions as The Guinness Book of World Records, is a British reference book published annually, listing world records both of human achievements and the extremes of the natural world.

History

The idea for the book originated with Sir Hugh Beaver, managing director of the Guinness brewery, who in 1951 became involved in an argument during a shooting party in County Wexford, Ireland, over which was the fastest game bird in Europe, the golden plover or the red grouse — and realised that a book answering such pub debates could be popular. He commissioned the fact-finding agency run by twins Norris and Ross McWhirter, who compiled the first edition in 1955. It became a surprise bestseller, and annual editions followed.

The franchise has expanded beyond print into television series, museums, video games, and record-adjudication services. The company licenses official adjudicators to certify record attempts.

Record categories and policies

Guinness World Records maintains a database of more than 40,000 records, of which only a selection appear in the annual book. Categories range from the tallest human beings and heaviest animals to speed-eating and mass-participation events. For safety and ethical reasons the organisation no longer accepts certain categories, including records involving excessive alcohol consumption, dangerous eating feats, and the heaviest pets (to discourage deliberate overfeeding).

Notable record holders include Robert Wadlow, the tallest man in recorded history (2.72 m / 8 ft 11.1 in), and Ashrita Furman, who has set hundreds of individual records. The annual book has sold more than 150 million copies in over 100 countries and 40 languages.

Criticism

The organisation has been criticised for commercialising record certification, charging fees for expedited applications, and for awarding records of questionable significance. Supporters counter that the fees fund the adjudication service and that strict evidence rules are applied.
