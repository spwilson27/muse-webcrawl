Pella (Greek: Πέλλα) was the ancient capital of the Kingdom of Macedon, located in what is now Central Macedonia in northern Greece. It rose to prominence at the end of the 5th century BC, when King Archelaus moved the Macedonian court there from Aegae, and it was the birthplace of both Philip II and Alexander the Great.

History

Although inhabited earlier, Pella was developed into the royal capital around 400 BC. Under Philip II (r. 359–336 BC) it became one of the largest and wealthiest cities of the Greek world, laid out on a regular Hippodamian grid of broad streets with water supply and drainage systems. The philosopher Aristotle was summoned to Pella to tutor the young Alexander.

After the Roman defeat of Macedon at the Battle of Pydna in 168 BC, Pella declined. The geographer Strabo described it as already small, and the city was largely destroyed by an earthquake in the 1st century BC, with settlement shifting to nearby Thessalonica. The Roman colony later founded nearby should not be confused with the Macedonian capital itself.

Archaeology

Excavations, carried out systematically since the 1950s, have uncovered the agora — one of the largest of the ancient Greek world — wealthy houses, and public buildings. Pella is especially famous for its pebble mosaics, dating to the late 4th century BC, which depict scenes such as Dionysus riding a panther, a lion hunt, and the abduction of Helen; the finest examples are displayed in the Archaeological Museum of Pella, opened in 2009. A palace complex on the northern hill, spanning some 60,000 square metres, is identified as the royal residence.
