Bushland

Bushland is a term used in Australia for land that retains, or has reverted to, a predominantly natural state, typically covered in native vegetation such as eucalypt woodland, heath, or scrub. Related to the broader Australian concept of "the bush", bushland ranges from small remnant patches within cities to vast tracts of near-wilderness. In other English-speaking countries, similar terms include bush (in New Zealand and parts of Africa) and backcountry.

Characteristics

Bushland is defined less by a single vegetation type than by its natural condition: native canopy trees, a shrub and ground layer, leaf litter, and fallen timber providing habitat. Because so much Australian flora is adapted to nutrient-poor soils and periodic fire, bushland communities include sclerophyll forests, banksia woodlands, heathlands, and mallee.

Urban bushland

Australian cities retain significant areas of remnant bushland in reserves, national parks adjoining suburbs (such as Ku-ring-gai Chase near Sydney and the Dandenong Ranges near Melbourne), and along waterways. Urban bushland remnants are valued for recreation, biodiversity, and water catchment but are threatened by clearing for development, weed invasion, altered fire regimes, stormwater runoff, and predation by domestic animals. Community "bushcare" volunteers play a major role in restoring and maintaining these areas.

Conservation

Bushland is protected in a system of national parks and nature reserves, and in some states by native vegetation legislation that restricts clearing. Offset schemes sometimes require equivalent bushland to be conserved when clearing is approved elsewhere. Bushfires are both an ecological necessity for many bushland species and a major hazard where bushland meets housing.
