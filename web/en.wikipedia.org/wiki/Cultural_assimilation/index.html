Cultural assimilation

Cultural assimilation
FieldSociology, anthropology
Related conceptsAcculturation, integration, multiculturalism, melting pot
Key theoristsRobert Park, Milton Gordon

Cultural assimilation is the process in which a minority group or culture comes to resemble a society's majority group, or assumes the values, behaviours and beliefs of another group, whether wholly or partially. A related concept is acculturation, which occurs through sustained first-hand contact between cultures.

Forms and theory

Sociologists distinguish cultural (acculturative), structural (entry into institutions and social networks), marital and identificational assimilation. Milton Gordon's Assimilation in American Life (1964) set out these stages for immigrant groups in the United States; the Chicago School's Robert Park framed assimilation as a race-relations cycle. Later scholars proposed "segmented assimilation", in which immigrants may assimilate into different strata of the host society, and transnational models in which ties to origin countries persist.

Voluntary and forced assimilation

Assimilation may be voluntary, as immigrants adopt a new language and customs for opportunity, or coerced by state policy: examples include the removal of Indigenous children to boarding schools in North America and Australia, Russification in the Russian Empire, and bans on minority languages. Critics contrast assimilation with multiculturalism and pluralism, arguing full assimilation erases heritage cultures, while defenders cite social cohesion and equal participation.

Measurement

Researchers measure assimilation through language shift across generations, intermarriage rates, residential integration and socioeconomic convergence. Language loss by the third generation is a classic finding in US immigrant studies. See also melting pot.
