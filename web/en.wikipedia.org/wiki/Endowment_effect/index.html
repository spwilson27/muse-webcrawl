Endowment effect

The endowment effect is the finding that people are more likely to retain an object they own than acquire that same object when they do not own it — in other words, people place a higher value on objects they own than on identical objects they do not. It is a central concept in behavioral economics and is closely linked to loss aversion and status quo bias.

FieldBehavioral economics, psychology

RelatedLoss aversion, WTA/WTP gap

Classic experiments

In a famous series of experiments by Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler (1990), participants randomly given a coffee mug demanded roughly twice as much to sell it as other participants offered to buy it, and trade volume was far below standard economic predictions. The gap between willingness to accept (WTA) and willingness to pay (WTP) for the same good is the standard measure of the effect.

Explanations

The leading explanation is loss aversion from prospect theory: giving up an owned item is coded as a loss, and losses loom larger than equivalent gains. Other accounts invoke ownership itself changing preferences, mere exposure, or reluctance to trade due to bargaining norms. Critics note the effect weakens with market experience and for goods held purely for exchange, such as tokens or inventory.

Implications

The endowment effect helps explain low trading volumes, the equity premium puzzle in part, resistance to policy changes that create losers, and marketing practices such as free trials and test drives, which create a sense of ownership before purchase.
