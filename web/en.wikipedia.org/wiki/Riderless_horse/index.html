Riderless horse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A riderless horse is a single horse without a rider, sometimes carrying a pair of riding boots reversed in the stirrups, that follows the caisson or hearse in a military or state funeral procession. It symbolises a fallen leader who will ride no more, and in the case of the reversed boots, a commander looking back toward his troops for the last time.

Origins and symbolism

The custom is often traced to the armies of Genghis Khan, in which a dead warrior's horse was led in the funeral procession, and to older steppe practices of honouring a fallen commander. In Western military tradition the practice was established by the 19th century. The pair of boots reversed in the stirrups is said to signify the leader's last look back at his formation; a sword or other accoutrements may also be carried on the saddle.

Notable examples

In the United States, a riderless horse — the "caparisoned horse" — forms part of the funeral arrangements for presidents, generals and other senior figures entitled to full military honours. At Abraham Lincoln's funeral in 1865, his horse followed the procession. The most famous American example is Black Jack, a coal-black Morgan gelding named after General John J. "Black Jack" Pershing, who served as riderless horse in the state funerals of Presidents John F. Kennedy, Herbert Hoover and Lyndon B. Johnson and of General Douglas MacArthur. At Kennedy's funeral in 1963 the image of Black Jack, skittish and tossing his head, became one of the enduring pictures of the occasion. In Britain, a riderless horse was included in the funeral procession of Queen Elizabeth II in 2022 — her fell pony Emma stood at the side of the route as the cortège passed.
