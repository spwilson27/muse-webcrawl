Culture of Ireland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Placeholder image

The culture of Ireland includes the language, literature, music, art, folklore, cuisine and sport associated with Ireland and the Irish people. Drawing on Gaelic, Norman, Anglo-Irish and later influences, it has been carried worldwide by a large diaspora — tens of millions claim Irish ancestry, especially in the United States, Britain, Canada and Australia.

Language and literature

Irish (Gaeilge), a Celtic language, is the first official language of the Republic, though English predominates in daily use. Ireland's literary tradition is disproportionately celebrated: Jonathan Swift, Bram Stoker, Oscar Wilde, W. B. Yeats, James Joyce and Seamus Heaney are among its best-known figures, and the country has produced four Nobel laureates in Literature. Storytelling, the seanchaí tradition and the pub session remain living forms.

Music, dance and folklore

Traditional music — reels and jigs on fiddle, flute, uilleann pipes, harp and bodhrán — is played in pub sessions across the country. Irish step dance was globalized by shows such as Riverdance (1994). Folklore includes the mythological cycles, heroes such as Cú Chulainn and Fionn mac Cumhaill, and figures like the leprechaun and the banshee.

Sport and festivals

The Gaelic games — hurling and Gaelic football, organized by the Gaelic Athletic Association (founded 1884) — are central to local identity, alongside rugby and soccer. Saint Patrick's Day (17 March) is celebrated in Ireland and by the diaspora worldwide.
