Balanced line

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In telecommunications and professional audio, a balanced line or balanced signal pair is an electrical circuit consisting of two conductors of the same type, both of which have equal impedances along their lengths and equal impedances to ground and to other circuits. The chief advantage of the balanced line format is good rejection of common-mode interference (noise) that is picked up equally on both conductors.

Principle

The signal is transmitted as the difference in voltage between the two conductors (differential signalling). Interference coupled into the line from external sources — mains hum, radio signals, crosstalk — tends to appear identically on both conductors and is therefore cancelled when the receiving device responds only to the difference, a property quantified by the common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR). Twisting the conductors together, as in twisted pair cable, keeps them exposed to nearly the same interference and also minimises radiation of the signal itself.

Applications

Balanced lines are standard in professional audio, where microphone and line-level interconnects use XLR connectors, and in telephone networks, where the local loop is a balanced pair. Data standards such as RS-422 and RS-485, Ethernet over twisted pair, and USB all use balanced differential pairs, allowing long cable runs and high data rates in noisy environments. An unbalanced line, by contrast, carries the signal on a single conductor referenced to ground, as in consumer RCA audio cables, and is much more susceptible to interference.

A balun (balanced-to-unbalanced transformer) is used to interface balanced and unbalanced equipment.
