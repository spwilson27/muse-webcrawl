Sabbath

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Abrahamic religions, the Sabbath (from Hebrew Shabbat, "rest" or "cessation") is a day set aside for rest and worship. According to the Book of Exodus, the Sabbath is a day of rest on the seventh day, commanded by God to be kept as a holy day of rest, as God rested from creation. The practice of observing the Sabbath originates in the biblical commandment "Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy".

Judaism

In Judaism, Shabbat is observed from a few minutes before sunset on Friday evening until the appearance of three stars in the sky on Saturday night. It is marked by lighting candles, festive meals, synagogue services, Torah reading, and abstention from the 39 categories of creative work (melakha) derived from the construction of the Tabernacle. Shabbat is considered the most important holiday in the Jewish calendar, a weekly foretaste of the world to come.

Christianity

Most Christians observe Sunday as the Lord's Day, commemorating the resurrection of Jesus, a practice that developed in the early church. Some traditions, notably Seventh-day Adventists and Seventh Day Baptists, continue to observe Saturday as the Sabbath. Historically, strict Sunday rest was enforced by "blue laws" in many Western countries, and debates over Sabbatarian observance shaped Puritan and post-Reformation practice.

Other traditions

In Islam, Friday (Jumu'ah) is a day of congregational prayer, though not a day of rest in the Sabbath sense. The Bahá’í Faith and various other movements have their own weekly holy days. The concept has also influenced secular ideas of a weekly rest day and workers' rights.
