Brewster's angle

Brewster's angle (also known as the polarization angle) is an angle of incidence at which light with a particular polarization is perfectly transmitted through a transparent dielectric surface, with no reflection. When unpolarized light strikes a surface at this angle, the reflected light is therefore perfectly polarized.

Explanation

The phenomenon was first described by the Scottish physicist Sir David Brewster in 1815. For light travelling from a medium of refractive index n1 to one of index n2, Brewster's angle θB satisfies

θB = arctan(n2 / n1)

At this angle, the reflected and refracted rays are perpendicular to each other. The reflection coefficient for light polarized parallel to the plane of incidence (p-polarized light) falls to zero, as can be derived from the Fresnel equations; only the s-polarized component, perpendicular to the plane of incidence, is reflected.

Applications

Polarized sunglasses exploit the effect to reduce glare: reflections from horizontal surfaces such as water and roads near Brewster's angle are strongly polarized horizontally, and can be blocked by a vertically oriented polarizing filter. In optics, windows placed at Brewster's angle in gas laser tubes ("Brewster windows") transmit p-polarized light without reflection losses, forcing the laser output to be polarized.

For ordinary glass in air, the angle is about 56°; for water, about 53°.
