Uncial script

Uncial is a majuscule book hand used from roughly the 4th to the 8th century CE for Latin and Greek manuscripts.

Uncial is a majuscule (capital-letter) script commonly used from the 4th to the 8th centuries AD by Latin and Greek scribes. Its rounded, unjoined letterforms — such as the characteristic forms of A, D, E, M and Q — were written with a broad-nibbed pen held at a shallow angle, giving the script a distinctive rounded appearance that distinguished it from the angular Roman square capitals used for inscriptions.

History

Uncial developed from the late Roman cursive and book hands in the early centuries AD. It became the prestige script for deluxe codices, including many of the oldest surviving copies of the Bible and the works of Virgil. Famous Greek uncial manuscripts include Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, and Codex Alexandrinus; textual critics designate Greek New Testament uncials by capital letters or numbers beginning with 0. In the Latin West, uncial gradually gave way to regional hands such as the Insular, Merovingian and Beneventan scripts, and later to Carolingian minuscule, though it survived for headings and display lines.

Terminology

The name derives from the Latin uncia ("inch"), possibly referring to the size of the letters, although the term was applied loosely by St. Jerome in his preface to the Book of Job. Scholars distinguish "half-uncial," a smaller, more cursive development with some ascenders and descenders, which became important in Insular manuscripts such as the Book of Kells. Modern typefaces modelled on uncial forms are often marketed as "Celtic," reflecting the script's later association with Irish and Northumbrian Gospel books.
