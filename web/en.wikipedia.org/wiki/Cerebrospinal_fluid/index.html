Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is a clear, colourless body fluid found within the tissue that surrounds the brain and spinal cord of all vertebrates. It is produced mainly by specialised ependymal cells in the choroid plexuses of the ventricles of the brain, circulates through the ventricular system and the subarachnoid space, and is absorbed into the venous blood through arachnoid granulations.

Functions

CSF cushions the brain within the skull, reducing the effective weight of the brain and protecting it from injury. It provides a stable chemical environment, removes metabolic waste products from the central nervous system (a clearance role extended by the glymphatic system during sleep), and distributes nutrients and signalling molecules.

Clinical significance

CSF can be sampled by lumbar puncture ("spinal tap") for diagnosis of meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, multiple sclerosis and other neurological diseases; its pressure, cell counts, protein, glucose and culture are key diagnostic measures. Failure of CSF absorption or flow produces hydrocephalus, an abnormal build-up of fluid in the brain, treatable with a shunt. Leaks of CSF through the skull base or dura can cause positional headaches.
