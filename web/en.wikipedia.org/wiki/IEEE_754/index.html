IEEE 754 is the technical standard for floating-point arithmetic established in 1985 by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE). The standard defines formats for representing floating-point numbers, rules for rounding, arithmetic operations, special values such as infinities and NaNs, and exception handling. It is implemented in virtually all modern processors and is the basis for floating-point behaviour in most programming languages.

Layout of a binary32 value: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 significand bits.

Representation

A binary floating-point number consists of a sign, a biased exponent, and a significand (mantissa). The common binary32 ("single precision") format uses 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits with a bias of 127, and 23 stored significand bits, giving roughly 7 decimal digits of precision. binary64 ("double precision") uses 11 exponent bits and 52 significand bits, giving about 15–17 decimal digits. The standard also defines subnormal numbers, which allow gradual underflow near zero; signed zeros; positive and negative infinity; and NaN (Not a Number) values for undefined results.

Rules and operations

IEEE 754 specifies five rounding modes (round-to-nearest-even being the default), requires correctly rounded basic operations (+, −, ×, ÷, square root), and defines exception flags for invalid operation, division by zero, overflow, underflow, and inexact results. The 2008 revision added decimal floating-point formats, fused multiply-add, and a 16-bit "half precision" interchange format widely used in graphics and machine learning; IEEE 754-2019 clarified and extended recommendations on elementary functions and reproducibility.

History

Before 1985, each computer manufacturer used its own floating-point formats, making portable numerical software difficult. The standard was developed by a committee championed by William Kahan and others, drawing heavily on the design of the Intel 8087 coprocessor. Its adoption is widely regarded as one of the most successful standardisations in computing history.
