The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, also known as the Hart–Celler Act and the Immigration Reform Act, is a federal law passed by the 89th United States Congress and signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson on 3 October 1965. It abolished the National Origins Formula, which had restricted immigration on the basis of national origin and race since the 1920s, and replaced it with a preference system focused on immigrants' skills and family relationships with US citizens or residents.

Provisions

The act set an annual cap of 170,000 visas for the Eastern Hemisphere with a per-country limit of 20,000, and for the first time capped the Western Hemisphere (120,000, without per-country limits initially). Immediate relatives of US citizens were exempt from the caps. Seven preference categories prioritised family reunification, followed by professionals, skilled and unskilled workers, and refugees. The labour certification requirement required many employment-based immigrants to show they would not displace US workers.

Consequences

Supporters, including Johnson and Senator Edward Kennedy, assured the public that the ethnic mix of the country would not be drastically altered; in fact the law transformed American demographics. Immigration from Asia, Africa, Latin America and the Middle East rose sharply through family-reunification "chain migration", while the European share of arrivals fell. The foreign-born share of the US population climbed from about 5 percent in 1965 to roughly 14 percent in the 21st century. The act is regarded as a landmark of the civil-rights era, ending four decades of explicitly discriminatory quotas dating from the Immigration Act of 1924.
