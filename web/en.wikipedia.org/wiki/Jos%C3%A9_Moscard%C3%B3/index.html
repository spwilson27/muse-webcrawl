José Moscardó Ituarte (October 26, 1878 – April 12, 1956) was a Spanish military officer who commanded the Nationalist garrison during the Siege of the Alcázar of Toledo in the opening months of the Spanish Civil War, an episode that became one of the Franco regime's foundational heroic legends.

Early career

Born in Madrid, Moscardó served as a young officer in the Philippines and later in the Moroccan campaigns. By 1936 he was a colonel and military governor of Toledo, where he also directed the Central School of Physical Education. His career had been unremarkable, and he was nearing retirement when the Civil War began.

Siege of the Alcázar

When the military uprising of July 1936 failed to take Toledo, Moscardó withdrew some 1,700 people—soldiers, Civil Guards, cadets of the Infantry Academy, and the families of right-wing supporters, along with hostages—into the Alcázar fortress. Republican forces besieged the fortress for over two months, subjecting it to artillery fire and mining. According to the famous account promoted by Nationalist propaganda, Moscardó refused a telephone demand to surrender under threat that his captured son Luis would be killed, telling the boy to die like a patriot; Luis was later executed. The siege ended on September 27, 1936, when Nationalist columns under General José Enrique Varela relieved the fortress. Historians have questioned details of the telephone episode, though the core of the story remains central to the Alcázar's commemoration.

Later career

Promoted to general, Moscardó commanded the Soria Division and later the Aragon Army Corps during the war. After 1939 he held posts including head of Franco's military household and president of the Spanish Olympic Committee, and was made Count of the Alcázar of Toledo. He died in Madrid in 1956 and was granted the rank of captain general posthumously.
