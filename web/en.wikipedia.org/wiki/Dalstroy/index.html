Dalstroy (Russian: Дальстрой), the Far North Construction Trust, was a Soviet organisation established in 1931 to develop the Kolyma region of the Russian Far East, principally through gold mining using the forced labour of Gulag prisoners. From its headquarters and port at Magadan, Dalstroy came to administer an area of some three million square kilometres almost as a state within a state.

Operations

Under its first director, Eduard Berzin, and successors including Ivan Nikishov, Dalstroy built mines, roads — most notoriously the Kolyma Highway, the "Road of Bones" — power stations, and settlements. Prisoners arrived by ship at Magadan, many transported across the USSR by rail to Vladivostok. Gold and tin output was strategically vital, and during the Second World War Kolyma gold helped sustain the Soviet war economy.

Human cost

Conditions in the Kolyma camps were among the harshest in the Gulag: extreme cold, starvation rations, and lethal work norms. Estimates of the number who died under Dalstroy's administration range from hundreds of thousands to over a million; the writer Varlam Shalamov, a survivor, immortalised the camps in his Kolyma Tales.

Dissolution

After Stalin's death in 1953 the camp system contracted rapidly. Dalstroy was transferred to civilian ministries and dissolved in 1957, though mining in the region continued with free labour.
