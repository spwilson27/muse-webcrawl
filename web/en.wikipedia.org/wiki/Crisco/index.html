Crisco is an American brand of shortening and cooking products introduced by Procter & Gamble in 1911. It was the first shortening made entirely from vegetable oil — originally hydrogenated cottonseed oil — and was marketed as a clean, modern, all-vegetable alternative to lard and butter.

A classic Crisco tin.

History

Procter & Gamble developed Crisco using the newly patented hydrogenation process (patents by Edwin Kayser and by the German chemist Wilhelm Normann) to solidify cottonseed oil, a by-product of cotton processing. Its launch was a landmark of modern advertising: the brand was sold with cookbooks, free samples, and claims of digestibility, and was notably marketed to Jewish households as a kosher fat usable with both meat and dairy. The name derives from "crystallised cottonseed oil."

Trans fats and reformulation

Partial hydrogenation produced large amounts of trans fat. As evidence mounted linking trans fat to heart disease, Crisco was reformulated; in 2007 P&G introduced a version with zero grams of trans fat per serving, based on fully hydrogenated and liquid soybean and palm oils. In 2002 the brand had been sold to the J. M. Smucker Company; Smucker sold Crisco to B&G Foods in 2020.

In cooking

Crisco shortening is prized in American baking for flaky pie crusts and tender biscuits, and for deep frying; the line now includes butter-flavoured shortening, sticks, oils, and cooking sprays.
