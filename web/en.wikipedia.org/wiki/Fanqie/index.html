Fanqie

Fanqie (Chinese: 反切; pinyin: fǎnqiè) is a traditional method used in Chinese dictionaries and rime books to indicate the pronunciation of a character. The system, developed in the late Han dynasty and refined during the Wei and Jin dynasties, spells the sound of a character by combining two other characters: the first supplies the initial consonant (shangzi, "upper character") and the second supplies the final — the remainder of the syllable, including its tone (xiazi, "lower character").

History

Before fanqie, pronunciations were indicated by loose methods such as describing a character as sounding "like" another (du ruo). Fanqie became the standard tool of Chinese philology with works such as Sun Yan's Erya Yinyi and, most famously, the rime dictionary Qieyun (601 CE) compiled by Lu Fayan, which used fanqie spellings throughout. The name itself combines fan (反, "to turn over") and qie (切, "to cut"), reflecting the analysis of a syllable into its two parts.

Method

For example, the character 東 ("east", modern Mandarin dōng) is spelled with the fanqie 德紅: 德 (Middle Chinese initial t-) contributes the onset and 紅 (rhyme -uwng, level tone) contributes the final and tone, yielding Middle Chinese tuwng. Because the reference characters themselves changed pronunciation over the centuries, fanqie spellings in old rime books became a cipher that modern scholars — beginning with pioneers such as Bernhard Karlgren — used, together with modern dialect evidence, to reconstruct Middle Chinese phonology.

Legacy

Fanqie remained the standard pronunciation notation in Chinese reference works for well over a thousand years, until it was supplemented and then largely replaced by systems such as zhuyin (bopomofo) and pinyin in the 20th century. It is still studied as the key to historical Chinese phonology.
