Selenoprotein

In molecular biology, a selenoprotein is any protein that includes a selenocysteine (Sec, U) amino acid residue. Selenocysteine is structurally like cysteine but with a selenium atom in place of sulfur, and it is incorporated into proteins during translation by recoding of the UGA codon, which normally signals termination. In humans, 25 selenoprotein genes are known.

Biosynthesis

Selenocysteine is synthesized on its own dedicated tRNA, tRNA[Ser]Sec. A stem-loop structure in the messenger RNA called the SECIS element (selenocysteine insertion sequence) — located in the 3' untranslated region in eukaryotes — directs the ribosome to insert selenocysteine at UGA codons rather than stopping translation. The pathway involves the enzymes selenophosphate synthetase and selenocysteine synthase, and uses selenium obtained from the diet in forms such as selenomethionine and selenite.

Functions

The selenium in selenocysteine sits at the active site of several important enzymes, where its lower pKa and strong nucleophilicity make it a highly effective redox catalyst. Well-characterized human selenoproteins include:

  • Glutathione peroxidases (GPx1–4, 6), which reduce hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides, protecting cells from oxidative damage.
  • Thioredoxin reductases, which maintain the reduced state of thioredoxin and support DNA synthesis and redox signalling.
  • Iodothyronine deiodinases, which activate and inactivate thyroid hormones.
  • Selenoprotein P, the main selenium transport protein in plasma.

Distribution

Selenoproteins occur in all three domains of life, but their repertoire varies widely: many organisms, including most fungi and higher plants, have lost them entirely, while some bacteria and algae encode dozens. Selenium deficiency in humans is associated with Keshan disease, a cardiomyopathy, and impaired thyroid function.
