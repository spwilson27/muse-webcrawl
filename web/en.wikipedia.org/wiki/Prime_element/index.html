Prime element

Factorization into prime elements in a commutative ring

In mathematics, a prime element is a generalization to arbitrary commutative rings of the notion of a prime number in the integers. An element p of a commutative ring R (with identity) is called prime if it is neither zero nor a unit, and whenever p divides a product ab, it divides a or it divides b.

Definition

Formally, an element p ∈ R is a prime element if:

  • p ≠ 0;
  • p is not a unit (that is, it has no multiplicative inverse in R);
  • for all a, b ∈ R, if p | ab then p | a or p | b.

Equivalently, the principal ideal (p) generated by a prime element is a nonzero prime ideal of the ring.

Relation to irreducible elements

A prime element is always an irreducible element (one that cannot be written as a product of two non-units) in an integral domain. The converse does not hold in general: in the ring ℤ[√−5], the element 3 is irreducible but not prime, since 3 divides (2 + √−5)(2 − √−5) = 9 without dividing either factor. In a unique factorization domain (UFD), however, the two notions coincide, and every irreducible element is prime.

Examples

  • In the integers ℤ, the prime elements are (up to sign) exactly the prime numbers.
  • In the Gaussian integers ℤ[i], the rational prime 5 is not prime because it factors as (2 + i)(2 − i), while primes congruent to 3 mod 4 remain prime.
  • In a polynomial ring k[x] over a field, the prime elements are the irreducible polynomials.
