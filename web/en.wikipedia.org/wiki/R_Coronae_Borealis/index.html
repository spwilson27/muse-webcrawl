R Coronae Borealis is a variable star in the constellation Corona Borealis, and the prototype of the R Coronae Borealis class of variables. It is a low-mass yellow supergiant, extremely deficient in hydrogen and rich in carbon, which at irregular intervals fades dramatically—sometimes by as much as eight magnitudes—before slowly recovering over months to years. Its variability was discovered by the English amateur astronomer Edward Pigott in 1795.

Variability

R Coronae Borealis normally shines at about magnitude 5.7, near the limit of naked-eye visibility. At unpredictable intervals it drops steeply in brightness, to magnitude 14 or fainter, as clouds of carbon-rich dust condense in material expelled from the star and obscure it. The fading typically takes days to weeks; recovery, as the dust disperses, can take months or years, often with hesitations and secondary dips. The star also shows small semi-regular pulsations of around 40 days. It spent an unusually long period in a deep minimum from 2007, remaining faint for over a decade, before recovering substantially in the 2020s.

Nature and origin

R CrB stars are thought to be the products of white dwarf mergers or final helium-shell flashes, which leave a hydrogen-poor, carbon-rich supergiant. R Coronae Borealis itself has a mass of roughly 0.8–0.9 solar masses but a luminosity thousands of times the Sun's. Spectroscopic monitoring and infrared observations reveal an extended dust shell and a large circumstellar envelope, consistent with heavy episodic mass loss.

Observing

The star is a long-standing target of amateur variable star observers, who report brightness estimates to organisations such as the AAVSO; its sudden fadings are sometimes called an "eclipse by soot". Its behaviour is contrasted with the related DY Persei variables and the more regular pulsating variables.
