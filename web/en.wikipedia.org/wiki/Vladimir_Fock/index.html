Vladimir Fock

Vladimir Aleksandrovich Fock (Russian: Влади́мир Алекса́ндрович Фок; 22 December 1898 – 27 December 1974) was a Soviet physicist who made fundamental contributions to quantum mechanics and quantum electrodynamics. He is best known for the Hartree–Fock method of calculating atomic wavefunctions, the Fock space and Fock states of quantum field theory, and the Klein–Gordon–Fock equation.

Life and work

Fock studied at Petrograd University, served as an artillery officer in the First World War, and after graduating in 1922 joined the State Optical Institute and Leningrad University, where he became a professor. Briefly arrested in the political repressions of the 1930s, he was released after interventions by senior colleagues and spent his career in Leningrad, weathering the siege of the city during the Second World War.

In 1926 he independently derived the relativistic wave equation usually called the Klein–Gordon equation. In 1930 he improved Douglas Hartree's self-consistent field method by including the exchange interaction required by the Pauli principle, producing the Hartree–Fock method, a cornerstone of computational chemistry and atomic physics. In 1932 he introduced Fock space — a Hilbert space built from states of arbitrary particle number — in the formalism of second quantisation, and the related Fock representation of the canonical commutation relations. He also worked on general relativity, including harmonic coordinates and the theory of motion of extended bodies, defended the wave interpretation of quantum mechanics against official Soviet philosophical criticism, and co-authored a classic work on the electromagnetic theory of diffraction. He was elected to the USSR Academy of Sciences and received the title Hero of Socialist Labour (1968).
