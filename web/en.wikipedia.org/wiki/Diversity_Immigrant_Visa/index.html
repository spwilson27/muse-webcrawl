Diversity Immigrant Visa

The Diversity Immigrant Visa program, also known as the green card lottery, is a United States Government lottery program for receiving a United States Permanent Resident Card. Administered by the Department of State, the program is mandated by the Immigration Act of 1990 and makes available up to 55,000 immigrant visas annually, drawn from random selection among all entries to individuals who are from countries with low rates of immigration to the United States.

Eligibility

Eligibility is determined primarily by country of birth: natives of countries that sent more than 50,000 immigrants to the United States in the previous five years are excluded (the list of ineligible countries regularly includes countries such as Mexico, China, India, the Philippines, Canada, and the United Kingdom, among others). Applicants must also meet education or work experience requirements — a high school education or its equivalent, or two years of qualifying work experience. Entries are submitted electronically during a short registration period each autumn through the State Department's Electronic Diversity Visa system; there is no fee to enter the lottery itself.

History and debate

The program grew out of earlier diversity visa schemes of the late 1980s, notably the NP-5 and OP-1 programs associated with Representative (later Senator) Chuck Schumer and others, which were aimed in part at Irish immigrants; the Immigration Act of 1990 created the permanent program, which began with fiscal year 1995. The lottery has repeatedly attracted political controversy and proposals for its abolition, including criticism over fraud, vetting, and the randomness of selection, balanced by supporters' arguments that it diversifies immigration and offers a legal pathway to people without family or employer sponsors. Roughly ten million or more people apply in a typical year, making selection odds low.

Fraud warnings

The State Department and the Federal Trade Commission regularly warn applicants about scams: many commercial websites imitate official registration and charge fees for the free entry process, and fraudulent emails claiming that recipients have won the lottery are common. Official results are available only through the Entrant Status Check on the State Department website.
