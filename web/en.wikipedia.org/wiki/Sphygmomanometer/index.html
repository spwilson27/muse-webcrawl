A sphygmomanometer (from Greek sphygmos, "pulse", plus manometer), also known as a blood pressure monitor or blood pressure gauge, is a device used to measure blood pressure. It consists of an inflatable cuff that restricts blood flow in an artery, a measuring unit, and a mechanism for inflation, which may be a manually operated bulb and valve or an electrically operated pump.

History

Non-invasive blood pressure measurement began with Samuel Siegfried Karl Ritter von Basch's sphygmomanometer of 1881. Scipione Riva-Rocci introduced the mercury manometer with an arm cuff in 1896, measuring systolic pressure by palpation. In 1905, Russian physician Nikolai Korotkoff described the sounds heard through a stethoscope as the cuff deflates, enabling measurement of both systolic and diastolic pressures — the auscultatory method still taught today. Harvey Cushing brought the device into routine American clinical use in the early 1900s.

How it works

The cuff is wrapped around the upper arm and inflated until arterial flow stops. Pressure is then released slowly. In the manual (auscultatory) method, the clinician listens with a stethoscope over the brachial artery: the pressure at which tapping sounds (Korotkoff sounds) first appear is the systolic pressure, and the pressure at which they disappear is the diastolic pressure. Automated devices instead use the oscillometric method, detecting pressure oscillations in the cuff to estimate systolic, mean, and diastolic values with proprietary algorithms.

Types

  • Mercury sphygmomanometers — long the gold standard for accuracy, now being phased out in many countries because of mercury's toxicity.
  • Aneroid sphygmomanometers — a mechanical dial gauge; compact but requiring periodic calibration.
  • Digital monitors — oscillometric arm or wrist devices, standard for home measurement and ambulatory 24-hour monitoring.

Clinical significance

Accurate measurement underpins the diagnosis and management of hypertension, a leading risk factor for stroke and cardiovascular disease. Technique matters: correct cuff size, a supported arm at heart level, and a rested, seated patient. Phenomena such as "white coat hypertension" — elevated readings in clinical settings — have driven wider use of home and ambulatory monitoring.
