Tilt–shift photography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tilt–shift photography is the use of camera movements that change the orientation or position of the lens with respect to the image sensor. "Tilt" rotates the lens plane relative to the image plane; "shift" moves the lens parallel to the image plane. Such movements, traditional on view cameras and available on special tilt–shift lenses for smaller formats, allow the photographer to control the plane of focus and the rendering of perspective.

Applications

Shift is used in architectural photography to prevent converging verticals: raising or lowering the lens keeps the sensor parallel to a building's facade while framing it. Tilt exploits the Scheimpflug principle to align the plane of sharp focus with an inclined subject, yielding front-to-back sharpness at wide apertures — or, used in the opposite direction, a wedge of focus with strong blur above and below.

Miniature faking

In popular usage, "tilt–shift" often refers to the miniature-faking effect: using selective blur (or digital simulation of it) to make a real scene look like a photograph of a scale model. The effect, popularised in the 2000s, frequently combines high viewpoint, saturated colour and shallow focus. Software filters and smartphone modes now simulate it widely.

Equipment

Canon and Nikon market dedicated tilt–shift (TS-E / PC-E) lenses; adapters and lens accessories can add movements to other systems. View cameras provide the most flexible movements through tilting and shifting both lens and film standards.
