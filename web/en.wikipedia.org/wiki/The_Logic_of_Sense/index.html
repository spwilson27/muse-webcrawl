The Logic of Sense (French: Logique du sens) is a 1969 book by the French philosopher Gilles Deleuze. Organised as thirty-four "series" of paradoxes followed by an appendix on simulacra and ancient philosophy, the book develops a theory of sense and events drawing on Stoic philosophy, structuralism, psychoanalysis, and the writings of Lewis Carroll.

Overview

Deleuze distinguishes between bodies, which act and suffer in depth, and incorporeal events that subsist at the surface of bodies and are expressed by propositions as their "sense". Sense is neither the thing denoted, nor the mental state of a speaker, nor the logical concept, but a fourth, impassive dimension of the proposition—an event that is produced yet neutral with respect to existence. The book explores the paradoxes this generates: regress, doubling, the sterile splitting of sense, and the "esoteric" and nonsense words (such as Carroll's portmanteau words) that circulate sense within language.

A central thread is the contrast, adapted from the Stoics and from Carroll's Alice books, between eating and speaking: the depths of the body, organised by drives and by a "body without organs", and the surface organisation of language, governed by codes Deleuze names the code of words and the code of bodies. His readings of Carroll, Antonin Artaud, and Pierre Klossowski develop themes of schizophrenia, humour, and the crack-up of the surface.

Reception and influence

Michel Foucault famously wrote, in his review "Theatrum Philosophicum" (1970), that "one day, perhaps, this century will be known as Deleuzian". The Logic of Sense became one of Deleuze's most discussed works, influential in literary theory, continental philosophy, and later studies of events and virtuality. Its theory of sense and the event anticipates the philosophy developed with Félix Guattari in Capitalism and Schizophrenia.
