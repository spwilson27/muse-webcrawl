Classical architecture

Classical architecture usually denotes architecture which is more or less consciously derived from the principles of Greek and Roman architecture of classical antiquity, or sometimes more specifically, from the works of the Roman architect Vitruvius. Different styles of classical architecture have existed since the Carolingian Renaissance and have been revived and reinterpreted in every era up to the present day.

The classical orders — column, capital, entablature, and pediment — form the grammar of the style.

The orders

The basic unit of classical design is the order: the Tuscan, Doric, Ionic, Corinthian, and Composite orders codified by Roman and Renaissance theorists dictate the proportions and ornament of columns and the entablature they support. Vitruvius's De architectura (1st century BC), rediscovered in the Renaissance, supplied the theory; Alberti, Serlio, Vignola, and Palladio systematized its practice.

Revivals

The Italian Renaissance (from Brunelleschi and Alberti onward) deliberately revived Roman forms; Palladianism spread the style to England and America; Neoclassicism of the later 18th and 19th centuries returned to archaeological Greek and Roman models for banks, museums, parliaments, and universities. Beaux-Arts architecture in France and America, and later "New Classical" architecture from the 1980s onward, continued the tradition even as Modernism — which repudiated applied classical ornament — dominated the 20th century.
