Hans Adolf Eduard Driesch (28 October 1867 – 16 April 1941) was a German biologist and philosopher. Trained as an experimental embryologist, Driesch became famous for his experiments on sea urchin embryos, in which separating the cells of an early embryo produced complete, though smaller, larvae. Because he believed no purely mechanical explanation could account for this regulative development, he turned to philosophy and proposed the concept of entelechy, a non-material directive factor in living organisms, making him the most prominent exponent of vitalism in the early twentieth century.

Early life and education

Driesch was born in Bad Kreuznach, the son of a goldsmith. He studied zoology at the universities of Freiburg, Munich and Jena, where he came under the influence of Ernst Haeckel, and received his doctorate in 1889 for work on hydroids. He also studied physics and mathematics at Hamburg. In 1891 he began working at the Stazione Zoologica in Naples, the centre of the new experimental embryology.

Embryological experiments

At Naples Driesch performed the experiments that made his reputation. Following Wilhelm Roux's famous frog experiments, Driesch separated the first two blastomeres of a sea urchin embryo by shaking them in calcium-free seawater. Contrary to the expectation of mosaic development derived from August Weismann's theory of determinants, each isolated cell developed into a complete, proportioned, though half-sized larva (a pluteus). He called such self-regulating systems "harmonious equipotential systems": each part retained the potential of the whole.

Driesch concluded that development could not be explained by any machine-like arrangement of material parts, since a machine divided in two does not produce two smaller working machines. In his Analytische Theorie der organischen Entwicklung (1894) he presented a rigorous analysis of development, and by the late 1890s he had concluded that a non-physical, goal-directed factor — which he named entelechy, borrowing Aristotle's term — was required to organise the embryo.

Philosophy and later career

After 1900 Driesch largely abandoned laboratory work for philosophy. He lectured at Aberdeen (the Gifford Lectures of 1907–1908, published as The Science and Philosophy of the Organism), and in 1911 became professor of philosophy at Heidelberg; in 1921 he moved to Cologne, and then to Leipzig, with visiting professorships in China and the United States. His major philosophical works include Philosophie des Organischen (1909) and Wirklichkeitslehre (1917).

Driesch was also active in parapsychology, serving as president of the Society for Psychical Research in 1926–1927. A liberal internationalist who opposed National Socialism, he was forced into premature retirement by the Nazi regime in 1933 and forbidden to lecture publicly. He died in Leipzig in 1941.

Legacy

Although vitalism was rejected by mainstream biology as genetics and biochemistry advanced, Driesch's experiments were foundational for experimental embryology and for concepts of regulation, fields and organisers later taken up by Hans Spemann and others. Historians of biology regard his careful experimental work and his unsuccessful theoretical conclusion as a classic case in the philosophy of science.
