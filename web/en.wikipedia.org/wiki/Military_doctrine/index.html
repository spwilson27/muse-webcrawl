Military doctrine

Military doctrine is the expression of how military forces contribute to campaigns, operations, battles and engagements. It is a guide to action, rather than hard and fast rules: doctrine provides a common frame of reference across the military, helping to standardise operations and facilitating readiness by establishing common ways of accomplishing military tasks. It links theory, history, experimentation and practice.

Levels and formation

Doctrine is usually articulated at the strategic, operational and tactical levels and embodied in field manuals, capstone publications and service regulations. It emerges from a state's strategic culture, geography, technology and historical experience, and is tested through exercises, wargaming and the lessons of war. Examples include the German emphasis on mission-type orders (Auftragstaktik), the Soviet concept of deep battle, the American AirLand Battle doctrine of the 1980s, and contemporary multi-domain operations.

Doctrine versus strategy

Doctrine differs from strategy: strategy is the art of employing military means to achieve political ends, while doctrine describes the preferred methods by which forces fight — the "how" that planners can assume when they design campaigns. Well-institutionalised doctrine allows decentralised initiative, because subordinates who share a doctrine can anticipate each other's actions; critics note that rigid doctrine can also blind forces to novel threats, as with the French army's methodical battle doctrine of 1940.
