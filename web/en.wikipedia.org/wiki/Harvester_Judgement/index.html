SignificanceEstablished the Australian "basic wage"

The Harvester Judgement (Ex parte H. V. McKay, 1907) is a landmark Australian labour law decision of the Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration, delivered by Justice H. B. Higgins, which established the concept of a basic wage — a minimum living wage sufficient for a worker to support a family.

Background

The case arose under the Excise Tariff Act 1906, which allowed manufacturers of agricultural machinery to claim an excise exemption if they paid their workers wages that were "fair and reasonable". Hugh Victor McKay, maker of the Sunshine Harvester, sought a declaration that the wages at his Sunshine Harvester Works satisfied the Act.

The decision

Higgins rejected union and employer submissions based merely on market rates and instead determined a fair wage from evidence of household living costs. He set seven shillings per day as the minimum for an unskilled labourer, calculated to support a man, a wife, and about three children in "frugal comfort". McKay's wages were found not to meet the standard, and the company was liable for excise.

Legacy

Although the underlying scheme was struck down by the High Court in 1908, the Harvester standard became the foundation of Australia's centralised wage-fixing system for most of the 20th century and remains a foundational reference point in Australian industrial relations.
