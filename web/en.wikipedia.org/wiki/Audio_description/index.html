Audio description

Audio description, also referred to as described video (DV), descriptive narration, or video description, is a form of narration used to provide information surrounding key visual elements in a media work for the benefit of blind and visually impaired consumers. These narrations are typically placed during natural pauses in the audio, and sometimes overlap dialogue if deemed necessary. In museums or visual art exhibitions, audio described tours (or universally designed tours that include description or the handling of objects) can be provided for visitors who are blind or have low vision.

History

Formal audio description was pioneered in the 1970s and 1980s. The Metropolitan Washington Ear in the United States began describing live theatre performances in 1974. In 1981, Margaret and Cody Pfanstiehl developed the first description service for theatre at Arena Stage in Washington, D.C. The Boston public broadcaster WGBH launched its Descriptive Video Service (DVS) in 1990, providing described television programming on the Secondary Audio Program channel. In the United Kingdom, the BBC and ITV began broadcasting audio description in the 1990s, delivered over a separate receiver or television feature.

Television and film

For recorded television and film, a trained describer writes a script narrating visual information—settings, actions, facial expressions, costumes, and scene changes—which is recorded and mixed so that it fits into pauses in the dialogue. Viewers typically access description through a secondary audio track, often labelled "AD" or "descriptive audio", on broadcast television, streaming services, and home video.

Streaming services have expanded the availability of described content considerably; major platforms such as Netflix, Amazon Prime Video, and Disney+ provide audio description tracks for much of their original programming.

Live events

In live theatre, a describer narrates the performance in real time via a transmitter, with patrons listening through earpieces. Audio description is also provided for live sports, museum exhibits, and other cultural events.

Legislation

Several countries impose quotas or requirements for described programming. In the United States, the Twenty-First Century Communications and Video Accessibility Act of 2010 (CVAA) reinstated Federal Communications Commission rules requiring major networks and cable channels to provide a set number of hours of described programming per quarter. In the United Kingdom, the Communications Act 2003 requires licensed broadcasters to audio-describe a proportion (generally 10%) of their output. Canada, Australia, and many European countries have similar requirements.
