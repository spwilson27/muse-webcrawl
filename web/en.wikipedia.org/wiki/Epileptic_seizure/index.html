An epileptic seizure is a period of symptoms due to abnormally excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. Outward effects vary greatly, from uncontrolled jerking movements (a convulsion) and loss of consciousness, to brief lapses of awareness, unusual sensations, or a spell of staring. Most seizures last from a few seconds to a few minutes and are followed by a postictal state of confusion, fatigue, or sleepiness.

Classification

The International League Against Epilepsy classifies seizures by onset: focal seizures begin in one region of one hemisphere and may or may not impair awareness; generalised seizures engage networks on both sides from the start and include tonic-clonic (formerly "grand mal") convulsions, absence seizures (formerly "petit mal"), myoclonic jerks, and atonic drop attacks. Seizures with unknown onset form a third category.

Causes and management

A single seizure may be provoked by fever (in children), head injury, stroke, infection, metabolic disturbance, alcohol withdrawal, or sleep deprivation; recurrent unprovoked seizures define epilepsy. Diagnosis relies on the clinical history, EEG, and brain imaging. First aid consists of protecting the person from injury and timing the seizure; a convulsion lasting more than five minutes — status epilepticus — is a medical emergency. Long-term treatment typically uses antiseizure medications, many of which are enzyme-modulating drugs, with surgery, neurostimulation, or dietary therapy for drug-resistant cases.
