Carbon black

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Carbon black (subtypes of which are known as acetylene black, channel black, furnace black, lamp black and thermal black) is a material produced by the incomplete combustion of coal tar, vegetable matter or petroleum products, including fuel oil and coal tar pitch. It is a form of paracrystalline carbon with a high surface-area-to-volume ratio, and it is one of the most widely used industrial materials in the world.

Production

The dominant process is the oil furnace process, in which a heavy aromatic oil feedstock is sprayed into a hot gas stream and partially combusted; the resulting carbon particles — primary particles typically 10–500 nm across that fuse into aggregates — are collected in filters. Channel black, made by impinging gas flames on iron channels, is now largely obsolete because of its low yield and environmental impact, and acetylene black is made by the thermal decomposition of acetylene gas for conductive applications.

Uses

Roughly 70% of carbon black output goes into the rubber industry as a reinforcing filler for tyres, where it dramatically improves abrasion resistance, tensile strength and durability, and also gives tyres their black colour and protection from ultraviolet light. Most of the remainder is used as a pigment — in printing inks, paints, plastics and toners — and as a conductive additive in batteries, cables and antistatic compounds. Speciality grades serve in metallurgy and as adsorbents.

Safety

Inhalation of carbon black dust is a workplace hazard; the International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies carbon black as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B). Modern production and handling use enclosed systems and pelletised product to reduce airborne dust.
