Hermitian matrix

In mathematics, a Hermitian matrix (or self-adjoint matrix) is a complex square matrix that is equal to its own conjugate transpose — that is, the element in the i-th row and j-th column equals the complex conjugate of the element in the j-th row and i-th column for all indices i and j: A = AH. Hermitian matrices can be understood as the complex analogue of real symmetric matrices.

Properties

Because a Hermitian matrix equals its conjugate transpose, its diagonal entries must be real (each equals its own conjugate). Two further properties make Hermitian matrices central to linear algebra and physics:

  • Real eigenvalues. Every eigenvalue of a Hermitian matrix is a real number. This is why Hermitian matrices model physical observables in quantum mechanics, whose measured values must be real.
  • Orthogonal eigenvectors and diagonalisability. Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, and by the spectral theorem every Hermitian matrix can be diagonalised by a unitary matrix: A = UDUH with D real and diagonal.

The set of n×n Hermitian matrices forms a real vector space of dimension n² (though not a complex one, since multiplication by i destroys Hermiticity); every complex matrix can be uniquely written as the sum of a Hermitian and a skew-Hermitian part.

Examples

The Pauli matrices of quantum mechanics are Hermitian, as is the identity matrix and any real symmetric matrix. A general 2×2 Hermitian matrix has the form with real a and d on the diagonal and conjugate entries b and b̄ off the diagonal.

Applications

In quantum mechanics the Hamiltonian operator and other observables are Hermitian (self-adjoint), guaranteeing real measurement outcomes and a complete basis of states. In signal processing and statistics, covariance matrices are Hermitian positive semi-definite, and in engineering the Hermitian structure underlies many eigenvalue algorithms.

The matrices are named after Charles Hermite, who showed in 1855 that their eigenvalues are always real.
