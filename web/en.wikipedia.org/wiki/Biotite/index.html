Biotite

Biotite, a dark mica mineral.

Biotite is a common group of phyllosilicate minerals within the mica group, with the approximate chemical formula K(Mg,Fe)3(AlSi3O10)(F,OH)2. It is primarily a solid-solution series between the iron-rich endmember annite and the magnesium-rich endmember phlogopite; more aluminous end-members include siderophyllite and eastonite.

Biotite was named in 1847 by J.F.L. Hausmann in honour of the French physicist Jean-Baptiste Biot, who performed early research into the many optical properties of mica.

Properties

Like other mica minerals, biotite has a highly perfect basal cleavage, yielding thin, flexible sheets ("flakes") that peel apart easily. It has a monoclinic crystal system, with tabular to prismatic crystals with obvious pinacoid termination. The mineral is dark green, brown, or black in colour, with a vitreous to pearly lustre. When weathered, biotite alters to chlorite, vermiculite or hydrated oxides, often turning a golden-bronze colour ("cat's gold").

Occurrence

Biotite is one of the most widespread rock-forming minerals. It is an essential constituent of many igneous rocks, such as granite, granodiorite, diorite and pegmatites, and of metamorphic rocks including gneisses and schists. Biotite forms under a wide variety of temperature and pressure conditions and is therefore useful in geothermobarometry. Because biotite contains potassium, it can be used for potassium–argon and argon–argon dating of igneous and metamorphic rocks.

Uses

Biotite has few commercial uses; ground mica derived from sheet mica scrap was once used as a filler and non-stick surface coating. Large crystals of biotite occur in pegmatites, and the mineral is a common accessory in geological specimens.
