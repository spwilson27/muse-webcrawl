Public transport

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Public transport (or public transit, mass transit) is a system of transport for passengers by group travel systems available for use by the general public, typically managed on a schedule, operated on established routes, and charging a posted fare: buses, trams and trains, rapid transit (metro/subway) systems like the London Underground, commuter rail, ferries and shared taxis. Unlike private cars or chartered vehicles, it trades door-to-door convenience for capacity, cost and land efficiency — a single metro line can move tens of thousands of people per hour through a corridor a road lane serves for a few thousand cars.

History and economics

Scheduled public transport begins with horse-drawn omnibuses (Paris 1828, London 1829), followed by horse trams, steam and then electric tramways, the first underground railways (London, 1863), elevated lines, and motor buses; suburban rail built the commuter city, and the 20th century's mass car ownership emptied transit in much of the English-speaking world while continental Europe and East Asia kept investing. Today the largest systems — Tokyo's railways, the Beijing and Shanghai metros (the world's longest networks), Moscow, Seoul, New York — carry billions of rides a year, while bus networks remain the workhorse almost everywhere. Transit is almost never profitable from fares alone at socially acceptable prices; it is funded as public infrastructure, like roads, for its external benefits: less congestion, lower emissions per trip, land values, and mobility for those who cannot drive. Debates recur over fares (free-fare experiments from Tallinn to Luxembourg), automation, service frequency versus coverage, and the post-2020 ridership shock from remote work — the standing challenge behind every city's transport politics.
