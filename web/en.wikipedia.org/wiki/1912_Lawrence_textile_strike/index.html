1912 Lawrence textile strike

Date11 January – 14 March 1912
LocationLawrence, Massachusetts, United States
MethodsStrike, picketing, demonstrations
ResultWage increases conceded; strikers returned to work
PartiesIndustrial Workers of the World and textile workers vs. mill owners (American Woolen Company et al.)

The 1912 Lawrence textile strike, also known as the Bread and Roses strike, was a strike of immigrant workers in Lawrence, Massachusetts, led by the Industrial Workers of the World (IWW). Prompted by a reduction in hours — and therefore pay — under a new Massachusetts law limiting the working week for women and children, the strike spread across the city's vast textile mills, involving some 25,000 workers of more than forty nationalities. It ended after two months with pay rises across the New England textile industry, and became one of the most famous strikes in American labour history.

Background

Lawrence was a planned mill city dominated by the American Woolen Company. Its workforce was overwhelmingly immigrant — Italians, Poles, Lithuanians, Syrians, French Canadians and others — many of them women and children, working 56-hour weeks for wages as low as $8 a week. Life expectancy was short, infant mortality was among the highest in the nation, and conditions in the mills were dangerous.

On 1 January 1912 a new state law reduced the maximum working week for women and minors from 56 to 54 hours. When the first pay envelopes under the new hours, issued on 11 January, showed roughly 32 cents less — the equivalent of several loaves of bread — Polish women weavers at the Everett Mill walked out, and the strike spread mill by mill.

Course of the strike

The IWW sent organisers Joseph Ettor and Arturo Giovannitti, later joined by Elizabeth Gurley Flynn and Bill Haywood. The union formed a strike committee with two representatives from each nationality group, held mass meetings translated into many languages, organised soup kitchens, and maintained disciplined picket lines in the face of the state militia, which occupied the city after clashes in which striker Anna LoPizzo was shot dead — a killing for which Ettor and Giovannitti were charged, though neither was present.

In February, with strikers' children suffering from hunger, the union arranged for hundreds of them to be sent to supporters' homes in New York and Vermont. When police violently prevented another group of children from boarding a train to Philadelphia on 24 February, beating mothers and children at the station, national outrage followed. A congressional investigation was held, at which young mill worker Carmela Teoli testified about being scalped by a mill machine; President William Howard Taft ordered a federal inquiry.

Outcome

Under mounting political pressure the mill owners settled on 12 March 1912, granting wage increases of 5 to 25 percent (largest for the lowest-paid), overtime premiums and a promise of no discrimination against strikers. Workers elsewhere in New England won raises by the strike's example — an estimated 275,000 textile workers benefited. Ettor and Giovannitti were acquitted in November 1912 after a celebrated trial in Salem.

The slogan "Bread and Roses" — associated with the strike through Rose Schneiderman's speeches and James Oppenheim's poem — came to symbolise the demand for both fair wages and dignified working conditions, and the strike remains a landmark of immigrant and women's labour organising.
