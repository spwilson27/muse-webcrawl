Dry lake

A dry lake, also known as a playa, is a basin or depression that formerly contained a standing surface water body, which has evaporated and left a flat bed of clay, silt, and salts. Dry lakes occur in arid and semi-arid regions in endorheic basins, where drainage has no outlet to the sea. When occasional rainfall collects in the basin, a shallow temporary lake may form and then evaporate, leaving the surface smooth and hard.

Formation

Playas form in closed basins in deserts such as the Basin and Range Province of the western United States, the Sahara, and central Australia. Dissolved minerals carried in by runoff precipitate as the water evaporates, creating evaporite deposits of halite, gypsum, and other salts. Repeated wetting and drying cracks the mud surface into polygonal plates.

Notable examples

Famous dry lakes include the Bonneville Salt Flats in Utah, Black Rock Desert in Nevada, Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia (the world's largest salt flat), Lake Eyre in Australia, and Racetrack Playa in Death Valley, known for its "sailing stones" that leave long tracks across the surface. The clay pan of the Makgadikgadi in Botswana is among the largest complexes of salt pans in the world.

Uses

Their exceptionally flat, hard surfaces make dry lakes valuable for land speed records (Bonneville and Black Rock), rocket and aircraft testing (Rogers Dry Lake at Edwards Air Force Base), and events such as the Burning Man festival, held on the Black Rock playa.
