Equalization (audio)

Equalization, or simply EQ, in audio processing is the process of adjusting the volume of different frequency bands within an audio signal. The circuit or equipment used to achieve this is called an equalizer. Most consumer audio equipment features simple bass and treble tone controls, while professional equipment and software offer multi-band graphic and parametric equalizers that allow detailed control of the frequency spectrum.

History

Equalization originated in telephony, where circuits were adjusted so that the response of long transmission lines was "equal" across the speech band. It became central to recording and broadcasting in the twentieth century, and was famously applied in phonograph recording standards such as the RIAA equalization curve, which boosts high frequencies and cuts low frequencies when recording, applying the inverse on playback.

Types of equalizer

  • Shelving controls boost or cut all frequencies above (treble shelf) or below (bass shelf) a corner frequency.
  • Graphic equalizers divide the spectrum into fixed bands, each with its own slider.
  • Parametric equalizers allow continuous adjustment of frequency, gain and bandwidth (Q) for each band, and are standard in mixing consoles and digital audio workstations.
  • Peaking filters create a bell-shaped boost or cut around a centre frequency.

Uses

EQ is used to correct deficiencies in recordings and playback spaces, to reduce feedback in live sound, to make instruments sit distinctly in a mix, and creatively to alter timbre. It is closely related to audio filters such as high-pass and low-pass filters.
