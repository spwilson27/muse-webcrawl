The IBM System/360 (S/360) is a family of mainframe computer systems announced by IBM on 7 April 1964 and delivered between 1965 and 1978. It was the first family of computers designed to cover a complete range of applications, small to large, both commercial and scientific, with a single architecture. The design made a clear distinction between architecture and implementation, allowing IBM to release a suite of compatible designs at different prices.

System/360 was an enormous commercial success and is often described as one of the riskiest and most consequential bets in business history: IBM invested some US$5 billion in the programme, more than the company's annual revenue at the time. The project was led by Fred Brooks, and chief architect Gene Amdahl shaped the design.

Architecture

The S/360 architecture standardised the 8-bit byte as the basic unit of addressing (popularising the byte itself), 32-bit words, hexadecimal floating-point arithmetic, and a set of sixteen 32-bit general-purpose registers. Models from the small Model 30 to the high-end Model 91 implemented the same instruction set, using microcode on lower-end machines. Customers could upgrade to a larger model without rewriting software — a revolutionary idea that created the concept of a compatible computer family and a large third-party plug-compatible industry.

Software

Operating systems included DOS/360 for smaller machines and OS/360 for larger ones. OS/360's development, notoriously delayed and over budget, became the subject of Fred Brooks's classic book The Mythical Man-Month (1975), which drew lessons about adding staff to late software projects. The system also popularised time-sharing variants such as CP/CMS, which pioneered virtual machines.

Legacy

The S/360 architecture's descendants — through System/370, System/390, and today's IBM Z — still run, and its instruction set remains binary compatible in large part more than half a century later. Peripheral standards such as the channel interface and the use of EBCDIC encoding also stem from the family. The name "360" referred to the full circle of applications the line was meant to cover.
