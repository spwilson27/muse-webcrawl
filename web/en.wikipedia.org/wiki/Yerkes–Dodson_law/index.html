Yerkes–Dodson law

The Yerkes–Dodson law is an empirical relationship between arousal and performance, originally described by psychologists Robert M. Yerkes and John Dillingham Dodson in 1908. It states that performance increases with physiological or mental arousal up to a point; when arousal becomes too high, performance declines. The relationship is commonly illustrated as an inverted U-shaped curve.

Original research

Yerkes and Dodson's 1908 paper, "The relation of strength of stimulus to rapidity of habit-formation", reported experiments in which mice learned tasks under varying strengths of electric shock. Learning was fastest at intermediate stimulus strengths, and the optimal level was lower for more difficult discriminations — an interaction between task difficulty and arousal that remains part of the law's modern formulation.

Modern interpretation

In modern psychology the law is used to explain phenomena in sports performance, test anxiety, and workplace productivity: moderate stress can sharpen attention, while excessive stress impairs working memory and complex reasoning. Simple or well-learned tasks tolerate — and may even benefit from — higher arousal than novel or complex ones. Critics note that "arousal" is difficult to measure as a single dimension and that the law's popularity in textbooks exceeds the strength of the original evidence, yet the inverted-U remains a widely used heuristic in performance psychology.
