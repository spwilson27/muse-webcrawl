Section 230

Section 230
Full titleSection 230 of the Communications Decency Act
Citation47 U.S.C. § 230
Enacted1996 (as part of the Telecommunications Act)
AuthorsChristopher Cox and Ron Wyden
Key clause"No provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider."

Section 230 is a section of Title 47 of the United States Code that generally provides immunity for online computer services with respect to third-party content generated by their users. Enacted as part of the Communications Decency Act of 1996, it has been described — notably in Jeff Kosseff's book The Twenty-Six Words That Created the Internet — as the legal foundation enabling the growth of social media, user-generated content, and much of the modern web.

Provisions

Section 230(c)(1) states that no provider or user of an "interactive computer service" shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of information provided by another content provider — immunizing platforms from liability for user posts in areas such as defamation. Section 230(c)(2), the "Good Samaritan" provision, protects services that voluntarily restrict access to material they consider obscene, harassing, or otherwise objectionable. The immunity does not extend to federal criminal law, intellectual property claims, or certain communications privacy laws. Congress amended the section with FOSTA-SESTA (2018), carving out claims related to sex trafficking.

Background

The provision, co-authored by Representatives Christopher Cox and Ron Wyden, responded to cases such as Stratton Oakmont, Inc. v. Prodigy Services Co. (1995), which held that a service that moderated content could be treated as a publisher of everything posted — creating a "moderator's dilemma." The Communications Decency Act's broader indecency provisions were struck down in Reno v. ACLU (1997), but Section 230 survived and was broadly construed by courts, beginning with Zeran v. AOL (1997).

Debate

Since the late 2010s, Section 230 has been a central target of internet policy debates in the United States. Critics from both parties have argued — for different reasons, from alleged anti-conservative bias to platforms' failure to remove harmful content — that the immunity should be conditioned or repealed, while defenders argue it is essential to free expression and competition online. The Supreme Court considered its scope in Gonzalez v. Google (2023) but resolved the case without ruling on Section 230.
