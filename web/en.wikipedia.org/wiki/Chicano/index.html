Chicano

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chicano (feminine: Chicana) is an ethnic identity for Mexican Americans, historically used by some to assert a distinct political and cultural identity rooted in indigenous and mestizo heritage. Once a pejorative term, it was reclaimed during the Chicano Movement (El Movimiento) of the 1960s and 1970s, when Mexican American activists adopted it as a symbol of pride, self-determination, and resistance to assimilation.

Etymology

"Chicano" derives from a shortened pronunciation of mexicano (Mexican) — meshicano → shicano — reflecting Nahuatl-influenced pronunciation of "x" as "sh". The term circulated in the early 20th century, often used disparagingly by more established Mexican Americans toward recent working-class immigrants, before its reclamation.

The Chicano Movement

The Chicano Movement paralleled the broader civil rights struggles of the 1960s. Key strands included the farmworkers' movement led by César Chávez and Dolores Huerta and the United Farm Workers; the land-grant movement of Reies López Tijerina in New Mexico; the urban youth and student movements, including the 1968 East Los Angeles school walkouts (the "Blowouts"); and political organising through La Raza Unida Party. Cultural expression flourished in theatre (Luis Valdez's El Teatro Campesino), poetry, and the mural art movement that transformed barrio walls across the Southwest.

Identity and usage

Chicano identity emphasises mestizaje — the mixing of Indigenous and Spanish heritage — and often invokes Aztlán, the mythic Aztec homeland placed in the US Southwest. Usage of the term varies by generation and region; survey data show many Mexican Americans prefer "Hispanic", "Latino", or simply "Mexican American", while "Chicano" retains strongest currency in activist, academic, and artistic contexts. Chicano studies programmes, established from 1969 onward following the Plan de Santa Bárbara, institutionalised the field in American universities.
