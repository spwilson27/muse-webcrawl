The 1950s quiz show scandals were a series of revelations that contestants on several popular American television quiz shows during the 1950s had been secretly given assistance by the shows' producers, who rigged the outcomes to produce more dramatic and popular television. The scandals broke publicly in 1958 and culminated in congressional hearings in 1959 and amendments to federal communications law in 1960 that made rigging a quiz show a federal crime.

Background

Big-money quiz shows such as The $64,000 Question (CBS) and Twenty-One (NBC) were among the most-watched programs of early television. Producers discovered that ratings rose when sympathetic contestants kept winning, and sponsors pressured producers to control outcomes. Contestants were coached, given questions in advance, and sometimes instructed to lose at dramatically opportune moments.

The Twenty-One scandal

The most famous case involved Twenty-One, produced by Jack Barry and Dan Enright. Contestant Herb Stempel, who had been fed answers, was instructed to lose to Charles Van Doren, a Columbia University instructor from a prominent literary family, whose telegenic winning streak made him a national celebrity. After being denied promised compensation, Stempel went to the press and to the Manhattan district attorney. A grand jury investigation followed in 1958, and its findings—initially sealed—were opened during House subcommittee hearings chaired after investigations led by Representative Oren Harris's Legislative Oversight Subcommittee in 1959.

Hearings and aftermath

Van Doren initially denied wrongdoing but confessed before Congress on November 2, 1959, admitting he had been given answers in advance. He resigned from Columbia and from NBC. Other shows, including Dotto—cancelled in 1958 after a standby contestant found a notebook of answers—and The $64,000 Question, were implicated. In 1960 Congress amended the Communications Act of 1934 to prohibit the prearrangement of quiz show outcomes, and networks took direct control of programming away from sponsors, ending the era of single-sponsor production.

Legacy

The scandals became a touchstone for television ethics and are dramatized in the 1994 film Quiz Show. They are frequently cited as an early example of manufactured reality in broadcasting.
