Laplace's equation

Laplace's equation is a second-order partial differential equation named after Pierre-Simon Laplace, who first studied its properties. It is usually written as

∇²f = 0,   equivalently   Δf = 0

where Δ is the Laplace operator, ∇² is the divergence of the gradient, and f is a twice-differentiable real-valued function. The Laplace operator thereby gives a second-order PDE. Functions that satisfy Laplace's equation are called harmonic functions. Laplace's equation is a special case of Poisson's equation, and appears throughout physics — in electrostatics, gravitation, fluid dynamics and heat conduction — wherever a potential field in a source-free region is described.

Forms in different coordinate systems

In three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates the equation reads

∂²f/∂x² + ∂²f/∂y² + ∂²f/∂z² = 0.

In cylindrical coordinates (r, θ, z) and spherical coordinates (r, θ, φ) the Laplacian takes different forms involving first-derivative terms, and separation of variables in these systems generates the classical special functions of mathematical physics: Bessel functions in cylindrical geometry and spherical harmonics in spherical geometry. Laplace himself derived the equation in work on gravitational attraction and celestial mechanics in the 1780s.

Boundary-value problems

For physical applications the equation must be supplemented with boundary conditions. In a Dirichlet problem, the value of f is specified on the boundary of the domain; in a Neumann problem, its normal derivative is specified. Under mild conditions (uniqueness theorems), these problems have at most one solution: two harmonic functions agreeing on the boundary (or with equal normal derivatives up to a constant) coincide throughout the domain. This uniqueness underlies the method of images in electrostatics.

Properties of solutions

Harmonic functions are analytic, satisfy the mean value property (the value at a point equals the average over any sphere centred at that point), and obey a maximum principle: a non-constant harmonic function attains its maximum and minimum only on the boundary of its domain. These properties imply that Laplace's equation describes equilibrium states — steady temperatures, electrostatic potentials in empty space, and irrotational incompressible flow potentials.

Applications

  • Electrostatics: the electric potential in a charge-free region satisfies Laplace's equation.
  • Gravitation: the Newtonian gravitational potential outside masses does likewise.
  • Fluid dynamics: the velocity potential of steady, incompressible, irrotational flow is harmonic.
  • Heat conduction: the steady-state temperature distribution satisfies Laplace's equation.
  • Complex analysis: the real and imaginary parts of a holomorphic function are harmonic, via the Cauchy–Riemann equations.
