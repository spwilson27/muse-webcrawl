Voir dire (from Old French, "to speak the truth") is a preliminary examination process in jury trials in which the judge and attorneys question prospective jurors—drawn from the venire—to determine their competence, impartiality, and suitability to serve. It is also used, in a second sense, for a preliminary hearing on the admissibility of evidence or the competence of a witness, held outside the jury's presence.

Jury selection

During voir dire, questioning explores jurors' backgrounds, experiences, relationships to the parties, exposure to pretrial publicity, and attitudes toward the issues in the case—for instance, views on the death penalty in capital trials. Based on the answers, the parties may ask the court to excuse jurors through a challenge for cause, which requires showing the juror cannot be fair, or may exercise a limited number of peremptory challenges, which historically needed no reason. The Supreme Court's decisions in Batson v. Kentucky (1986) and its progeny prohibit peremptory strikes motivated by race, and later by sex.

Conduct

Depending on the jurisdiction, voir dire may be conducted primarily by the judge (as in most federal courts), primarily by the attorneys (as in many state courts, notably Texas), or by both. Questionnaires are sometimes used in complex or high-profile cases. Effective voir dire is regarded by trial lawyers as among the most consequential phases of a case, because the selected jurors will weigh the evidence and may ultimately need guidance such as an Allen charge if they deadlock.

Other uses

In Commonwealth practice, a voir dire can be a "trial within a trial" to decide the admissibility of a confession or other contested evidence—a usage that survives in Canada, Australia, and elsewhere, while jury-selection questioning in those countries is far more limited than in the United States.
