The recorded history of Morocco begins with Phoenician colonisation of the coast in the first millennium BCE and continues through Roman, Vandal, and Byzantine rule in ancient Mauretania. Since the Arab conquests of the seventh century CE, Morocco has been governed by a succession of Islamic dynasties, several of which ruled empires stretching into Iberia and West Africa. Remarkably, Morocco preserved its independence into the modern era longer than most of its neighbours, becoming a French and Spanish protectorate only in 1912, and regaining independence in 1956.

Pre-Islamic period

The indigenous Amazigh (Berber) peoples inhabited the region from prehistory. Phoenician trading posts such as Lixus and Mogador were founded along the coast, later falling under Carthage. After the Punic Wars, the Berber kingdom of Mauretania became a Roman client and then, under Claudius, a Roman province. Christianity spread in late antiquity; Vandals crossed from Spain in the fifth century and the Byzantines later held coastal enclaves.

The Islamic dynasties

Arab armies under Uqba ibn Nafi reached Morocco around 682, and Islam spread among the Berbers. In 788 Idris I founded the Idrisid dynasty, the first Moroccan Islamic state; his son founded Fes, which became a great centre of learning with the University of al-Qarawiyyin (859).

The Almoravids, Sanhaja Berbers from the Sahara, built an empire in the eleventh century that included al-Andalus and founded Marrakesh (c. 1070). They were overthrown by the Almohads, whose caliphate was the largest Moroccan empire, spanning North Africa and Iberia. The Marinids (thirteenth–fifteenth centuries) left much celebrated madrasa architecture. After a period of fragmentation, the Saadians reunified the country, repelled the Portuguese at the Battle of the Three Kings (1578), and under Ahmad al-Mansur conquered the Songhai Empire's Niger bend in 1591.

Alaouites and the protectorate

The Alaouite dynasty, sharifs claiming descent from the Prophet, came to power in the 1660s under Moulay Rashid; Moulay Ismail ruled for over half a century and built Meknes into an imperial capital. Morocco remained independent as European powers expanded, though it lost territory and influence in the nineteenth century. The Treaty of Fes (1912) made most of the country a French protectorate under Resident-General Lyautey, while Spain held the north and far south.

Modern Morocco

Nationalist pressure and the exile of Sultan Mohammed V to Madagascar (1953) hastened the end of the protectorate; Morocco regained independence in March 1956. Hassan II ruled from 1961 to 1999, a period including the "Years of Lead" and the Green March of 1975, which asserted Morocco's claim to Western Sahara — a disputed territory that remains the country's central foreign-policy question. Mohammed VI, king since 1999, has overseen gradual economic liberalisation and a new constitution in 2011 following the Arab Spring protests.
