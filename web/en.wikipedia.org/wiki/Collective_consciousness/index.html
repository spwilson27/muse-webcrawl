Collective consciousness

Collective consciousness
French: conscience collective
Coined by: Émile Durkheim (1893)
Field: Sociology

Collective consciousness (French: conscience collective) is a concept developed by the French sociologist Émile Durkheim to describe the set of shared beliefs, ideas, attitudes, and moral attitudes which operate as a unifying force within a society. Durkheim introduced the term in The Division of Labour in Society (1893).

Durkheim's account

For Durkheim, collective consciousness is external to individuals yet coercive upon them: it is the source of law, religion, and morality, and exists as a reality sui generis distinct from individual minds. In societies held together by mechanical solidarity, the collective consciousness is strong and detailed, covering most of life; in modern societies based on organic solidarity and the division of labour, it becomes weaker and more abstract, leaving more room for individual autonomy.

Related concepts

The idea is related to, but distinct from, collective representations, which are the symbols and concepts through which a society thinks about itself, and from later notions such as the collective unconscious of Carl Jung and modern work on shared intentionality and group cognition.

Criticism and influence

Critics have questioned how a collective mind can exist apart from individuals and whether the concept reifies society. Nevertheless, the term influenced functionalist sociology, anthropology, and cultural studies, and remains a standard part of sociological vocabulary.
