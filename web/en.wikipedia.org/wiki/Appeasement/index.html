Appeasement

Appeasement, in an international context, is a diplomatic negotiation policy of making political, material, or territorial concessions to an aggressive power with intention to avoid conflict.

Origins and usage

The term is most often applied to the foreign policy of the British governments of Prime Ministers Ramsay MacDonald, Stanley Baldwin and Neville Chamberlain towards Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy between 1935 and 1939. Under British pressure, appeasement of Nazism and Fascism also played a role in French foreign policy of the period but was always much less popular there than in the United Kingdom.

Munich Agreement

In September 1938, Chamberlain, French Premier Édouard Daladier, Hitler and Mussolini signed the Munich Agreement, permitting German annexation of the Sudetenland in Czechoslovakia. Chamberlain returned to London proclaiming "peace for our time". The policy is now widely regarded as a failure: Hitler invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, precipitating World War II.

Debate

Early explanations were that Britain was too weak militarily, traumatised by World War I, and fearful of communism. Later historians debated whether appeasement bought time for rearmament or emboldened aggression. Since the Second World War the term became derogatory, used to argue against concessions to perceived aggressors, influencing debates from the Korean War to the Cold War and beyond.
