Paper wasp

Paper wasps are vespid wasps of the subfamily Polistinae that gather fibres from dead wood and plant stems, mix them with saliva, and use the resulting papery material to construct water-resistant nests. The group contains more than 1,100 described species worldwide, including the familiar genera Polistes, Mischocyttarus, and Ropalidia. Paper wasps are generally less aggressive than yellowjackets or hornets, stinging mainly in defence of their nests.

Biology and behaviour

Paper wasp colonies are founded in spring by one or more inseminated queens (foundresses). Typical nests consist of a single open comb of hexagonal cells hanging by a stalk (petiole), often under eaves or branches; some tropical species build enclosed nests. Adults feed on nectar and other sweet liquids, while larvae are fed chewed-up insects, especially caterpillars, making paper wasps valued predators in gardens and agriculture. Colony hierarchies are maintained through dominance interactions and, in species such as Polistes fuscatus, individual facial recognition.

Species and distribution

Well-known species include the European paper wasp (Polistes dominula), which has spread widely in North America, the red paper wasp (Polistes carolina), and the golden paper wasp (Polistes aurifer). Paper wasps occur on every continent except Antarctica, with the greatest diversity in the tropics.

Sting and human interaction

Paper wasp venom can cause painful stings and, in sensitized individuals, allergic reactions. Because nests are often built near human habitation, wasps may be removed, but entomologists note their ecological benefits as pollinators and predators of crop pests.
