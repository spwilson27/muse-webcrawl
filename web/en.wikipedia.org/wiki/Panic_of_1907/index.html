The Panic of 1907 — also known as the 1907 Bankers' Panic or Knickerbocker Crisis — was a financial crisis that took place in the United States over a three-week period starting in mid-October 1907. The panic occurred during a recession, when a failed attempt to corner the market in United Copper stock led to runs on banks and trust companies associated with the speculators.

Course of the crisis

On 22 October 1907 the Knickerbocker Trust Company, New York's third-largest trust, suspended payments, triggering runs across the city's trusts and banks. With no central bank to act as lender of last resort, the financier J. P. Morgan organised rescues from his library, pledging his own money, locking bankers in until they agreed to support the trust companies, and persuading the US Treasury to deposit government funds. Call money rates spiked to 100 percent and the New York Stock Exchange nearly failed to open on 24 October.

Aftermath

Industrial production fell sharply in the ensuing recession. The crisis convinced Congress of the need for banking reform, leading to the Aldrich–Vreeland Act of 1908, the National Monetary Commission, and ultimately the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, which created the Federal Reserve System.
