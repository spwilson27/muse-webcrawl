Cosmochemistry

Cosmochemistry (from the Greek kosmos, "universe", and chemeia, "chemistry") is the study of the chemical composition of matter in the universe and of the processes that produced it. It is closely related to astrochemistry and to nuclear astrophysics, and it underpins much of what is known about the origin of the chemical elements and the formation of the Solar System.

Scope

Cosmochemists analyse extraterrestrial materials — meteorites, cosmic dust, lunar and Martian samples returned by missions, and comet material — together with remote spectroscopic observations of stars, interstellar clouds and planetary atmospheres. Key questions include the abundances of the elements in the Sun and stars, stellar nucleosynthesis, the isotopic composition of presolar grains, and the chronology of the early Solar System.

Methods and findings

Mass spectrometry, especially isotope-ratio measurements, is central to the field. Tiny variations in isotopes of elements such as oxygen, titanium, chromium and molybdenum distinguish meteorite parent bodies and trace their formation regions. Radiometric dating of calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions in chondrites gives the age of the Solar System as about 4.567 billion years. Presolar grains in primitive meteorites carry isotopic signatures of individual stars that existed before the Sun. The discovery of amino acids and other organic compounds in carbonaceous chondrites such as the Murchison meteorite links cosmochemistry to the study of the origin of life.
