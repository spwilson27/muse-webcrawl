The Kuleshov effect is a mental phenomenon by which viewers derive more meaning from the interaction of two sequential shots than from a single shot in isolation. Demonstrated by the Soviet filmmaker Lev Kuleshov in the early 1920s, the effect became a cornerstone of Soviet montage theory and of film editing generally.

The experiment

As described by Kuleshov's student Vsevolod Pudovkin, the same close-up of the actor Ivan Mozzhukhin, wearing a neutral expression, was intercut with three different images: a bowl of soup, a dead child in a coffin, and a woman reclining on a divan. Audiences reportedly praised Mozzhukhin's acting — his "hunger", "grief", and "desire" — although the face was identical in each case. Kuleshov concluded that meaning in cinema arises from the juxtaposition of shots (montage) rather than from any single image, and that editing could create entirely new ideas, places, and emotions.

Related experiments

Kuleshov's workshop produced related demonstrations: "creative geography", in which shots filmed in different locations were combined into an apparently continuous space, and synthetic bodies assembled from shots of different people. The original Mozzhukhin footage has not survived, and scholars debate the details of the account, but the effect has been replicated in various forms in psychology and neuroscience.

Influence

The Kuleshov effect underlies much of film practice, from Hitchcock's celebrated explanations of "pure cinema" — contrasting a smiling man with a mother and child versus a woman in a bikini — to modern editing for reaction shots. It is also discussed in cognitive film theory as evidence that viewers actively construct narrative and emotion.
