Effects of climate change on oceans

The oceans absorb most of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases

The effects of climate change on oceans include rising ocean temperatures, sea level rise, ocean acidification, deoxygenation, changes in circulation, and intensifying impacts on marine ecosystems and coastal communities. The oceans have absorbed more than 90 percent of the excess heat in the climate system, buffering land warming but transforming marine environments.

Warming and heat content

Ocean heat content has risen steadily since the mid-20th century, with the rate of warming accelerating in recent decades. Warmer waters fuel coral bleaching, drive marine species poleward, increase stratification that blocks nutrient mixing, and contribute to more intense tropical cyclones and marine heatwaves.

Sea level rise

Sea level rises because seawater expands as it warms and because melting glaciers and ice sheets add water. Global mean sea level rose about 20 cm between 1901 and 2018, and the rate has accelerated to roughly 3.7 mm per year in recent decades, threatening low-lying coasts, deltas and island nations with flooding, erosion and saltwater intrusion.

Acidification and deoxygenation

About a quarter to a third of human-caused carbon dioxide emissions dissolve in seawater, forming carbonic acid. Surface ocean acidity has increased roughly 30 percent since the industrial era, reducing carbonate availability for corals, shellfish and plankton. At the same time, warmer, more stratified oceans hold and circulate less oxygen; dissolved oxygen has declined by about 2 percent since 1960, expanding oxygen minimum zones.

Ecosystems and fisheries

Shifting temperatures redistribute fish stocks, stress kelp forests and seagrass meadows, and compound pressures from overfishing and pollution. IPCC assessments project declining global marine biomass and fishery yields this century, with the severest impacts in tropical regions least responsible for emissions.
