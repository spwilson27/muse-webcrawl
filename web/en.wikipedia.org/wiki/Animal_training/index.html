Animal training

Animal training is the act of teaching animals specific responses to specific conditions or stimuli. Training may be undertaken for companionship, detection, protection, entertainment, assistance to people with disabilities, or husbandry and medical care in zoos and laboratories. Most modern professional training rests on the principles of operant conditioning described by B. F. Skinner and applied systematically by behaviourists such as Keller and Marian Breland.

Methods

Trainers use positive reinforcement (adding a reward such as food or praise), negative reinforcement (removing something aversive), and, controversially, positive punishment. Marker-based training—such as clicker training, popularised by Karen Pryor from her work with dolphins—uses a distinct sound to mark the exact behaviour being rewarded, followed by a reinforcer. Complex behaviour sequences are built by shaping successive approximations, and by chaining individual behaviours together.

Applications

Working animals include guide dogs and other assistance dogs, police and military dogs, search-and-rescue dogs, detection dogs for explosives, drugs, and disease, and animals in film and television. In zoos, "cooperative care" training teaches animals to participate voluntarily in weighing, blood sampling, and other veterinary procedures, reducing the need for anaesthesia.

Welfare considerations

Regulation and professional standards vary widely. Animal welfare science has increasingly favoured reinforcement-based methods over aversive tools, and many countries restrict or ban certain devices and the use of wild animals in circuses.
