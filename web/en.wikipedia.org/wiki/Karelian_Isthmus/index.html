The Karelian Isthmus (Finnish: Karjalankannas; Russian: Карельский перешеек) is the stretch of land, roughly 45 to 110 kilometres wide, lying between the Gulf of Finland to the west and Lake Ladoga to the east. It is traversed by the Vuoksi River system and dotted with numerous lakes. The historic city of Vyborg stands at its western end, and Saint Petersburg lies just to its south.

History

The isthmus was contested for centuries between Sweden, Novgorod and later Russia. Under the Treaty of Stolbovo (1617) it passed to Sweden; Russia regained it during the Great Northern War, and after 1809 the western part was attached to the Grand Duchy of Finland within the Russian Empire. When Finland became independent in 1917, most of the isthmus was Finnish territory, and its proximity to Leningrad (Saint Petersburg) made it strategically sensitive.

The Soviet Union demanded the isthmus in 1939 to push the border away from Leningrad. Finland's refusal helped precipitate the Winter War (1939–40), after which the Moscow Peace Treaty ceded the isthmus to the USSR and its roughly 400,000 Finnish inhabitants were evacuated. Finland temporarily retook the area during the Continuation War (1941–44), but the armistice of 1944 confirmed the cession, and the territory was resettled by Soviet citizens.

Geography and present day

Today the isthmus lies entirely within Russia's Leningrad Oblast. It is a popular recreation area for residents of Saint Petersburg, with forests, lakes and dacha settlements. The Mannerheim Line, Finland's interwar defensive line, crossed the isthmus, and its remains are still visible.
