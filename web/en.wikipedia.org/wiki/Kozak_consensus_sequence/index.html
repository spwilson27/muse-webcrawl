Kozak consensus sequence

The Kozak consensus sequence (or Kozak motif) is a short nucleotide sequence surrounding the start codon of eukaryotic messenger RNA that is important for the initiation of translation. It is named after the American biochemist Marilyn Kozak, who characterised it in the 1980s by compiling and experimentally testing the sequences flanking the initiation sites of hundreds of vertebrate genes.

The sequence

For vertebrate mRNAs the consensus is commonly written:

(gcc)gccRccATGG

where ATG is the start codon (AUG in the mRNA), R denotes a purine (A or G) three bases upstream of the codon — the single most influential position — and a G immediately after the start codon (the +4 position) is the next most important. The leading (gcc) segment is of uncertain significance and is sometimes omitted, giving the shorter form gccRccATGG.

Mechanism and significance

During translation initiation in eukaryotes, the small ribosomal subunit, loaded with initiation factors and the initiator tRNA, binds the 5′ cap of the mRNA and scans along it until it recognises the start codon. A good Kozak context makes the ribosome stop and initiate efficiently at that AUG; a poor context can cause "leaky scanning", in which the ribosome skips the first AUG and starts at a later one — a mechanism some genes exploit to produce protein variants. Mutations that weaken or strengthen the Kozak sequence of a gene can alter protein output and are occasionally the cause of inherited disease.

In molecular cloning, the sequence is routinely engineered into expression constructs to maximise production of a desired protein in eukaryotic cells.
