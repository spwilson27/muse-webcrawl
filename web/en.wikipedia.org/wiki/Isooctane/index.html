Isooctane, more precisely 2,2,4-trimethylpentane, is an isomer of octane (C8H18) used as the reference standard for the octane rating scale. By definition, pure isooctane is assigned an octane number of 100, while normal heptane (n-heptane) is assigned 0; the octane rating of a petrol sample is then the percentage of isooctane in a reference blend of isooctane and n-heptane with equivalent knock resistance.

Properties and production

The highly branched structure of isooctane resists premature ignition ("knocking") in spark-ignition engines, which is why it defines the top of the scale. Isooctane is produced industrially by the alkylation of isobutene with isobutane, catalysed by sulfuric or hydrofluoric acid; related "alkylate" streams are major high-octane blending components in modern petrol.

Terminology

The name "isooctane" strictly applies to the 2,2,4-trimethylpentane isomer, though in older or informal usage the term was sometimes applied to octane isomers in general. It should not be confused with 2,2,3-trimethylpentane, which is occasionally called "neo-octane".
