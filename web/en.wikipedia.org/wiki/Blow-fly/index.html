Blow-flies (family Calliphoridae) are a family of flies commonly known as blow flies, carrion flies, bluebottles, greenbottles, or cluster flies. Many species have metallic blue, green or black colouring and are among the first insects to arrive at a corpse, making them of central importance in forensic entomology. The name derives from the old habit of meat "blown" by flies being infested with their eggs and maggots.

Biology

Adult calliphorids feed on nectar and other sugary substances and are important pollinators of some crops, including mango and onion. Females lay batches of eggs on carrion, dung or wounds; the larvae (maggots) feed on decaying tissue. Familiar genera include Calliphora (bluebottles), Lucilia (greenbottles), Phormia and Chrysomya. Some species, such as the New World screw-worm Cochliomyia hominivorax, are obligate parasites of living mammals and serious livestock pests; others cause facultative myiasis in sheep ("blowfly strike"), a major economic problem in Australia and New Zealand.

Forensic importance

Because blow-flies colonise a body within minutes to hours of death, the developmental stage of their larvae allows forensic entomologists to estimate the minimum post-mortem interval. Species succession on carrion also gives information about the circumstances of death and whether a body has been moved.

Medical use

Sterile larvae of Lucilia sericata are used in maggot therapy to clean necrotic wounds, since they consume dead tissue while sparing healthy flesh.
