Twelfth Amendment to the United States Constitution

[Image: The enrolled Twelfth Amendment document in the National Archives]

The Twelfth Amendment (Amendment XII) to the United States Constitution, adopted in 1804, refined the procedure by which the Electoral College elects the president and vice president. It replaced the original system in Article II, under which electors cast two undifferentiated votes and the runner-up became vice president.

Background

The original procedure broke down with the rise of parties. In 1796 it produced a president and vice president from opposing parties (John Adams and Thomas Jefferson). In 1800, Democratic-Republican electors voting uniformly for Jefferson and Aaron Burr produced a tie, throwing the election into the House of Representatives, which deadlocked through dozens of ballots before choosing Jefferson — a crisis that spurred reform.

Provisions

The Amendment requires electors to cast distinct ballots for president and for vice president. A majority of electoral votes is needed for each office. If no presidential candidate has a majority, the House chooses from the top three, voting by state delegations, each state having one vote; if no vice-presidential candidate has a majority, the Senate chooses from the top two. The Amendment also provides that if the House has not chosen a president by inauguration day, the vice-president-elect acts as president — a provision later modified by the Twentieth Amendment — and confirms that no person ineligible to the presidency is eligible to the vice presidency.

Use

The House contingent procedure has been used once under the Amendment, in 1824, when it chose John Quincy Adams over Andrew Jackson despite Jackson's plurality of electoral and popular votes; the Senate chose the vice president in 1836 after Virginia's electors refused to support Richard Mentor Johnson (a case involving faithless electors). The Amendment remains the framework for every modern presidential election.
