Horology (from Latin hora, "hour", and Greek λόγος, "study") is the science of measuring time and the art of making instruments that measure and display it—clocks, watches, chronometers and their components. Horology spans astronomy, mechanics, materials science and craftsmanship, and its practitioners are called horologists.

History

Timekeeping began with sundials and water clocks in ancient Egypt, Babylon, Greece and China. The first mechanical clocks, driven by weights and regulated by the verge-and-foliot escapement, appeared in Europe in the late 13th century and were housed in towers and monasteries. The pendulum, applied by Christiaan Huygens in 1656 following Galileo's observations, improved accuracy a hundredfold; Huygens's balance spring (1675) did the same for portable watches. The 18th century saw the marine chronometers of John Harrison solve the longitude problem. Industrialisation brought mass-produced clocks and watches, notably in Switzerland, the United States and later Japan. In the 20th century, electric, quartz (1969) and atomic clocks succeeded one another, and timekeeping migrated into networks and satellites, though mechanical watchmaking survived as a luxury craft.

Components of mechanical timekeepers

A mechanical clock or watch combines a power source (weight or mainspring), a gear train (the going train), an escapement that parcels out energy in equal beats, an oscillator (pendulum or balance wheel) that defines the unit of time, and a display. Added complications—calendar work, repeaters, chronographs, tourbillons—distinguish haute horlogerie. Watch movements are measured in lines and described by their jewel count, frequency (commonly 21,600 or 28,800 vibrations per hour) and finishing.

Institutions and preservation

Horology is served by museums (such as the British Museum's clocks and watches collection, the Musée international d'horlogerie at La Chaux-de-Fonds and the Patek Philippe Museum), societies and institutes for training and repair, and a scholarly literature on historic instruments. Conservation of tower clocks, automata and early scientific instruments is a recognised specialism, and traditional watchmaking skills have been inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity (craftsmanship of mechanical watchmaking and art mechanics, 2020, Switzerland and France).
