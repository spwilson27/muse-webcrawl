Dijon mustard

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A stoneware pot of pale, sharp Dijon mustard with a wooden spoon]
A stoneware pot of pale, sharp Dijon mustard with a wooden spoon

Dijon mustard is a traditional mustard of Dijon, the capital of Burgundy, first used in the table service of King Philip VI in 1336 and given its characteristic recipe in 1752, when Jean Naigeon substituted Verjuice (the juice of unripe grapes) for vinegar. Made from brown or black mustard seeds, white wine or verjuice and salt, it is pale yellow, smooth and sharply pungent. Although named for the city, most 'Dijon mustard' today is produced elsewhere; the term denotes a style, not a protected origin.

History and production

Medieval Dijon was France's mustard capital under ducal patronage; its mustard-makers' guild guarded recipes and quality. Naigeon's verjuice innovation made the smoother, winey style famous. Twentieth-century consolidation moved production: the celebrated Grey Poupon brand (founded Dijon 1866) is now made in the United States, and Canada supplies most of the world's mustard seed. A 2022 seed shortage, caused by Canadian drought and the Ukraine war, briefly emptied French supermarket shelves and became a national talking point.

Use

Dijon mustard is a kitchen staple — in vinaigrettes (where it emulsifies oil and vinegar), sauces, glazes, sandwiches and the classic accompaniment to charcuterie and steak — valued for clean heat that does not linger like chilli.
