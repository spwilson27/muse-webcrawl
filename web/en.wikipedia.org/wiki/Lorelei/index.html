Lorelei

The Lorelei (also spelled Loreley) is a 132-metre-high slate rock on the right bank of the River Rhine near Sankt Goarshausen in Rhineland-Palatinate, Germany. It marks the narrowest and one of the most dangerous stretches of the Middle Rhine, long associated with shipwrecks, a famous echo, and a legend of a siren-like maiden whose song lured boatmen to their deaths.

Geography and hazard

At the Lorelei the Rhine narrows to about 113 metres and reaches depths of some 25 metres; strong currents and submerged reefs made the passage perilous for shipping. The rock's name may derive from the Old German lureln, "to murmur", and ley, "rock", referring to the murmuring of the rapids and the rock's celebrated echo.

The legend

The tale of the beautiful Lorelei was popularised by Clemens Brentano's ballad in his novel Godwi (1801), in which a betrayed maiden throws herself into the Rhine and becomes a siren. Heinrich Heine's poem "Die Lore-Ley" (1824), beginning "Ich weiß nicht, was soll es bedeuten" ("I know not what it means"), was set to music by Friedrich Silcher in 1837 and became one of the best-known German folk songs. In the legend, the Lorelei sits atop the rock combing her golden hair and singing, distracting sailors who perish on the reefs below.

Today

The Lorelei forms part of the UNESCO World Heritage Upper Middle Rhine Valley. A bronze statue of the maiden stands on the Lorelei plateau, and an open-air stage built in the 1930s hosts concerts. Despite modern navigation aids the stretch remains hazardous; a tanker capsized near the rock in 2011.
