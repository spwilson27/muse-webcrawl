Citadel of Namur

The Citadel of Namur (French: Citadelle de Namur) is a fortress in the Walloon capital city of Namur, at the confluence of the Sambre and Meuse rivers in Belgium. It is originally from the Roman era, and has been rebuilt several times. Its current form was designed by the Dutch military engineer Menno van Coehoorn and improved by Vauban after the capture of the city by the French in 1692. Together with Dinant, Huy and Liège it forms the "Citadels of the Meuse".

History

The rocky spur above the confluence has been fortified since Roman times and was a stronghold of the counts of Namur in the Middle Ages. The citadel was repeatedly besieged because of its strategic position controlling the Meuse valley: it was taken by Louis XIV's armies in 1692 and retaken by William III in 1695. Vauban rebuilt its defences, and its extensive network of underground galleries — some 7 km of tunnels — earned it the nickname "the termite mound of Europe" from Napoleon.

The fortress saw further action in both World Wars, when its outlying forts formed part of the fortified position of Namur. The military finally left the site in the late 20th century, and the citadel is now a tourist attraction with museums, guided tours of the underground galleries, and a cable car linking it to the city centre.
