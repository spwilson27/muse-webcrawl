Lieutenant Governor of Texas

Lieutenant Governor of Texas
Term length: Four years, no term limits
Formation: 1845
First holder: Albert Clinton Horton
Succession: First in line to the governorship

The Lieutenant Governor of Texas is the second-highest executive office in the government of Texas and is widely considered the most powerful office in the state. Unlike in most states, the Texas lieutenant governor exercises extensive legislative power as the presiding officer of the Texas Senate, and is elected separately from the governor, potentially from a different party.

Powers

As president of the Senate, the lieutenant governor appoints committee chairs and members, assigns bills to committees, and controls the flow of legislation. The officeholder also co-chairs the Legislative Budget Board, which prepares the state budget, and serves on boards dealing with redistricting and state finance. In the executive branch the lieutenant governor succeeds to the governorship if that office becomes vacant, and becomes acting governor whenever the governor is out of the state.

History

The office was created by the Constitution of 1845 when Texas entered the Union; Albert Clinton Horton was the first holder. Because of the office's legislative dominance, long-serving lieutenant governors such as Bob Bullock (1991–1999) have been central figures in Texas policymaking, often more influential than the governor. The term was extended from two to four years in the 1970s.
