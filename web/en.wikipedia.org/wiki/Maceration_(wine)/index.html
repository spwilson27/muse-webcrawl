Maceration (wine)

Maceration is the winemaking process where the phenolic materials of the grape — tannins, colouring agents (anthocyanins) and flavour compounds — are leached from the grape skins, seeds and stems into the must. To macerate is to soften by soaking, and maceration is the process by which the red wine receives its red colour, since raw grape juice (excluding teinturiers) is clear-greyish in colour. In the production of white wines, maceration is either avoided or allowed only in a limited manner.

Process

During fermentation, the skins float to form a "cap" that must be mixed back into the juice by punching down, pumping over or other means, since extraction occurs at the surface. Extraction increases with temperature, skin contact time and alcohol level; alcohol is a solvent for phenolics, so extraction continues more readily late in fermentation.

Variations

  • Cold soak (pre-fermentation maceration): crushed grapes are held at low temperature before fermentation to extract water-soluble colour and fruit flavours without extracting as much tannin.
  • Extended maceration: skins remain in contact with the wine for days or weeks after fermentation, softening and polymerising tannins; widely used for structured reds such as Barolo and Bordeaux blends.
  • Carbonic maceration: whole, uncrushed grapes ferment in a carbon-dioxide-rich environment, so fermentation begins inside each berry. It produces soft, intensely fruity wines low in tannin, famously used for Beaujolais Nouveau.
  • Skin-contact white wines: extended maceration of white grapes produces "orange wines", a traditional style in Georgia and Friuli.

Effects

Maceration determines a wine's colour depth, tannin structure, ageing potential and much of its flavour profile. Excessive maceration can extract harsh seed tannins and bitterness, so winemakers taste regularly and press when balance is reached.
