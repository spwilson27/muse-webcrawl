The small Indian mongoose (Urva auropunctata) is a mongoose species native to Iraq and northern India, and widely introduced to many Caribbean and Pacific islands, where it has become a damaging invasive species. It was formerly classified together with the Javan mongoose under the name Herpestes javanicus, but the two are now considered separate species, and both have been moved to the genus Urva.

Description

The small Indian mongoose is a slender carnivore with a head-and-body length of roughly 50–67 cm including the tail, short legs, and a grizzled brown to golden-flecked coat — the species name auropunctata refers to the golden speckling of its fur. Males are larger than females and have broader heads. It is an opportunistic feeder, eating rodents, birds, reptiles, frogs, crabs, insects and fruit.

Introduction and impact

Beginning in the 1870s, small Indian mongooses were deliberately released on sugar-cane plantations in Jamaica, Hawaii, Fiji, Okinawa and numerous other islands to control rats and snakes. The introductions largely failed at their purpose — rats are mainly nocturnal while mongooses are active by day — and instead devastated native wildlife: ground-nesting birds, reptiles and amphibians, many already reduced to small island populations, declined or became extinct. The species is listed among the world's worst invasive alien species, and its import is prohibited in many countries.
