Paolo Caliari (1528 – 19 April 1588), known as Paolo Veronese (US: /ˌvɛrəˈneɪzeɪ, -zi/, Italian: [ˈpaːolo veroˈneːze, -eːse]), was an Italian Renaissance painter based in Venice, known for extremely large history paintings of religion and mythology, such as The Wedding at Cana (1563) and The Feast in the House of Levi (1573). In his day Veronese, Titian and Tintoretto constituted the great trio of Venetian late-Renaissance painting.

Career

Born in Verona—the source of his nickname—he trained locally before moving to Venice in the 1550s. There he painted ceiling cycles at the Villa Barbaro at Maser, frescoes and canvases for the Doge's Palace, the church of San Sebastiano (his masterpiece of the early period), and the Marciana Library. His works are characterised by luminous colour, silvery light, sumptuous contemporary costume and elaborate classical architecture framing crowds of figures.

The Feast in the House of Levi affair

In 1573 Veronese was summoned before the Inquisition over a huge canvas commissioned as a Last Supper for the Dominican convent of Santi Giovanni e Paolo, objected to for its inclusion of "buffoons, drunken Germans, dwarfs" and other figures judged indecorous. Defending artistic licence ("we painters take the same liberties as poets and madmen"), he was ordered to repaint it; instead he simply retitled the work The Feast in the House of Levi, under which name it remains in the Gallerie dell'Accademia.

Legacy

His workshop, continued by his brother Benedetto and sons, produced a large body of work. Veronese's radiant palette and grand architectural pageantry influenced Rubens and later the French classical tradition; The Wedding at Cana, looted by Napoleon, still hangs opposite the Mona Lisa in the Louvre.
