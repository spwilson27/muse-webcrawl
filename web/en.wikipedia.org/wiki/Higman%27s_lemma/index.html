Higman's lemma

Higman's lemma is a result in order theory and combinatorics proved by Graham Higman in 1952. It states that for any finite alphabet, the set of finite sequences (words) over that alphabet, ordered by the subsequence (embedding) relation, is a well-quasi-order: it contains no infinite antichain and no infinite strictly decreasing sequence. Equivalently, in any infinite sequence of finite words over a finite alphabet, there exist two words, one occurring earlier, such that the earlier is a subsequence of the later.

Statement

Let A be a finite set with a quasi-order. Then the set A* of finite sequences of elements of A, under the subsequence ordering in which one sequence embeds in another if it can be obtained by deleting elements, is also a well-quasi-order, provided A itself is a well-quasi-order. The special case where A is a finite alphabet under equality gives the classical form of the lemma.

Applications

Higman's lemma is a special case of Kruskal's tree theorem and is used in computer science to prove termination and decidability results, such as the termination of certain rewriting systems and the decidability of reachability in lossy channel systems. Its proof is a classic early example of a minimal bad sequence argument.
