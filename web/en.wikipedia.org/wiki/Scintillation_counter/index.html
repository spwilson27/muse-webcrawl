Scintillation counter

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A scintillation counter is an instrument for detecting and measuring ionizing radiation by using the excitation effect of incident radiation on a scintillating material, and detecting the resultant light pulses. It consists of a scintillator that generates photons in response to incident radiation, a sensitive photodetector — usually a photomultiplier tube (PMT) or silicon photomultiplier — that converts the light to an electrical signal, and electronics to process this signal.

History

The scintillation technique dates to 1903, when William Crookes observed flashes of light produced by alpha particles on a zinc sulfide screen — the basis of the spinthariscope — and Ernest Rutherford's group used visual scintillation counting in the experiments that revealed the atomic nucleus. Visual counting was laborious, and the method was eclipsed by the Geiger–Müller counter until 1944, when the modern counter was developed by combining scintillators with the newly available photomultiplier tube; Samuel Curran and others built the first electronic instruments, and Robert Hofstadter's 1948 discovery of the excellent scintillation properties of sodium iodide activated with thallium, NaI(Tl), made the device practical for gamma-ray spectroscopy.

Operation

When an ionizing particle or photon enters the scintillator, it excites atoms or molecules, which emit light as they de-excite. Because the amount of light is proportional to the energy deposited, measuring the pulse height allows the energy of the incident radiation to be determined — the basis of gamma spectroscopy. Common scintillators include inorganic crystals such as NaI(Tl), CsI(Tl), and bismuth germanate (BGO); organic plastic and liquid scintillators; and noble gases. Liquid scintillation counting is the standard method for measuring low-energy beta emitters such as tritium and carbon-14 in laboratory samples.

Applications

Scintillation counters are used in radiation protection surveys, environmental monitoring, nuclear medicine (gamma cameras and PET detectors use scintillating crystals), high-energy physics calorimeters, well logging in the oil industry, airport security scanners, and space science. Their advantages over gas-filled detectors include higher detection efficiency for gamma rays, fast timing, and energy resolution good enough to identify radioisotopes by their characteristic gamma-ray lines.
