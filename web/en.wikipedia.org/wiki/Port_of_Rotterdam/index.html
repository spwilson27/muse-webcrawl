The Port of Rotterdam is the largest seaport in Europe, and the largest outside of Asia, located in and near the city of Rotterdam, in the province of South Holland in the Netherlands. From 1962 until 2004 it was the world's busiest port by annual cargo tonnage. It now lies behind Asian ports such as Shanghai, Singapore and Ningbo-Zhoushan, but remains by far Europe's largest port, ahead of Antwerp-Bruges and Hamburg, and a principal gateway for trade between Europe and the rest of the world.

Geography

The port stretches about 42 kilometres from the historic city harbours along the Nieuwe Maas and the Nieuwe Waterweg to the Maasvlakte reclamation projects at the North Sea coast. It comprises a succession of harbour areas — among them Waalhaven, Eemhaven, Botlek, Europoort (opened from the 1950s for crude oil and bulk carriers) and Maasvlakte (from the 1970s, extended by Maasvlakte 2, opened in 2013) — and serves the Rhine–Meuse–Scheldt delta, with barges carrying goods deep into Germany, France, Belgium and Switzerland.

History

Rotterdam grew from a dam on the Rotte in the 13th century into a major port in the Dutch Golden Age. Its modern expansion dates from the digging of the Nieuwe Waterweg (completed 1872), engineered by Pieter Caland, which gave large sea-going ships direct access. After heavy destruction in the Second World War, the port was rebuilt and expanded westwards; the opening of the Europoort and the arrival of containerisation in the 1960s made it the world's largest port for over four decades. The Betuweroute freight railway to Germany opened in 2007.

Operations

The port handles crude oil and refined products, chemicals, coal, iron ore, agribulk, liquefied natural gas and about 14 million TEU of containers annually. It hosts one of the world's largest refining and petrochemical clusters and Europe's largest bunkering port. The Port of Rotterdam Authority, owned by the municipality of Rotterdam and the Dutch state, manages the infrastructure; terminals are operated by private companies, several of them largely automated, notably on Maasvlakte 2.

Energy transition

In the 2020s the port has positioned itself as a hub for hydrogen imports and production, carbon capture and storage (such as the Porthos project), offshore wind and shore power, aiming to cut its large share of Dutch CO₂ emissions.
