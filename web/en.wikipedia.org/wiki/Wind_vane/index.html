Wind vane
Other names: Weather vane, weathercock
Function: Indicates wind direction
Invented: Antiquity

A wind vane, weather vane, or weathercock is an instrument used for showing the direction of the wind. It is typically used as an architectural ornament on the highest point of a building. The vane rotates freely on a vertical axis so that its pointer aligns with the wind: the tail fin presents a large surface area to the wind, causing the arrow or figure to point in the direction from which the wind is blowing.

Design

A simple wind vane consists of a rotating pointer with an arrowhead at one end and a broad fin or tail at the other, mounted above fixed directional letters indicating north, south, east and west. Because the tail has a larger surface area than the head, the wind pushes the tail downwind, and the arrowhead points into the wind — the direction from which the wind comes, which is how wind direction is conventionally reported in meteorology.

History

Wind vanes are among the oldest weather instruments. The Tower of the Winds in Athens, built around 48 BC, carried a bronze Triton vane. In the ninth century, Pope Nicholas I is said to have ordered a rooster placed on every church as a reminder of Saint Peter's betrayal, spreading the familiar cockerel design across Europe. Large vanes became emblems of farms and public buildings; the oldest surviving American weathervane, a copper Indian archer made in 1710 for Boston, and the famous grasshopper vane on Faneuil Hall (1742) are notable examples.

Modern use

Today wind vanes are used at weather stations, airports, farms and on wind turbines, where a vane keeps the rotor facing the wind. Modern instruments convert the vane's orientation into an electrical signal for automatic recording, though traditional decorative vanes remain common on barns, gazebos and houses.
