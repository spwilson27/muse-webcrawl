Collective effervescence

A crowd united in celebration: Durkheim's collective effervescence generates and renews shared symbols.

Collective effervescence is a concept in the sociology of Émile Durkheim describing the heightened energy, emotion and sense of unity experienced when a group of people come together and participate in the same action or ritual. Durkheim developed the idea in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), his study of Aboriginal Australian religion.

Theory

When individuals assemble and their actions and emotions synchronise—in dance, chanting, mourning or celebration—the group experiences an almost electric intensity that participants attribute to a sacred force outside themselves. For Durkheim, this force is society itself: in collective effervescence the group generates and periodically renews its sacred symbols, totems and moral bonds, which is why he located the origin of religion in such gatherings.

Influence

The concept has been applied far beyond religion: to political rallies, sporting crowds, concerts and festivals, protest movements and even online pile-ons. Later theorists, including Randall Collins in his theory of interaction ritual chains, built on it to explain how shared focus and mood produce solidarity and "emotional energy". Critics note the difficulty of measuring the phenomenon and Durkheim's reliance on second-hand ethnography.
