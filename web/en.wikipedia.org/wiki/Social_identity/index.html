Social identity

A social identity is the portion of an individual's self-concept derived from perceived membership in a relevant social group. The concept is central to social identity theory, developed by Henri Tajfel and John Turner in the 1970s, which explains intergroup behaviour in terms of categorisation, identification and comparison: people sort themselves and others into groups (in-groups and out-groups), adopt the identity of groups they belong to, and seek a positive self-image by comparing their group favourably with others — a process that can produce in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination even between randomly formed "minimal groups".

Components

Tajfel proposed that social identity has three components: a cognitive component (knowledge of membership), an evaluative component (the value attached to the group), and an emotional component (feelings toward the group and its members). Individuals hold multiple social identities — based on nationality, profession, religion, fandom, gender and more — and different identities become salient in different contexts, a process elaborated in Turner's self-categorization theory.

Applications

The social identity approach has been applied to organisational behaviour (how employees identify with firms), leadership, health behaviour, political mobilisation, prejudice and intergroup conflict. The "social identity model of crowd behaviour" revises older deindividuation accounts by arguing that crowd members act according to the norms of a salient shared identity rather than losing their identity.
