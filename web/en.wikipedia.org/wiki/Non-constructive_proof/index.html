Non-constructive proof

A non-constructive proof is a proof that an object with a certain property exists, without providing a specific example of such an object or a procedure to find one. It contrasts with a constructive proof, which exhibits the object explicitly. Non-constructive methods often rely on proof by contradiction or on the law of excluded middle.

Classic example

A famous example proves there exist irrational numbers a and b such that ab is rational: consider √2√2. If it is rational, the claim is shown; if it is irrational, then (√2√2)√2 = 2 is rational, and the claim holds either way — without deciding which case is true. (In fact Gelfond–Schneider theory shows √2√2 is irrational.) Other standard non-constructive tools include counting arguments and the pigeonhole principle, and the probabilistic method of Erdős.

Philosophical status

Schools of constructive mathematics, such as intuitionism, reject some non-constructive reasoning, especially appeals to the law of excluded middle over infinite domains. Most mainstream mathematicians accept non-constructive proofs as valid, though existence proofs are often considered less satisfying when a construction is also available.
