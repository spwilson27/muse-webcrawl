Demonology

Demonology is the study of demons within religious belief and occultism. It is the branch of theology dealing with supernatural beings that are not gods, including the classification, nature, names and hierarchies of demons, and their supposed relations with human beings. Demonological traditions exist in Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Zoroastrianism, Hinduism and many folk religions, though the field's best-known literature is the demonology of late medieval and early modern Christian Europe.

Classifications and grimoires

Christian demonologists devised elaborate hierarchies of fallen angels, often mirroring the nine choirs of angels described by Pseudo-Dionysius. Johann Weyer's Pseudomonarchia Daemonum (1577) catalogued 69 demons with their ranks, legions and offices; the Ars Goetia, the first part of the seventeenth-century grimoire The Lesser Key of Solomon, lists 72 demons said to have been commanded by King Solomon. Jacques Collin de Plancy's illustrated Dictionnaire Infernal (1818; expanded 1863) became the most famous popular catalogue. Heinrich Kramer's Malleus Maleficarum (1486) — the notorious witch-hunting manual — and King James I's Daemonologie (1597) shaped the demonology of the witch trials.

Demonology in world religions

In Islam, the study of the jinn, shayatin (devils) and Iblis occupies a related place; jinn are created from smokeless fire and, unlike demons in the Christian scheme, may be Muslim or unbelieving. Jewish tradition developed accounts of shedim and figures such as Lilith, expanded in medieval Kabbalah. Zoroastrianism's daevas, opposed to the ahuras, represent one of the earliest systematic demonologies and influenced later Abrahamic traditions. Hindu and Buddhist traditions describe asuras, rakshasas and maras within their own cosmologies.

Modern perspectives

Within mainstream modern theology, demonology has been reinterpreted demythologically (as in Rudolf Bultmann's programme) or treated as a pastoral matter in churches that retain exorcism ministries. Academic study of demonology continues in religious studies, history and anthropology, examining how beliefs about demons structured early modern law, medicine and gender politics, and how they persist in contemporary Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, where "spiritual warfare" theology maintains a robust practical demonology.
