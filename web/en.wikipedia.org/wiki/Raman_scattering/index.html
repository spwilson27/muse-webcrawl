Raman scattering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Raman scattering is the inelastic scattering of photons by matter, in which scattered light changes in frequency as photons exchange energy with the vibrational and rotational modes of molecules. It was discovered in 1928 by the Indian physicist C. V. Raman (with K. S. Krishnan), a discovery for which Raman received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930. Raman scattering is the basis of Raman spectroscopy, a widely used analytical technique.

Physics

Most photons scatter elastically (Rayleigh scattering), keeping their energy. A small fraction — roughly one in ten million — scatter inelastically: if the photon loses energy to excite a molecular vibration, it emerges with lower frequency (Stokes scattering); if it gains energy from an excited molecule, it emerges with higher frequency (anti-Stokes scattering). The frequency shift, the Raman shift, equals the energy of the vibrational mode and is characteristic of the molecule's chemical bonds.

Applications

Because each substance produces a unique Raman spectrum, Raman spectroscopy acts as a chemical fingerprint. It is used to identify materials in chemistry, pharmaceuticals, forensics, geology and art conservation, to monitor semiconductor quality, and in medicine for non-invasive tissue analysis. Variants such as surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) and stimulated Raman scattering improve the weak signal. Raman lasers and Raman amplifiers in fibre-optic communications also exploit the effect.
