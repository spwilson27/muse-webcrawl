Edward Lawrie Tatum (1909–1975)

Edward Lawrie Tatum (December 14, 1909 – November 5, 1975) was an American geneticist. He shared the 1958 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with George Beadle and Joshua Lederberg for discoveries concerning the genetic control of biochemical processes.

Early life and education

Tatum was born in Boulder, Colorado, the son of Arthur Lawrie Tatum, a chemistry instructor. He studied at the University of Chicago and received his PhD in biochemistry from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in 1934. He then worked in the Netherlands and at Stanford University.

One gene–one enzyme hypothesis

Working with George Beadle at Stanford, Tatum used the bread mold Neurospora crassa to show that genes act by regulating distinct chemical events. By exposing the mold to X-rays they produced mutants unable to synthesize particular vitamins and amino acids, demonstrating that each mutation disabled a single enzyme in a metabolic pathway. This became known as the one gene–one enzyme hypothesis, later refined to "one gene–one polypeptide."

Later career

Tatum moved to Yale University in 1945, where he mentored Joshua Lederberg, then to Stanford in 1956, and finally to Rockefeller University in 1957, where he remained until his death. His later work included studies of bacterial genetics and cell culture.
