Fondant icing, also commonly shortened to fondant (from French fondant, "melting"), is a sugar-based icing used to decorate or sculpt cakes and pastries. Unlike most icings it forms a smooth, dough-like coating that can be draped over a cake to give a flawless matte finish, and it can be modelled into shapes and figures.

Types

Rolled fondant is the most common form for celebration and wedding cakes. It is made by kneading icing (powdered) sugar into a base of gelatine or agar, glycerine, glucose syrup and flavouring until it forms a pliable paste. It is rolled into sheets with a rolling pin and laid over a cake sealed with buttercream or marzipan and jam.

Poured fondant is a glossy liquid made by heating sugar, water and cream of tartar to the soft-ball stage and then beating it as it cools. It is used warm to coat petits fours, éclairs and the tops of biscuits such as iced gems and fondant fancies.

Modelling fondant stiffened with gum tragacanth or tylose powder (sometimes called gum paste or flower paste when extra firm) is used for sugar flowers and figurines that must hold fine detail.

Preparation and use

Rolled fondant is kneaded until smooth, coloured with gel food colouring, and rolled on a surface dusted with icing sugar or cornflour. It hardens gradually on exposure to air but remains soft enough to cut. Fondant-covered cakes are usually eaten within a few days; the coating also protects the cake and prolongs freshness.

Some consumers find the taste and texture of commercial fondant unpleasant compared with buttercream, and many simply peel it off before eating — a recurring topic of debate among bakers, though fondant remains prized for the polished appearance it gives showpiece cakes.
