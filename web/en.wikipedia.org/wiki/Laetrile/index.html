Laetrile

Laetrile is a synthetic compound closely related to amygdalin, a naturally occurring cyanogenic glycoside found in the pits of apricots, bitter almonds, and other stone fruits. Since the 1950s it has been promoted as a treatment for cancer, most famously under the misleading name "vitamin B17", although it is not a vitamin.

Promotion and rationale

The substance was developed and promoted by Ernst T. Krebs Jr., who patented a semi-synthetic version in the 1950s. Proponents claimed that cyanide released from amygdalin by the enzyme β-glucosidase would selectively kill cancer cells, which were said to be rich in the enzyme and deficient in the detoxifying enzyme rhodanese. The scientific consensus is that this rationale is false: the relevant enzymes are not distributed as claimed, and amygdalin can release dangerous quantities of cyanide in the body.

Evidence and regulation

Clinical evaluation, including a 1982 trial sponsored by the U.S. National Cancer Institute and published in the New England Journal of Medicine, found no anticancer activity and documented cases of cyanide poisoning. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has never approved laetrile, and its interstate distribution is illegal in the United States; a major legal and political campaign in the 1970s, and the Supreme Court case United States v. Rutherford (1979), failed to secure an exemption for terminally ill patients. Despite this, laetrile clinics operated in Mexico and elsewhere, and cases of cyanide toxicity from apricot kernels and supplements continue to be reported.
