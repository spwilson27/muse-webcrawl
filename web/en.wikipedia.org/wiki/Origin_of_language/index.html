Origin of language

The origin of language (spoken and signed, as well as language-related technological systems such as writing) in the human species is a widely debated topic in the fields of linguistics, anthropology, psychology, neurology and philosophy. Despite extensive archaeological and genetic research, no consensus exists on when or how language first emerged, nor whether it evolved gradually or appeared suddenly. The difficulty of the problem led the Linguistic Society of Paris in 1866 to ban discussion of the topic, and serious scientific study resumed only in the late 20th century.

Symbolic artefacts such as cave paintings are often taken as indirect evidence of language.

Approaches

Two broad positions structure the debate. Continuity theories hold that language evolved gradually from pre-linguistic communication systems of earlier primates and hominins. Discontinuity theories, associated with Noam Chomsky and Stephen Jay Gould, propose that language arose suddenly, possibly through a single genetic mutation or as a by-product (exaptation) of other evolutionary processes. Estimates of the date range from over a million years ago to as recently as 50,000–100,000 years ago, around the emergence of behaviourally modern humans.

Hypotheses on first forms

Speculative 19th-century labels remain famous: the "bow-wow" theory (language from imitating natural sounds), the "pooh-pooh" theory (from instinctive emotional cries), the "ding-dong" theory (an innate resonance between objects and sounds) and the "yo-he-ho" theory (from rhythmic chants coordinating labour). Modern hypotheses emphasise either the primacy of gesture — language beginning in sign and later shifting to voice — or vocal grooming and "motherese" (infant-directed speech) as evolutionary bridges.

Evidence

Evidence is indirect. The descended larynx, the hyoid bone (a modern-shaped specimen is known from Neanderthals), the size of the hypoglossal canal, and the FOXP2 gene — mutations of which impair speech and language — all constrain plausible dates. Archaeological proxies such as personal ornaments, engraved ochre from Blombos Cave (c. 75,000 years old) and cave art suggest symbolic behaviour. Whether Neanderthals possessed language remains disputed, supported by their FOXP2 sequence and anatomy but not conclusively settled.

From protolanguage to language

Many researchers posit an intermediate protolanguage: a system with words but little or no syntax, comparable to pidgins or the two-word stage of child speech. Derek Bickerton argued that protolanguage could persist for long periods before a rapid transition to full syntax; others argue grammar and vocabulary co-evolved incrementally through cultural transmission.
