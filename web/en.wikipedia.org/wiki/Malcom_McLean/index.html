Malcom Purcell McLean (1913–2001) was an American businessman and entrepreneur, often called the "father of containerization". He developed the modern intermodal shipping container, which revolutionised world trade and transport in the second half of the 20th century.

Early career in trucking

McLean bought a used truck in 1934 during the Great Depression and built McLean Trucking Company into one of the largest trucking firms in the United States. Watching dockworkers slowly load cargo piece by piece in 1937, he conceived the idea of standardised boxes that could move directly between trucks and ships without being unpacked.

Container shipping

Barred by regulation from owning both a trucking company and a shipping line, McLean sold his trucking interests and bought the Pan-Atlantic Steamship Company (later Sea-Land). On 26 April 1956 his converted tanker Ideal X sailed from Newark, New Jersey to Houston carrying 58 of his containers. Loading costs fell from about $5.86 per ton for loose cargo to roughly 16 cents per ton. McLean patented a strong, stackable container with corner castings, and released the patent royalty-free to encourage standardisation by the ISO — a decision that enabled a universal system.

Impact

Containerisation slashed freight costs, shrank port turnaround from days to hours, fuelled the growth of global supply chains and helped make modern globalisation possible. Forbes ranked McLean among the businessmen who "changed the world", and his story is told in Marc Levinson's The Box.

Later ventures

McLean sold Sea-Land in 1969, later repurchased and built up the United States Lines with some of the largest container ships of their day, and continued in business until shortly before his death in 2001.
