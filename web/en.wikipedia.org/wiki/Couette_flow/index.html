Couette flow

In fluid dynamics, Couette flow is the flow of a viscous fluid in the space between two surfaces, one of which is moving tangentially relative to the other. The configuration often studied is that of two parallel plates separated by a distance h, with the top plate moving at constant speed U and the bottom plate fixed. The flow is named after Maurice Couette, the French physicist who used a related configuration — flow between rotating cylinders — in an early viscometer.

Plane Couette flow

For steady, fully developed plane Couette flow with no imposed pressure gradient, the Navier–Stokes equations reduce to d²u/dy² = 0, giving the linear velocity profile u(y) = U·y/h. The shear stress is constant across the gap and equal to μU/h, where μ is the dynamic viscosity. When an adverse or favorable pressure gradient is superimposed, the profile becomes a combination of the linear Couette profile and the parabolic Poiseuille profile. Despite its simple laminar solution, plane Couette flow can sustain turbulence in practice: it is linearly stable for all Reynolds numbers yet undergoes subcritical transition to turbulence at Reynolds numbers of a few hundred, making it a canonical problem in the study of hydrodynamic stability and transition.

Taylor–Couette flow

In Taylor–Couette flow the fluid occupies the annulus between two coaxial cylinders, typically with the inner cylinder rotating. Above a critical Taylor number, the flow develops toroidal Taylor vortices, and with increasing rotation speed passes through wavy-vortex and chaotic regimes into turbulence. The system is a standard experimental and theoretical model for pattern formation and rotating-flow stability, with applications to rotating machinery, journal bearings, and viscometry.
