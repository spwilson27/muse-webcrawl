Collenchyma

Cross-section of collenchyma showing unevenly thickened primary walls, thickest at the cell corners.

Collenchyma is a supporting tissue in the stems, leaves and petioles of many plants, composed of living cells with unevenly thickened primary cell walls. Together with parenchyma and sclerenchyma, it forms the ground tissue system of plants, and is the main mechanical tissue of young, still-growing organs.

Structure

Collenchyma cells are elongated and retain their protoplasts at maturity, unlike the dead cells of sclerenchyma. Their walls are rich in cellulose and pectin, contain much water, and can stretch as the organ grows while still resisting bending—ideal for herbaceous stems and for structures such as the "strings" of celery stalks and the ribs of rhubarb petioles.

Types

Botanists classify collenchyma by the pattern of wall thickening: angular collenchyma (thickened at the corners where cells meet), lamellar or plate collenchyma (thickened on the tangential walls), lacunar collenchyma (with intercellular spaces), and annular collenchyma (with more uniform, ring-like thickening).

Function and occurrence

Collenchyma occurs just beneath the epidermis of young stems, in leaf midribs and petioles, and around vascular bundles. Because its cells are alive and its walls can thicken in response to mechanical stress, it provides flexible support that strengthens with wind exposure and load, without preventing growth.
