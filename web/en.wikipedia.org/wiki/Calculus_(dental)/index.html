In dentistry, calculus or tartar is a form of hardened dental plaque. It is caused by precipitation of minerals from saliva (and gingival crevicular fluid) into the plaque on the teeth. This process of precipitation kills the bacterial cells within dental plaque, but the rough and hardened surface that is formed provides an ideal surface for further plaque formation, leading to calculus build-up, which compromises the health of the gingiva (gums).

Formation

Plaque begins to mineralise within days of forming. Supragingival calculus forms above the gumline, deriving its minerals from saliva, and is most abundant on the cheek side of the upper molars and the tongue side of the lower front teeth, near the openings of major salivary ducts. Subgingival calculus forms below the gumline, is darker and harder, and gets its minerals from crevicular fluid and blood products.

Composition

Mature calculus is roughly 70–90% inorganic material, chiefly calcium phosphates, including hydroxyapatite, whitlockite, octacalcium phosphate and brushite, embedded in an organic matrix of bacteria, proteins and lipids.

Clinical significance

Calculus itself is not directly harmful, but its rough surface retains plaque and makes oral hygiene less effective; accumulation is associated with gingivitis and periodontitis. The word "calculus" derives from the Latin for "small stone".

Removal

Once formed, calculus cannot be removed by brushing alone. A dental professional removes it by scaling, using hand instruments or ultrasonic scalers, often as part of scaling and root planing. Anticalculus dentifrices containing pyrophosphates or zinc citrate can slow formation.
