Worm gear

TypeGear arrangement
ComponentsWorm (screw-like gear) and worm wheel
Typical ratios5:1 to over 100:1 in a single stage
PropertyUsually self-locking (wheel cannot drive worm)

A worm gear is a gear arrangement consisting of a worm — a shaft with a helical thread resembling a screw — meshing with a toothed wheel called a worm wheel or worm gear. Worm drives achieve very large speed reductions in a compact space and transmit motion between shafts at right angles that do not intersect.

Operation

As the worm rotates, its thread advances and pushes the teeth of the wheel. Each complete turn of a single-start worm advances the wheel by one tooth, so with a 40-tooth wheel a single stage gives a 40:1 reduction. Worms may have one or several thread "starts"; more starts reduce the ratio and increase efficiency but reduce or eliminate self-locking. The wheel's teeth are often "throated" (curved) to wrap partially around the worm and increase contact area.

Self-locking

Because friction between the worm and wheel is high, in most designs the wheel cannot drive the worm: the drive is said to be self-locking or irreversible. This is exploited in hoists, lifts and tuning machines, where the load must not drive the mechanism backward when power is removed, though designers caution that vibration can defeat static self-locking.

Types and applications

Variants include single-enveloping drives (throated wheel) and double-enveloping (globoid) drives, in which the worm is also hourglass-shaped to wrap the wheel, increasing load capacity. Worm gears are used in steering systems, elevator and escalator drives, conveyor systems, guitar tuning pegs, rudders, and speed reducers of all kinds. Their disadvantages are sliding friction, heat generation, lower efficiency (often 40–85%) and the need for good lubrication; wheels are frequently made of bronze and worms of hardened steel.
