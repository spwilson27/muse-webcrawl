Russian opposition

The Russian opposition comprises political parties, movements and activists in Russia who oppose the policies of the federal government led by President Vladimir Putin. It is conventionally divided between the "systemic opposition" — parties represented in the State Duma, such as the Communist Party, the Liberal Democratic Party and A Just Russia, which operate within limits tolerated by the authorities — and the "non-systemic" or extra-parliamentary opposition, which has been largely excluded from elections and subject to repression, especially since 2011–2012 and drastically after Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022.

Systemic opposition

Parties in the Duma have historically criticised particular government policies while broadly supporting the presidency. In presidential elections their candidates have typically placed far behind the incumbent. Western observers and opposition figures often describe them as a managed or "pocket" opposition, though the Communist Party has occasionally mounted strong regional challenges.

Non-systemic opposition

The extra-parliamentary opposition gained prominence with the mass protests of 2011–2013 against electoral fraud, which drew tens of thousands in Moscow. Liberal parties such as Yabloko, the movement around Alexei Navalny and his Anti-Corruption Foundation, and figures including Boris Nemtsov (assassinated in 2015), Ilya Yashin and Vladimir Kara-Murza became its best-known faces. Navalny's poisoning in 2020, his imprisonment on return to Russia in 2021, the designation of his organisations as "extremist", and his death in prison in February 2024 marked escalating repression.

After February 2022, laws criminalising "false information" about the armed forces were used to imprison critics of the war, including Yashin and Kara-Murza (the latter freed in a 2024 prisoner exchange). Much of the non-systemic opposition now operates in exile, running media outlets and advocacy organisations from European capitals.
