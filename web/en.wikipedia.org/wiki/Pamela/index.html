Pamela; or, Virtue Rewarded is an epistolary novel first published in 1740 by the English writer Samuel Richardson. It tells the story of Pamela Andrews, a beautiful fifteen-year-old maidservant whose master, Mr B, makes repeated unwanted advances toward her after the death of his mother. Pamela resists, keeping a record of her experiences in letters to her parents, until Mr B reforms and, impressed by her virtue, marries her.

Plot

After the death of her mistress, Pamela Andrews determines to remain honest despite Mr B's escalating attempts to seduce — and later to abduct and imprison — her at his Lincolnshire estate. Her letters and journal, which Mr B eventually reads, convince him of her worth; he proposes marriage, and Pamela, her virtue "rewarded," rises to become lady of the house, facing the snobbery of his sister Lady Davers before winning acceptance.

Reception and controversy

Pamela was a runaway bestseller and one of the earliest genuine novels in English, helping to popularise the epistolary form and the novel of sensibility. It also provoked a famous backlash: Henry Fielding's parody Shamela (1741) mocked Pamela as a scheming social climber, and his Joseph Andrews (1742) began as a further parody. The division between "Pamelists" and "Anti-Pamelists" is a landmark episode in the history of the novel.

Influence

The novel's enormous popularity spawned merchandise, prints and sequels; Richardson himself wrote a continuation, Pamela in her Exalted Condition. The name "Pamela," invented by Sir Philip Sidney for his Arcadia, became a common given name largely because of Richardson's heroine.
