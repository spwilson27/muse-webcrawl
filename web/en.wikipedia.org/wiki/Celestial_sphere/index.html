Overview

The celestial sphere is an imaginary sphere of immense radius centred on Earth, onto which all the stars and other celestial objects are projected. Although the stars lie at wildly different distances, the model works because only their directions matter for observation. It has been the working picture of the sky since Greek astronomy.

Earth's axis, extended, pierces the sphere at the north and south celestial poles — the north one near the star Polaris — and the plane of the Equator, extended, cuts it in the celestial equator. Positions on the sphere are given by declination (the analogue of latitude) and right ascension (the analogue of longitude), just as Hipparchus first systematised with terrestrial coordinates.

The Sun's annual path against the stars, the ecliptic, is a great circle tilted 23.44° to the celestial equator, crossing it at the two equinoxes and reaching its extremes at the solstices.
