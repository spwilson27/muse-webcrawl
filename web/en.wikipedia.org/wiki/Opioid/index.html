Opioid

Opioids
ClassAnalgesics; central nervous system depressants
MechanismAgonism of opioid receptors (mainly μ)
ExamplesMorphine, codeine, oxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl, heroin, methadone, tramadol
Main risksDependence, tolerance, overdose (respiratory depression)
AntidoteNaloxone

Opioids are a class of drugs that act on opioid receptors in the nervous system to produce morphine-like effects, principally pain relief (analgesia), sedation and euphoria. The term includes natural opiates derived from the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum)—such as morphine and codeine—as well as semi-synthetic opioids (oxycodone, heroin) and fully synthetic ones (fentanyl, methadone, tramadol). Medically, opioids are among the most effective analgesics known and are essential for severe acute pain, cancer pain and palliative care, but they carry high risks of tolerance, physical dependence, addiction and fatal overdose.

Opioids share a characteristic chemical scaffold and bind opioid receptors throughout the brain, spinal cord and gut.

Pharmacology

Opioids act mainly at the μ-opioid receptor, with additional activity at κ and δ receptors. Receptor activation inhibits pain transmission in the spinal cord and alters the perception of pain in the brain; it also depresses the brainstem respiratory centres—the mechanism by which overdose kills—and slows gastrointestinal motility, causing constipation. Repeated use produces tolerance and physical dependence; abrupt cessation triggers a characteristic withdrawal syndrome. Naloxone, a competitive antagonist, rapidly reverses opioid overdose.

Medical use

Opioids are used for moderate-to-severe pain after surgery and trauma, in cancer and end-of-life care, and in specific roles such as cough suppression (codeine), treatment of diarrhoea, and—critically—maintenance treatment of opioid use disorder with methadone or buprenorphine. The WHO lists morphine on its Model List of Essential Medicines.

Harms and the opioid epidemic

From the late 1990s, aggressive marketing and liberal prescribing of opioid analgesics in the United States and Canada contributed to widespread misuse, addiction and overdose deaths—a crisis that later evolved through waves driven by heroin and then illicit fentanyl, which is roughly 50 times more potent than heroin. Opioid overdose deaths in the United States exceeded 80,000 per year in the early 2020s. Responses have included prescribing guidelines, prescription monitoring, wider naloxone availability, and addiction treatment expansion, alongside litigation against manufacturers and distributors.
