Anosmia

SpecialtyOtorhinolaryngology, neurology
SymptomsTotal loss of sense of smell
RelatedHyposmia (reduced), parosmia (distorted), phantosmia

Anosmia is the complete loss of the sense of smell. It may be temporary or permanent, and can result from nasal congestion, damage to the olfactory epithelium or nerves, head injury, neurodegenerative disease, congenital conditions, or viral infection. Reduced smell is called hyposmia, and distorted smell parosmia.

Causes

Common causes include chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps, upper respiratory infections, allergic rhinitis and ageing. Post-viral anosmia attracted wide attention during the COVID-19 pandemic, when sudden smell loss became a hallmark symptom of SARS-CoV-2 infection; in most patients it resolves within weeks, though a minority suffer long-term dysfunction and parosmia. Head trauma can shear the olfactory nerve fibres at the cribriform plate. Anosmia can also be an early sign of Parkinson's and Alzheimer's diseases. Congenital anosmia occurs in conditions such as Kallmann syndrome.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis involves smell identification tests (such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test), nasal endoscopy and imaging. Treatment targets the underlying cause: steroids or surgery for sinonasal disease, management of allergies, and olfactory training (repeated structured sniffing of strong odours), which has evidence of benefit in post-viral cases.

Impact

Because flavour depends heavily on retronasal smell, anosmia dulls taste, reduces appetite and enjoyment of food, and impairs detection of hazards such as smoke, gas leaks and spoiled food. It is associated with depression and reduced quality of life.
