Marburg virus

Family: Filoviridae
Genus: Marburgvirus
Genome: Negative-sense single-stranded RNA
Disease: Marburg virus disease (MVD)
Case fatality rate: 24–88% in past outbreaks

Marburg virus (MARV) is a hemorrhagic fever virus of the Filoviridae family — the same family as Ebola virus — and the causative agent of Marburg virus disease in humans and non-human primates. It is an enveloped, filamentous, negative-sense RNA virus and is considered a highly dangerous pathogen requiring maximum containment (BSL-4).

Discovery and outbreaks

The virus was first recognised in 1967, when simultaneous outbreaks occurred among laboratory workers in Marburg and Frankfurt, Germany, and in Belgrade, Yugoslavia, who had been exposed to tissues of African green monkeys imported from Uganda. Subsequent outbreaks have occurred mainly in sub-Saharan Africa, including large epidemics in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (1998–2000) and Angola (2004–2005), the latter with a case fatality rate near 90%. Outbreaks were also reported in Guinea, Ghana, Equatorial Guinea, Tanzania, and Rwanda in the 2020s.

Natural reservoir and transmission

The Egyptian fruit bat (Rousettus aegyptiacus) is considered the natural reservoir. Human infection follows contact with bat-inhabited caves or mines, or with blood and bodily fluids of infected people and animals. Initial symptoms — fever, severe headache, malaise — progress to vomiting, diarrhoea, and in many cases severe haemorrhagic manifestations.

Treatment

No specific antiviral treatment or licensed vaccine exists; care is supportive, including rehydration and treatment of secondary infections, though candidate vaccines and antibody therapies are in development.
