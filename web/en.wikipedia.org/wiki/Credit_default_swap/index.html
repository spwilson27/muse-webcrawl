Credit default swap

A credit default swap (CDS) is a financial swap agreement that the seller of the CDS will compensate the buyer in the event of a debt default by the debtor or other credit event. The buyer makes periodic payments to the seller, and in return receives a payoff if an underlying financial instrument defaults.

Credit default swap

Type: Derivative
Underlying: Bond, loan, sovereign debt
Risk transferred: Credit risk

History

Forms of credit default swaps existed from the early 1990s. The market grew rapidly in the early 2000s, reaching an estimated tens of trillions of dollars in notional value by 2007. JPMorgan is often credited with creating the modern CDS in 1994, in connection with a credit line to Exxon following the Exxon Valdez oil spill. CDS contracts played a prominent role in the 2008 financial crisis, notably in relation to American International Group (AIG).

How it works

The buyer pays a spread, expressed in basis points, typically quarterly. If the reference entity defaults, fails to pay, or restructures, the seller compensates the buyer, either by physical delivery of the defaulted bond for its par value or by cash settlement of the difference.

Risks and criticism

Critics argue CDS allow speculation on default without owning the underlying bond — a so-called naked CDS — and can create interconnected counterparty risk. Defenders argue they provide price discovery and a means to hedge credit risk.
