Hawthorne effect

The Hawthorne effect is a type of reactivity in which individuals modify an aspect of their behaviour in response to their awareness of being observed. The effect takes its name from the Hawthorne Works, a Western Electric factory in Cicero, Illinois, where a series of experiments on worker productivity were carried out between 1924 and 1932.

The Hawthorne studies

The studies began as lighting experiments intended to measure the effect of illumination levels on output. Researchers found that productivity tended to rise whether lighting was increased or decreased, and even when other conditions such as rest breaks and working hours were changed. In the best-known phase, the Relay Assembly Test Room, a small group of women assembling telephone relays was observed over several years, and their output rose through a series of experimental changes — and remained high even when conditions were returned to the original arrangement.

Interpretations

The results were popularised by Elton Mayo and by Fritz Roethlisberger and William Dickson, who argued that workers responded not to the physical changes themselves but to the attention they received, the sense of being part of a group, and the interest shown by management. The studies became a founding episode of the human relations movement in management theory. Later re-analyses have questioned how strongly the original data support the popular version of the effect, and the term is now used more generally in research methodology for any change in behaviour caused by subjects' awareness that they are being studied.
