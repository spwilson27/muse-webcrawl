An astronomer is a scientist who studies celestial objects and phenomena — stars, planets, galaxies and the universe as a whole — in the field of astronomy. Historically, astronomers were priest-scholars and navigators who tracked the heavens for calendars and astrology; today the term usually denotes a professional researcher, while a large community of amateur astronomers also makes genuine contributions.

Professional astronomers

Modern professional astronomers typically hold a PhD in astronomy or physics and work at universities, research institutes and space agencies such as NASA and ESA. Few spend nights at eyepieces: telescopes are operated by specialist staff or robotic systems, time is allocated competitively through proposals, and the astronomer's work centres on data analysis, modelling and theory across the electromagnetic spectrum and, recently, gravitational waves and neutrinos.

Amateur astronomers

Amateurs number in the hundreds of thousands worldwide. They discover comets, supernovae and asteroids, monitor variable stars (coordinated by organisations such as the AAVSO), and increasingly contribute to citizen-science projects such as Galaxy Zoo. Several notable professionals, including Clyde Tombaugh, discoverer of Pluto, began as amateurs.

History

Figures such as Hipparchus, Galileo Galilei, Johannes Kepler, Isaac Newton, William Herschel, Annie Jump Cannon, Edwin Hubble and Vera Rubin trace the transformation of astronomy from naked-eye positional measurement to modern astrophysics, in which the astronomer's role merged with that of the physicist.
