Valet

A valet (in British English sometimes pronounced /ˈvælɪt/, traditionally /ˈvæleɪ/ for a personal servant) is a personal attendant, historically a male servant responsible for his employer's clothes, dressing, and personal arrangements. The female equivalent attending a mistress is a lady's maid. The term derives from the Old French valet or varlet, originally denoting a young nobleman's attendant or squire.

History

In the medieval household, a valet or varlet was a youth of good family serving an apprenticeship in arms and manners in a lord's household. By the early modern period the role had become that of a professional body servant. In the great households of the 18th and 19th centuries the valet (in French, valet de chambre) ranked among the senior "upper servants", answerable only to the steward and his master.

Duties

The traditional valet's duties included selecting, laying out, brushing and mending his employer's clothing; assisting him to dress; shaving him or preparing his bath; packing for journeys and travelling with him; keeping his wardrobe, brushes, razors and personal effects in order; and handling confidential errands and correspondence. A skilled valet was expected to be discreet, well-mannered and invisible in company. The novelist P. G. Wodehouse immortalised the type in Jeeves, the supremely competent valet to the hapless Bertie Wooster.

Modern usage

As domestic service declined in the 20th century, personal valets became rare outside royal households, the military (where an officer's attendant is more usually called a batman or orderly), and the service of the very wealthy. The word has acquired new meanings: valet parking denotes a service, common in the United States, in which an attendant parks a guest's car; hotels may offer a valet service for laundry and pressing; and "valet" is used for various personal-assistance and equipment-stand products.
