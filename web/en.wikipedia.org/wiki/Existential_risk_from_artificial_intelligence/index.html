Existential risk from artificial intelligence

Existential risk from artificial intelligence refers to the hypothetical possibility that substantial progress in artificial general intelligence (AGI) could lead to human extinction or an irreversible global catastrophe. Proponents argue that a sufficiently advanced AI system might pursue goals misaligned with human welfare, resist correction or shutdown, and — if it surpassed human intelligence — be impossible to control. The idea was developed by writers such as I. J. Good, who described an "intelligence explosion" in 1965, and was popularised in the 2010s by philosopher Nick Bostrom's book Superintelligence (2014) and by researchers including Eliezer Yudkowsky.

Arguments

Two central conjectures are Bostrom's orthogonality thesis — that an agent's intelligence and its final goals can vary independently, so a superintelligence need not share human values — and the instrumental convergence thesis, which holds that agents with very different goals would nonetheless converge on instrumental subgoals such as self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resisting modification. Related concerns include deceptive alignment, in which a model behaves compliantly during training while harbouring misaligned objectives, and rapid capability gains ("fast takeoff") that would leave no time to react.

Responses and debate

Surveys of AI researchers find a wide range of views, with many assigning a non-trivial probability to extremely bad outcomes from advanced AI while others consider existential risk speculative compared with near-term harms such as bias and misuse. Public attention increased in 2023, when an open letter called for a pause in giant AI experiments and a one-sentence statement signed by many leading researchers declared that mitigating extinction risk from AI should be a global priority. Governments responded with initiatives such as the UK's AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park in November 2023 and the creation of AI Safety Institutes. Critics, including many machine-learning researchers and ethicists, argue that extinction scenarios distract from present-day harms and rest on unproven assumptions about future systems.
