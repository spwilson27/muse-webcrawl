The Battle of Cajamarca, also known as the Massacre of Cajamarca, was the ambush and capture of the Inca ruler Atahualpa by a small Spanish force led by Francisco Pizarro on 16 November 1532. Though often called a battle, it was effectively a surprise attack on a largely unarmed ceremonial retinue: the Spanish, hidden in the buildings around Cajamarca's plaza, opened fire with arquebuses and small cannon and charged with cavalry and infantry, killing thousands and seizing the emperor on his litter. No Spaniard was killed. The capture paralysed the Inca Empire and opened the way to its conquest.

Background

Atahualpa had just won the Inca civil war against his half-brother Huáscar and was resting at the hot springs near Cajamarca with his victorious army when news came of the strangers advancing from the coast. Pizarro's 168 men marched up into the Andes and sent envoys — Hernando de Soto and later Hernando Pizarro — to invite Atahualpa to a meeting in the town. Confident in his overwhelming numbers, Atahualpa agreed to come the following day with a large but lightly armed procession.

The ambush

On the afternoon of 16 November 1532 Atahualpa entered the plaza carried on a litter, surrounded by several thousand nobles and attendants. The Spanish friar Vicente de Valverde approached with an interpreter and, according to Spanish accounts, presented the Requerimiento and a breviary, which Atahualpa examined and threw to the ground. At Pizarro's signal the hidden Spaniards attacked: artillery and arquebus fire from the rooftops, cavalry charges from the great halls (kallankas) on three sides of the plaza, and infantry with steel swords. Panic crushed many against the walls; a section of the plaza wall collapsed under the press of fleeing people. Pizarro himself seized Atahualpa, receiving a slight wound — the only Spanish injury recorded — from one of his own men.

Aftermath

Atahualpa, held prisoner in Cajamarca for eight months, offered and delivered the famous ransom: a room filled to a marked line with gold objects and twice with silver. He continued to rule through his captors, ordering Huáscar's execution to prevent a rescue, but in July 1533 the Spanish tried and executed him — baptised and then garroted — on charges including treason and fratricide. The road to Cusco now lay open, and the empire, deprived of its divine ruler at the moment of victory in civil war, could not mount a unified resistance.
