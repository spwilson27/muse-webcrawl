The iris (pl. irides or irises) is a thin, annular structure in the eye in most mammals and birds, responsible for controlling the diameter and size of the pupil, and thus the amount of light reaching the retina. Eye colour is defined by the colour of the iris. In optical terms, the pupil is the eye's aperture, while the iris is the diaphragm.

Structure

The iris consists of two layers: the front pigmented fibrovascular layer known as the stroma, and beneath it pigmented epithelial cells. The stroma connects to a sphincter muscle (the sphincter pupillae), which contracts the pupil in a circular motion, and a set of dilator muscles (the dilator pupillae), which pull the iris radially to enlarge the pupil.

Colour

The colour of the iris ranges from light blue to dark brown. It depends principally on the concentration of melanin in the iris pigment epithelium and stroma, and on light-scattering effects in the stroma itself. Blue eyes result from low stromal melanin combined with scattering, whereas brown eyes have high melanin concentrations. Conditions such as heterochromia produce irises of different colours, and albinism can leave the iris nearly unpigmented.

Control of pupil size

In bright light the sphincter muscle constricts the pupil (miosis); in dim light the dilator muscle enlarges it (mydriasis). These reflexes are mediated by the parasympathetic and sympathetic nervous systems respectively, via the Edinger–Westphal nucleus and the ciliary ganglion for constriction.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the iris is called iritis, a form of anterior uveitis. Congenital absence of part of the iris (coloboma) and complete absence (aniridia) also occur. The iris's unique pattern is used in iris recognition biometric systems.
