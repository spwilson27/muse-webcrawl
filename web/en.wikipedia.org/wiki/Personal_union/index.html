Personal union

A personal union is the combination of two or more states that have the same person as their monarch, while their boundaries, laws, and interests remain distinct. It differs from a real union or federation, in which the component states share some institutions of government, and from a mere shared succession in a composite monarchy. A personal union may arise by inheritance, election, or marriage, and typically dissolves when the successions of the partner states diverge.

Historical examples

  • Poland and Lithuania — from the Union of Krewo (1385), Jogaila became king of Poland while remaining grand duke of Lithuania; the personal union later deepened into the real union of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (1569).
  • England and Scotland — the Union of the Crowns (1603), when James VI of Scotland inherited the English throne; the kingdoms remained separate states until the Acts of Union 1707.
  • Great Britain and Hanover (1714–1837) — ended when Victoria, barred by Salic law from the Hanoverian throne, succeeded in Britain while Ernest Augustus became king of Hanover.
  • The Iberian Union (1580–1640) — Portugal under the Spanish Habsburg kings, keeping its own laws and administration.
  • Denmark and Norway, Sweden and Norway (1814–1905), and the Netherlands and Luxembourg (1815–1890) are further examples.

Present day

The closest modern analogue is the Commonwealth realms, fifteen independent states sharing Charles III as head of state; whether this constitutes a personal union is debated, since the Crown is legally divided into separate crowns for each realm. The co-principality of Andorra, whose co-princes are the president of France and the bishop of Urgell, also preserves an archaic shared arrangement.
