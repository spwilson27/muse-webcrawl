Insolvency

Insolvency is the state of being unable to pay one's debts. In legal terms, a debtor is insolvent when they cannot pay their debts as they fall due (cash-flow insolvency) or when their liabilities exceed their assets (balance-sheet insolvency). Insolvency is not the same as bankruptcy: insolvency is a financial condition, whereas bankruptcy (and corporate procedures such as liquidation and administration) is a legal process that may result from it. Insolvency law aims to balance the interests of the debtor, their creditors and the wider public, either by rescuing the debtor as a going concern or by winding up their affairs in an orderly way.

Insolvent businesses commonly enter liquidation or administration.

Tests for insolvency

Under the UK Insolvency Act 1986 (section 123), a company is deemed unable to pay its debts if, among other things, it fails to satisfy a statutory demand for a debt over £750 within three weeks, a judgment remains unsatisfied, it is proved the company cannot pay debts as they fall due, or the value of its assets is less than its liabilities. Similar twin tests appear in most common law systems; in the United States the federal Bankruptcy Code defines "insolvent" principally by the balance-sheet test, while fraudulent-transfer law also uses cash-flow concepts.

Processes

Companies

  • Liquidation (winding up) — a liquidator realises the company's assets, distributes proceeds to creditors in a statutory order, and the company is dissolved. It may be compulsory (court-ordered) or voluntary (creditors' or members').
  • Administration — an administrator (a licensed insolvency practitioner) takes control with the aim of rescuing the company, or achieving a better result for creditors than liquidation; a statutory moratorium blocks creditor action.
  • Company voluntary arrangement (CVA) — a binding compromise with creditors approved by the requisite majorities.
  • Receivership — historically, appointment by a secured creditor holding a floating charge; largely replaced by administration in the UK since 2003.

Individuals

Individuals may be made bankrupt on their own or a creditor's petition; alternatives include individual voluntary arrangements (IVAs) and debt relief orders for low-asset debtors. In the US, individuals and businesses file under Chapter 7 (liquidation), Chapter 11 (reorganisation) or Chapter 13 (repayment plans) of the Bankruptcy Code.

Creditor priority and wrongful trading

Distributions follow a statutory hierarchy: secured creditors, preferential creditors (including certain employee claims and tax authorities), unsecured creditors, and shareholders last. Directors of insolvent companies face duties to creditors; wrongful trading (continuing to trade when insolvent liquidation was unavoidable) and fraudulent trading can lead to personal liability and disqualification.

Cross-border insolvency

Multinational failures are coordinated through instruments such as the UNCITRAL Model Law on Cross-Border Insolvency (1997) and the EU Insolvency Regulation, which provide for recognition of foreign proceedings and cooperation between courts.
