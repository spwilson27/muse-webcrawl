The Spree (German: [ʃpʁeː]; Sorbian: Sprjewja) is a river in eastern Germany that flows through the states of Saxony, Brandenburg, and Berlin and joins the Havel at Spandau. About 400 kilometres (250 mi) long, it is best known as the river of Berlin: the city's historic centre grew up on its banks, and its channels define landmarks such as Museum Island, the Reichstag, and the East Side Gallery stretch of the former Berlin Wall.

Course

The Spree rises in the Lusatian Highlands near the Czech border, from springs traditionally counted at Ebersbach, Neugersdorf, and the Kottmar mountain. It flows north through Bautzen and Spremberg, then spreads into the branching channels and wetlands of the Spreewald, a UNESCO biosphere reserve famous for its punting boats, sorbian culture, and pickled gherkins. North of the Spreewald the river crosses the lakes east of Berlin — including the Schwielochsee and Müggelsee — and winds through the capital in a series of channels and canals before emptying into the Havel.

Navigation and canals

Long stretches of the Spree are navigable and form part of the federal waterway network linking Berlin with the Oder via the Oder–Spree Canal and with the Elbe via the Havel and the Elbe–Havel Canal. The Landwehr Canal and the Berlin–Spandau Ship Canal provide alternative routes through the city. Although commercial freight has declined, the river carries heavy tour-boat and leisure traffic.

Ecology and use

The river supplied Berlin's drinking water through bank filtration and historically received the city's sewage; water quality has improved greatly since reunification. A well-known challenge is ochre pollution from former lignite mining in Lusatia, which affects upstream reaches. A bathing project, Flussbad Berlin, has proposed restoring an inner-city stretch for swimming.
