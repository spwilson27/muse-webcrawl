Mole poblano

Mole poblano is the best known of all mole varieties. The dish originated in the state of Puebla, Mexico, and is often regarded as the national dish of Mexico. The term mole comes from the Nahuatl word molli, meaning a sauce or mixture, while poblano refers to something from Puebla.

The sauce is typically a dark, reddish-brown mixture of dried chili peppers, spices, and a small amount of chocolate, and generally contains about twenty ingredients. Classic components include mulato, ancho, and pasilla chiles, along with spices such as cinnamon, cloves, and black pepper; nuts and seeds such as almonds, peanuts, and sesame seeds; raisins; tomatoes or tomatillos; and stale tortillas or bread as a thickener. The chocolate is used to round out the flavor rather than to make the dish sweet.

History

A famous legend holds that the sauce was invented by nuns at the Convent of Santa Rosa in Puebla in the late 17th century, who, upon learning that the archbishop (in some versions the viceroy) would visit, hurriedly combined the ingredients they had on hand. Another legend credits the wind, which supposedly blew spices into pots of sauce being prepared for a celebration. Whatever its true origin, the dish almost certainly evolved from a blending of indigenous Mesoamerican cooking with ingredients and techniques brought from Spain.

Preparation and serving

Preparing mole poblano is labor-intensive: the chiles are toasted and soaked, and all the ingredients are ground together — traditionally on a metate, now usually in a blender — and then simmered for hours. The sauce is most commonly served over boiled chicken or turkey and sprinkled with sesame seeds. Enmoladas (enchiladas covered in mole) are another common presentation. Mole poblano is central to celebrations in Puebla, notably Cinco de Mayo, and is a fixture of festivals such as the Festival del Mole Poblano held in Puebla each June.
