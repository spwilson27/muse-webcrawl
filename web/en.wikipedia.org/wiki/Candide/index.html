Full titleCandide, ou l'Optimisme

Candide, ou l'Optimisme (Candide, or Optimism) is a French satirical novella by Voltaire, first published in 1759. It follows the adventures of the young Candide, who is indoctrinated with the philosophy of optimism by his tutor Pangloss — the doctrine that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds" — and is then subjected to a relentless series of disasters that test that belief.

Plot

Expelled from the castle of the Baron Thunder-ten-Tronckh in Westphalia for kissing the baron's daughter Cunégonde, Candide is pressed into the Bulgarian army, witnesses war, the Lisbon earthquake and an auto-da-fé, travels to the utopian land of Eldorado, and journeys through South America and Europe. Along the way he is repeatedly reunited with and separated from Cunégonde and Pangloss, encounters the pessimist Martin, and finally settles on a small farm near Constantinople.

Themes

The novella satirises the optimistic philosophy associated with Leibniz, as well as organised religion, war, colonialism and human cruelty, many of its episodes echoing real events such as the 1755 Lisbon earthquake and the Seven Years' War. Its famous conclusion rejects abstract philosophy in favour of practical work: "we must cultivate our garden."

Reception and legacy

Banned soon after publication for religious blasphemy and political sedition, Candide was an immediate bestseller and is now regarded as Voltaire's masterpiece and a classic of Western literature. It has been adapted many times, including Leonard Bernstein's 1956 operetta Candide.
