Flint–Worcester tornado outbreak sequence

The Flint–Worcester tornado outbreak sequence was a severe multi-day outbreak of tornadoes that affected the Midwestern and Northeastern United States between June 6 and June 11, 1953. It is remembered chiefly for two exceptionally destructive F5 tornadoes: one that struck Flint, Michigan, on June 8, and another that struck Worcester, Massachusetts, on June 9 — the latter the deadliest tornado in New England history.

Damage from the Worcester tornado of June 9, 1953

Flint tornado

On the evening of June 8, 1953, a violent tornado cut a path through the northern suburbs of Flint, Michigan, particularly the Beecher district. It killed 116 people and injured 844, making it the deadliest tornado in Michigan history and the last tornado in the United States to claim more than 100 lives as a single event until the Joplin tornado of 2011.

Worcester tornado

The next afternoon, June 9, a tornado from the same weather system developed over central Massachusetts and tore through Worcester and surrounding towns. It killed 94 people, injured over 1,200, and left about 10,000 homeless. Debris from the storm was carried as far as the Boston suburbs and fell into the Atlantic Ocean. The Worcester tornado remains the deadliest ever recorded in New England, and its unexpected occurrence in the region prompted debate in Congress that helped spur the development of a national severe-weather warning and radar network.

Meteorological background

The outbreak occurred as a strong upper-level trough, very warm and humid air at the surface, and a stalled frontal boundary combined over the Great Lakes and Northeast. In this era before weather satellites and Doppler radar, warnings were rudimentary, and both the Flint and Worcester storms struck with little or no advance notice.
