Entasis

In architecture, entasis is the application of a slight convex curve to a surface, such as the shaft of a column, to counteract the optical illusion that straight-sided columns appear concave. The term derives from Greek enteinein, "to strain or stretch".

Classical use

Entasis is most famously applied in Greek Doric temples, notably the Parthenon in Athens (5th century BC), where columns also lean slightly inward and the stylobate curves upward—refinements documented by Vitruvius and measured in the 19th century. Earlier examples occur in Egyptian architecture and in archaic Greek temples at Paestum.

Later use

Renaissance architects including Bramante revived entasis, and it appears in Japanese temple pillars and in modern works. The degree of curvature is subtle: on the Parthenon the deviation is only a few centimetres over the column's height.
