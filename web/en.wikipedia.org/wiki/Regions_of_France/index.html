Regions of France

France is divided into eighteen administrative regions (French: régions, singular région), of which thirteen are located in metropolitan France (including the territorial collectivity of Corsica), while the other five are overseas regions. Regions are the first-level administrative divisions of the country, above departments and communes. Each region is administered by an elected regional council (conseil régional) and a prefect, and has competences including regional transport, economic development, high schools (lycées) and vocational training.

History

Regional groupings date from the creation of regional prefectures in 1960 and "regional action districts" in 1964, evolving from proposals by the geographer Jean-François Gravier and others. Regions became public establishments in 1972 and territorial collectivities with directly elected councils (first elections 1986) under the decentralisation laws of 1982. A major reform under President François Hollande reduced the number of metropolitan regions from 22 to 13 on 1 January 2016, merging, for example, Alsace, Champagne-Ardenne and Lorraine into Grand Est, and Nord-Pas-de-Calais and Picardy into Hauts-de-France, in the name of scale and efficiency. New official names for the merged regions were adopted later in 2016.

Overseas regions

The five overseas regions are simultaneously overseas departments: Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana (Guyane), Réunion and Mayotte (which became a department and region in 2011). They are integral parts of the French Republic and of the European Union, with adaptations permitted by Article 73 of the Constitution.

Competences and debate

Regions manage regional rail services (TER), build and maintain lycées, fund vocational training and apprenticeships, administer EU structural funds and promote economic development and tourism. Critics of the 2016 mergers argue they created oversized regions remote from citizens and erased historic identities — a point pressed especially in Alsace, which in 2021 obtained a partial remedy as the European Collectivity of Alsace within Grand Est.
