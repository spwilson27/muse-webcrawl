Homelessness in the United States

Homelessness in the United States refers to the phenomenon of people lacking stable, safe, and adequate housing. The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) conducts an annual Point-in-Time count on a single night in January; the 2024 count recorded more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness, the highest figure since national reporting began, with roughly two-thirds sheltered and one-third unsheltered. Counts are widely considered undercounts, and over the course of a year considerably more people experience homelessness than on any single night.

Causes

Research and federal reports identify the primary drivers as the shortage of affordable rental housing relative to incomes, stagnant wages, and the cost of housing in high-demand metropolitan areas. Individual-level risk factors include poverty, unemployment, eviction, mental illness, substance use disorders, domestic violence, and discharge from institutions (foster care, prisons, hospitals) without housing. Veterans, unaccompanied youth, families with children, and people with chronic homelessness form specially tracked populations; veterans' homelessness fell substantially after 2009 under targeted federal programs, while family and unsheltered homelessness rose in many cities during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.

Responses

The federal framework derives from the McKinney–Vento Homeless Assistance Act of 1987, the first major federal homelessness legislation, amended by the HEARTH Act of 2009. Interventions include emergency shelters, transitional housing, rapid rehousing, and permanent supportive housing under the Housing First model, which provides housing without preconditions of sobriety or treatment and has been shown to improve housing stability for chronically homeless people. Cities have also adopted contested measures regulating sleeping and camping in public spaces; in 2024 the Supreme Court held in City of Grants Pass v. Johnson that enforcing such ordinances against homeless people does not violate the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment.

History

Modern mass homelessness is generally dated to the late 1970s and 1980s, attributed to deinstitutionalization of people with mental illness, cuts to federal housing budgets, loss of single-room-occupancy housing, and economic changes — building on longer histories of transient poverty from the "tramps" of the 1870s to the Hoovervilles of the Great Depression.
