Franco-Spanish War (1635–1659)

The Franco-Spanish War (1635–1659) was fought between France and Spain, with the participation of a large number of other powers. It began as part of the wider Thirty Years' War but continued for eleven years after the Peace of Westphalia ended that conflict in 1648, finally concluding with the Treaty of the Pyrenees in 1659. The war marked the decline of Spain as the dominant power in Europe and the rise of France under Louis XIV.

Background

By the 1630s, Bourbon France felt encircled by the territories of the Spanish and Austrian Habsburgs. Cardinal Richelieu, chief minister to Louis XIII, subsidised Spain's enemies — Sweden, the Dutch Republic, and German Protestant princes — before deciding on open war. France declared war on Spain on 19 May 1635 after Spanish troops seized the Electorate of Trier, a French protectorate.

Course of the war

Early fighting went badly for France: Spanish forces invaded from Flanders in 1636, the "Year of Corbie," advancing close to Paris. France gradually recovered, winning important successes under commanders such as the Prince de Condé, whose victory at the Battle of Rocroi in 1643 shattered the reputation of the Spanish tercios. France also intervened in Catalonia, where a revolt against Madrid began in 1640, and supported Portugal's restoration of independence from Spain.

The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended France's war with the Holy Roman Empire but not with Spain. The conflict was prolonged by the Fronde, a series of French civil wars (1648–1653) during which Condé defected to Spanish service. After the Fronde, Cardinal Mazarin rebuilt French strength and allied with Oliver Cromwell's England. The Anglo-French victory at the Battle of the Dunes near Dunkirk in 1658 finally pushed Spain to negotiate.

Treaty of the Pyrenees

The war ended with the Treaty of the Pyrenees, signed on 7 November 1659 on Pheasant Island in the Bidasoa River. France gained Roussillon, northern Cerdanya, Artois, and several fortresses in Flanders and Luxembourg, fixing the Franco-Spanish border along the Pyrenees. The treaty also arranged the marriage of Louis XIV to Maria Theresa, daughter of Philip IV of Spain — a union whose unpaid dowry later served as a pretext for further French claims.
