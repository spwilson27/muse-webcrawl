Coefficient of friction

A block on a surface: friction opposes the applied force up to a maximum proportional to the normal force.

The coefficient of friction, commonly symbolized by the Greek letter μ (mu), is a dimensionless scalar value that describes the ratio of the force of friction between two bodies to the force pressing them together. It depends on the materials used; for example, ice on steel has a low coefficient of friction, while rubber on pavement has a high coefficient.

Types

Two kinds of friction coefficient are distinguished. The coefficient of static friction (μs) applies when the surfaces are at rest relative to each other: the maximum static friction force is F = μsN, where N is the normal force. The coefficient of kinetic friction (μk) applies once sliding has begun, and is usually somewhat smaller than the static coefficient for the same pair of surfaces.

Typical values

Approximate values include: steel on steel (dry) about 0.5–0.8; steel on steel (lubricated) about 0.16; rubber on dry concrete about 0.6–0.85; wood on wood about 0.25–0.5; ice on ice about 0.03; and Teflon on steel about 0.04, among the lowest of common solid pairs. Values above 1 are possible where surfaces adhere or deform.

Measurement

The static coefficient can be measured with an inclined plane: the angle of repose θ at which an object just begins to slide satisfies μs = tan θ. Kinetic coefficients are measured by dragging an object at constant velocity and dividing the pulling force by the normal force.

Limitations

The coefficient of friction is an empirical property, not a fundamental material constant. It varies with surface roughness, contamination, temperature, sliding speed and contact pressure, and Amontons' laws—that friction is proportional to load and independent of apparent contact area—are approximations that hold well for many dry contacts but fail for polymers, lubricated surfaces and very smooth or adhesive contacts.
