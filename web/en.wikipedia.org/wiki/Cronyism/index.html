Cronyism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cronyism
Type: Form of favouritism / corruption
Related: Nepotism, patronage, conflict of interest
Etymology: "Crony" (17th-century Cambridge slang for a close friend) + -ism
An editorial-style illustration of business and political insiders exchanging favours, the classic depiction of cronyism in appointments and contracts.

Cronyism is the practice of partiality in awarding jobs, contracts, and other advantages to friends or trusted colleagues ("cronies"), especially in politics and business, regardless of their qualifications. It is related to nepotism, which concerns favouritism toward family members, and to patronage, the distribution of offices by political leaders to their supporters.

Description

Cronyism arises where personal relationships displace merit and open competition: appointments to boards and public office, the award of government contracts, access to permits and credit, and regulatory leniency. Economists associate entrenched cronyism with "crony capitalism", an economy in which business success depends on close relationships between business people and government officials, distorting markets and deterring investment and competition.

Effects and criticism

Cronyism is criticised for wasting public resources, entrenching incompetence, corroding trust in institutions, and narrowing opportunity to insiders. Anti-corruption frameworks — procurement rules, conflict-of-interest law, merit-based civil service systems, and transparency in appointments — are largely designed to counter it. In the United States, the spoils system of the nineteenth century, in which federal jobs went to political supporters, prompted civil service reform beginning with the Pendleton Act of 1883.

Examples in public debate

Accusations of cronyism feature in scandals across many countries, from the allocation of state contracts to politically connected firms, to the "revolving door" between regulators and the industries they regulate. Because cronies are by definition friends rather than relatives, the practice can be harder to police than nepotism and is often lawful in form even when corrosive in effect.
