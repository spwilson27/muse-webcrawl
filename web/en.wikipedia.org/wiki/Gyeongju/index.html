Gyeongju (Korean: 경주) is a coastal city in the far south-east of North Gyeongsang Province, South Korea. It was the capital of the ancient kingdom of Silla for nearly a millennium — from 57 BC until AD 935 — and at its height in the 8th–9th centuries was one of the most prosperous cities in East Asia. Often described as an "open-air museum", the city preserves an exceptional concentration of Korean Buddhist art, royal tombs and palace sites.

History

Gyeongju began as Saro-guk, a chiefdom of the Jinhan confederacy, whose ruling clans — Park, Seok and Kim — founded the Silla kingdom; the Kim clan held the throne for most of its history from King Naemul (356). Silla unified most of the Korean peninsula by 668. The unified capital, then called Seorabeol or Geumseong, featured the Wolseong palace, the artificial Anapji (Donggung Palace and Wolji Pond), and great temples such as Hwangnyongsa, whose nine-storey wooden pagoda was destroyed in the Mongol invasions. After Silla's fall to Goryeo in 935, Gyeongju declined to provincial status under its present name (given in 987).

Heritage sites

The Gyeongju Historic Areas, designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000, comprise five zones including the Tumuli Park belt of huge grass-covered royal burial mounds (such as Cheonmachong, the "Heavenly Horse Tomb", which yielded a famous gold crown), the Namsan mountain belt of Buddhist sculpture, and the Wolseong palace belt. Nearby stand Bulguksa temple and the Seokguram grotto (together a separate World Heritage listing, 1995), the 7th-century Cheomseongdae observatory — the oldest surviving astronomical observatory in Asia — and the Gyeongju National Museum, home to the Divine Bell of King Seongdeok.

Modern city

Today Gyeongju is a major domestic tourist destination and an administrative and energy centre: the Wolseong Nuclear Power Plant and the Korea Hydro & Nuclear Power headquarters are located there, as is a national radioactive-waste repository. It is also famed for Gyeongju-ppang (red-bean "Hwangnam bread").
