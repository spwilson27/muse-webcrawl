Algol, designated Beta Persei, is a bright multiple star system in the constellation of Perseus and one of the best-known eclipsing variable stars. Its name derives from the Arabic Raʾs al-Ghūl, "the Demon's Head", and it has long been associated with the severed head of Medusa carried by the hero Perseus.

Variability

Algol's brightness drops from magnitude 2.1 to 3.4 for about ten hours every 2.867 days, when the dimmer companion star passes in front of the brighter primary. This variability had evidently been noticed in antiquity, but it was first recorded scientifically by Geminiano Montanari in 1669, and John Goodricke explained the eclipsing mechanism in 1783. Algol gave its name to the class of "Algol variables" — eclipsing binaries with similar light curves.

The Algol paradox

The system posed a famous puzzle, the Algol paradox: the less massive component is a subgiant — a more evolved star — while the more massive component remains on the main sequence, apparently contradicting the principle that more massive stars evolve faster. The paradox was resolved by recognising mass transfer: the now-less-massive star was originally the more massive and evolved first, donating most of its envelope to its companion.

System

Algol Aa1 is a hot B-type main-sequence star; Aa2 is a cooler K-type subgiant that fills its Roche lobe. A third star, Algol Ab, orbits the pair with a period of about 681 days. The system lies roughly 90 light-years from the Sun.
