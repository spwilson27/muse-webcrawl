Error theory (ethics)

Error theory in ethics is a metaethical view holding that ordinary moral judgments are truth-apt but systematically false, because they presuppose the existence of objective moral values or categorical reasons that do not in fact exist. The position is most closely associated with J. L. Mackie, who defended it in Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977), and with Richard Joyce.

Argument

Mackie's case rested principally on two arguments. The argument from relativity points to persistent moral disagreement between cultures and eras as best explained by differing ways of life rather than perception of objective values. The argument from queerness holds that objectively prescriptive values would be entities "of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe", knowable only by a special faculty of moral intuition. Since moral discourse nonetheless commits speakers to such objectivity, all positive first-order moral claims — "stealing is wrong" — come out false on the error theorist's view, in the way that claims about unicorns' habits are false because unicorns do not exist.

Variants and responses

Error theorists differ on what should follow. Some, like Joyce, advocate moral fictionalism: continue using moral language as a useful fiction. Others propose abolishing or reforming moral discourse. Critics object that error theory proves too much (threatening epistemic norms as well), that its account of moral language's commitments is contestable, and that companions-in-guilt arguments show parallel reasoning would undermine reasons for belief generally. Error theory is a form of moral nihilism and contrasts with non-cognitivism, which denies that moral judgments are truth-apt at all, and with moral realism, which accepts the objective values error theory rejects.
