Ranch-style house

A ranch-style house (also American ranch, California ranch, rambler, or rancher) is a domestic architectural style that originated in the United States in the 1920s and became the dominant house type in the post-war building boom of the 1950s and 1960s. It is noted for its long, close-to-the-ground profile and wide open layout.

Characteristics

  • Single story, often with a low-pitched roof with wide eaves
  • Long, horizontal silhouette, frequently L-shaped or U-shaped
  • Open floor plan combining living and dining areas
  • Sliding glass doors opening to a patio, emphasising indoor–outdoor living
  • Integrated attached garage
  • Simple trim and mixed exterior materials such as brick, wood, and stucco

History

The style drew on the Spanish colonial ranchos of the American Southwest, the Prairie School, and the work of architects such as Cliff May in California, whose 1930s ranch houses popularised the form. After World War II, developers such as Joseph Eichler built thousands of modernist ranch homes, and by the 1950s nine of every ten new houses in the United States were ranches. The style fell from favour in the 1970s as neo-eclectic designs rose, but ranch homes remain a large share of the existing housing stock and have seen revived interest for their single-level accessibility.

Variations

Variants include the split-level ranch, the raised ranch, the California ranch, and the suburban ranch; later "storybook" and contemporary versions added decorative detailing. The rambler is a nearly identical style, with the terms used interchangeably in many regions.
