Al-Hakim Mosque

The Al-Hakim Mosque (Arabic: مسجد الحاكم, Masjid al-Ḥākim), also known as al-Jamiʿ al-Anwar ("the Mosque of Light"), is a major Fatimid-era mosque in Islamic Cairo, Egypt. Construction was begun by the caliph al-Aziz Billah in 990 CE and completed in 1013 under his son al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah, after whom it is named, making it the second-largest mosque in Cairo after the Mosque of Ibn Tulun. Its two massive stone minarets flanking the monumental entrance portal are among the oldest surviving in Egypt.

History and restoration

Over the centuries the mosque served many purposes: it was used as a fortress by Crusader prisoners, as a stable and warehouse under Saladin and Napoleon, later as a school and a storehouse for the Museum of Islamic Art. By the 1970s it was semi-ruinous. A controversial restoration completed in 1980 by the Dawoodi Bohra community under Syedna Mohammed Burhanuddin rebuilt the interior with white marble and gold ornament, transforming the building into a functioning mosque whose gleaming interior contrasts with its weathered Fatimid exterior. The mosque stands just inside Bab al-Futuh at the northern wall of Fatimid Cairo. See also Al-Azhar Mosque, Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah and Fatimid Caliphate.
