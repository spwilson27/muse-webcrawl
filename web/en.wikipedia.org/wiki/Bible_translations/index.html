Bible translations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Bible has been translated into many languages from the biblical languages of Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek. As of the 21st century, the full Bible has been translated into over 750 languages, the New Testament into well over 1,600 languages, and portions of Scripture into more than 3,500 languages in total — making it by far the most translated book in history.

Ancient translations

The earliest translation was the Septuagint, a Greek version of the Hebrew Bible produced in Alexandria from the 3rd century BCE for Greek-speaking Jews. Early Christian translations included the Syriac Peshitta, Coptic versions, and Old Latin texts. Around 382 CE Jerome produced the Latin Vulgate, revising the Gospels and translating the Old Testament largely from Hebrew; it became the standard Bible of Western Christianity for over a thousand years.

Medieval and Reformation translations

Vernacular translations appeared throughout the Middle Ages, though often controversially — the Wycliffe Bible in English (1380s) was produced by followers of John Wycliffe. The printing press transformed translation: Gutenberg printed the Latin Bible around 1455. Martin Luther translated the New Testament into German in 1522 (full Bible 1534), a foundational work for the German language. William Tyndale's English New Testament (1526), translated from Greek, underlies much of the later King James Version (1611), whose translators drew heavily on his phrasing.

Modern era

The 19th and 20th centuries saw an explosion of missionary translation. Organisations such as the British and Foreign Bible Society (founded 1804), the United Bible Societies, and Wycliffe Bible Translators (SIL) have coordinated translation into thousands of languages. Modern approaches debate "formal equivalence" (word-for-word) versus "dynamic/functional equivalence" (meaning-for-meaning), reflected in versions ranging from the literal NASB to the idiomatic NIV, NRSV and The Message.
