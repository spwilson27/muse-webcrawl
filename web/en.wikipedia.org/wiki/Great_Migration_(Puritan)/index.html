Great Migration (Puritan)

The arrival of John Winthrop's fleet at Salem, 1630.

The Great Migration was the migration in the period 1628 to 1640 of some 20,000 English Puritans to New England in North America, chiefly to the Massachusetts Bay Colony. Unlike many other colonial ventures, the migration was heavily composed of entire families and was driven primarily by religious motives: the desire to worship free of the policies of the Church of England under Archbishop William Laud and Charles I, and to build a godly commonwealth in the wilderness.

Course of the migration

The vanguard arrived with John Endecott at Salem in 1628 under the New England Company. In 1630 a fleet of eleven ships led by John Winthrop — famous for his sermon "A Model of Christian Charity", with its image of a "city upon a hill" — brought about 700 settlers and founded Boston. Over the following decade ships arrived almost every year, founding towns such as Charlestown, Dorchester, Ipswich, New Haven, Hartford and Providence. The migrants were disproportionately drawn from East Anglia and the West Country, were more literate and middling in status than typical emigrants, and travelled as households with servants.

End and significance

Migration slowed sharply after 1640, when the English Civil War made reform at home seem possible; some colonists even returned to fight for Parliament. The settlers established a distinctive society of self-governing congregational towns, high literacy and Harvard College (founded 1636). Their descendants and institutions profoundly shaped the culture of New England and, through it, the United States. The migration also brought devastating consequences for Indigenous peoples, whose lands were taken and whose populations were ravaged by European diseases, culminating in conflicts such as the Pequot War (1636–1638).
