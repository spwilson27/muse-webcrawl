Angel

In various theistic religious traditions, an angel is a supernatural spiritual being who serves God (or the gods) and often acts as a messenger to humanity. The English word derives from Greek angelos (ἄγγελος), "messenger", used in the Septuagint to translate the Hebrew mal'akh. Angels are central to Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, and comparable beings appear in Zoroastrianism, Sikhism, and other faiths.

Angels are conventionally depicted in Western art as winged human figures.

In Judaism

In the Hebrew Bible, angels appear as messengers of God — visiting Abraham, wrestling with Jacob, and announcing divine judgement or blessing. Named angels such as Michael and Gabriel emerge especially in Second Temple literature, notably the Book of Daniel. Rabbinic tradition developed elaborate angelology, including angels assigned to nations and to individuals.

In Christianity

Christian tradition orders angels into a hierarchy articulated by Pseudo-Dionysius (c. 5th century): three triads comprising seraphim, cherubim, and thrones; dominions, virtues, and powers; principalities, archangels, and angels. Angels announce the Incarnation (Gabriel to Mary), guard individuals (guardian angels), and war against demonic forces; the archangel Michael leads the heavenly host against Satan in the Book of Revelation. Fallen angels — those who rebelled — are identified with demons.

In Islam

Angels (malāʾika) are beings created from light who unfailingly obey God. Jibril (Gabriel) transmitted the Qur'an to Muhammad; Mikail (Michael) provides sustenance; Israfil will blow the trumpet at the Day of Judgement; and Azrael is the angel of death. Each person is accompanied by recording angels (Kiraman Katibin) who note their deeds.
