Lich

In fantasy fiction, a lich is a type of undead creature, usually a powerful spellcaster — often a necromancer or wizard — who has used magic to bind their intellect and soul to a physical object, commonly called a phylactery, thereby achieving a form of immortality. The lich's body withers into a skeletal or desiccated state, but its mind and magical power persist as long as the phylactery survives.

Etymology

The word lich comes from Old English līċ, meaning "corpse" or "body", cognate with German Leiche (corpse). It survives in words such as lychgate, the covered gateway to a churchyard. Its modern fantasy sense of an undead sorcerer was popularised in the 20th century.

History in fiction and games

Undead magicians who cheat death appear in the sword-and-sorcery stories of Clark Ashton Smith and in Fritz Leiber's Fafhrd and the Gray Mouser stories. The creature entered popular culture through the role-playing game Dungeons & Dragons, whose 1977 Monster Manual codified the lich as a skeletal undead spellcaster that stores its life force in a phylactery and is destroyed only when the phylactery is also destroyed. From there the lich spread widely through fantasy games, novels and films.

Characteristics

  • Undeath achieved deliberately through ritual and necromancy, rather than by curse or accident
  • Soul bound to a phylactery; destroying the body alone only delays the lich
  • Retention of intelligence, personality and spellcasting ability, often exceeding living mages
  • Immunity to many effects that harm the living, and typically an aura of fear

Notable liches in popular culture include Vecna and Acererak in Dungeons & Dragons lore, and Kel'Thuzad in the Warcraft universe.
