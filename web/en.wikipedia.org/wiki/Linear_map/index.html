Linear map

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, and more specifically in linear algebra, a linear map (also called a linear mapping, linear transformation, vector space homomorphism, or in some contexts linear function) is a mapping V → W between two vector spaces that preserves the operations of vector addition and scalar multiplication.

Definition

A function f: V → W between two vector spaces over the same field is linear if for all vectors u, v in V and all scalars c,

f(u + v) = f(u) + f(v)    and    f(cu) = c·f(u).

Equivalently, f preserves all linear combinations. Every linear map sends the zero vector of V to the zero vector of W.

Examples

Rotation, reflection, scaling and projection in the plane or in space are linear maps, as are differentiation and integration of functions, the Fourier transform, and multiplication by a fixed matrix. Indeed, in finite dimensions every linear map between coordinate spaces is given by matrix multiplication: choosing bases for V and W yields a matrix A with f(x) = Ax, and the columns of A are the images of the basis vectors of V.

Kernel and image

The kernel (null space) of f is the subspace of V mapped to zero; the image (range) is the subspace of W consisting of all values f(v). The rank–nullity theorem states that the dimension of V equals the dimension of the kernel plus the dimension of the image. A linear map is an isomorphism precisely when its kernel is trivial and its image is all of W, in which case the two spaces have the same dimension and an inverse linear map exists.
