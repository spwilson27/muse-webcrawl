Corpus linguistics is the study of language as expressed in corpora — large, structured collections of authentic texts assembled for linguistic analysis. Corpus linguists use computational tools to search, count and compare linguistic features, deriving evidence about usage, frequency and patterning that introspection alone cannot supply. The field underlies modern lexicography, language teaching materials, forensic linguistics and much of natural language processing.

History

Although scholars used text collections earlier (including concordances of the Bible and Shakespeare), modern corpus linguistics began with the Brown Corpus, compiled at Brown University by Henry Kučera and W. Nelson Francis and published in 1964: one million words of American English sampled from texts of 1961. The British National Corpus (100 million words, 1990s) and the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA, over one billion words) extended the scale. John Sinclair's work at Birmingham, including the COBUILD dictionary project, championed the "corpus-driven" approach, letting patterns emerge from the data.

Methods

Corpora are typically annotated with metadata and linguistic tags such as part-of-speech labels and lemmas. Analysts use concordancers to view keywords in context (KWIC), compute word frequencies and collocations (words that habitually co-occur), and compare sub-corpora statistically. Key concepts include representativeness, balance, corpus-based versus corpus-driven methodology, and the distinction between monitor corpora (continuously updated) and static sample corpora.

Applications

Corpus evidence revolutionised dictionary-making, revealing the most common senses and collocations of words; it informs grammar writing, translation, language assessment, stylistics, discourse analysis and the training and evaluation of language models.
