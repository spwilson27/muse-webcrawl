Reims (also spelled Rheims in older English usage) is a city in northeastern France, in the Marne department of the Grand Est region, about 130 kilometres east-northeast of Paris. It is famous as the traditional site of the coronation of the kings of France and as a principal centre of Champagne production.

History

Reims began as Durocortorum, the capital of the Gallic Remi tribe and a major city of Roman Gaul; the Porte de Mars triumphal arch survives from this period. Around 496 Clovis, king of the Franks, was baptised at Reims by Bishop Remi, an event that bound the Frankish monarchy to the Catholic Church. From the coronation of Louis the Pious in 816, and definitively after Hugh Capet, nearly all French kings were crowned in Reims Cathedral with the Sainte Ampoule chrism; Charles VII was crowned there in 1429 in the presence of Joan of Arc. The last coronation was that of Charles X in 1825.

The city suffered heavily in the First World War: its Gothic cathedral, begun in 1211 and a masterpiece of High Gothic architecture, was shelled and burned in 1914 and painstakingly restored between the wars with international, notably Rockefeller, funding. In the Second World War, the German surrender was first signed at Eisenhower's headquarters in Reims on 7 May 1945. The Cathedral of Notre-Dame, the adjacent Palace of Tau and the former Abbey of Saint-Rémi are a UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 1991).

Champagne

Reims, with Épernay, is one of the two capitals of the Champagne wine region. The great champagne houses — Veuve Clicquot, Taittinger, Ruinart, Pommery, Lanson and others — have their headquarters and cellars (crayères, chalk pits of Roman origin) in the city, which is a hub for wine tourism.
