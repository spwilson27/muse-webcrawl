Ghrelin

Gene: GHRL (chromosome 3)
Source: Ghrelinergic cells of the gastric fundus
Receptor: Growth hormone secretagogue receptor (GHS-R)
Discovered: 1999 (Kojima et al.)

Ghrelin is a hormone produced mainly by enteroendocrine cells of the stomach — specifically the ghrelinergic cells of the gastric fundus — and circulated in the bloodstream. Often called the "hunger hormone", ghrelin levels rise before meals and fall after eating; the hormone acts on the hypothalamus to increase appetite and food intake, stimulates the release of growth hormone from the pituitary gland, and promotes fat storage. It is the only known peripherally produced orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) hormone.

Discovery and structure

Ghrelin was discovered in 1999 by Masayasu Kojima and colleagues as the endogenous ligand of the growth hormone secretagogue receptor. The name derives from "ghre", the Proto-Indo-European root for "grow", with echoes of "growth hormone release". The mature peptide has 28 amino acids and carries an unusual octanoyl modification on its third residue (serine), added by the enzyme ghrelin O-acyltransferase (GOAT); this acylation is essential for activity at the receptor. The same precursor protein also yields obestatin, a peptide once thought to oppose ghrelin's effects.

Functions

Beyond appetite, ghrelin influences reward and motivation circuits in the brain, learning and memory, sleep, gastric motility and acid secretion, glucose metabolism, and cardiovascular function. Circulating levels are typically low in obesity and high in conditions of energy deficit such as anorexia nervosa and after weight loss — including after bariatric surgery — which has made the ghrelin system a long-standing target of interest for anti-obesity drug research, though no ghrelin-blocking drug has yet reached clinical use.
