Wharenui

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A wharenui ([ˈfaɾɛnʉi]; literally "large house") is a communal meeting house of the Māori people of New Zealand, generally situated as the focal point of a marae. A wharenui is built to represent the chief ancestor of the tribe, with the building's architecture symbolising the ancestor's body: the koruru carved figure at the apex of the front gable represents the head, the maihi (bargeboards) the arms, the tāhuhu (ridge pole) the spine, the heke (rafters) the ribs, and the interior side posts (pou) the internal organs and supporting ancestors.

Function

The wharenui is used for meetings, funerals (tangihanga), weddings and other significant gatherings. Guests sleep communally on mattresses on the floor. The walls are decorated with carved panels (whakairo), woven panels (tukutuku) and painted patterns (kōwhaiwhai) that record genealogy (whakapapa), tribal history and cosmology.

Notable examples

Famous wharenui include Te Hau ki Tūranga, one of the oldest surviving carved meeting houses (built in the 1840s and now held at the Museum of New Zealand Te Papa Tongarewa), and the meeting houses built under the influence of Sir Āpirana Ngata during the Māori cultural revival of the early 20th century.
