Realism (art movement)

[Image: A Realist canvas of rural labour — stone breakers at work by a roadside, painted life-size in muted earth colours.]

Realism was an artistic movement that began in France in the 1850s, after the Revolution of 1848. The Realists rejected the idealisation and exotic subject matter of Romanticism and academic history painting, seeking instead to depict contemporary people — peasants, labourers and the urban poor — and everyday scenes with truth and accuracy, often on the grand scale previously reserved for history.

Gustave Courbet

The movement's leader was Gustave Courbet, whose A Burial at Ornans (1849–50) and The Stone Breakers scandalised the Paris Salon by presenting provincial life at monumental size without ennoblement. When his works were refused by the Exposition Universelle of 1855, Courbet mounted his own Pavilion of Realism, issuing a manifesto declaring he would "translate the customs, the ideas, the appearance of my epoch". Jean-François Millet's images of peasant labour, such as The Gleaners (1857), and Honoré Daumier's lithographs of Parisian life extended the movement's reach.

Spread and influence

Realism spread across Europe and America: to the Russian Peredvizhniki (Wanderers) led by Ilya Repin, to German painters such as Adolph Menzel, and to the United States in the work of Thomas Eakins and Winslow Homer. Its insistence on observed contemporary life fed into Naturalism in literature (Émile Zola), Impressionism, and later movements from the Ashcan School to twentieth-century social realism.
