Sir John Soane RA FSA (10 September 1753 – 20 January 1837) was an English architect who specialised in the Neoclassical style. The son of a bricklayer, he rose to become a professor of architecture at the Royal Academy and the official architect to the Bank of England, whose vast, top-lit banking halls — almost entirely demolished in the 1920s–30s — were his greatest public work. His own London house is now Sir John Soane's Museum.

Early career

Born John Soan (he added the final "e" as a young man), he trained in the offices of George Dance the Younger and Henry Holland and won the Royal Academy's gold medal in 1776. A travelling scholarship took him to Italy (1778–80), where he studied classical ruins and met Piranesi. Returning to England, he built up a country-house practice in East Anglia and married Elizabeth Smith, whose inherited fortune freed him to take on major work.

Bank of England and major works

Appointed architect and surveyor to the Bank of England in 1788, Soane rebuilt and expanded the Bank over 45 years into a top-lit complex of domed, incised-line interiors of striking originality, screened by his windowless curtain wall — the only substantial part to survive the 1920s–30s rebuilding. Other major works include the Dulwich Picture Gallery (1811–14), the first purpose-built public art gallery in England, lit by innovative roof lanterns, and his own country villa, Pitzhanger Manor in Ealing.

The house-museum

From 1792 Soane bought and rebuilt three houses at 12–14 Lincoln's Inn Fields as his home, office and a museum for his collections of antiquities, paintings (including Hogarth's A Rake's Progress and An Election), architectural models and books. By a private Act of Parliament in 1833 he bequeathed the house and collections to the nation, requiring that they be kept "as nearly as possible" as he left them; the museum opened after his death in 1837 and remains one of London's most atmospheric interiors.

Style and legacy

Soane distilled classicism into an austere personal language of shallow domes, incised ornament, concealed top-lighting and spatial surprise, sometimes called "Soanean" classicism. His influence waned in the Gothic Revival but revived in the twentieth century; the Bank of England's destruction is now widely lamented as the greatest architectural loss of interwar London.
