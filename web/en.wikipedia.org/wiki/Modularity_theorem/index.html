Modularity theorem

The modularity theorem (formerly called the Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture) states that every elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular: it arises from a modular form in the sense that its L-series coincides with that of a weight-2 Hecke eigenform. The theorem is one of the landmark results of modern number theory and is best known for implying Fermat's Last Theorem as a corollary, via the work of Andrew Wiles and Richard Taylor (1995), before the full conjecture was proved in 2001.

Statement

Every elliptic curve E over ℚ with conductor N admits a nonconstant rational map from the modular curve X0(N) onto it; equivalently, the Hasse–Weil L-function of E equals the L-function of a modular newform of weight 2 and level N. In terms of point counts, for all but finitely many primes p, the number of points on E modulo p is determined by the p-th Fourier coefficient of the modular form.

History

The conjecture originated in questions posed by Yutaka Taniyama in 1955 and was developed by Goro Shimura; André Weil's 1967 paper, which tied the conjecture to L-functions and identified the exact level predicted for the parametrisation, spread it widely in the West. In 1984 Gerhard Frey suggested that a counterexample to Fermat's Last Theorem would produce a semistable elliptic curve that could not be modular, and Jean-Pierre Serre's epsilon conjecture, proved by Ken Ribet in 1986, made this precise: Taniyama–Shimura for semistable curves implies Fermat's Last Theorem. Andrew Wiles, with Richard Taylor, proved the semistable case in 1995, establishing Fermat's Last Theorem after more than three centuries. The remaining cases were completed in 2001 by Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond and Richard Taylor.

Significance

Beyond Fermat, modularity is a cornerstone of the Langlands program: it is the case of the general principle that Galois representations arising from geometry should be automorphic. Modularity results now extend to elliptic curves over real quadratic fields and other number fields, and the methods developed by Wiles — Galois deformation theory — underpin much of contemporary arithmetic geometry.
