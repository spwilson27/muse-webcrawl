Winter storm
Type: Meteorological event
Precipitation: Snow, sleet, freezing rain
Season: Late autumn to early spring
Effects: Travel disruption, power outages, cold exposure

A winter storm is a storm in which the precipitation falls mainly as snow, sleet or freezing rain, or in which rain is accompanied by dangerously cold temperatures. Winter storms form where warm, moist air is lifted over cold air masses, as in mid-latitude cyclones and nor'easters, and they range from moderate snowfalls lasting a few hours to multi-day blizzards that paralyze large regions.

Types

Snowstorms produce accumulating snow; a blizzard adds sustained winds of at least 56 km/h (35 mph) and visibility below 400 m for three hours or more. Ice storms coat surfaces with freezing rain, bringing down trees and power lines. Mixed events cycle between snow, sleet and rain along a storm's warm front, and lake-effect snow bands can drop many tens of centimetres downwind of the Great Lakes. "Bomb cyclones" are winter cyclones that deepen explosively.

Warnings

Weather services issue winter storm watches and warnings when significant snow or ice is expected; in the United States, the National Weather Service issues warnings for specified snowfall or ice-accretion thresholds, and Environment Canada and European agencies operate comparable systems.

Impacts

Winter storms disrupt road, rail and air travel, cause widespread power outages from ice and wind loading, close schools and businesses, and endanger people and livestock through extreme cold — especially when followed by Arctic air outbreaks. Notable examples include the Great Blizzard of 1888, the US "Storm of the Century" of March 1993, the January 1998 North American ice storm, and the February 2021 Texas freeze.
