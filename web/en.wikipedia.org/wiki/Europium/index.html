Europium is a chemical element with the symbol Eu and atomic number 63. It is a moderately hard, silvery metal that readily oxidizes in air. Europium is the most reactive lanthanide by far; it must be stored under an inert fluid to protect it from atmospheric oxygen and moisture. It is also the least dense, softest, and most volatile lanthanide.

History

Europium was isolated in 1901 by French chemist Eugène-Anatole Demarçay, who named it for the continent of Europe. It had earlier been suspected in spectroscopic observations of samarium–gadolinium concentrates, where an unidentified spectral line pattern suggested a new element. Demarçay separated it from samarium after laborious fractional crystallizations.

Properties

Europium is a ductile metal with a hardness similar to that of lead. It crystallizes in a body-centered cubic lattice. Unlike most lanthanides, which are typically trivalent, europium commonly forms divalent compounds because the half-filled 4f7 electron configuration is particularly stable. Europium metal tarnishes quickly in air, forming a dull oxide layer, and it reacts with water similarly to calcium, producing europium hydroxide and hydrogen gas.

Occurrence and production

Europium is never found in nature as a free element. It occurs in small quantities in minerals such as bastnäsite and monazite, from which it is recovered as a by-product of mining other rare-earth elements. World production amounts to only a few hundred tonnes of oxide per year, making europium one of the rarest of the rare-earth elements in commercial terms.

Applications

The dominant use of europium is in phosphors. Europium-doped yttrium oxide (Y2O3:Eu3+) is the red phosphor long used in color television screens and fluorescent lamps, while divalent europium gives blue emission in other phosphor hosts. Together with terbium green phosphors, these made trichromatic lighting possible. Europium compounds are also used in the anti-counterfeiting fluorescent marks on euro banknotes, and europium's enormous neutron absorption cross-section makes it useful in nuclear reactor control applications.

Isotopes

Naturally occurring europium consists of two isotopes, 151Eu and 153Eu, with 153Eu slightly more abundant at about 52%. 151Eu was long thought to be stable but is now known to be very slightly radioactive, with a half-life on the order of 5×1018 years.
