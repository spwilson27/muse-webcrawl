Animal consciousness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Animal consciousness, or animal awareness, is the quality or state of self-awareness within a non-human animal, or of being aware of an external object or something within itself. In humans, consciousness has been defined as sentience, awareness, subjectivity, qualia, and the ability to perceive the relationship between oneself and one's environment.

The scientific study of animal consciousness has been transformed by research into animal cognition, including mirror self-recognition in great apes, dolphins, elephants, and magpies; episodic-like memory in scrub jays; metacognition in monkeys and rats; and evidence of pain and affective states in fish, cephalopods, and crustaceans.

Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness

In 2012, a group of prominent neuroscientists signed the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, which concluded that non-human animals, including all mammals and birds and many other creatures such as octopuses, possess the neurological substrates that generate consciousness.

Philosophical background

René Descartes argued that animals are automata lacking minds, a view challenged by later thinkers and by Charles Darwin's arguments for the continuity of mental traits between humans and other animals. Thomas Nagel's 1974 essay "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" raised the problem of subjective experience in other species. Donald Griffin's work on cognitive ethology helped establish the scientific study of animal awareness.

Indicators studied

  • Mirror test of self-recognition
  • Tool manufacture and use
  • Planning and future-oriented behaviour
  • Grief-like and empathic responses
  • Play behaviour and dreaming (REM sleep)
