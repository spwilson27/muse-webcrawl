Robert Beamon (born 29 August 1946) is an American former track and field athlete, best known for his world record in the long jump at the Mexico City Olympics in 1968. On 18 October 1968 he jumped 8.90 metres (29 ft 2½ in), improving the existing world record by 55 cm — the greatest single improvement in the event's history — in what is often called the greatest athletic performance of the twentieth century.

The jump

Beamon had nearly fouled out of qualifying and was coached by teammate Ralph Boston on adjusting his run-up. In the final, on his first attempt, he hit the board perfectly and soared beyond the optical measuring device's range; officials had to measure the jump manually with a steel tape. When the distance was announced, Beamon — unfamiliar with metric measurement — did not at first understand; on being told he had jumped nearly 29 feet 2½ inches, he collapsed in an emotional episode later described as a "cataplectic" reaction. The thin air of Mexico City (altitude 2,240 m) and a maximum allowable tailwind of 2.0 m/s assisted the jump, but its magnitude astonished the sport: defending champion Lynn Davies remarked that Beamon had "destroyed this event".

Legacy

The record stood for almost 23 years, until Mike Powell jumped 8.95 m at the 1991 World Championships; it remains the Olympic record and the second-longest legal jump in history. The adjective "Beamonesque" entered sports language for a feat so far beyond expectation. Beamon's mark is a fixture of the United States Olympic Hall of Fame, and his jump is routinely ranked the greatest single moment in Olympic history.
