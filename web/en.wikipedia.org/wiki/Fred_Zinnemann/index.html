Fred Zinnemann (29 April 1907 – 14 March 1997) was an Austrian-born American film director whose career spanned five decades and many genres. He won Academy Awards for Best Director for From Here to Eternity (1953) and A Man for All Seasons (1966), and is also celebrated for the classic Western High Noon (1952). His films are noted for their craftsmanship, moral seriousness, and heroes who stand by their principles under pressure.

Early life and career

Zinnemann was born in Rzeszów, then in Austria-Hungary, to a Jewish family, and grew up in Vienna. He studied law before turning to film, working as a camera assistant in Berlin and Paris and collaborating on the semi-documentary Menschen am Sonntag (1930). Emigrating to the United States, he directed shorts at MGM — one of which, That Mothers Might Live (1938), won an Oscar — before moving to features. His parents died in the Holocaust, an experience that informed his film The Search (1948), shot in postwar Germany, which brought Montgomery Clift to prominence.

Major films

Zinnemann's reputation rests on a remarkable run of films: The Men (1950), Marlon Brando's debut; High Noon (1952), with Gary Cooper as a marshal abandoned by the town he defends; From Here to Eternity (1953), the Pearl Harbor drama that won eight Academy Awards including Best Picture; Oklahoma! (1955); The Nun's Story (1959) with Audrey Hepburn; The Sundowners (1960); A Man for All Seasons (1966), the story of Sir Thomas More, which won six Oscars including Best Picture and Best Director; The Day of the Jackal (1973), a meticulous thriller about an assassination plot against Charles de Gaulle; and Julia (1977).

Legacy

Four of Zinnemann's films have been selected for the United States National Film Registry. Directors including Steven Spielberg have cited him as an influence. He died in London in 1997 at the age of 89.
