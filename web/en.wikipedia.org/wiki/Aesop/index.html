Aesop

Aesop (Greek: Αἴσωπος, c. 620 – 564 BCE) was a Greek fabulist and storyteller credited with a collection of fables now known as Aesop's Fables. According to ancient tradition, he was an enslaved man who gained his freedom through his wit, and his stories — short tales, usually featuring animals with human characteristics, each concluding with a moral — have been retold for more than two millennia.

Life

The details of Aesop's life are largely legendary. Herodotus records that he was a slave of Iadmon of Samos and was killed by the people of Delphi. Later tradition, including the partly fictional Life of Aesop, describes him as extremely ugly, clever, and a favourite at the court of Croesus of Lydia. No writings survive in his own hand; the fables were transmitted orally and first written down by Demetrius of Phalerum in the 4th century BC, then versified by Babrius in Greek and Phaedrus in Latin.

Fables

Famous fables attributed to Aesop include "The Tortoise and the Hare" ("slow and steady wins the race"), "The Boy Who Cried Wolf", "The Fox and the Grapes" (the origin of "sour grapes"), "The Ant and the Grasshopper", and "The Lion and the Mouse". The Aesopica corpus numbers over 600 tales.

Legacy

Aesop's fables have been a staple of moral education for children across many cultures, retold by writers from La Fontaine to modern picture-book authors, and their morals have passed into everyday proverbial language.
