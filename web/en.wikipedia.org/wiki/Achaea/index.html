Achaea (Greek: Αχαΐα, Achaḯa) is a historical region and modern regional unit of Greece, occupying the northern part of the Peloponnese peninsula along the Gulf of Corinth and the Gulf of Patras. Its capital is Patras, Greece's third-largest city and a major port for ferry traffic to Italy.

The Rio–Antirrio Bridge links Achaea with Aetolia-Acarnania across the Gulf of Corinth.

History

In Homer, "Achaeans" is one of the names used for the Greeks collectively. The historical region of Achaea was settled by Achaean Greeks and comprised twelve cities, including Patrae (Patras), Dyme and Aegium. These cities formed the Achaean League, a confederation revived in 280 BC that grew to dominate much of Greece before its defeat by Rome at the Battle of Corinth in 146 BC, after which Achaea became the name of the Roman province covering most of Greece.

Under Roman, Byzantine, Frankish, Venetian and Ottoman rule the region changed hands repeatedly. After the Crusades the Principality of Achaea (1205–1432) was one of the Latin states established in Greece. Achaea joined the modern Greek state during the War of Independence.

Geography

Achaea is mountainous in the south, where Mount Erymanthos and the Panachaiko rise above Patras, and flatter along the northwestern coastal plain, one of Greece's most fertile agricultural areas, known for currants, citrus and olives. The Rio–Antirrio Bridge (Charilaos Trikoupis Bridge), opened in 2004, spans the strait at the mouth of the Gulf of Corinth.

Modern administration

Achaea is a regional unit of the Western Greece region, subdivided into five municipalities: Patras, Aigialeia, Erymanthos, Kalavryta and West Achaea. The monastery of Mega Spilaio and the rack railway from Diakopto to Kalavryta are notable landmarks.
