Endochondral ossification

Endochondral ossification is one of the two essential pathways by which a rudimentary bone structure is formed, the other being intramembranous ossification. In endochondral ossification, bone develops by replacing a hyaline cartilage model. In long bones, the process begins around the sixth to eighth week of embryonic development and continues at the epiphyseal (growth) plates until skeletal maturity. Most bones of the skeleton — the long bones of the limbs, the vertebrae, the pelvis and the base of the skull — form this way; only the flat bones of the skull, the mandible and the clavicles form mainly by intramembranous ossification.

A cartilage model is progressively replaced by bone from primary and secondary ossification centres.

Stages

Formation of the cartilage model. Mesenchymal cells condense and differentiate into chondrocytes, which secrete a hyaline cartilage matrix shaped like the future bone, surrounded by the perichondrium.

Growth and calcification. Chondrocytes in the centre of the shaft hypertrophy, the matrix calcifies, and the chondrocytes die, leaving cavities. A periosteal bud of blood vessels, nerves and osteoprogenitor cells invades the calcified cartilage.

Primary ossification centre. Osteoblasts deposit bone matrix on the remnants of calcified cartilage in the diaphysis, forming trabecular (spongy) bone, while osteoclasts help hollow out the medullary cavity. A bony collar forms around the shaft beneath the periosteum.

Secondary ossification centres. After birth, similar centres appear in the epiphyses (ends) of the bone. Cartilage persists at two sites: the articular cartilage covering the joint surface, and the epiphyseal plate between epiphysis and diaphysis.

Growth in length

Lengthwise growth occurs at the epiphyseal plate, whose chondrocytes are organised into resting, proliferative, hypertrophic and calcification zones. New cartilage is produced on the epiphyseal side while older cartilage is replaced by bone on the diaphyseal side. At the end of adolescence the cartilage is entirely replaced by bone, leaving only an epiphyseal line, and longitudinal growth ceases.

Regulation and clinical relevance

The process is coordinated by signalling pathways including Indian hedgehog (Ihh), parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP), fibroblast growth factors and the transcription factor RUNX2. Mutations in the FGFR3 receptor cause achondroplasia, the commonest form of dwarfism, by suppressing chondrocyte proliferation in the growth plate. Fracture healing in long bones recapitulates endochondral ossification through a cartilage callus.
