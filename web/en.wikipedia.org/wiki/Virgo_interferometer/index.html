Virgo interferometer

The Virgo site near Pisa.

The Virgo interferometer is a large Michelson interferometer designed to detect gravitational waves predicted by General relativity. Located at the European Gravitational Observatory at Cascina, near Pisa in Italy, it has two arms 3 km long (slightly shorter than LIGO's 4 km) in vacuum tubes 1.2 m in diameter. It is named for the Virgo Cluster of galaxies, some 65 million light-years away, an early target source region.

Proposed by Alain Brillet and Jean-Yves Vinet and built by a French–Italian collaboration (CNRS and INFN, later joined by institutes in the Netherlands, Poland, Hungary and Spain), Virgo began science runs in 2007 and, upgraded as Advanced Virgo, joined LIGO's second observing run in August 2017 — immediately enabling the first three-detector detection (GW170814), whose polarisation measurements favoured general relativity's tensor modes, and the era of precise source localisation that made the GW170817 multi-messenger campaign possible.

Role in the network

With LIGO's two detectors and KAGRA in Japan, Virgo completes a global array: signals shared among sites fix a source's sky position to tens of square degrees instead of hundreds, let telescopes find counterparts, and allow null tests (polarisations, propagation speed) impossible with fewer detectors. Its suspension and seismic-isolation systems — chains of pendulums ('superattenuators') isolating its mirrors from ground motion — are among the most sophisticated in experimental physics.
