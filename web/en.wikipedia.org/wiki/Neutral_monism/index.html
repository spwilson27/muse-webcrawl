Neutral monism

Neutral monism is a metaphysical theory in the philosophy of mind. The neutral monist believes that mind and body are not separate substances, nor is one reducible to the other; instead, both mental and physical phenomena are constructed from more fundamental elements that are themselves neither mental nor physical.

History

The view has roots in the work of Ernst Mach and was developed by William James in his essays on "radical empiricism," especially "Does 'Consciousness' Exist?" (1904). Bertrand Russell adopted and developed neutral monism in The Analysis of Mind (1921) and The Analysis of Matter (1927), holding that the ultimate constituents of the world are events that physics and psychology describe from different perspectives.

Variants and criticism

Neutral monist theories differ over the nature of the neutral basis: James spoke of "pure experience," while Russell treated sensations and images as the neutral stuff ordered differently in mental and physical series. Critics have objected that the notion of a neutral element is obscure and that the theory struggles to explain how subjective experience arises from the neutral basis. Contemporary interest in neutral monism has revived through its perceived affinity with panpsychism and with Russellian monism in response to the hard problem of consciousness.
