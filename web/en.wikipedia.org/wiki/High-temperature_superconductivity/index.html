High-temperature superconductivity

High-temperature superconductors (abbreviated high-Tc) are materials that behave as superconductors at temperatures above 77 K, the boiling point of liquid nitrogen — far above the roughly 30 K ceiling of conventional superconductors described by BCS theory. The class is dominated by layered copper-oxide ceramics (cuprates), discovered in 1986 by Georg Bednorz and Karl Alexander Müller, whose barium–lanthanum–copper oxide reached about 35 K; within a year, yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO) exceeded 90 K.

Families of materials

Beyond the cuprates (including BSCCO and mercury-based compounds, the latter reaching 133 K at ambient pressure and about 164 K under pressure), the iron-based pnictide superconductors discovered in 2008 form a second family, with critical temperatures up to about 55 K. Under extreme pressures, hydrogen-rich compounds such as H3S (about 203 K at 155 GPa, reported in 2015) extend the record further.

Mechanism

Unlike conventional superconductors, the cuprates are doped Mott insulators with d-wave pairing symmetry, and the pairing mechanism remains an open problem in condensed matter physics — one of the major unsolved questions that grew out of Superconductivity research. The pseudogap regime above Tc and the strange-metal linear resistivity are among the phenomena a complete theory must explain.

Applications

High-Tc materials, especially REBCO coated conductors, are used in high-field magnets, fault-current limiters, cables and the toroidal field coils of compact fusion experiments such as tokamaks (see ITER-class devices and private ventures).
