Nephrology

Nephrology is a specialty of adult internal medicine and pediatrics that concerns the study of the kidneys, specifically normal kidney function (renal physiology) and kidney disease (renal pathophysiology), the preservation of kidney health, and the treatment of kidney disease, from diet and medication to renal replacement therapy (dialysis and kidney transplantation). The word "renal" is an adjective meaning "relating to the kidneys", and its roots are French or late Latin. Whereas, per Greek rules, nephrology is about the anatomical or pathologic features of the kidneys, "renal" is about the broader functional aspects.

The kidney and its functional unit, the nephron.

Nephrology also studies systemic conditions that affect the kidneys, such as diabetes and autoimmune disease; and systemic diseases that occur as a result of kidney disease, such as renal osteodystrophy and hypertension. A physician who has undertaken additional training and become certified in nephrology is called a nephrologist.

Scope

Nephrology concerns the diagnosis and treatment of kidney diseases, including primary kidney diseases and secondary kidney diseases which are due to systemic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, systemic lupus erythematosus, vasculitis, and infection. Nephrologists also manage electrolyte disorders, acid–base disorders, kidney stones, hypertension (high blood pressure), acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, and end-stage kidney disease.

Procedures performed by nephrologists may include native kidney and transplant kidney biopsy, dialysis access insertion (temporary vascular access lines, tunnelled vascular access lines, peritoneal dialysis access lines), fistula management (angiographic or surgical fistulogram and plasty), and bone biopsy.

History

The modern study of the kidney began in the 17th century, when Marcello Malpighi described the microscopic structure of the kidney. In the 19th century, Richard Bright described glomerulonephritis and linked kidney disease to hypertension and edema. The 20th century saw the development of hemodialysis by Willem Kolff during the Second World War, and the first successful kidney transplant between identical twins in 1954 at the Peter Bent Brigham Hospital in Boston.

Training

In the United States, after medical school nephrologists complete a residency in internal medicine followed by a fellowship in nephrology, typically two to three years. Board certification is administered by the American Board of Internal Medicine. Pediatric nephrology is a separate subspecialty entered after a pediatrics residency.
