Umrah

Umrah (Arabic: عُمْرَة) is the Islamic "lesser pilgrimage" to Mecca, which may be performed at any time of year.

The Umrah (Arabic: عُمْرَة‎, lit. "to visit a populated place") is an Islamic pilgrimage to Mecca, the holiest city for Muslims, that can be undertaken at any time of the year, in contrast to the Hajj, which has specific dates according to the Islamic lunar calendar.

The pilgrim performs a series of ritual acts that include entering a state of consecration known as ihram, circumambulating the Kaaba seven times (tawaf), praying behind the Station of Abraham, and walking seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah (sa'i), commemorating Hagar's search for water for her son Ishmael. The rite concludes with shaving the head or cutting the hair, after which the pilgrim exits the state of ihram.

Differences from Hajj

While Hajj is one of the five pillars of Islam and is obligatory once in a lifetime for those able to perform it, Umrah is considered voluntary by most schools of law, though the Hanbali and Shafi'i schools regard it as obligatory. Umrah also omits several Hajj rites, including the standing at Arafat, the stoning of the pillars at Mina, and the sacrifice associated with Eid al-Adha.

Types of Umrah

Umrah may be performed independently (Umrat al-mufradah) or combined with Hajj. In Hajj al-tamattu', the pilgrim performs Umrah during the Hajj months and exits ihram before re-entering it for Hajj; in Hajj al-qiran, the two are performed without leaving ihram in between.

History

According to Islamic tradition, the Prophet Muhammad performed four Umrahs during his lifetime. The Treaty of al-Hudaybiyyah (628 CE) led to the so-called "compensatory Umrah" the following year. In modern times, Saudi Arabia regulates Umrah travel through electronic visa systems, and millions of pilgrims perform the rite each year, with numbers peaking during the month of Ramadan.
