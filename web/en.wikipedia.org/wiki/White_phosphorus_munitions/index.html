White phosphorus munitions are weapons that use one of the common allotropes of phosphorus, white phosphorus, which ignites spontaneously on contact with air and burns at temperatures of roughly 800 °C (1,470 °F) or higher. They are used primarily to produce smoke screens, to mark targets and to illuminate battlefields at night, but their incendiary effects — which can cause severe and deep burns to people struck by burning particles — have made their use controversial, especially in or near concentrations of civilians.

Properties and effects

When a white phosphorus munition bursts, it scatters burning wedges or particles that produce an immediate, dense white smoke (phosphorus pentoxide). The smoke is highly effective for obscuration because it forms almost instantly, and it also interferes with some infrared sensors. Burning particles that embed in skin or clothing can continue to burn until deprived of oxygen, causing deep and painful burns that are difficult to extinguish and can re-ignite when dressings are removed; absorbed phosphorus can also cause systemic poisoning affecting the liver, kidneys and heart.

Uses and historical employment

White phosphorus grenades, shells and bombs have been employed since the First World War — when "Willie Pete" entered soldiers' slang — through the Second World War, Korea, Vietnam and into the 21st century. Reported or documented uses include the 2004 Battle of Fallujah in Iraq, the 2008–09 Gaza War, conflicts in Syria, and Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine from 2022, where WP airburst munitions were filmed over several towns and cities.

International law

White phosphorus is not classified as a chemical weapon under the Chemical Weapons Convention when used for its incendiary or smoke properties. However, Protocol III to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (1980) restricts incendiary weapons — defined by the primary purpose of setting fires or causing burn injuries — and prohibits making civilians or civilian objects the object of attack with them, as well as air-delivered incendiary attacks on military objectives within concentrations of civilians. Critics, including Human Rights Watch, argue the protocol's definition leaves loopholes for munitions classed as "smoke" weapons. The International Committee of the Red Cross and general principles of distinction, proportionality and precaution in customary international humanitarian law also constrain its use in populated areas.
