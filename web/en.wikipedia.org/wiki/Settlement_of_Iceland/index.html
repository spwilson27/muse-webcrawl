Settlement of Iceland

The settlement of Iceland (Icelandic: landnámsöld, literally "land-taking age") is generally believed to have begun in the second half of the 9th century, when Norse settlers migrated across the North Atlantic. The reasons for the migration are partly documented in written sources such as the Landnámabók (Book of Settlements), and partly a matter of scholarly speculation, including population pressure in Scandinavia, the search for new farmland, and flight from the centralising rule of King Harald Fairhair of Norway.

First visitors

Icelandic sources mention Irish monks (papar) present on the island before the Norse arrived, who left when unwilling to live among pagans. Norse discovery is credited in the sagas to Naddodd, Garðar Svavarsson — who circumnavigated the island and gave it the name Garðarshólmi — and Flóki Vilgerðarson, who named it Ísland ("Iceland") after seeing drift ice in the fjords.

The settlement era

The first permanent settler is traditionally held to be Ingólfr Arnarson, who established his farm at Reykjavík ("smoky bay") around 874 CE, having thrown his high-seat pillars overboard and resolved to settle where they washed ashore. According to Landnámabók, some 400 principal settlers and thousands of dependants — including many of Celtic origin, as modern genetic studies confirm — took land throughout Iceland over the following sixty years; by about 930 the land was considered fully settled, and the national assembly, the Althing, was founded at Þingvellir.

A distinctive volcanic ash layer, the "settlement layer" (landnámslag) dated to 871±2 CE, provides archaeologists with a chronological marker found across the island, and the deforestation of Iceland's birch woodland by settlers began an enduring pattern of soil erosion.

Legacy

The settlement era laid the foundations of the Icelandic Commonwealth and of a uniquely detailed medieval literature — the sagas and settlement records — that preserves the names, farms, and genealogies of the first generations of Icelanders.
