Yang–Mills theory

FieldQuantum field theory, gauge theory
Introduced1954, by Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills
ApplicationsQuantum chromodynamics, electroweak theory

Yang–Mills theory is a gauge theory based on a non-abelian Lie group, introduced by physicists Chen Ning Yang and Robert Mills in 1954. It generalises the gauge invariance of electromagnetism to internal symmetry groups such as SU(2) and SU(3), and it underlies the Standard Model of particle physics.

Overview

In a Yang–Mills theory, the force fields themselves carry the charge of the interaction and therefore interact with each other, unlike photons in electromagnetism. The theory is described by the Yang–Mills action, built from the field-strength tensor formed from the gauge potentials. Quantised Yang–Mills theory applied to the SU(3) colour group gives quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction; combined SU(2)×U(1) gauge theory describes the electroweak interaction.

Mathematical significance

Yang–Mills theory is also a central subject in pure mathematics and geometry, through instantons and Donaldson theory. The existence of a mass gap in quantum Yang–Mills theory is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, still unsolved.
