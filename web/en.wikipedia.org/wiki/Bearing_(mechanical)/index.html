Bearing (mechanical)

A bearing is a machine element that constrains relative motion to only the desired motion — typically rotation or linear movement — and reduces friction between moving parts. Bearings support loads, maintain the position of shafts and axles, and are among the most fundamental components of machinery, found in everything from electric motors and vehicle wheels to watches and jet engines.

Types

Plain bearings (sliding bearings or bushings) rely on sliding contact between surfaces, usually separated by a lubricant film. Rolling-element bearings place balls (ball bearings) or rollers (roller bearings) between inner and outer races, converting sliding friction into much lower rolling friction. Other types include fluid bearings, which support loads on a thin film of pressurised liquid or gas; magnetic bearings, which levitate the shaft using electromagnetic forces; flexure bearings, which permit motion through elastic deformation; and jewel bearings used in precision instruments and watches.

Characteristics

Bearings are rated by the loads they can carry (radial and axial), their limiting speeds, and their expected service life, commonly expressed as the L10 life — the number of revolutions at which 10 percent of a population of bearings would be expected to have failed by fatigue. Correct lubrication, mounting, sealing against contamination, and control of clearance are critical to bearing longevity; failure modes include fatigue spalling, abrasive wear, corrosion, and brinelling (indentations caused by excessive static or impact loads).

History

Rolling supports were used in antiquity, and a wooden ball-bearing assembly supported the rotating table of a Roman ship from Lake Nemi. Leonardo da Vinci sketched ball bearing designs around 1500. The modern ball bearing industry developed in the nineteenth century with the bicycle boom, and Sven Wingquist's self-aligning ball bearing (1907) led to the founding of SKF, still a major manufacturer.
