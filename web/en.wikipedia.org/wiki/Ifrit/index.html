Ifrit

An ifrit (Arabic: عفريت, ʿifrīt, plural ʿafārīt) is a powerful type of supernatural being in Islamic mythology and Arabic folklore, classed among the jinn. Ifrits are often depicted as enormous, winged creatures of fire and smoke, inhabiting the underworld or ruins, and are noted for their strength, cunning, and resistance to ordinary magic. In modern usage the word can denote any especially mighty or malevolent jinn.

In the Qur'an and tradition

The word ifrit appears once in the Qur'an, in Surah An-Naml (27:39), where an ifrit from among the jinn offers to bring the throne of the Queen of Sheba to Solomon before he can rise from his seat — an offer surpassed by one who "had knowledge of the Book." Islamic tradition generally regards ifrits as a rank or class of jinn created from smokeless fire; some theologians classified them among the rebellious devils (shayatin), while storytellers treated them as proud, ancient beings bound by strict rules of obligation and vengeance.

In folklore and literature

Ifrits are prominent in the One Thousand and One Nights, where they appear as dangerous spirits who may be imprisoned in bottles, lamps, and other vessels — as in "The Fisherman and the Jinni" — and who grant or refuse aid according to oaths and bargains. In Egyptian folklore the ifrit is also associated with the vengeful spirit of a murdered person, rising from the victim's blood to pursue the murderer. Folklore holds that ifrits can be killed only with difficulty, though they are vulnerable to iron and to certain religious invocations.

In popular culture

The ifrit has been widely adopted in fantasy fiction, role-playing games, and video games, often as a fire-aligned counterpart to the water-associated marid. Notable appearances include the summonable fire entity in the Final Fantasy series and the titular spirit of Neil Gaiman's American Gods, which presents an ifrit living unrecognized in New York City.
