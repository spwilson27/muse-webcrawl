Remand (detention)

Remand, also known as pre-trial detention, preventive detention, or provisional detention, is the process of detaining a person until their trial after they have been arrested and charged with an offence. A person who is on remand is held in a prison or other detention facility and has not been convicted of the charge; they are legally presumed innocent.

Grounds for remand

Courts generally remand a defendant in custody where there are substantial grounds for believing that, if released on bail, the person would:

  • Fail to surrender to custody (abscond)
  • Commit further offences while on bail
  • Interfere with witnesses or otherwise obstruct the course of justice

Defendants who are not remanded are released on bail, sometimes with conditions such as residence requirements, reporting to a police station, or a surety. In the United States, a bail bondsman may secure release by posting a bond.

Duration and concerns

Time limits usually govern how long a person may be held on remand before trial, though extensions are common. In many countries remand prisoners form a large share of the prison population, and long pre-trial detention — sometimes longer than the sentence eventually imposed — is a recurring human-rights concern. Time spent on remand is normally deducted from any custodial sentence later passed.
