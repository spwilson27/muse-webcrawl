Mean sea level

Mean sea level (MSL, often shortened to simply sea level) is an average surface level of one or more of Earth's coastal bodies of water from which heights such as elevation may be measured. The global MSL is one kind of vertical datum — a standardised geodetic datum — that is used, for example, in cartography and marine navigation, as well as in aviation as the standard sea level at which atmospheric pressure is measured to calibrate altitude.

Measurement

Local mean sea level is defined by averaging hourly tide-gauge observations over a full tidal epoch, conventionally 19 years, so as to cancel tidal, seasonal and inter-annual variations. Because of winds, currents, temperature, salinity and atmospheric pressure, the sea surface is not level at all: mean sea level varies by up to about two metres between ocean basins relative to a perfect ellipsoid, and it departs from the geoid — the equipotential surface that defines "level" for surveyors — in a pattern called sea surface topography.

Since 1992, satellite altimeters such as TOPEX/Poseidon and the Jason series have measured global sea level directly, providing the primary record of its rise.

Sea level change

Global mean sea level rose by roughly 20 cm over the twentieth century and the rate has accelerated to more than 3.5 mm per year in the satellite era, driven by thermal expansion of warming ocean water and the melting of glaciers and ice sheets. Local relative sea level can differ greatly from the global mean because the land itself is rising or subsiding — for instance through post-glacial rebound in Scandinavia and Canada, or compaction-driven subsidence in major deltas.

Use as a datum

National height systems are referenced to local determinations of mean sea level, such as the Ordnance Datum at Newlyn in Great Britain or the North American Vertical Datum of 1988. Elevations "above sea level" (for example the 8,849 m of Mount Everest) are ultimately tied to these datums or to the geoid.
