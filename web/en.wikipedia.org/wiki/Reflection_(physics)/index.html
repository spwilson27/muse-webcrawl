Reflection (physics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Reflection is the change in direction of a wavefront at an interface between two different media so that the wavefront returns into the medium from which it originated. Common examples include the reflection of light, sound and water waves.

Law of reflection

For a smooth surface, the law of reflection states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, both measured from the normal (the line perpendicular to the surface), and that the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the same plane. Reflection from a smooth surface is called specular and produces mirror images; reflection from a rough surface scatters light in many directions and is called diffuse — which is why most objects are visible but do not act as mirrors.

Across physics

Sound reflection produces echoes and underlies sonar and ultrasound imaging; seismic reflections reveal underground structure. When a wave passes from a denser to a less dense medium beyond the critical angle, total internal reflection occurs, the principle behind optical fibres. Reflection of light is described quantitatively by the Fresnel equations, which give the reflected and transmitted amplitudes for each polarisation.
