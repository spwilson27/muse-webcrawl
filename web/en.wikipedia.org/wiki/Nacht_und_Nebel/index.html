Nacht und Nebel

Nacht und Nebel decree
Date7 December 1941
Issued byAdolf Hitler; implemented by Wilhelm Keitel (OKW)
TargetResistance members and political opponents in occupied territories
EffectSecret deportation to Germany; no information to families

Nacht und Nebel (German for "Night and Fog") was a directive issued by Adolf Hitler on 7 December 1941 that was primarily intended to serve as a deterrent to resistance activity in Nazi-occupied Europe. Persons endangering German security who were not to be executed immediately were to be made to disappear without trace into "night and fog": secretly transported to Germany, held incommunicado, and denied any information to their families, so that uncertainty about their fate would intimidate the population. The decree's name echoes the magic spell in Wagner's Das Rheingold by which Alberich vanishes.

Many Nacht und Nebel prisoners were held at Natzweiler-Struthof and other camps in Germany.

The directive

Field Marshal Wilhelm Keitel, chief of the Oberkommando der Wehrmacht, issued the implementing orders. Civilians in occupied territories accused of offences such as resistance activity, espionage, sabotage, or aiding escapes were to be sentenced to death only if a court could conclude proceedings within about a week; otherwise they were to be handed to the Gestapo and deported secretly to Germany for trial by special courts or detention in concentration camps. No information about the prisoner's whereabouts or fate, including death, was to be released.

Application

The decree applied mainly in Western Europe — France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Norway, and Denmark. Thousands of resistance members, among them French résistants, Norwegian teachers, and Dutch resisters, were deported as "NN" prisoners, marked with the letters NN and subjected to particularly harsh treatment. Many were held at Natzweiler-Struthof, the principal NN camp in the west, and at prisons and camps including Gross-Rosen and Hinzert; mortality was very high. Estimates of the number of people deported under the decree run to at least 7,000, and many thousands more disappeared under similar practices in the East, where the regime operated without legal formalities.

Legal judgment

At the Nuremberg trials, the Nacht und Nebel decree was treated as a war crime and a crime against humanity; Keitel's role in implementing it formed part of the case against him, and he was executed in 1946. The practice it codified — enforced disappearance — informed later international law prohibiting disappearances, reflected in instruments such as the UN Declaration on the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance (1992) and the International Convention of 2006.

In culture

Alain Resnais's documentary Night and Fog (Nuit et brouillard, 1956), with commentary by deportation survivor Jean Cayrol, took its title from the decree's phrase and remains one of the most influential films about the concentration camps. The term has since passed into general use as a byword for secret state abduction.
