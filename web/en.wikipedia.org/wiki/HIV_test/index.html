HIV test

HIV tests are used to detect the presence of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in serum, saliva or urine, and form the basis of HIV diagnosis, blood-donation screening and public-health surveillance. Modern testing algorithms begin with a fourth-generation antigen/antibody immunoassay that detects both HIV-1 p24 antigen and antibodies to HIV-1 and HIV-2, followed by a differentiation immunoassay and, if needed, nucleic acid testing (NAT) to resolve results.

PurposeDiagnosis and screening for HIV infection
SpecimensBlood (venous or fingerstick), oral fluid
MethodsAg/Ab immunoassay, antibody differentiation assay, NAT, rapid tests, self-tests

Window period

The window period is the interval between infection and the time a test can reliably detect it: roughly 10 days for RNA tests, about 18 days for laboratory antigen/antibody tests on venous blood, and up to 90 days for antibody-only and oral-fluid tests. A negative result during the window period may require retesting.

Types of tests

Laboratory immunoassays are the standard for diagnosis. Rapid point-of-care tests give results in under 30 minutes and are widely used in outreach and antenatal care, and HIV self-tests — such as oral-fluid and blood-based kits — allow private testing at home. All donated blood in developed countries is screened with both serology and NAT, making transfusion-transmitted HIV extremely rare.
