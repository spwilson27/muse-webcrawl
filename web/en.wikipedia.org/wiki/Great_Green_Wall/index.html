The Great Green Wall is an African Union–led initiative launched in 2007 aiming to restore the degraded landscapes of the Sahel, the semi-arid transition zone south of the Sahara. Originally conceived as a literal wall of trees about 8,000 km long and 15 km wide running from Dakar, Senegal, in the west to Djibouti in the east, the project has evolved into a mosaic of land-restoration and sustainable land-management interventions — agroforestry, farmer-managed natural regeneration, water harvesting (such as zaï pits and half-moon bunds), and community woodlots — intended to combat desertification, improve food security, and create jobs.

Aims and targets

The initiative's 2030 ambitions include restoring 100 million hectares of degraded land, sequestering 250 million tonnes of carbon, and creating 10 million jobs in rural areas. A 2020 UN assessment estimated that only a modest fraction of the target (often cited around 15–18 per cent) had been achieved, with Senegal, Ethiopia, and Niger among the most active implementers; Niger's farmer-managed regeneration of some five million hectares is frequently cited as a related success story.

Funding and criticism

At the One Planet Summit in January 2021, donors pledged about US$14.3 billion to a "Great Green Wall Accelerator". Critics note slow disbursement, governance challenges, and the mismatch between the memorable "wall of trees" metaphor and the reality that survival of planted trees has often been low; supporters counter that the initiative's true model — locally managed restoration — is sound but under-resourced. A separate Chinese "Great Green Wall" (Three-North Shelter Forest Program) pursues similar anti-desertification aims around the Gobi.
