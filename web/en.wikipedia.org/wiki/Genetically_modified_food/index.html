Genetically modified foods (GM foods) are foods produced from organisms whose genetic material has been altered using genetic engineering techniques, introducing traits such as resistance to pests, herbicides, or disease, or improved nutritional content. GM crops were first planted commercially in 1996 and are now grown in dozens of countries, principally the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Canada, and India.

GM maize is among the most widely planted genetically modified crops.

Methods and traits

Genetic modification inserts or alters genes using techniques such as Agrobacterium-mediated transformation, gene guns, and, more recently, gene editing such as CRISPR. The dominant commercial traits are tolerance to herbicides such as glyphosate and production of Bt insecticidal proteins borrowed from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis. Other products include virus-resistant papaya, non-browning apples and potatoes, and biofortified crops such as Golden Rice, engineered to provide vitamin A.

Safety and regulation

There is a broad scientific consensus, reflected in assessments by the World Health Organization, the US National Academies, and the European Commission's research reviews, that approved GM foods are as safe to eat as their conventional counterparts, though each new product is assessed case by case. Regulation differs sharply by jurisdiction: the United States regulates largely by product characteristics, the European Union applies a process-based, precautionary regime with mandatory labelling and traceability, and many countries require labels disclosing GM content.

Controversy

Public debate over GM foods encompasses environmental questions (gene flow, herbicide-resistant weeds, effects on non-target insects), corporate control of seed markets, and consumer choice. Critics and supporters dispute the interpretation of evidence on each point, and acceptance remains much lower in Europe than in the Americas.
