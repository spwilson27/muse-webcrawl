West Indian manatee

The West Indian manatee (Trichechus manatus), also known as the North American manatee, is a large, aquatic mammal native to warm coastal areas of the Caribbean, the Gulf of Mexico and the Atlantic coast of Florida. It is the largest surviving member of the order Sirenia. Two subspecies are recognised: the Florida manatee (T. m. latirostris) and the Antillean manatee (T. m. manatus).

Description

Adults average about 3 metres in length and 400–550 kg in weight, with paddle-shaped tails and two forelimb flippers bearing toenails. Manatees are herbivores, feeding on seagrasses and freshwater vegetation for many hours a day. Their slow metabolism and low body fat make them vulnerable to cold stress in winter, when Florida manatees congregate at warm-water springs and power-plant outflows.

Conservation

The species is listed as Vulnerable by the IUCN. Principal threats include collisions with boats, habitat loss, harmful algal blooms and cold stress. The Florida population recovered sufficiently for the subspecies to be downlisted from endangered to threatened under the US Endangered Species Act in 2017, though an unusual mortality event in 2021–2022, linked to seagrass die-off in the Indian River Lagoon, caused hundreds of deaths.
