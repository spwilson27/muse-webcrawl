Tuff

Tuff
TypeIgneous rock (pyroclastic)
CompositionConsolidated volcanic ash, lapilli, and rock fragments
FormationDeposition and lithification of ejected volcanic material
VarietiesWelded tuff (ignimbrite), lapilli tuff, crystal tuff, lithic tuff, vitric tuff

Tuff is a type of rock made of volcanic ash ejected from a vent during a volcanic eruption. Following ejection and deposition, the ash is lithified into a solid rock. Tuffs consist of more than 75% volcanic ash and fragments (tephra) less than 2 mm in size, though many contain larger clasts of lapilli and volcanic bombs. Deposits may be emplaced by fallout from an eruption column or by pyroclastic density currents.

Classification

Tuffs are classified by the nature of their fragments: vitric tuffs are rich in glass shards and pumice, crystal tuffs in mineral crystals such as quartz and feldspar, and lithic tuffs in fragments of older rock. When hot pyroclastic flows come to rest, the material may fuse into welded tuff, also called ignimbrite, which can form vast sheets — for example, the tuffs of Yellowstone Caldera and the Bishop Tuff of California, used as a marker horizon in western North America.

Occurrence and use

Tuff is relatively soft and has been quarried since antiquity as a building stone. The ancient Romans used local tuff extensively; the soft volcanic tuffs of Cappadocia were carved into cave dwellings and churches, and the moai of Easter Island were mostly carved from tuff at the Rano Raraku quarry. Tuff deposits also preserve exceptional fossil assemblages, since ash falls can bury organisms rapidly. Not to be confused with tufa, a porous limestone deposited from mineral springs — a distinction maintained in modern geological usage though the words share an origin in Latin tofus.
