Thermosiphon

Thermosiphon
Also spelt: thermosyphon
Principle: passive circulation driven by buoyancy — hot fluid rises, cold fluid sinks
Requirements: heat source below the heat sink; a closed loop of liquid or gas
Applications: solar water heaters, engine cooling (historical), heat pipes, CPU coolers, reactor passive cooling

A thermosiphon (or thermosyphon) is a method of passive heat exchange in which a fluid circulates in a closed loop without any mechanical pump, propelled solely by natural convection: fluid heated at the bottom of the loop becomes less dense and rises, while cooler, denser fluid from the top sinks to replace it, setting up a continuous circulation that carries heat from source to sink.

Principle

The driving force is the density difference between the hot and cold legs of the loop, acting over their vertical separation. For circulation to start and persist, the heat source must be below the point where heat is removed; if the geometry is reversed, the loop is stable and nothing flows. Flow rate increases with temperature difference and loop height, and decreases with friction in the piping. The same principle operating in an open system — for example hot water rising from a boiler to a tank above — is the basis of the gravity hot-water systems used in older buildings.

Applications

Solar water heating. In a thermosiphon solar system, the storage tank sits above the solar collector; sun-warmed water rises into the tank while cooler water descends to the collector. Such systems are simple and reliable and are extremely common on rooftops in Mediterranean and other sunny climates.

Engine cooling. Early automobiles and tractors, including the Ford Model T, cooled their engines by thermosiphon circulation between engine block and radiator, before water pumps became standard.

Heat pipes and CPU cooling. A sealed two-phase thermosiphon — liquid evaporating at the hot end, condensing at the cold end, and returning by gravity — transfers heat very effectively and is used in electronics cooling, permafrost stabilisation along pipelines, and spacecraft thermal control (in the form of loop heat pipes).

Nuclear safety. Passive natural-circulation cooling, the large-scale thermosiphon principle, is a key safety feature of modern reactor designs, able to remove decay heat for extended periods without electrical power.
