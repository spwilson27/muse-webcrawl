Epigoni

In Greek mythology, the Epigoni (Ancient Greek: Ἐπίγονοι, "offspring" or "born after") are the sons of the Argive heroes known as the Seven against Thebes, who had attacked the city of Thebes and been destroyed. Ten years after their fathers' defeat, the Epigoni avenged them by a second, successful assault on Thebes.

The expedition

The Epigoni traditionally listed include Alcmaeon and Amphilochus (sons of Amphiaraus), Aegialeus (son of Adrastus), Diomedes (son of Tydeus), Promachus (son of Parthenopaeus), Sthenelus (son of Capaneus), Thersander (son of Polynices) and Euryalus (son of Mecisteus). Urged on by their mothers and, in Alcmaeon's case, by the oracle of Apollo, they marched on Thebes under Alcmaeon's leadership. The Thebans, advised by the seer Teiresias, abandoned the city at night, and the Epigoni captured and razed Thebes, installing Thersander — the son of Polynices — on its throne. Aegialeus was the only Epigonus killed in the war, slain by Laodamas, son of Eteocles.

In literature

The expedition was the subject of an early Greek epic, the Epigoni, part of the Theban cycle, attributed to Antimachus of Teos or to Homer; it survives only in fragments. The story was also treated by the tragedians and appears in Apollodorus's Library and Pausanias. The Epigoni were honoured with statues at Delphi described by Pausanias.

Other uses

The word "epigone" (plural "epigones" or "epigoni") entered general usage for a less distinguished follower or imitator of someone, especially an artist or thinker of an earlier generation.
