The Gododdin (Old Welsh Guotodin; Latin Votadini) were a Brittonic people of north-eastern Britain, occupying what is now south-east Scotland and north-east England — roughly Lothian, the Borders and Northumberland — during late antiquity and the early Middle Ages. Their territory ran from the Firth of Forth towards the River Tyne. They are famous above all through the early Welsh poem Y Gododdin, attributed to the bard Aneirin, which elegises a warband of the Gododdin king Mynyddog Mwynfawr destroyed at the Battle of Catraeth around the year 600.

History

The Votadini appear in Roman sources, notably Ptolemy's Geography (2nd century AD), which lists their towns, including Bremenium. Major hillforts such as Traprain Law (East Lothian) — source of a famous hoard of late Roman hacksilver — and Yeavering Bell were centres of their power. Unlike peoples north of the Antonine Wall, the Votadini seem generally to have maintained friendly or client relations with Rome, and their territory may have formed a buffer zone. After the Roman withdrawal they formed one of the kingdoms of the Hen Ogledd, the "Old North", their royal seat at Din Eidyn, on the site of Edinburgh Castle.

Catraeth and decline

Around 600 AD, according to Y Gododdin, Mynyddog Mwynfawr feasted a warband of some 300 picked warriors for a year at Din Eidyn before they rode south to attack Catraeth — usually identified with Catterick, North Yorkshire, then held by the Anglian kingdoms of Deira and Bernicia. Nearly all were killed. Historians differ over the poem's reliability, but it preserves the memory of the struggle between the Britons and the expanding Angles. In 638 Din Eidyn itself was besieged and taken by the Northumbrians, and the Gododdin kingdom was absorbed into what became the Anglian kingdom of Northumbria, though Brittonic language and culture persisted in the region for centuries.

Legacy

Y Gododdin, preserved in the 13th-century Book of Aneirin, is one of the most celebrated works of early Welsh literature and among the earliest surviving literature of Scotland. It contains a possible early reference to Arthur ("though he was not Arthur"), often cited in Arthurian scholarship. The name Gododdin survives in the modern study of the Hen Ogledd and in the identity of Edinburgh and the Lothians.
