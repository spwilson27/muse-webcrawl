Lunar soil

Lunar soil (regolith)
FormationMechanical weathering by meteoroid impacts over billions of years
Typical depth4–5 m in maria; 10–15 m in highlands
CompositionRock and mineral fragments, agglutinate glass, solar-wind-implanted volatiles

Lunar soil is the fine fraction of the lunar regolith, the layer of loose, heterogeneous material covering solid rock on the surface of the Moon. Unlike terrestrial soil, it contains no organic matter and has not been shaped by wind or water; instead it was produced almost entirely by the mechanical disintegration of bedrock through meteoroid and micrometeoroid impacts — a process called comminution — over billions of years.

A boot print in lunar soil, Apollo 11

Properties

Lunar soil consists of rock fragments, mineral grains, glassy agglutinates formed by impact melting, and tiny beads of glass. Its particles are sharp and abrasive because no weathering rounds them, and they cling electrostatically to suits and equipment — a serious concern during Apollo. Solar wind implantation has enriched the soil with volatiles including helium-3, which has been proposed as a potential fusion fuel. The regolith also contains nanophase iron, produced by space weathering, which darkens and reddens the surface over time.

Significance

Apollo missions returned 382 kg of lunar rock and soil, supplemented by Soviet Luna samples such as those of Luna 16. Proposed future uses of lunar soil include radiation shielding, construction material, and in-situ resource utilisation — extracting oxygen from iron oxides and manufacturing solar cells — to support a sustained human presence on the Moon. Lunar dust is considered a health hazard because its fine, sharp particles can irritate lungs and damage equipment.
