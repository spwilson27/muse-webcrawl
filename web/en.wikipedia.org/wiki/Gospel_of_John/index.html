Gospel of John

The Gospel of John (Ancient Greek: Εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Ἰωάννην, romanized: Euangélion katà Iōánnēn) is the fourth of the four canonical gospels in the New Testament. It follows the Synoptic Gospels (Matthew, Mark and Luke) but differs greatly from them in style, content and chronology, and is generally agreed to be the last of the four to be written, around AD 90–110. Tradition attributes it to John the Apostle, son of Zebedee, though most modern scholars regard it as the product of a community ("the Johannine community") and identify its author as John the Evangelist, an unknown figure.

Content and structure

John opens not with a birth narrative but with a poetic Prologue (John 1:1–18) identifying Jesus with the divine Logos (Word):

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God... And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us."

The gospel can be divided into major sections:

  • The Book of Signs (chapters 1–12): Jesus's public ministry, built around seven miraculous "signs," including turning water into wine at Cana, healing the blind man, and raising Lazarus from the dead. Interspersed are long theological discourses, such as the conversations with Nicodemus and the Samaritan woman, and the seven "I am" sayings ("I am the bread of life," "I am the light of the world," and so on).
  • The Book of Glory (chapters 13–20): the Last Supper (which in John includes the foot-washing rather than the institution of the Eucharist), the long Farewell Discourse, the prayer of chapter 17, and the Passion, crucifixion and resurrection narratives.
  • Epilogue (chapter 21): an appearance of the risen Jesus in Galilee and the restoration of Peter; most scholars regard it as an addition to an originally complete work ending at chapter 20.

John 20:30–31 gives the author's purpose: "these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in his name."

Differences from the Synoptic Gospels

John's gospel differs from Matthew, Mark and Luke in chronology (Jesus's ministry spans multiple Passovers, suggesting roughly three years), in content (it omits parables, exorcisms, the Transfiguration and the Lord's Prayer, and adds the wedding at Cana, Lazarus, and long discourses), and in the timing of events such as the cleansing of the Temple and the crucifixion relative to Passover. Jesus in John speaks in extended, self-referential theological discourses rather than the aphorisms and parables of the Synoptics. These differences have long been debated; ancient commentators already called John the "spiritual gospel."

Authorship and dating

The gospel itself is anonymous. Chapter 21 identifies its source as "the disciple whom Jesus loved," later traditionally identified with the apostle John. The earliest clear attribution comes from Irenaeus (c. AD 180), who knew Polycarp, traditionally a disciple of John. Papyrus P52 (the Rylands fragment), dated to roughly AD 125–160, shows the gospel circulating in Egypt within decades of composition. Most scholars date it to AD 90–110 and associate it with a Johannine community, possibly in Ephesus. The story of the woman taken in adultery (John 7:53–8:11) is absent from the earliest manuscripts and is considered a later interpolation.

Theology

John presents the highest Christology of the four gospels: Jesus is the pre-existent, divine Word made flesh, and his crucifixion is portrayed as his "glorification." Prominent themes include light versus darkness, life, truth, belief, and "abiding" in Christ. The gospel's repeated references to "the Jews" as opponents of Jesus have been the subject of extensive scholarly and ethical discussion regarding anti-Judaism in later Christian history.
