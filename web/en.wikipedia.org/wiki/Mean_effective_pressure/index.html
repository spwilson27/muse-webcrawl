Mean effective pressure

A pressure–volume diagram; mean effective pressure is the enclosed work divided by the displacement volume

The mean effective pressure (MEP) is a quantity relating to the operation of a reciprocating engine and is a valuable measure of an engine's capacity to do work that is independent of engine displacement. It is the average pressure over a complete engine cycle that would produce the same net work output as the actual cycle.

Mean effective pressure is defined as the net work of the cycle divided by the piston displacement volume:

MEP = Wnet / Vd

where Wnet is the net work per cycle and Vd is the displacement volume. On a pressure–volume diagram, it corresponds to the height of a rectangle with the same area as the area enclosed by the cycle curve and the same width as the displacement.

Types

Indicated mean effective pressure (IMEP) is computed from the pressure measured inside the cylinder and corresponds to the work delivered to the piston. Brake mean effective pressure (BMEP) is calculated from the torque measured at the crankshaft (for example with a dynamometer) and therefore accounts for friction and accessory losses; it is the most commonly quoted figure for comparing engines. Friction mean effective pressure (FMEP) is the difference between the two and represents the work consumed by friction and pumping.

Use

Because BMEP normalises torque by displacement, it allows engines of different sizes to be compared directly. Naturally aspirated spark-ignition engines typically achieve peak BMEP around 10–13 bar, while turbocharged engines may reach 20 bar or more; modern diesel engines also operate at high mean effective pressures. Engineers use MEP in sizing engines, since for a target power output the product of MEP, displacement, and engine speed determines the power.
