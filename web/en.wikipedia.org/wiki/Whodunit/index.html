Whodunit

A whodunit (a contraction of "who [has] done it?") is a complex, plot-driven variety of detective fiction in which the puzzle regarding who committed a crime is the main focus. The reader is provided with clues from which the perpetrator may be deduced before the story's climax reveals the answer. The investigation is usually conducted by an eccentric, amateur or professional detective, and a closed circle of suspects — often gathered in an isolated setting — provides the classic structure.

The drawing-room denouement is the whodunit's signature scene.

Conventions

Golden Age writers codified the form: Ronald Knox published his "Ten Commandments" of detective fiction (1929) and S. S. Van Dine his "Twenty Rules", insisting the reader must have equal opportunity with the detective and that the culprit be a significant character. Stock elements include red herrings, the least-likely suspect, locked-room mysteries, a final gathering where the detective explains the solution, and "fair play" clueing.

Masters of the form

Agatha Christie — creator of Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple, and of The Murder of Roger Ackroyd and And Then There Were None — is the genre's best-selling exponent; contemporaries include Dorothy L. Sayers, John Dickson Carr and Ellery Queen. The form survives in "cosy" mysteries, television series, stage plays such as The Mousetrap, and films like Knives Out (2019), which revived the all-star ensemble whodunit. Its opposite number is the "howcatchem" or inverted detective story, in which the criminal is known from the start.
