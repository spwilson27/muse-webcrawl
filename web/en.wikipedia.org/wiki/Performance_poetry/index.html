Performance poetry

Performance poetry is poetry that is specifically composed for or during a performance before an audience, rather than being written primarily for the page. It draws on the voice, body, timing, and stage presence of the poet, and often incorporates elements of theatre, music, and audience interaction.

History

Although poetry was performed aloud for millennia, performance poetry as a self-conscious movement emerged in the 20th century through the Beat poets, the Black Arts Movement, and the Caribbean-influenced dub poetry of artists such as Linton Kwesi Johnson and Mutabaruka, which set verse to reggae rhythms. In the 1980s the poetry slam — a competitive format invented by Marc Smith in Chicago and popularised at venues such as the Nuyorican Poets Cafe in New York — brought a demotic, audience-judged style of performance to wide attention.

Forms

The field overlaps with spoken word, slam poetry, dub poetry, and sound poetry, and shades into stand-up comedy, hip hop, and performance art. Performers such as John Cooper Clarke in Britain and, later, poets who emerged through slams and online video, helped carry the form to mainstream audiences and festivals. Critics debate the relationship between page and stage poetry: performance work is sometimes dismissed as theatrical rather than literary, while its defenders stress that dramatic delivery is itself a poetic craft.
