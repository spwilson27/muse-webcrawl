Vehicle size class is a ranking system used to classify cars by their size and utility for regulatory, taxation and marketing purposes. Different systems are used in different regions: in the United States, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) classifies passenger cars by interior volume, while in Europe informal lettered market segments (A through F, plus specialist categories) are in common use.

United States

The US EPA defines size classes for passenger automobiles based on combined passenger and cargo interior volume. Cars are grouped as minicompact, subcompact, compact, mid-size and large; two-seaters form a separate class. Sport utility vehicles, pickup trucks, vans and minivans are classified separately, largely by gross vehicle weight rating and footprint. These classes feed into fuel economy labelling and the corporate average fuel economy (CAFE) standards, which since 2011 have been footprint-based, tying efficiency targets to a vehicle's length multiplied by width.

Europe

European segments are not formally defined by law but are widely used by manufacturers, regulators such as Euro NCAP, and the motoring press. The A-segment covers city cars, B-segment superminis, C-segment small family cars, D-segment large family cars, E-segment executive cars and F-segment luxury cars. Additional categories cover sports cars (S-segment), multi-purpose vehicles (M-segment) and off-road and SUV models (J-segment), which has grown enormously in popularity.

Other systems

Japan's kei car category imposes strict limits on dimensions and engine displacement (660 cc) in exchange for tax and insurance benefits. China, India and other markets maintain their own classifications, often based on wheelbase or engine size, which may differ substantially from Western norms — a car marketed as "compact" in one market may be "mid-size" in another.
