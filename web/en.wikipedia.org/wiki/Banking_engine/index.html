Banking engine

A banking engine is a software system that performs the core transaction-processing functions of a bank. Also described as part of a core banking system, the banking engine maintains the institution's books of record: it processes deposits, withdrawals, payments, transfers, interest accrual and charges against customer accounts, posts entries to the general ledger, and enforces product rules such as limits, holds and fees in real time or in end-of-day batch runs.

Functions

Typical functions of a banking engine include account and customer information management, a transaction-processing and posting engine, interest and tax calculation, standing orders and direct debits, clearing and settlement interfaces to payment schemes, and regulatory and financial reporting. Modern engines expose these functions through application programming interfaces (APIs) so that digital channels, mobile apps and third-party services can transact against the same central ledger — an arrangement sometimes called "hollowing out the core", in which product logic moves to surrounding services while the engine retains the authoritative record of balances.

Architecture

Historically, banking engines ran on mainframes in COBOL and were organised around nightly batch cycles. From the 2000s onwards, banks have migrated toward modular, service-oriented and cloud-hosted engines, including packages from vendors and newer "neo-core" platforms used by digital-only banks. Because the engine underpins virtually all customer activity, its availability, consistency and auditability are critical design requirements.
