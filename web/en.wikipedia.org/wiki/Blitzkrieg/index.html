Blitzkrieg
Literal meaning"Lightning war" (German)
Period of prominence1939–1941

Blitzkrieg (German for "lightning war") is a term used to describe the style of warfare by which Germany achieved rapid victories in the opening years of the Second World War. It emphasised speed, surprise and concentration: armoured divisions, closely supported by aircraft and followed by motorised infantry, broke through at a narrow point (Schwerpunkt), drove deep into the enemy rear, and encircled and destroyed defending forces rather than pushing them back.

Origins

The word appeared in German military writing before 1939 but was popularised by Western journalism during the invasion of Poland. Blitzkrieg had roots in German stormtrooper infiltration tactics of 1918 and in interwar theorists of armoured warfare such as Heinz Guderian, whose advocacy — assisted by the army's thorough use of radio and mission-style command (Auftragstaktik) — gave German panzer divisions unusual flexibility. British theorists such as J. F. C. Fuller and Basil Liddell Hart were also influential, though the German doctrine developed largely indigenously.

Method

In practice the method combined: reconnaissance and air superiority; dive-bomber (Stuka) attacks functioning as flying artillery; panzer and motorised divisions thrusting deep without waiting for the marching infantry; and vast encirclements in which pockets of enemy troops were reduced by follow-on forces. Most of the German army remained horse-drawn and foot-mobile — Blitzkrieg was waged by a mechanised minority at the decisive point, supplied often at the edge of logistical feasibility.

Campaigns

Poland was overrun in five weeks in September 1939. The classic Blitzkrieg campaign was the invasion of France in May–June 1940: the main blow fell through the Ardennes, crossed the Meuse at Sedan, and raced to the Channel, trapping the Allied northern armies and leading to the fall of France and the armistice of 22 June 1940. Similar methods conquered Yugoslavia and Greece in April 1941 and won enormous encirclements in the opening months of Operation Barbarossa against the Soviet Union — yet the vast distances, deteriorating supply and Soviet depth exhausted the method, and after 1941 the Allies and Soviets developed counters: defence in depth, massed artillery and their own operational-level armoured warfare.

Historiographical debate

Historians debate whether Blitzkrieg was ever a formal doctrine or an improvised practice rationalised after the fact. Some (notably Karl-Heinz Frieser) argue that the 1940 campaign succeeded partly by unauthorised boldness of commanders like Guderian and Rommel rather than by a master plan, and that Germany's strategy envisaged short wars from economic necessity rather than tactical theory. Others treat it as a coherent operational method that grew from German traditions of wars of movement and annihilation.
