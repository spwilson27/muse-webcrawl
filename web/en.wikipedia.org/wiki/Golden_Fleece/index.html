In Greek mythology, the Golden Fleece is the fleece of the golden-woolled, winged ram Chrysomallos. The fleece is a symbol of authority and kingship. It figures in the tale of the hero Jason and his crew of Argonauts, who set out on a quest for the fleece by the order of King Pelias in order to place Jason rightfully on the throne of Iolcus in Thessaly.

The ram

Chrysomallos rescued the children Phrixus and Helle from their stepmother Ino; Helle fell into the sea (the Hellespont), while Phrixus reached Colchis, where he sacrificed the ram to Zeus and gave its fleece to King Aeëtes. Aeëtes hung it in an oak grove sacred to Ares, guarded by a sleepless dragon.

Jason's quest

A generation later, Jason sailed in the Argo with heroes including Heracles, Orpheus, and the Dioscuri. In Colchis, Aeëtes set Jason impossible tasks — ploughing with fire-breathing bulls, sowing dragon's teeth, fighting the earth-born warriors — which he accomplished with the help of the king's daughter, the sorceress Medea. Medea charmed the dragon to sleep, and Jason seized the fleece and fled with her.

Interpretations and legacy

Scholars have linked the legend to real practices: Strabo reported that peoples of the Caucasus used fleeces to trap gold dust in streams, a technique still used in Georgia's Svaneti region. In 1430 Philip the Good of Burgundy founded the chivalric Order of the Golden Fleece, whose collar remains associated with the Habsburgs.
