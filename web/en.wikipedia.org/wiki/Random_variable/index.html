Random variable

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A bar chart of a probability distribution over dice-roll totals]

In probability theory and statistics, a random variable is a variable whose values depend on outcomes of a random phenomenon. Formally it is a measurable function from a sample space to the real numbers (or another space), assigning a numerical value to each outcome.

Random variables are discrete (e.g. the count of heads in ten coin tosses — binomial distribution), continuous (e.g. a person's exact height — described by a probability density), or mixed. Capital letters (X, Y) conventionally denote random variables and lowercase their realised values.

Summaries

The expected value E[X] is the probability-weighted average of values — the long-run mean. The variance Var(X) and its square root, the standard deviation, measure spread. The cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) characterises the distribution completely; independence of random variables underpins summation results such as the law of large numbers.

Joint, marginal, and conditional distributions describe relationships between variables, and transformations of variables (sums, functions) extend the machinery — e.g. the sum of two dice, whose distribution is triangular over 2..12.
