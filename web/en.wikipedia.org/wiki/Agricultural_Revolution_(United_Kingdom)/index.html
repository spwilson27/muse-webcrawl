Agricultural Revolution (United Kingdom)

British Agricultural Revolution
Periodc. 1700–1850
Key changesEnclosure, Norfolk four-course rotation, selective breeding, seed drill, improved drainage

The British Agricultural Revolution, or Second Agricultural Revolution, was the unprecedented increase in agricultural production in Britain arising from increases in labour and land productivity between the mid-17th and late 19th centuries. Agricultural output grew faster than population over the century from 1700, and productivity remained among the highest in the world, helping to feed the growing industrial workforce and freeing labour for the Industrial Revolution.

Key developments

Enclosure consolidated scattered strips in open fields and common land into compact, individually farmed holdings, facilitated by a series of Inclosure Acts. The Norfolk four-course rotation — wheat, turnips, barley, and clover — replaced fallowing, provided winter fodder, and restored soil nitrogen. Jethro Tull's seed drill (1701) mechanized sowing, while selective breeding, championed by Robert Bakewell, greatly increased the size and quality of livestock. New crops from the Americas, drainage of fens, marling and liming, and the spread of the turnip enabled year-round feeding of larger herds, whose manure in turn raised cereal yields.

Debates

Historians debate the dating, pace, and social costs of the changes. Revisionists have argued that much improvement was gradual and regionally uneven, and that the sharp rise in output owed much to developments before 1700. Critics have emphasized that enclosure and engrossing of farms displaced smallholders and cottagers, swelling the rural poor even as national output rose.
