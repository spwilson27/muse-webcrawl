Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture

Integrated multi-trophic aquaculture (IMTA) provides the by-products, including waste, from one aquatic species as inputs (fertilizers, food) for another. Farmers combine fed species (such as finfish or shrimp) with extractive species — filter-feeding shellfish, deposit-feeding invertebrates such as sea cucumbers, and seaweeds — that remove particulate and dissolved wastes from the water. The aim is to create balanced systems for environmental sustainability (biomitigation), economic stability (product diversification and risk reduction) and social acceptability (better management practice), mimicking natural ecosystems in which every organism occupies a trophic level and nothing is wasted.

How it works

Fed aquaculture releases dissolved nutrients (ammonia, phosphate) and particulate organic matter (uneaten feed, faeces). In an IMTA system:

  • Seaweeds (e.g., kelps such as Saccharina latissima, Gracilaria) absorb dissolved inorganic nutrients, converting waste nitrogen into harvestable biomass.
  • Suspension feeders (mussels, oysters, scallops) filter fine particles from the water column.
  • Deposit feeders (sea cucumbers, sea urchins, polychaete worms) consume larger particles that settle beneath the cages.

Species are selected not only for waste uptake but for their own market value, so the "waste treatment" components are themselves crops.

History and development

Polyculture has ancient roots — Chinese freshwater farmers have combined carp species with complementary feeding niches for over a thousand years, and rice–fish systems are similarly old. The modern IMTA concept and term were developed from the 1990s–2000s by researchers including Thierry Chopin in Canada, formalising earlier "integrated aquaculture" work in Israel and elsewhere. Pilot and commercial systems operate in Canada (Bay of Fundy salmon–mussel–kelp), China (large-scale IMTA in Sungo Bay), Norway, Scotland, and other countries, increasingly framed within an "ecosystem approach to aquaculture" promoted by the FAO.

Challenges

Obstacles include matching the scale and timing of extractive crops to waste output, food-safety rules (shellfish grown near fish cages may be restricted in some jurisdictions), licensing regimes designed for single-species farms, market development for products such as kelp, and the complexity of managing multiple species. Research continues on carrying-capacity modelling and the placement of components in currents to maximise nutrient capture.
