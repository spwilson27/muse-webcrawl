Community mental health service

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A community mental health service is a system of mental health care delivered in community settings — clinics, health centres, day services, and patients' own homes — rather than in large psychiatric hospitals. Community services became the dominant model in many countries from the mid-twentieth century with deinstitutionalisation, the closure of the old asylums, and legislation such as the United States' Community Mental Health Act of 1963 and Britain's shift to "care in the community."

Organisation

Services are typically organised around multidisciplinary community mental health teams (CMHTs) serving a defined geographic population. A team may include psychiatrists, community psychiatric nurses, social workers, clinical psychologists, occupational therapists, and support workers, coordinating assessment, medication, psychological therapies, care planning (in England, the Care Programme Approach), and crisis intervention. Specialist teams commonly developed alongside generic CMHTs include:

  • Assertive outreach teams, for patients with severe illness who are hard to engage.
  • Crisis resolution and home treatment teams, providing intensive short-term care as an alternative to admission.
  • Early intervention in psychosis teams, for young people with a first episode.
  • Child and adolescent mental health services (CAMHS) and older-adult teams.

Rationale and criticism

The model's rationale is humane and practical: treatment near home preserves social ties, reduces the stigma and institutionalisation of asylum care, and matches resources to need. Persistent criticisms concern underfunding relative to need, gaps between hospital discharge and community provision, homelessness and imprisonment of people with untreated illness, and heavy reliance on medication and on families as unpaid carers. The balance between community care and retaining adequate inpatient beds remains a live policy debate.
