Fescue toxicosis

Fescue toxicosis is a condition of livestock grazing on tall fescue (Festuca arundinacea) infected with the endophytic fungus Epichloë coenophiala (formerly Neotyphodium coenophialum). The fungus produces ergot alkaloids, especially ergovaline, that cause vasoconstriction and disrupt thermoregulation in cattle, sheep, and horses.

Syndromes

"Summer slump" is marked by reduced weight gain, lower milk production, poor conception rates, rough hair coat, and heat intolerance as animals cannot dissipate heat. "Fescue foot" occurs mainly in winter, when cold plus vasoconstriction can cause dry gangrene of the feet, tail, and ears. In horses, mares consuming endophyte-infected fescue may suffer prolonged gestation, thickened or separated placentas, dystocia, and agalactia due to suppression of prolactin.

Management

Control strategies include planting endophyte-free or "novel endophyte" fescue cultivars that retain pest resistance without producing toxic alkaloids, diluting pastures with legumes, rotational grazing, and avoiding feeding infected hay to pregnant mares.
