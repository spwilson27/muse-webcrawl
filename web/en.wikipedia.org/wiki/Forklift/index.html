Forklift

A forklift (also called a lift truck, fork truck, or fork hoist) is a powered industrial truck used to lift and move materials over short distances, typically loads carried on wooden pallets. Two horizontal fork tines slide under the load and raise it hydraulically on a mast. Forklifts are indispensable in warehouses, distribution centres, manufacturing plants, docks, and construction sites.

History

Precursors appeared in the early 20th century, including hoists built by the Clark Equipment Company and the Yale lock company in the 1920s. The introduction of the standardised wooden pallet and the demands of logistics in the Second World War drove rapid development, and the modern counterbalance forklift — with a weight at the rear offsetting the load on the forks — became the standard design.

Design and operation

A typical forklift consists of a chassis, a power source (internal combustion engine, or an electric motor with battery or fuel cell), a hydraulic mast assembly, and the forks. Steering is by the rear wheels, giving a tight turning radius but handling unlike a car. The load centre, rated capacity, and stability triangle govern safe operation: exceeding the rated capacity or travelling with a raised load can tip the truck forward or sideways. Operators control lift, tilt, and travel from a seated or standing position, and overhead guards protect against falling objects.

Types

  • Counterbalance forklifts — the most common type, sitting or standing.
  • Reach trucks — narrow-aisle warehouse trucks that extend their forks forward.
  • Order pickers — raise the operator with the load for picking individual items.
  • Pallet jacks — small hand- or motor-powered fork devices for ground-level moves.
  • Rough-terrain forklifts — large-tyred trucks for construction and agriculture.
  • Telehandlers — combine a telescopic boom with forks.
  • Sideloaders — carry long loads such as timber alongside the chassis.

Safety and regulation

Forklifts are involved in a significant number of workplace injuries, from tip-overs, crushing between the truck and surfaces, and loads falling. Regulations such as the US OSHA powered industrial truck standard (29 CFR 1910.178) require formal operator training, evaluation, and certification; many other countries have equivalent licensing regimes.
