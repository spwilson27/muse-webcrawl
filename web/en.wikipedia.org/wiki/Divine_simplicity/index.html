Divine simplicity

Classical theism holds that God is not composed of parts.

In classical theism, divine simplicity is the doctrine that God is not composed of parts — whether physical parts, metaphysical parts such as form and matter, or distinctions between essence and existence, or between God and his attributes. On this view, God is identical with his own nature: God's goodness, wisdom and power are not separate properties God possesses but are one and the same with God himself. The doctrine was developed by thinkers including Augustine, Anselm, Maimonides, Avicenna and Thomas Aquinas, and is affirmed in much traditional Jewish, Christian and Islamic theology.

Arguments

Defenders argue that anything composed of parts depends on those parts and on whatever assembled them, whereas God, as the first cause, must be absolutely independent (a se). Simplicity is also held to guarantee divine perfection and unity, and to follow from God's pure actuality in Thomistic metaphysics, in which there is no unrealised potentiality in God.

Criticisms

Critics, including Alvin Plantinga, have argued that the doctrine is difficult to make coherent: if God is identical with his properties, God seems to become a property rather than a person, and it is hard to see how attributes as apparently different as mercy and justice could be strictly identical. Defenders respond with distinctions between the attributes as they are in God and as they are conceived by human minds. The doctrine interacts with related questions about the Trinity, divine foreknowledge and the problem of evil.
