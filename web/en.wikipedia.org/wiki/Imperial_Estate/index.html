Imperial Estate

An Imperial Estate (German: Reichsstand, plural Reichsstände) was an entity or individual in the Holy Roman Empire that held a seat and vote in the Imperial Diet (Reichstag). Imperial Estates enjoyed imperial immediacy (Reichsunmittelbarkeit), meaning they held their territories directly from the Emperor rather than through an intermediate lord, and they formed the constitutional backbone of the Empire.

Composition

The Imperial Estates were organised into three colleges within the Diet: the Council of Electors (Kurfürstenrat), the Council of Princes (Fürstenrat) and the Council of Imperial Cities (Städterat). The Council of Princes was further divided into an ecclesiastical bench (prince-bishops, prince-abbots and other prelates) and a secular bench (dukes, margraves, landgraves, counts and lords).

Rights and duties

Imperial Estates owed allegiance and military and financial contributions to the Empire, and in return their rulers exercised territorial sovereignty (Landeshoheit) within their lands, including rights of taxation, coinage in some cases, and high justice. The system was formalised by instruments such as the Golden Bull of 1356 and the religious settlements of the sixteenth century.

The number of Imperial Estates shrank dramatically with the mediatisation of 1803 and the dissolution of the Empire in 1806, when most smaller estates were absorbed into larger states.
