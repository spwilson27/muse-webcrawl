Creep (deformation)

In materials science, creep (sometimes called cold flow) is the tendency of a solid material to undergo slow, permanent deformation under the influence of a constant mechanical load. It can occur as a result of long-term exposure to high levels of stress that are still below the yield strength of the material. Creep is more severe in materials subjected to heat for long periods and generally increases as they approach their melting point. The rate of deformation is a function of the material's properties, exposure time, exposure temperature and the applied structural load.

Temperature dependence

Depending on the magnitude of the applied stress, the temperature and the material, creep may be negligible or may govern the useful life of a component. As a rule of thumb, creep becomes significant for metals at temperatures above roughly 0.4–0.5 of their absolute melting temperature; for polymers it can occur near room temperature. Creep behaviour is commonly measured in a creep test, in which a specimen is held at constant temperature and load while strain is recorded over times that may extend to thousands of hours, producing a creep curve and, by extrapolation, design data such as the Larson–Miller parameter.

Stages of creep

A creep curve is usually divided into three stages. In primary (transient) creep the strain rate decreases with time as the material strain-hardens. In secondary (steady-state) creep the rate is approximately constant, reflecting a balance between hardening and recovery; the steady-state rate is often described by Norton's power law, in which the rate depends on stress raised to a power n. In tertiary creep the rate accelerates — owing to necking, internal cavitation and grain-boundary void formation — until rupture occurs.

Mechanisms

Several microscopic mechanisms can produce creep. At high temperatures and moderate stresses, dislocation creep dominates: dislocations move by a combination of glide and climb over obstacles. At lower stresses, diffusion creep occurs by the transport of atoms through the lattice (Nabarro–Herring creep) or along grain boundaries (Coble creep), causing grains to elongate. Grain-boundary sliding contributes in fine-grained materials and underlies superplastic deformation. These mechanisms are summarised in deformation-mechanism maps, such as those developed by Michael Ashby, which plot the dominant mechanism against stress and temperature.

Engineering significance

Creep limits the life of components that operate hot under load: turbine blades and discs in jet engines and gas turbines (made from nickel-based superalloys, and protected by thermal barrier coatings), steam pipes and boiler tubes in power stations, and nuclear reactor components. Designers use creep-rupture data, apply safety factors, and may monitor components in service. Creep also matters outside metals: in the sagging of old glass, the deformation of glaciers, the settlement of structures on clay or permafrost, and the slow flow of the Earth's mantle that drives post-glacial rebound.
