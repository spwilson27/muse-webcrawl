The Münster rebellion (German: Täuferreich von Münster, "Anabaptist kingdom of Münster") was an attempt by radical Anabaptists to establish a communal sectarian government in the German city of Münster. From February 1534 to June 1535 the city was ruled by Anabaptists who proclaimed it the "New Jerusalem", abolished private property in goods, and — under Jan van Leiden — introduced polygamy and a theocratic kingship. The city was besieged by its expelled prince-bishop, Franz von Waldeck, starved into submission, and retaken by storm; its leaders were tortured and executed, and their corpses displayed in cages hung from the tower of St. Lambert's Church, where the cages remain to this day.

The cages in which the leaders' bodies were displayed still hang from St. Lambert's Church.

Background

Münster, a prosperous trading city in Westphalia, was ruled by a prince-bishop but had a strong civic council and guild tradition. In the early 1530s the preacher Bernhard Rothmann led the city toward evangelical reform; the Lutheran-leaning council expelled the bishop's party in 1532–33. From the Netherlands, followers of the executed Anabaptist prophet Melchior Hoffman — especially Jan Matthys of Haarlem — preached adult baptism and an imminent end of the world, and converts streamed into Münster, which Matthys identified as the New Jerusalem.

The Anabaptist kingdom

In February 1534 the Anabaptists won control of the city council. Catholics and Lutherans who refused rebaptism were expelled in late February, many into the winter cold; their property was confiscated and a system of communal ownership of goods was proclaimed. Books other than the Bible were burned. Jan Matthys arrived and became the city's prophetic leader.

At Easter 1534 Matthys, expecting divine deliverance, led a small sortie against the besieging army and was killed. Leadership passed to his disciple Jan van Leiden, a young tailor's apprentice from Leiden, who in September 1534 was proclaimed king of the New Jerusalem. Jan instituted a royal court, took sixteen wives — citing Old Testament patriarchs — and made polygamy compulsory; resistance, including criticism of the regime, was punished by execution. Money was abolished within the city and food was rationed as the siege tightened.

Siege and fall

Franz von Waldeck blockaded Münster with landsknecht mercenaries, supported by neighbouring princes and, eventually, both Catholic and Lutheran estates alarmed at the precedent. Two assaults in 1534 were repulsed. Through the winter of 1534–35 famine reduced the population to eating cats, dogs, and worse; attempts to break out or send children out of the city ended in massacre or forced return. On the night of 24–25 June 1535, defectors guided the bishop's troops through a weakly guarded stretch of wall, and after bitter fighting in the market square the city fell.

Aftermath

Jan van Leiden, Bernhard Knipperdolling, and Bernhard Krechting were tortured and executed on 22 January 1536; their bodies were placed in three iron cages suspended from the tower of St. Lambert's Church, and their bones left on display for fifty years. The cages remain a famous landmark. Münster was re-Catholicised, and its civic constitution replaced by a more authoritarian settlement.

The catastrophe discredited revolutionary Anabaptism across Europe. Surviving Anabaptists regrouped under leaders such as Menno Simons, who repudiated violence and worldly dominion — the origin of the Mennonite tradition. The rebellion became a lasting polemical symbol: Catholic and Lutheran controversialists cited it against all Anabaptists, and later writers have treated it as a case study in millenarianism, charismatic authority, and siege psychology.

In popular culture

The rebellion is the subject of Giacomo Meyerbeer's opera Le prophète (1849), Friedrich Reck-Malleczewen's novel Bockelson: Geschichte eines Massenwahns (1937), and Marguerite Yourcenar's novel The Abyss (1968). Norman Cohn analysed it in The Pursuit of the Millennium (1957) as a paradigmatic revolutionary millenarian movement.
