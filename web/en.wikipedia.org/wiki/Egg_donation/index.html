Egg donation

Egg donation (oocyte donation) is the process by which a woman donates her eggs (oocytes) so that another person may conceive a child, usually as part of assisted reproductive technology such as in vitro fertilisation (IVF). Donor eggs are used when the intended mother cannot use her own eggs because of age, premature ovarian failure, genetic conditions, cancer treatment, or repeated IVF failure. The first pregnancy achieved with a donated egg was reported in 1983–84 in Australia.

Process

Donors undergo screening for infectious and genetic diseases and psychological assessment. The donor's ovaries are stimulated with gonadotropin injections so that multiple follicles mature; the menstrual cycles of donor and recipient may be synchronised. Mature eggs are retrieved by transvaginal ultrasound-guided aspiration, fertilised with sperm in the laboratory, and the resulting embryos transferred to the recipient's uterus, which is prepared with estrogen and progesterone.

Risks

For donors, risks include ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, bleeding or infection from retrieval, and the effects of anaesthesia; long-term risks are still studied. Recipients face the general risks of IVF and pregnancy.

Legal and ethical issues

Rules vary widely by country. Some jurisdictions (such as the United Kingdom) require donors to be identifiable to offspring at age 18 and prohibit payment beyond expenses, while in the United States paid and anonymous donation is common and compensation can be substantial. Ethical debates concern the health risks to donors, commercialisation, the rights of donor-conceived people to know their origins, and anonymity.
