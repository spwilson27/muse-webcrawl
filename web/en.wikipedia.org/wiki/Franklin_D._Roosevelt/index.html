Franklin D. Roosevelt

Franklin Delano Roosevelt (January 30, 1882 – April 12, 1945), often referred to by his initials FDR, was the 32nd president of the United States, serving from 1933 until his death in 1945 — the longest tenure of any American president, and the only one to be elected to more than two terms. A Democrat from a wealthy New York family, a cousin of President Theodore Roosevelt, he led the country through the Great Depression with the New Deal, a sweeping programme of relief, recovery and reform that reshaped the American state, and through most of World War II, directing the mobilisation that made the United States the "arsenal of democracy" and sitting with Churchill and Stalin at the wartime summits of Casablanca, Tehran and Yalta. He died in office weeks before the German surrender, his health long undermined by the paralytic illness (diagnosed at the time as polio) that had struck him in 1921 and that he concealed from the public throughout his career.

Early life and early career

Roosevelt was born at Hyde Park on the Hudson to James Roosevelt and Sara Delano Roosevelt, grew up in privilege, and was educated at Groton and Harvard. He married his distant cousin Eleanor Roosevelt in 1905 — Theodore Roosevelt, then president, gave her away — and followed his cousin's path into politics as a Democrat: New York state senate in 1910, then Assistant Secretary of the Navy under Woodrow Wilson, where he built a lifelong attachment to the Navy. In 1920 he was the Democratic nominee for vice president on the losing ticket of James M. Cox. In August 1921, on holiday at Campobello Island, he contracted a paralytic illness that left him unable to walk unaided; with iron braces, a cane and a son's or aide's arm — and the connivance of a deferential press — he maintained the fiction of vigorous mobility for the rest of his life, founding the Warm Springs rehabilitation centre in Georgia in the process. He returned to politics nominating Al Smith in 1924 and 1928, and succeeded Smith as governor of New York in 1929, where his response to the Depression — state relief, regulation, public power — made him the obvious Democratic nominee in 1932.

The New Deal

Roosevelt took office on 4 March 1933 at the depth of the Great Depression, with the banking system collapsing, and answered with the "First Hundred Days": a bank holiday and Emergency Banking Act, the end of Prohibition, and a cascade of "alphabet agencies" (CCC, TVA, NRA, AAA) for relief, jobs and recovery. His first "fireside chat" restored confidence in the banks within days. A Second New Deal (1935) added the Social Security Act, the Wagner Act protecting organised labour, and the WPA jobs programme. The New Deal built the modern American welfare and regulatory state and a durable Democratic coalition — though it did not end the Depression (a sharp recession followed his 1937 attempt to balance the budget, and his plan to enlarge the Supreme Court, which had struck down several New Deal measures, failed amid accusations of "court-packing"). Unemployment remained high until war spending arrived.

World War II

From 1939 Roosevelt steered an isolationist country toward the Allied cause: destroyers-for-bases (1940), Lend-Lease (1941), the Atlantic Charter with Churchill, and an undeclared naval war with Germany in the Atlantic. The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941) brought the declaration of war he asked Congress for in the "Day of Infamy" speech; days later Germany declared war on the United States. Roosevelt presided over the vast machinery of mobilisation (16 million in uniform, war production on an unprecedented scale), the "Germany first" grand strategy, and the summit diplomacy of the Grand Alliance, while at home his administration authorised the internment of Japanese Americans (Executive Order 9066, 1942) — the great stain on its civil-liberties record. Re-elected narrowly in 1944 with Harry Truman as his running mate, he attended Yalta visibly failing, and died of a cerebral haemorrhage at Warm Springs on 12 April 1945, sitting for a portrait. His death, weeks before victory in Europe, was mourned around the world; Stalin, it was said, proposed a moment of silence at the Kremlin.

Legacy

Roosevelt is consistently ranked by historians among the three greatest American presidents, with Washington and Lincoln. He transformed the presidency itself into the centre of national life, created the institutional architecture of modern American liberalism, led the democracies to victory in the largest war in history, and — with the United Nations he championed — attempted to institutionalise the peace; his four terms prompted the Twenty-second Amendment (1951) limiting presidents to two. Critics then and since have faulted the court-packing plan, internment, the Yalta concessions, and the precedents of executive power — but the scale of the crises he met, and of his response, remains unmatched in American political history.
