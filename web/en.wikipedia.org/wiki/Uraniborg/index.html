Uraniborg, "Castle of Urania", Tycho Brahe's observatory on Hven

Uraniborg (Danish: Uranienborg) was an astronomical observatory and alchemical laboratory established and operated by the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe on the island of Hven (now Ven, Sweden) in the Øresund. Built between about 1576 and 1580 with the support of King Frederick II of Denmark, who granted Tycho the island and generous funding, it is regarded as one of the first purpose-built research institutes in Europe and the site of the most accurate naked-eye astronomical observations ever made.

The complex

Named after Urania, the muse of astronomy, Uraniborg was a square Renaissance palace roughly 15 metres on a side, built of brick with sandstone ornament, surrounded by extensive gardens laid out in geometric patterns and enclosed by a square earthwork wall with corner pavilions. The main building housed observation platforms with large fixed instruments, a library, living quarters, and an alchemical laboratory in the basement. Because wind and vibration limited the instruments there, Tycho later built the partly underground observatory Stjerneborg ("Castle of the Stars") nearby, where great instruments such as the equatorial armillary and large quadrants were mounted in pits under protective domes.

Work and legacy

At Uraniborg, Tycho and a team of assistants — including, for a time, his sister Sophie Brahe — carried out a systematic programme of observations over some twenty years, achieving positional accuracies of about one arcminute, far better than any predecessors. The complex also had its own paper mill and printing press, workshops for instrument-making, and facilities functioning as an early research community. After falling out with the young King Christian IV, Tycho left Hven in 1597; his observations travelled with him and, after his death, enabled his assistant Johannes Kepler to derive the laws of planetary motion. Uraniborg itself was demolished after Tycho's departure, and only its foundations and garden outlines survive, marked today by a museum and reconstructed features at the Tycho Brahe site on Ven.
