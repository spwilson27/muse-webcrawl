Piracetam is a drug in the racetam family, marketed under the brand name Nootropil and others. It is sold in many countries as a treatment for myoclonus and, in some jurisdictions, cognitive disorders, though evidence for its effectiveness in dementia is weak. It is widely used off-label as a supposed cognitive enhancer, and is regarded as the first nootropic.

History

Piracetam was first synthesised in 1964 by Corneliu E. Giurgea and colleagues at the Belgian company UCB, originally as a derivative of GABA being developed for motion sickness and sleep. Observing unexpected effects on learning in animal tests, Giurgea coined the term "nootropic" in 1972 to describe drugs that might enhance cognition while being protective and low in toxicity.

Pharmacology

Piracetam's mechanism of action is incompletely understood. It is thought to act on cell membranes, increasing their fluidity, and to modulate AMPA-type glutamate receptors and cholinergic neurotransmission; it also reduces platelet aggregation and improves red-cell deformability. It is excreted largely unchanged by the kidneys.

Medical uses and regulation

In Europe piracetam is licensed mainly for cortical myoclonus. Systematic reviews have found insufficient evidence to support its use for dementia or cognitive impairment. In the United States it is not approved by the FDA as a drug or dietary supplement but is sold as a research chemical and supplement ingredient in a legal grey area. Reported side effects include nervousness, insomnia, and headache; it is generally considered well tolerated.
