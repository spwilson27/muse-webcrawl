The American alligator (Alligator mississippiensis), sometimes called a gator, is a large crocodilian native to the southeastern United States. It is one of two living species of alligator (the other is the much smaller Chinese alligator) and inhabits freshwater wetlands — marshes, swamps, rivers and lakes — from coastal North Carolina to the Rio Grande in Texas, with the largest populations in Florida and Louisiana.

Biology

Adult males average about 3.4 metres and can exceed 450 kg; exceptionally large individuals approach 4.6 metres. Alligators are distinguished from crocodiles by their broad, U-shaped snout and by the fourth lower tooth fitting into a socket inside the upper jaw, so it is not visible when the mouth is closed. They are apex predators whose diet shifts from invertebrates and frogs as juveniles to fish, turtles, birds and mammals as adults. Females build mound nests of vegetation and guard their hatchlings; sex is determined by nest temperature. During winter and drought, alligators excavate "gator holes" that retain water and shelter other wildlife, making them a keystone species of their wetlands.

Conservation

Overhunting for hides and habitat loss reduced the species to endangered status by the 1960s; it was among the first species listed under the U.S. Endangered Species Preservation Act in 1967. Strict protection, regulated ranching and habitat conservation produced one of conservation's best-known success stories: the American alligator was declared fully recovered and removed from the endangered list in 1987, and sustainable-use harvests of hides and meat are now permitted. Florida's population is estimated at around 1.3 million.

Relations with humans

Attacks on humans are uncommon but do occur, and nuisance alligators are trapped in Florida and other states. The alligator is the official state reptile of Florida, Louisiana and Mississippi and the mascot of the University of Florida Gators.
