Desensitization (medicine)

In medicine, desensitization is a method to reduce or eliminate an organism's negative reaction to a substance or stimulus. It is used in several distinct senses: in allergology, as drug desensitization to temporarily render a patient tolerant of a medication; in pharmacology, as the diminished response of a receptor or cell to continued stimulation; and in psychology, as the process — including systematic desensitization — by which repeated exposure reduces an emotional response such as fear or anxiety.

Allergen immunotherapy

Allergen-specific immunotherapy, historically called desensitization or hyposensitization, involves administering gradually increasing doses of an allergen over months to years, either subcutaneously or sublingually. It induces immunological tolerance — including regulatory T cells and blocking IgG4 antibodies — and is the only disease-modifying treatment for allergic rhinitis, allergic asthma, and venom allergy.

Drug desensitization

When a patient with a drug allergy urgently needs the offending drug, a rapid desensitization protocol gives minute, doubling doses at short intervals until the full therapeutic dose is tolerated. The state is temporary and lasts only while the drug is administered regularly. Protocols exist for penicillins, chemotherapy agents, monoclonal antibodies, and aspirin (aspirin desensitization is used in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease).

Pharmacological desensitization

In pharmacology, desensitization (tachyphylaxis in its rapid form) is the waning of a response following repeated or continuous exposure to an agonist, often through receptor phosphorylation, arrestin binding, internalisation, or down-regulation — for example, at β-adrenergic receptors.
