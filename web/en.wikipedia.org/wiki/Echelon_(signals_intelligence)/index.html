ECHELON

ECHELON
TypeSignals intelligence (SIGINT) collection and analysis network
OperatorsFive Eyes: United States (NSA), United Kingdom (GCHQ), Canada (CSE), Australia (ASD), New Zealand (GCSB)
Established1971 (reported)
PurposeInterception of satellite, telephone, fax, and internet communications

ECHELON, originally a code name, has become a term in popular usage for a signals intelligence (SIGINT) collection and analysis network operated on behalf of the five signatory states to the UKUSA Agreement — the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, collectively known as the Five Eyes. The system is reported to intercept satellite transmissions, public switched telephone networks, microwave links, and later internet traffic, searching vast volumes of communications for keywords of intelligence interest.

Origins and capabilities

The network grew out of Cold War satellite interception programmes established from the 1960s and 1970s at ground stations such as RAF Menwith Hill in England, Pine Gap in Australia, Waihopai in New Zealand, and sites in Canada and the United States. Reports — including a 2001 investigation by the European Parliament — described a system able to intercept much of the world's satellite-borne telephone, fax, and data traffic, with computers ("dictionaries") scanning content for pre-selected words, phrases, and addresses.

Public disclosure and debate

ECHELON entered public awareness through the work of journalist Duncan Campbell and investigator Nicky Hager, whose 1996 book Secret Power detailed New Zealand's role. The European Parliament's 2001 report concluded that the existence of a global interception system was "no longer in doubt," and raised concerns about economic espionage and privacy. The Five Eyes governments have generally neither confirmed operational details nor denied the system's existence; further revelations about bulk collection followed the 2013 disclosures by Edward Snowden. ECHELON remains a touchstone in debates over mass surveillance, oversight, and the balance between security and civil liberties.
