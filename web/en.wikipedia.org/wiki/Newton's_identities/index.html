Newton's identities

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, Newton's identities, also known as the Newton–Girard formulae, are relations between two kinds of symmetric polynomials, namely between power sums and elementary symmetric polynomials. Evaluated at the roots of a monic polynomial P in one variable, they relate the power sums of the roots to the coefficients of the polynomial.

Newton's identities were published in 1707 in Isaac Newton's Arithmetica Universalis, although the cases for degrees up to 2 and 3 were already given by Albert Girard in 1629. They can be used to compute the elementary symmetric polynomials solely from the power sums, and to determine the discriminant of a polynomial, or the number of distinct roots, from power sums.

Statement

Let x1, …, xn be variables, denote by pk the k-th power sum

pk(x1, …, xn) = x1k + ⋯ + xnk,

and by ek the k-th elementary symmetric polynomial, the sum of all distinct products of k distinct variables (with e0 = 1 and ek = 0 for k > n). Then Newton's identities state that for 1 ≤ k ≤ n,

k ek = Σi=1..k (−1)i−1 ek−i pi,

and for k > n (still with ek = 0),

0 = Σi=k−n..k (−1)i−1 ek−i pi.

First identities

  • e1 = p1
  • 2e2 = e1p1 − p2
  • 3e3 = e2p1 − e1p2 + p3
  • 4e4 = e3p1 − e2p2 + e1p3 − p4

Application to roots of a polynomial

If the xi are the roots of a monic polynomial P(t) = tn + c1tn−1 + ⋯ + cn, then the elementary symmetric polynomials of the roots are (up to sign) the coefficients: ek = (−1)k ck. The identities therefore convert the power sums of the roots into the coefficients of P and vice versa. This is used, for example, to compute the discriminant via the product of squared differences of roots, expressed as a determinant of power sums, and in the theory of companion matrices, whose traces of powers are the power sums of the eigenvalues.

Related identities

Analogous identities relate the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials hk to the power sums: k hk = Σi=1..k hk−i pi. The identities have a conceptual proof via generating functions — differentiating the identity relating the product Π(1 + xit) to the sums of powers of the xi — and generalisations to other settings, including identities for the coefficients of characteristic polynomials and for traces in linear recurrences.
