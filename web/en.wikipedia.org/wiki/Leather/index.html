Leather

Leather is a strong, flexible and durable material obtained from the tanning, or chemical treatment, of animal skins and hides to prevent decay. The most common leathers come from cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, and horses, although skins from deer, kangaroos, reptiles, and fish are also used. Leather has been made since prehistoric times and remains a major material for footwear, clothing, upholstery, bags, bookbinding, and saddlery.

Production

Making leather involves three broad stages. In the preparatory stages, hides are cured, soaked, dehaired with lime, fleshed, and treated to remove non-collagen material. In tanning, the collagen fibres are stabilised so they no longer rot: chrome tanning with chromium salts, the dominant modern method, produces soft, blue-grey "wet blue" leather quickly, while vegetable tanning with plant tannins produces firmer leather traditionally used for soles, belts, and saddlery; alum, oils, and synthetic agents are also used. In crusting and finishing, the leather is thinned, dyed, lubricated with oils (fatliquoring), dried, softened, and given surface finishes.

Grades and types

Full-grain leather retains the natural grain surface and is considered the highest quality; top-grain is sanded to remove imperfections; split leather is made from the fibrous lower layers, often as suede; and bonded leather is reconstituted from shredded fibres. Nubuck and suede are distinguished by their napped finishes.

Uses and issues

Global leather production exceeds a billion square metres annually, largely as a by-product of the meat industry. The trade raises environmental concerns — particularly chromium-laden effluent from poorly regulated tanneries — and animal-welfare objections that have spurred development of alternatives, including synthetic microfibre materials and lab-grown or plant-based "vegan leathers".
