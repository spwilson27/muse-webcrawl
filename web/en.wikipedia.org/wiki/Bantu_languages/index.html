The Bantu languages are a large branch of the Niger–Congo language family, comprising several hundred languages spoken across central, eastern and southern Africa. The word bantu means "people" in many of the languages (the plural of muntu, "person"). The Bantu-speaking peoples spread from a homeland near the present-day Nigeria–Cameroon border across most of sub-equatorial Africa in the Bantu expansion, one of the major population movements of prehistory, beginning roughly 4,000–5,000 years ago.

Features

Bantu languages are characterised by an elaborate noun-class system — typically 10 to 20 classes marked by prefixes, with agreement running through verbs, adjectives and pronouns — and by agglutinative verb morphology encoding subject, tense, aspect, mood and object markers. Most are tonal. Word order is generally subject–verb–object. Swahili, with roughly 16 classes in its reduced system and millions of second-language speakers, is the best-known example; other widely spoken Bantu languages include Zulu, Xhosa, Shona, Kinyarwanda, Kirundi, Lingala, Kikuyu, Chichewa and the Sotho–Tswana group.

Classification

Malcolm Guthrie's influential referential classification divided the Bantu zone into fifteen geographic zones (A–S) with numbered groups, still used as reference codes (e.g. Swahili G42, Zulu S42), though genetic classifications continue to be revised. The closely related "Narrow Bantu" grouping excludes some north-western relatives sometimes classed as semi-Bantu.

History

Comparative linguistics and archaeology link the expansion of Bantu speech to the spread of farming and iron-working. By the early centuries AD Bantu-speaking communities had reached the Great Lakes and southern Africa, displacing or absorbing Khoisan and pygmy populations; loanwords from click languages into Zulu and Xhosa preserve traces of this contact.
