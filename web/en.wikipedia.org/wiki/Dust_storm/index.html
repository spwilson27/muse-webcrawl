Dust storm

A dust storm, also called a sandstorm, is a meteorological phenomenon common in arid and semi-arid regions, arising when a gust front or other strong winds blow loose sand and dirt from a dry surface into the air. Particles are transported by saltation and suspension, sometimes over thousands of kilometres. Dust storms can reduce visibility to near zero, damage crops and machinery, and harm respiratory health.

Causes

As wind passes over loose, dry soil, fine particles are lifted; larger grains bounce along the surface in saltation, dislodging still more material. Thunderstorm outflow boundaries can raise towering dust walls called haboobs. Drought, overgrazing and poor land management increase susceptibility — famously contributing to the American Dust Bowl of the 1930s.

Notable events and regions

Major dust source regions include the Sahara (whose dust fertilizes the Amazon rainforest with phosphorus), the Gobi Desert, the Arabian Peninsula, central Australia and the North American Great Plains. The 2009 Australian dust storm turned Sydney's skies red-orange; huge Martian dust storms can envelop the entire planet Mars for weeks.

Effects

Beyond visibility and health hazards, dust storms strip topsoil, abrade machinery and buildings, disrupt aviation and solar power, and transport minerals, microbes and pollutants across oceans. Airborne mineral dust also influences climate by scattering and absorbing radiation and seeding clouds.
