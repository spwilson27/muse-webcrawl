Magna Carta Libertatum ("Great Charter of Freedoms"), commonly called Magna Carta, is a royal charter of rights agreed to by King John of England at Runnymede, near Windsor, on 15 June 1215. Drafted amid a baronial revolt against John's heavy taxes, arbitrary justice and failed wars, it was the first formal document stating that the king and his government were not above the law: it limited scutage and feudal dues, protected the Church's freedoms, standardised measures, reformed the courts, and — in its most celebrated clauses — promised that no free man would be seized, imprisoned or ruined except by lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land (clause 39), and that justice would not be sold, denied or delayed (clause 40).

Fate and legacy

The 1215 charter failed within weeks — Pope Innocent III annulled it and England fell into the First Barons' War — but it was reissued in the names of the boy-king Henry III (1216, 1217) and definitively in 1225, entering English law as the price of taxation. Its practical content largely concerned a narrow class of "free men", yet its myth outgrew its text: rediscovered by lawyers like Sir Edward Coke in the 17th century, it became the symbol of due process, habeas corpus and constitutional limits on power, cited by the American founders and echoed in the US Constitution and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Only three clauses of the 1225 version remain in English statute law today (including the famous clause 29), but Magna Carta endures as the founding icon of the rule of law — the judicial idea that government itself is under law.
