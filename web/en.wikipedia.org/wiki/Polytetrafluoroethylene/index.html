Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) is a synthetic fluoropolymer of tetrafluoroethylene. It is a high-molecular-weight polymer consisting wholly of carbon and fluorine, best known by the DuPont (now Chemours) brand name Teflon. PTFE is a hydrophobic, waxy white solid famous for its non-stick properties and for having one of the lowest coefficients of friction of any solid; it is chemically inert to almost all reagents and an excellent electrical insulator.

Discovery

PTFE was discovered by accident in 1938 by Roy J. Plunkett, a chemist at DuPont investigating new refrigerants. A cylinder of tetrafluoroethylene gas appeared to have lost pressure while retaining its weight; cutting it open revealed a slippery white powder — the gas had polymerized spontaneously. DuPont patented the material, registered the Teflon trademark in 1945, and commercialized it after the Second World War; an early classified use was in valves and seals for the Manhattan Project's uranium enrichment plant, where its resistance to corrosive uranium hexafluoride was essential.

Properties

The strength of the carbon–fluorine bond gives PTFE its extreme chemical resistance and thermal stability, with a melting point of 327 °C and continuous service up to about 260 °C. It does not melt into a flowing liquid but forms a translucent gel, so parts are made by cold pressing and sintering rather than ordinary melt processing. Its dielectric properties are outstanding, and its surface energy is so low that almost nothing sticks to it. Above roughly 300–400 °C it decomposes, releasing fumes that can cause polymer fume fever in humans and are lethal to birds.

Applications

PTFE is used in non-stick cookware coatings; plumber's thread-seal tape; gaskets, seals, bearings, and linings in chemical processing; insulation for high-performance wiring and coaxial cables; expanded PTFE (ePTFE) membranes in waterproof-breathable fabrics (Gore-Tex) and medical implants; and as a lubricant additive. Its production historically used the processing aid PFOA, a persistent pollutant ("forever chemical") linked to health concerns, which manufacturers have phased out in favour of alternatives.
