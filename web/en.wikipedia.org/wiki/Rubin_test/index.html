Rubin test

The Rubin test, also known as tubal insufflation, is a diagnostic procedure used to determine whether the fallopian tubes of a woman are patent (open and unobstructed). It was developed by Isidor Clinton Rubin, an American gynecologist, who first described the technique in 1920.

Procedure

In the Rubin test, carbon dioxide gas is introduced through the uterus into the fallopian tubes under controlled pressure. If the tubes are patent, the gas passes through them into the peritoneal cavity, which can be detected by auscultation (listening for bubbling sounds over the abdomen), by a drop in pressure on a manometer, or afterwards by shoulder-tip pain caused by the gas irritating the diaphragm.

History and use

The test was once widely used in the investigation of female infertility, since tubal blockage is a common cause. It has largely been superseded by more informative imaging techniques, such as hysterosalpingography (HSG), in which contrast dye is injected and followed by X-ray, and by laparoscopy with chromopertubation, which allow direct visualisation of the tubes.

Risks

Possible complications include gas embolism, infection and the spread of an undiagnosed pelvic infection; the test is contraindicated in the presence of active pelvic inflammatory disease or pregnancy.
