The Battle of Caporetto (also known as the Twelfth Battle of the Isonzo) was fought from 24 October to 19 November 1917 on the Italian Front of the First World War. A combined Austro-Hungarian and German offensive shattered the Italian Second Army along the upper Isonzo River and forced a general Italian retreat of more than 100 kilometres to the Piave River. In Italian usage, "Caporetto" (present-day Kobarid, Slovenia) became a byword for a catastrophic defeat.

Background

After eleven costly battles of the Isonzo, the Italian army under Chief of Staff Luigi Cadorna had made only limited gains. Alarmed by the Italian successes of the Eleventh Battle, Austria-Hungary requested German assistance. Germany formed the new Fourteenth Army under General Otto von Below, stiffened with elite divisions and artillery, and planned a breakthrough using short, intense bombardment, poison gas, and infiltration tactics.

The offensive

The attack opened before dawn on 24 October 1917 with a massive artillery and gas bombardment. German and Austro-Hungarian assault troops bypassed strongpoints and penetrated deep into the valleys behind the Italian lines. The Italian Second Army, under General Luigi Capello, who was ill and whose defensive dispositions were disputed, disintegrated within days. Caporetto itself fell on the first day, and Udine, Cadorna's headquarters, was abandoned on 28 October.

The retreat

The Italian army fell back across the Tagliamento and finally to the line of the Piave River, which it held in November. Italian losses were enormous: roughly 13,000 killed, 30,000 wounded, and some 265,000–300,000 taken prisoner, with hundreds of thousands more straggling or deserting, and about 3,000 guns lost. Cadorna was dismissed and replaced by Armando Diaz, who rebuilt the army with better treatment of the troops and a renewed defensive strategy.

Consequences

The disaster brought Britain and France to send divisions to Italy and led to the creation of the Supreme War Council at the Rapallo Conference. Italy's recovery on the Piave and its victory at Vittorio Veneto a year later redeemed the defeat in national memory, but Caporetto remains the classic Italian example of military collapse and a central episode in works such as Ernest Hemingway's A Farewell to Arms.
