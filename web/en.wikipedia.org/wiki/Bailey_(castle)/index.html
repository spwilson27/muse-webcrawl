Bailey (castle)

Bailey

TypeCastle courtyard / enclosure
Typical defencesCurtain wall or palisade, ditch, gatehouse
ContentsGreat hall, chapel, kitchen, stables, workshops, well

A bailey or ward in a castle is a courtyard enclosed by a curtain wall. In particular, an early type of European castle was known as a motte and bailey: a wooden or stone keep (the fortified residence) sat on a raised earthwork called a motte, accompanied by a walled courtyard — the bailey — attached at its foot.

Function

Whereas the motte and keep formed the last defensive refuge, the bailey was the working heart of the castle. It contained the great hall, chapel, kitchens, bakehouses, stables, smithies, stores and lodgings for the garrison and household, and a well or cistern to withstand siege. In peacetime the bailey served as an administrative and economic centre for the surrounding lordship, sheltering livestock and retainers in wartime.

Layout

Early baileys were defended by a timber palisade on an earth rampart, usually with an external ditch; later examples were walled in stone with mural towers and a gatehouse. Larger castles might possess two or more wards — an outer bailey for services and stabling and an inner bailey containing the principal buildings. The word derives from the Old French baille, a palisade or enclosure.

Examples

Well-preserved baileys can be seen at the Tower of London (whose wards grew around the White Tower), Windsor Castle (with upper and lower wards), and many Norman castles across Britain and France that began as motte-and-bailey earthworks in the 11th century.
