Schmidt–Cassegrain telescope

Light path in a Schmidt–Cassegrain telescope

The Schmidt–Cassegrain telescope is a catadioptric telescope that combines a Cassegrain reflector's optical path with a Schmidt corrector plate to create a compact instrument with a long effective focal length. It merges the wide-field correction of Bernhard Schmidt's camera design (1930) with the folded Cassegrain configuration.

Design

Light enters through a thin aspheric corrector plate at the front of the tube, strikes a spherical primary mirror at the back, is reflected forward to a convex secondary mirror near the corrector, and is reflected again through a hole in the centre of the primary to a focal point behind the telescope. The design corrects spherical aberration via the corrector plate and gives a relatively flat field, though residual coma and field curvature remain.

Popularity

Because the folded path makes the tube much shorter than a Newtonian of equivalent focal length, Schmidt–Cassegrains became the most popular type of telescope among advanced amateur astronomers and educational institutions from the 1970s onward. Commercial models by companies such as Celestron and Meade are commonly produced in apertures from 5 to 16 inches, typically at focal ratios around f/10.

Variants

Related designs include the Maksutov–Cassegrain, which replaces the Schmidt corrector with a thick meniscus lens, and specialised corrected variants such as Celestron's EdgeHD and Meade's Advanced Coma-Free (ACF) optics, which reduce off-axis coma.
