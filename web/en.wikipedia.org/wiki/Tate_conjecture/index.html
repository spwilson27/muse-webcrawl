Tate conjecture

Tate conjecture
Field: Arithmetic geometry
Proposed by: John Tate (1963)
Status: Open in general

In arithmetic geometry, the Tate conjecture is a description of algebraic cycles on a variety in terms of Galois representations. Proposed by John Tate in 1963, it is one of the central open problems linking geometry and number theory.

Statement

For a smooth projective variety X over a finitely generated field, the conjecture says that algebraic cycle classes are exactly those classes in ℓ-adic cohomology that are fixed by the absolute Galois group — in other words, that the cycle class map onto Galois-invariant cohomology classes is surjective.

Significance and progress

The conjecture is an arithmetic analogue of the Hodge conjecture and implies important results about ranks of groups of cycles and L-functions. It is known for divisors in many settings, for abelian varieties over finite fields (Tate, with later work by others), and for some classes of surfaces; Gerd Faltings proved the Tate conjecture for abelian varieties over number fields as part of his proof of the Mordell conjecture. The general case remains open.
