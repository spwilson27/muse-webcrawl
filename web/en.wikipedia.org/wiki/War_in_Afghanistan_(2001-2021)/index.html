The War in Afghanistan was a conflict that took place from 2001 to 2021 in Afghanistan. It began when the United States and its allies invaded the country following the September 11 attacks, seeking to deny al-Qaeda a safe haven and to remove the Taliban government that harboured it. It ended with the Taliban's return to power in August 2021 as US and NATO forces withdrew.

Background

After the 9/11 attacks, US President George W. Bush demanded that the Taliban hand over Osama bin Laden. When the Taliban refused, Operation Enduring Freedom began on 7 October 2001 with airstrikes, followed by ground operations alongside the Northern Alliance. Kabul fell in November 2001 and the Taliban regime collapsed within weeks.

Insurgency and nation-building

An interim government under Hamid Karzai was installed, and the NATO-led International Security Assistance Force (ISAF) expanded across the country. However, the Taliban regrouped in Pakistan's tribal areas and launched a growing insurgency. A US troop "surge" in 2009–2011 temporarily reversed Taliban gains. Osama bin Laden was killed by US forces in Abbottabad, Pakistan, in May 2011.

Drawdown and Taliban offensive

ISAF ended combat operations in 2014, handing security responsibility to Afghan forces. In February 2020 the United States signed the Doha Agreement with the Taliban, committing to a full withdrawal. In the summer of 2021, as the withdrawal proceeded under President Joe Biden, the Taliban launched a sweeping offensive; provincial capitals fell in rapid succession and Kabul was captured on 15 August 2021. President Ashraf Ghani fled the country, and the last US military aircraft departed Kabul airport on 30 August 2021.

Human cost

The war killed well over 170,000 people, including tens of thousands of Afghan civilians, Afghan security personnel, Taliban fighters, and about 2,400 US service members. It was the longest war in United States history.
