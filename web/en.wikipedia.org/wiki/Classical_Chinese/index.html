Classical Chinese

Classical Chinese (古文 gǔwén, "ancient text", or 文言文 wényánwén, "literary language") is the language of the classic literature of China from the end of the Spring and Autumn period (5th century BC) through the end of the Han dynasty (220 AD), and the written form modelled on it that remained the formal standard for writing in China and the wider Sinosphere — Korea, Japan, and Vietnam — until the early twentieth century. It differs markedly from modern spoken Chinese varieties and from written vernacular Chinese (baihua).

Features

Classical Chinese is terse and largely monosyllabic: most words consist of a single character, grammar words carry much of the structure, and subjects and tense markers are frequently omitted. It lacks punctuation in traditional editions, and meanings depend heavily on parallel prose patterns and context. Pronouns, particles such as 之, 乎, 者, 也, and flexible use of words across parts of speech distinguish it from vernacular writing.

History and influence

Based on the language of texts such as the Analects of Confucius, the Mencius, and the historical chronicles, Classical Chinese became the vehicle of government, scholarship, and literature for two millennia. Korea (hanmun), Japan (kanbun), and Vietnam (Hán văn) adapted it for their own literatures. In China it was displaced as the standard by written vernacular Chinese after the New Culture Movement of the 1910s–20s, championed by figures such as Hu Shih, though it is still taught in schools across China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong.
