Protein engineering

Protein engineering is the process of developing useful or valuable proteins through the design and production of unnatural polypeptides, often by modifying amino acid sequences found in nature. It is a young discipline, with much research taking place into the understanding of protein folding and recognition of factors governing protein structure and function. Two broad strategies are used: rational design, based on structural and functional knowledge, and directed evolution, which mimics natural selection in the laboratory.

Rational design

In rational design, detailed knowledge of a protein's structure and function is used to make desired changes, typically by site-directed mutagenesis of the encoding gene. Changes may be as small as a single amino acid substitution or involve constructing fusion proteins and swapping domains. Rational design is limited by how well structure–function relationships are understood.

Directed evolution

Directed evolution creates large libraries of protein variants through random mutagenesis (e.g. error-prone PCR) or recombination of related genes (DNA shuffling), then screens or selects those variants with improved properties. Repeated rounds imitate natural evolution. The approach requires no structural knowledge and has produced enzymes with dramatically improved stability, activity and altered substrate specificity. Frances Arnold pioneered the method and shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.

De novo design and computational methods

De novo design aims to construct proteins from scratch, using physical principles and computation. Software such as Rosetta, and laboratories such as David Baker's, have designed novel folds and binders. Machine-learning advances — including AlphaFold for structure prediction and sequence-generating models — have rapidly expanded the reach of computational protein design in the 2020s.

Applications

Engineered proteins are used as industrial enzymes (detergents, food processing, biofuels), therapeutic proteins (engineered insulin analogues, monoclonal antibodies), diagnostics and biosensors, and research tools such as fluorescent proteins and optogenetic actuators.
