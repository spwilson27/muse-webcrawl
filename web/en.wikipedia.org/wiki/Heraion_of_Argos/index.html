Heraion of Argos

LocationBetween Argos and Mycenae, Argolis, Greece
TypeSanctuary of Hera
BuiltCult from 8th century BC; classical temple c. 420 BC
Excavated1892–1895 (Charles Waldstein, American School of Classical Studies)

The Heraion of Argos (Greek: Ἡραῖον Ἄργους) was the principal sanctuary of the goddess Hera in the Argolis, situated on terraced slopes below Mount Euboea, midway between the cities of Argos and Mycenae. Hera was the patron deity of Argos — "Hera of Argos" is one of her standard Homeric epithets — and the sanctuary served as a religious and political centre for the whole Argive plain.

History

Cult activity began in the Geometric period (8th century BC) on a site above a Mycenaean settlement. The first temple, built around the early 7th century BC, belonged to the earliest generation of Greek monumental temples. The priestesses of the Argive Heraion were figures of Panhellenic record: Hellanicus of Lesbos organised Greek chronology by their succession, and Herodotus tells the famous story of the brothers Cleobis and Biton, who yoked themselves to their mother's cart to draw the priestess Cydippe to the festival and were granted death in their sleep as the best gift for mortals.

The early temple burned in 423 BC, when the priestess Chrysis fell asleep and her lamp set the festival garlands alight (Thucydides). A new Doric temple, designed by the architect Eupolemos, was built around 420–410 BC on a lower terrace; it housed a chryselephantine cult statue of Hera by Polykleitos, rivalling his brother-artist Phidias's works at Olympia. Pausanias describes its sculptural programme, including the birth of Zeus and the Gigantomachy.

Site

The sanctuary is arranged on three terraces: the upper terrace with the old temple foundations, the middle terrace with the classical temple, and a lower terrace with a stoa, gymnasium-like buildings and processional way. A sacred road connected the Heraion with Argos, along which the annual procession of the Heraia festival travelled, culminating in sacrifice and games; the prize for victors was a bronze shield, giving the games the name "the Shield of Argos."

Excavation

The site was excavated in 1892–1895 by Charles Waldstein for the American School of Classical Studies at Athens — the school's first major excavation — yielding rich votive deposits of bronzes, terracottas and the celebrated Late Geometric "Argive Heraion krater" fragments.
