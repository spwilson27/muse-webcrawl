Xingu River

The Xingu River (Portuguese: Rio Xingu) is a 1,640-kilometre (1,020 mi) river in north-eastern Brazil and a major south-bank tributary of the Amazon River. Its basin covers some 520,000 km² across the states of Mato Grosso and Pará, and it is the home of many indigenous peoples, including those of the Xingu Indigenous Park.

Course

The Xingu rises in the cerrado highlands of northern Mato Grosso, formed by the union of several headwater streams (including the Culuene, Ronuro and Batovi rivers). It flows generally northward through the rainforest, joined by its main tributary, the Iriri, before bending eastward around the Volta Grande ("Big Bend") and emptying into the Amazon near Porto de Moz. The river is a clearwater river, with relatively low sediment loads compared with the muddy Amazon mainstream, and passes over numerous rapids where it crosses the Brazilian Shield.

Indigenous peoples

The upper Xingu is one of the most culturally diverse regions of Amazonia. The Xingu Indigenous Park, created in 1961 after a campaign by the Villas-Bôas brothers, was Brazil's first large indigenous reserve; it protects some 6,000 people from peoples including the Kuikuro, Kalapalo, Kamayurá, Wauja, Kayapó (in adjacent territories) and others, who share an inter-village ceremonial system centred on rituals such as the Kuarup. Archaeology shows that large, planned pre-Columbian towns once existed in the upper basin.

Belo Monte dam

On the Volta Grande, Brazil built the Belo Monte Dam, one of the largest hydroelectric projects in the world by installed capacity (11,233 MW). Completed in the late 2010s, it diverts most of the river's flow through canals, generating persistent controversy over its effects on riverine communities, fisheries and the flow regime of the Big Bend.
