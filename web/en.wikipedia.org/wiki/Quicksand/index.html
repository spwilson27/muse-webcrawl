Quicksand

Quicksand is a mixture of sand, clay, and water that behaves as a liquid when disturbed. It forms in saturated loose sand when the sand is suddenly agitated: when water in the sand cannot escape, it creates a liquefied soil that loses strength and cannot support weight. Quicksand occurs in places such as river estuaries, tidal flats, beaches, and where underground springs flow upward through loose sand.

Properties

Undisturbed quicksand appears solid, but a small change in stress — a footstep, for instance — causes liquefaction: the friction between sand grains drops sharply and the mixture behaves like a viscous liquid. Research published in 2005 showed that the friction between particles, and the resulting force needed to extract an object, can be extremely large, so that pulling a trapped foot free at even moderate speed would require the force needed to lift a medium-sized car. Slow, gradual movements, by contrast, allow the mixture to reorganize and are the recommended method of escape.

Danger to humans

Contrary to popular depictions in film and television, a person cannot be fully submerged and swallowed by quicksand: the density of the liquefied mixture is about twice that of the human body, so a person floats at about waist to chest level once fully immersed. The real dangers are exposure, dehydration, hypothermia, and drowning by an incoming tide while trapped. Escape is best achieved by slowly wiggling the legs to let water flow into the voids, leaning back to distribute weight, and working free gradually.

In popular culture

Quicksand was a ubiquitous peril in adventure films and television of the mid-20th century, appearing in a strikingly large share of movies of the 1960s. Its frequency in fiction far exceeds its actual danger and it has been described as a trope of jungle adventure stories.
