The Hawaiian Islands (Hawaiian: Mokupuni Hawaiʻi) are an archipelago of eight major volcanic islands, several atolls and numerous islets in the North Pacific Ocean, extending some 2,400 kilometres (1,500 mi) from the island of Hawaiʻi in the south to Kure Atoll in the north. Formerly known to Europeans as the Sandwich Islands, the group — together with the Northwestern Hawaiian Islands (the Leeward Islands), largely protected within Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument — makes up most of the U.S. state of Hawaii.

Geology

The islands were formed by volcanic activity from an undersea magma source called the Hawaii hotspot. As the Pacific Plate moves northwest over the stationary hotspot, new volcanoes form at the south-east end of the chain while older islands erode and subside to the northwest — continuing into the Emperor Seamount chain. The Big Island of Hawaiʻi, home to the active volcanoes Kīlauea and Mauna Loa, is less than a million years old; Kauaʻi, at the northwestern end of the main group, is about five million years old. A new island, Kamaʻehuakanaloa (formerly Lōʻihi), is growing on the seafloor southeast of Hawaiʻi.

The main islands

  • Hawaiʻi ("the Big Island") — largest, with Mauna Kea, the tallest mountain on Earth measured from its base.
  • Maui — "the Valley Isle," dominated by Haleakalā volcano.
  • Oʻahu — "the Gathering Place," seat of the state capital Honolulu and Pearl Harbor.
  • Kauaʻi — "the Garden Isle," oldest of the main islands.
  • Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, Niʻihau (privately owned, restricted access) and uninhabited Kahoʻolawe.

History

Polynesian voyagers, probably from the Marquesas and later Tahiti, settled the islands between about 1000 and 1200 CE. Captain James Cook reached them in 1778 and named them the Sandwich Islands. Kamehameha I unified most of the islands into the Kingdom of Hawaii by 1810. The kingdom was overthrown in 1893, annexed by the United States in 1898, and Hawaii became the 50th state in 1959.

Ecology

Isolated for millions of years, the islands evolved extraordinary endemic biodiversity — including the Hawaiian honeycreepers and the silversword alliance — much of it now threatened by introduced species, habitat loss and disease. The islands span climates from tropical rainforest on windward slopes to alpine tundra on Mauna Kea's summit.
