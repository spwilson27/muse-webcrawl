The 2003 invasion of Iraq was the first stage of the Iraq War. A coalition led by the United States and the United Kingdom invaded Iraq on 20 March 2003, asserting that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction (WMD) and had ties to terrorism. The invasion began with an air campaign and a ground assault that rapidly overwhelmed Iraqi forces; Baghdad fell on 9 April 2003, and the statue of Saddam Hussein in Firdos Square was toppled the same day. On 1 May 2003, US President George W. Bush declared the end of major combat operations.

Background and rationale

The United States and Britain justified the invasion principally on claims that Saddam Hussein's regime was developing weapons of mass destruction in defiance of UN Security Council resolutions, citing Resolution 1441 (2002), which had returned weapons inspectors to Iraq. No stockpiles of WMD were found after the invasion, and the Iraq Survey Group concluded that Iraq's programmes had largely ended in the 1990s. The invasion proceeded without an explicit further Security Council authorisation, and its legality was widely contested; UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan later called it illegal. Mass protests against the war took place worldwide in February 2003.

Course of the invasion

Coalition forces attacked from Kuwait in the south, with special forces operations in the north and west. Key battles included Umm Qasr, Nasiriyah and Basra; British forces secured the south while US armoured columns drove on Baghdad in "thunder runs". Saddam Hussein went into hiding; he was captured in December 2003, tried by an Iraqi tribunal and executed in 2006.

Aftermath

The Coalition Provisional Authority governed Iraq until mid-2004. Decisions to disband the Iraqi army and pursue de-Ba'athification are widely seen as contributing to a violent insurgency, sectarian civil war (2006–2008) and instability that persisted long after the formal end of the Iraq War in 2011. Casualty estimates vary widely; Iraqi civilian and combatant deaths from the invasion phase alone are estimated in the tens of thousands, and total war-related deaths over the conflict's course are far higher. Official inquiries, including the US Senate intelligence reports and the British Chilcot Report (2016), criticised the intelligence handling and planning for the aftermath.
