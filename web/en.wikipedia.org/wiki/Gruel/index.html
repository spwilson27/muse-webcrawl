Gruel

Gruel is a food consisting of some type of cereal — such as ground oats, wheat, rye or rice — heated or boiled in water or milk. It is a thinner version of porridge that may be more often drunk rather than eaten. Historically, gruel has been a staple of the Western diet, especially for peasants, and was also fed to invalids and prisoners.

History

Gruel was a basic food in Europe from antiquity through the early modern period, made from whatever grain was available. In the 19th century it became notorious as the meagre fare of workhouses and institutions; Charles Dickens immortalised it in Oliver Twist (1838), in the scene where Oliver asks for more. Gruel was also a standard invalid food, recommended in Victorian cookery books for the sickroom.

Varieties

Oatmeal gruel, barley gruel and rice gruel ("water gruel", sometimes flavoured with butter, sugar, or wine) are among the traditional forms. Similar thin cereal preparations exist worldwide, from Chinese congee to Scandinavian velling.

In culture

Because of its institutional associations, "gruel" acquired a connotation of meagreness and misery, giving rise to the adjective "gruelling" for an exhausting experience.
