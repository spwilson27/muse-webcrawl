Great Peacemaker

The Great Peacemaker (in Mohawk, Skén:nen rahá:wi), sometimes known as Deganawida or Tekanawí:ta, was, according to Haudenosaunee oral tradition, the prophet and statesman who, together with Hiawatha and the clan mother Jigonhsasee, founded the Iroquois Confederacy (Haudenosaunee) by uniting the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca nations under the Great Law of Peace (Kaianere'kó:wa). The date of the founding is debated, with proposals ranging from the 12th to the 16th century.

The tradition

According to the epic tradition, the Peacemaker was born to a virgin mother and prophesied to bring a message of peace to warring nations. He travelled by a white stone canoe, first converting the cannibal Hiawatha, whose grief over the loss of his daughters he soothed with the Condolence Ceremony and strings of wampum. Together they won over the nations one by one; the last holdout was the Onondaga sorcerer-chief Tadodaho, whose snakes-for-hair were combed out by the united nations, after which he accepted the Great Law and became its keeper at Onondaga, the confederacy's capital and firekeeper.

The Great Law of Peace

The constitution the Peacemaker proclaimed, the Kaianere'kó:wa, established a federal council of fifty sachems (rotiyaner) nominated by clan mothers, procedures for condolence, decision-making by consensus, and rituals to replace blood feud with law. A central symbol was the Tree of Peace, beneath which weapons were buried. Some scholars have argued that the confederacy's structure influenced colonial American political thought and, indirectly, the United States Constitution — a thesis popularized in the 20th century and still debated.
