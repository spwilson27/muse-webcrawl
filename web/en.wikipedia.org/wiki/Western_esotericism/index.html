Western esotericism

Western esotericism, also known as the Western mystery tradition, is a wide range of loosely related ideas and movements that developed within Western society from late antiquity onward. These traditions include Hermeticism, alchemy, astrology, Kabbalah, Neoplatonism, Gnosticism, Rosicrucianism, ceremonial magic, Freemasonry-related currents and modern occultism. Scholars such as Antoine Faivre characterised esotericism by doctrines of universal correspondences, a living nature, imagination and mediation, and transmutation.

Development

Its roots lie in the Hellenistic world: the Corpus Hermeticum, Gnostic sects and Neoplatonic philosophy. Transmitted partly through the medieval Islamic and Jewish worlds, these ideas re-entered Latin Europe in the Renaissance, when Marsilio Ficino translated Hermes Trismegistus and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola Christianised Kabbalah. Reformation-era figures such as Paracelsus and Jakob Böhme, the Rosicrucian manifestos (1614–16), and Enlightenment secret societies carried the currents forward even as mainstream science and theology marginalised them — a process historian Wouter Hanegraaff calls the construction of "rejected knowledge".

Modern occultism

The nineteenth century saw an occult revival: Éliphas Lévi, the Theosophical Society of Helena Blavatsky, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, and Aleister Crowley's Thelema. In the twentieth century these influenced Neopaganism, the New Age movement, and popular culture. Since the 1990s Western esotericism has become a recognised academic field, with dedicated chairs and journals.
