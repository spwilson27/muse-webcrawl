Eutheria (from Greek εὐ-, "good/true", and θηρίον, "beast"; meaning "true beasts") is a clade of mammals consisting of the placental mammals and all fossil mammals more closely related to placentals than to the metatherians (marsupials and their relatives). Eutherians are distinguished from non-eutherian mammals by various skeletal and reproductive features, and they are the most diverse and widely distributed group of living mammals, with more than 5,000 species.

Characteristics

Distinguishing features of eutherians include the absence of epipubic bones (which in other mammals support the abdominal muscles and, in marsupials, the pouch), an enlarged malleolus at the base of the fibula, particular ankle and jaw joint structures, and — in the crown group — a well-developed placenta that nourishes the young during a prolonged gestation inside the uterus.

Evolutionary history

The earliest known eutherian is Juramaia sinensis from the Jurassic of China (~160 million years ago), though its classification has been debated; undisputed early eutherians such as Eomaia and Acristatherium are from the Early Cretaceous (~125 million years ago). After the Cretaceous–Palaeogene extinction event 66 million years ago removed the non-avian dinosaurs, placental mammals radiated rapidly into most terrestrial niches. Molecular and fossil evidence groups living placentals into four superorders: Xenarthra, Afrotheria, Euarchontoglires, and Laurasiatheria.
