Hiroo Onoda (小野田 寛郎, Onoda Hiroo; 19 March 1922 – 16 January 2014) was an Imperial Japanese Army intelligence officer who fought in World War II and did not surrender until 1974. Onoda was stationed on Lubang Island in the Philippines and refused to believe the war was over, remaining in hiding with a small group for nearly 30 years after Japan's surrender. He was found by Norio Suzuki, a Japanese explorer, but refused to leave until he received formal orders. On March 9, 1974, Onoda's former commanding officer, Major Yoshimi Taniguchi, travelled to Lubang and personally relieved him of duty.

Early life and wartime service

Onoda was born in Kainan, Wakayama Prefecture. At 17 he went to work for a trading company in Hankou, China. He was conscripted into the Imperial Japanese Army in 1942 and trained at the Nakano School, the army's intelligence school, in guerrilla warfare and intelligence operations.

In December 1944, Onoda was sent to Lubang Island in the Philippines. His orders were to impede the enemy advance and never surrender or take his own life. When Allied forces landed in February 1945, Onoda and three other soldiers retreated into the jungle.

Years in hiding

For nearly three decades, Onoda and his companions lived in the hills of Lubang, raiding farms for food and occasionally clashing with local police and residents; over the years the group was blamed for the deaths of some 30 islanders. Leaflets, newspapers, letters, and search parties failed to convince them the war had ended — Onoda regarded every attempt as an enemy trick. One companion surrendered in 1950; another was killed by police in 1954; the last, Kinshichi Kozuka, was shot dead in 1972, leaving Onoda alone.

Discovery and surrender

In February 1974, Japanese adventurer Norio Suzuki, who was travelling in search of "Lieutenant Onoda, a panda, and the Abominable Snowman", found Onoda's camp and befriended him, but could not persuade him to surrender without orders. Suzuki returned to Japan with photographs, and the government located Onoda's former commander, Yoshimi Taniguchi, by then a bookseller. Taniguchi flew to Lubang and on 9 March 1974 formally read the order relieving Onoda of duty. Onoda emerged in uniform, wearing his still-serviceable sword and carrying his rifle, nearly 29 years after the end of the war.

Later life

Onoda received a hero's welcome in Japan but struggled to adjust to modern life and public attention. The Philippine government pardoned him for the killings attributed to his group. In 1975 he emigrated to Brazil, where he became a cattle rancher and a leader of the Japanese-Brazilian community. After reading about a Japanese teenager who murdered his parents in 1980, he returned to Japan in 1984 and founded the Onoda Nature School, an outdoor education camp for young people. He visited Lubang again in 1996 and donated money for a local school. Onoda died of heart failure in Tokyo on 16 January 2014, aged 91.

His autobiography, No Surrender: My Thirty-Year War, was published in 1974.
