Fluctuation theorem

The fluctuation theorem (FT) is a result in statistical mechanics that quantifies the probability of observing entropy-consuming trajectories in a small, driven, non-equilibrium system over a finite time. Whereas the second law of thermodynamics says that entropy production is overwhelmingly likely on macroscopic scales, the fluctuation theorem shows that for small systems and short times there is a finite, precisely calculable probability that entropy will decrease — the theorem provides an arrow of time in a way consistent with the time-reversible laws of microscopic physics.

Statement

In one common form, for the time-averaged entropy production rate Σ, the theorem states that the ratio of the probability of observing a value A to the probability of observing −A grows exponentially:

P(Σ = A) / P(Σ = −A) = eA t

so that entropy-consuming trajectories are exponentially suppressed as system size or observation time grows, recovering the second law in the thermodynamic limit.

History

The theorem was first stated and simulated by Denis Evans, E. G. D. Cohen and Gary Morriss in 1993 using computer simulations of a sheared fluid, and was proved by Evans and Debra Searles in 1994 (the Evans–Searles fluctuation theorem). Giovanni Gallavotti and Cohen derived a related result for chaotic systems (the Gallavotti–Cohen fluctuation theorem). Closely related non-equilibrium results include the Jarzynski equality (1997) and the Crooks fluctuation theorem (1999), which relate free-energy differences to the statistics of work performed along non-equilibrium paths.

Significance

Fluctuation theorems are among the founding results of the modern field of stochastic thermodynamics. They have been verified experimentally in systems including colloidal particles dragged through water by optical tweezers, electrical circuits, and RNA molecules unfolded by laser tweezers, and they give quantitative content to the second law for nanoscale machines and biological motors, where fluctuations are unavoidable.
