Noise shaping

Noise shaping is a technique used in digital audio, image and video processing, usually in combination with dithering, to minimise the audibility or visibility of quantisation error. It works by filtering the error signal so that the noise spectrum is reshaped — typically pushing noise energy out of the frequency band where human perception is most sensitive and into bands where it is less noticeable. Noise shaping is a defining property of delta-sigma modulation.

Principle

When a signal is quantised, the quantisation error is fed back through a filter and subtracted from the input of the quantiser. If the feedback filter is a simple delay, the error transfer function becomes a high-pass characteristic: the noise is unchanged in total power but its frequency distribution is altered, falling at low frequencies and rising at high frequencies. Higher-order loops and carefully designed filters produce more aggressive shaping curves.

Applications in audio

Noise shaping is used when reducing bit depth, for example converting a 24-bit master to the 16-bit format of the compact disc; without dither and shaping, low-level signals suffer correlated distortion, whereas shaped dither leaves a smooth but spectrally shifted noise floor. Systems such as Sony's Super Bit Mapping and the POW-R consortium's algorithms implemented psychoacoustically optimised curves. Delta-sigma analogue-to-digital and digital-to-analogue converters — the dominant converter architecture — use noise shaping intrinsically: most noise is pushed above the audible band and removed by a low-pass filter.

Analogue feedback

The same principle appears in analogue-to-digital converter design, where integrators in a feedback loop around a coarse quantiser shape quantisation noise out of band. The order of the loop and the oversampling ratio together determine how much in-band noise reduction is achieved.
