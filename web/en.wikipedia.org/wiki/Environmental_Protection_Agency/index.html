The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) is an independent agency of the United States federal government tasked with environmental protection matters. It was established by President Richard Nixon under Reorganization Plan No. 3 of 1970 and began operations on December 2, 1970, consolidating environmental programs previously scattered across several departments. The agency is led by an Administrator appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate.

Responsibilities

The EPA writes and enforces regulations based on laws passed by Congress, including the Clean Air Act, the Clean Water Act, the Safe Drinking Water Act, the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (hazardous and solid waste), CERCLA (the "Superfund" law for contaminated site cleanup), FIFRA (pesticides), and the Toxic Substances Control Act. Its work includes setting national air and water quality standards, regulating emissions from vehicles and industry, registering pesticides and chemicals, conducting and funding environmental research, and administering grants to states, which carry out much day-to-day permitting and enforcement.

Organization

The agency's headquarters are in Washington, D.C., with ten regional offices covering groups of states and several research laboratories, including facilities in Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, and Cincinnati, Ohio. Major program offices cover air and radiation; water; chemical safety and pollution prevention; and land and emergency management.

History

The EPA's creation reflected the surge of environmental concern around 1970, the year of the first Earth Day. In its first decade it banned the pesticide DDT, set the first automobile tailpipe standards under the Clean Air Act, and began the Superfund program in 1980. In later decades its agenda expanded to acid rain controls, lead phase-outs, energy-efficiency programs such as Energy Star (launched 1992), and — following the 2007 Supreme Court decision in Massachusetts v. EPA and the agency's 2009 endangerment finding — regulation of greenhouse gases. The scope of its authority remains a recurring subject of litigation and political debate.
