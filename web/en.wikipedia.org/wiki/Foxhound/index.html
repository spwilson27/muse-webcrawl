Foxhound

A foxhound is a type of large scent hound bred for the strong hunting instinct required in fox hunting. Foxhounds pursue the quarry — usually a red fox — by scent, in packs, accompanied by hunters on horseback. The principal breeds are the English Foxhound and the American Foxhound, with related types including the Welsh Foxhound and regional American strains.

English Foxhound

The English Foxhound was developed in Britain over several centuries by crossing various hounds — including the Staghound, the Bloodhound and the now-extinct Talbot — for speed, stamina, voice and scenting ability. Stud books have been kept by the Masters of Foxhounds Association since the 19th century. The dogs stand about 58–64 cm at the shoulder, with a short, dense, weatherproof coat in any hound colour, often tricolour or bicolour with white.

American Foxhound

The American Foxhound descends from English hounds imported to Virginia in the 17th and 18th centuries — George Washington was an avid breeder — later crossed with French and Irish hounds. It is somewhat taller, lighter and faster than its English cousin, bred for the rougher terrain and larger country of North America, and it is the state dog of Virginia. American strains, such as the Walker, Trigg and Goodman hounds, are also used for competitive field trials and hunting other quarry.

Temperament and use

Foxhounds are energetic, pack-oriented dogs with a loud, musical bay. Kept traditionally in kennels as packs under a huntsman, they require substantial exercise. Since the Hunting Act 2004 restricted hunting wild mammals with dogs in England and Wales, many packs follow artificial trails (drag hunting) instead.
