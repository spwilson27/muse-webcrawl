Immune checkpoint

Checkpoint molecules regulate T-cell responses at the interface with other cells.

Immune checkpoints are regulators of the immune system. These pathways are crucial for self-tolerance, which prevents the immune system from attacking cells indiscriminately. However, some cancers can protect themselves from attack by stimulating immune checkpoint targets, which suppress the anti-tumour immune response.

Checkpoint molecules include stimulatory and inhibitory receptors expressed mainly on T cells and their ligands on antigen-presenting cells and tissues. The best-known inhibitory checkpoints are CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4) and the PD-1 receptor with its ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2.

Mechanism

When a T cell recognises its antigen, co-stimulatory signals such as CD28 binding B7 molecules promote activation, while inhibitory receptors dampen the response to limit tissue damage and maintain tolerance. CTLA-4 competes with CD28 for B7 ligands and acts early in T-cell activation, mainly in lymph nodes. PD-1 acts later, in peripheral tissues, where its engagement by PD-L1 reduces T-cell proliferation, cytokine production and survival.

Checkpoint inhibitors in cancer therapy

Monoclonal antibodies that block inhibitory checkpoints — checkpoint inhibitors — release these "brakes" and can produce durable anti-tumour responses. Ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) was approved for melanoma in 2011; nivolumab and pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1) and atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) followed for many cancer types. James P. Allison and Tasuku Honjo received the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

Adverse effects

Because checkpoint blockade weakens self-tolerance, it can cause immune-related adverse events, including colitis, dermatitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis and endocrinopathies such as thyroiditis and hypophysitis. These are usually managed with corticosteroids and treatment interruption.
