Hershey–Chase experiment

Hershey–Chase experiment
Conducted1952
ResearchersAlfred Hershey and Martha Chase
InstitutionCold Spring Harbor Laboratory
ConclusionDNA, not protein, is the genetic material

The Hershey–Chase experiment, conducted in 1952 by Alfred Day Hershey and Martha Cowles Chase at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, helped confirm that DNA is the genetic material, rather than protein.

The experiment

Working with the T2 bacteriophage, a virus that infects the bacterium E. coli, Hershey and Chase labelled the phage's two components differently: DNA with radioactive phosphorus-32 (since DNA contains phosphorus but no sulfur) and the protein coat with radioactive sulfur-35. After allowing the phages to infect bacteria, they agitated the mixture in a blender to shear the empty protein coats from the cell surfaces and separated the fractions by centrifugation.

Results

Most of the phosphorus-32 entered the bacterial cells and appeared in the progeny phages, while most of the sulfur-35 remained outside with the discarded coats. The experiment — nicknamed the "Waring blender experiment" — together with the earlier Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment, convinced the scientific community that DNA carries hereditary information. Hershey shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work on viruses.
