The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), also called the Law of the Sea Convention or the Law of the Sea Treaty, is an international agreement that establishes a legal framework for all marine and maritime activities. Concluded in 1982 after nine years of negotiation, it is often described as a "constitution for the oceans".

Provisions

The convention defines maritime zones: internal waters; a territorial sea of up to 12 nautical miles, in which the coastal state is sovereign; a contiguous zone to 24 nautical miles; an exclusive economic zone (EEZ) extending to 200 nautical miles, in which the coastal state holds sovereign rights over natural resources; and the continental shelf. Beyond national jurisdiction, the high seas are open to all states, and the deep seabed ("the Area") and its mineral resources are declared the common heritage of mankind, administered through the International Seabed Authority.

Navigation and disputes

UNCLOS guarantees freedoms of navigation and overflight, innocent passage through territorial seas, and transit passage through international straits. It also establishes compulsory dispute-settlement procedures, centred on the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in Hamburg.

Status

The convention entered into force in 1994 after a supplementary agreement addressed objections of industrialised states to the deep-seabed mining regime. The United States has signed the 1994 agreement but has not ratified the convention, though it regards most of its provisions as customary international law.
