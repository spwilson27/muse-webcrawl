Digital humanities (DH) is an area of scholarly activity at the intersection of computing or digital technologies and the disciplines of the humanities. It includes the systematic use of digital resources in the humanities, as well as the analysis of their application. DH can be defined as new ways of doing scholarship that involve collaborative, transdisciplinary research, teaching and publishing, using tools such as text encoding, data mining, geographic information systems, and large digital archives.

History

The field descends from "humanities computing", whose landmark early project was Father Roberto Busa's Index Thomisticus, a lemmatised concordance of the works of Thomas Aquinas begun with IBM in 1949. Journals such as Literary and Linguistic Computing (1986) and centres at universities consolidated the field, and the term "digital humanities" was popularised in the early 2000s (notably by the 2004 Blackwell Companion to Digital Humanities), signalling a broadening from technical support toward a scholarly field in its own right.

Methods and debates

Typical DH work includes creating digital scholarly editions (often encoded in TEI XML), distant reading and computational text analysis, historical GIS and spatial humanities, 3D modelling of heritage sites, and building open digital libraries and exhibitions. The field has generated lively debate: about the role of theory versus building ("hack versus yack"), about quantitative methods in interpretive disciplines, and about openness, collaboration and public engagement as scholarly values.
