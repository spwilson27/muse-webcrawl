Spatial memory

Spatial memory is a cognitive process responsible for recording and recovering information about one's environment and its spatial orientation. It enables an individual to remember the locations of objects, navigate through familiar surroundings, and plan routes to desired destinations. Spatial memory draws on both short-term (working) memory and long-term memory systems.

A cognitive map: the hippocampus encodes the spatial layout of an environment.

Neural basis

In rodents and humans, the hippocampus is central to spatial memory. It contains place cells, neurons that fire when an animal occupies a particular location, first described by John O'Keefe in 1971. The entorhinal cortex contains grid cells, discovered by May-Britt and Edvard Moser, which fire in a hexagonal pattern across space, and head-direction cells and border cells complete the circuit. O'Keefe and the Mosers shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work. Damage to the hippocampus impairs the formation of new cognitive maps, as dramatically illustrated by patient H.M., who could not learn the layout of new environments.

Working spatial memory

Spatial working memory holds a limited amount of visuospatial information over short intervals; it is measured by tasks such as the Corsi block-tapping test and the radial arm maze. In Baddeley's model of working memory it is associated with the visuospatial sketchpad.

Development and individual differences

Spatial abilities develop through childhood as children move from landmark-based to route-based and finally survey (map-like) knowledge of environments. Average sex differences in some spatial tasks, such as mental rotation, are among the larger reported cognitive differences, though training substantially reduces them. London taxi drivers, who must memorize the city's street layout ("The Knowledge"), show enlarged posterior hippocampi, a famous demonstration of experience-dependent plasticity.

Disorders

Impairments include topographical disorientation, developmental topographical disorientation, and the spatial neglect that can follow right parietal stroke. Spatial memory decline is an early marker in Alzheimer's disease.
