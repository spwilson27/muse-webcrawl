Hongan-ji (本願寺, "Temple of the Primal Vow") is the collective name for the head temples of the Jōdo Shinshū school of Pure Land Buddhism in Japan. After a schism in 1602–1603 the institution divided into two complexes in Kyoto: Nishi Hongan-ji (Western Hongan-ji) and Higashi Hongan-ji (Eastern Hongan-ji), which remain the largest organisations of the school.

History

The Hongan-ji tradition descends from Shinran (1173–1263), founder of Jōdo Shinshū, whose teaching centred on entrusting faith in the Primal Vow of Amida Buddha and recitation of the nembutsu. His descendants, the monshu, led the community from a memorial hall at Ōtani in Kyoto. Under the eighth monshu Rennyo (1415–1499) the Hongan-ji became a mass movement of peasants, artisans, and provincial warriors, headquartered first at Yamashina and then at the fortified Ishiyama Hongan-ji in Osaka.

The temple's military followers, the Ikkō-ikki leagues, resisted Oda Nobunaga in the decade-long Ishiyama war (1570–1580) — the longest siege of the Sengoku period — before surrendering. Toyotomi Hideyoshi relocated the temple to Kyoto, and Tokugawa Ieyasu split it into eastern and western branches, in part to limit its power.

The two temples

Nishi Hongan-ji preserves celebrated Momoyama-period architecture, including the Goei-dō and Amida-dō halls and the Hiunkaku pavilion; its precinct is a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Historic Monuments of Ancient Kyoto. Higashi Hongan-ji, a short walk east, is dominated by its Goei-dō of 1895, one of the largest wooden structures in the world, rebuilt after repeated fires with the help of parishioner donations — famously including ropes woven from women's hair to haul the great beams.

Significance

The Hongan-ji denominations together count millions of adherents and are among the largest Buddhist organisations in Japan, with overseas missions (the Hongwanji tradition) in Hawai'i, North and South America, and elsewhere.
