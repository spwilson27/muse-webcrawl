Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome

The adrenal glands (highlighted) are destroyed by massive hemorrhage in this syndrome.

Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome is a rare but life-threatening condition characterized by adrenal gland failure caused by massive hemorrhage into the adrenal glands, most commonly during overwhelming bacterial sepsis, above all meningococcal sepsis. It typically presents with the features of septic shock: high fever, a rapidly spreading purpuric (bruise-like) rash, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), falling blood pressure and rapidly progressing coma and organ failure.

Cause and mechanism

The syndrome is most often due to infection with Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcal septicemia), though it can also complicate infections with Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b, Staphylococcus aureus and others. Endotoxin from the bacteria damages the walls of small blood vessels, including the adrenal veins; thrombosis and hemorrhage then destroy the adrenal cortex, causing acute adrenal insufficiency (an adrenal crisis) on top of septic shock.

Diagnosis, treatment and history

Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture in a severely ill, febrile patient with a purpuric rash, supported by blood cultures and imaging or, historically, autopsy findings of bilateral adrenal hemorrhage. Treatment is emergency resuscitation and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics given immediately, plus fluid resuscitation, vasopressors and stress-dose corticosteroids such as hydrocortisone to replace lost adrenal function. Even with modern intensive care, mortality is high. The syndrome is named after Rupert Waterhouse, who described a case in 1911, and Carl Friderichsen, who described the syndrome in detail in 1918.
