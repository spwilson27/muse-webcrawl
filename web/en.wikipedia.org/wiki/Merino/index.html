Merino

The Merino is a breed or group of breeds of domestic sheep, characterised by very fine soft wool. It was established in Spain towards the end of the Middle Ages and was kept as a strict Spanish monopoly for centuries; exporting the sheep was a crime punishable by death. In the eighteenth century, flocks were sent to other European countries and eventually to Australia and New Zealand, which became the great centres of Merino breeding.

Characteristics

Merinos are bred principally for their wool, which is among the finest and softest of any sheep, with fibre diameters commonly between 12 and 24 micrometres; ultrafine Merino wool is under 15.5 micrometres. Rams may be horned (with long spiral horns) or polled. The breed is hardy, long-lived, and an excellent forager, though its dense fleece makes it susceptible to flystrike in some climates, which led to the controversial practice of mulesing in Australia.

History

Spanish Merino flocks, managed by the Mesta guild under royal privilege, underpinned Spain's wool wealth. After Spain relaxed the export ban in the 1700s, Saxon, French (Rambouillet), and other national flocks were developed. In Australia, breeders such as the Macarthur family built vast flocks; Australia became the world's largest producer of Merino wool, with the Peppin Merino influential in many flocks. Other notable types include the Delaine Merino in the United States and the Poll Merino.

Wool and uses

Merino wool is prized for fine suiting, knitwear, and performance sportswear because its fine fibres are soft against the skin, breathable, moisture-wicking, and naturally odour resistant. It commands premium prices compared with coarser wools.
