Hundred Years' War

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Medieval illumination of the Battle of Agincourt, 1415]

The Hundred Years' War (1337–1453) was a series of conflicts between the kingdoms of England and France, lasting 116 years, over the succession to the French throne and control of French territory. English kings, descended from William the Conqueror and holding Gascony as French vassals, claimed France itself through Edward III's mother. The war fell into phases — early English victories under Edward III and Henry V, French collapse and partition, then a French recovery inspired in part by Joan of Arc — and ended with England retaining only Calais on the continent.

English ascendancy

English longbowmen destroyed French chivalry at Crécy (1346), Poitiers (1356, capturing King John II) and Agincourt (1415). The Black Death, peasant revolts (the Jacquerie, 1358) and civil war between Armagnacs and Burgundians deepened France's misery; the Treaty of Troyes (1420) made Henry V heir to the French crown, and an Anglo-Burgundian alliance held Paris and the north for the infant Henry VI.

French recovery

The relief of Orléans by Joan of Arc (1429) transformed French morale; Charles VII, crowned at Reims, built Europe's first standing army, reformed taxation, and used professional artillery (the Bureau brothers) to crush English field armies at Formigny (1450) and Castillon (1453). By 1453 England held only Calais (lost in 1558). The war forged both French and English national identities and ended the age of the mounted knight.
