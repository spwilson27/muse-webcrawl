An electrical circuit (or electrical network) is an interconnection of electrical components — such as batteries, resistors, inductors, capacitors, switches and transistors — that forms a closed loop through which electric current can flow. A network that contains at least one closed path is a circuit; networks may also be studied when open. Circuit theory analyses such networks using idealised component models and laws derived from electromagnetism.

Fundamental laws

Analysis rests on Ohm's law (V = IR) and Kirchhoff's laws: the current law states that the sum of currents entering a node is zero (conservation of charge), and the voltage law states that the sum of voltages around any closed loop is zero (conservation of energy). Systematic techniques built on these include nodal and mesh analysis, superposition, and the Thévenin and Norton equivalent circuits.

Series, parallel and behaviour

Components in series share the same current and divide the voltage; components in parallel share the same voltage and divide the current. Resistors combine accordingly, and similar rules govern capacitors and inductors. Circuits respond differently to direct current and alternating current: with AC, impedance generalises resistance, and resonance occurs in circuits containing both inductance and capacitance. Transient behaviour — how a circuit settles after switching — is described by time constants and differential equations.

Types and applications

Circuits are classified as analog or digital, passive or active, linear or nonlinear. Practical embodiments range from household wiring and power grids to printed circuit boards and integrated circuits containing billions of transistors. Electronic design automation tools simulate circuits using numerical methods such as SPICE before fabrication.
