English units

English units were the units of measurement used in England up to 1826, when they were replaced by imperial units under the Weights and Measures Act 1824. The system evolved from the combination of the Anglo-Saxon and Roman systems of measures and was influenced over the centuries by various standards, including those set by royal edicts and physical standards kept at Winchester and London.

History

Many English units descend from Roman measures introduced after the conquest of Britain, blended with Germanic measures brought by the Angles and Saxons. Successive monarchs attempted to standardise the system: the Compositio ulnarum et perticarum attributed to Edward I or Edward II, the yard and weights standardised under Henry VII, and Elizabeth I's Exchequer standards of 1588. Distinct, locally varying measures long coexisted — such as the Winchester bushel for dry goods — and Scots, Irish and Welsh measures also differed from the English ones.

Relation to later systems

The 1824 Act abolished the older patchwork and created the imperial system, defining a single imperial gallon and standardising the yard and the avoirdupois pound. The imperial units inherited most English unit names and many of their values. The United States customary units descend from the English units in use in the American colonies at independence, which is why some US measures (notably the gallon and bushel) differ from the later imperial ones.
