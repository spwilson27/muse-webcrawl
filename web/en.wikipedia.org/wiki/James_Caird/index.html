The James Caird is a 22.5-foot (6.9 m) ship's lifeboat that became famous for the open-boat voyage undertaken by Sir Ernest Shackleton and five companions in April and May 1916, during the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition. The voyage of some 800 nautical miles (1,500 km) across the Southern Ocean, from Elephant Island to South Georgia, is regarded as one of the greatest feats of seamanship in history.

Background

The boat was one of three lifeboats carried by Shackleton's ship Endurance and was named after Sir James Key Caird, a Dundee jute manufacturer whose philanthropy had helped finance the expedition. When Endurance was crushed by ice in the Weddell Sea in 1915, the crew escaped in the boats to Elephant Island. With winter approaching and no prospect of rescue, Shackleton decided to sail the strongest boat to the whaling stations of South Georgia.

The voyage

The ship's carpenter, Harry McNish, strengthened the James Caird, raising her gunwales and decking her over with canvas and packing-case timber. On 24 April 1916 Shackleton, Frank Worsley (who navigated), Tom Crean, Timothy McCarthy, John Vincent and McNish set out. In sixteen days of storms, freezing spray and mountainous seas, Worsley navigated by a handful of sun sights to bring the boat to South Georgia on 10 May 1916 — just clearing the island after being driven toward its lee shore during a hurricane.

Shackleton, Worsley and Crean then made the first crossing of the island's unmapped interior to reach the whaling station at Stromness, and all the men left on Elephant Island were eventually rescued.

Preservation

The James Caird was brought back to Britain and presented in 1922 to Dulwich College, Shackleton's old school, where it remains on display. In 2013 the explorer Tim Jarvis and his team sailed a replica, the Alexandra Shackleton, over the same route using period equipment, to commemorate the voyage.
