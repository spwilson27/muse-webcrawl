The history of the metre begins with the French Revolution's drive for a universal, rational system of measurement. The metre has since been redefined several times as the science of measurement (metrology) advanced, from a fraction of the Earth's meridian, to a metal bar, to a wavelength of light, and finally to a definition based on the fixed speed of light.

Origins

Before the Revolution, France alone had hundreds of local units of length. In 1790–1791 the National Assembly, advised by the Academy of Sciences, adopted the proposal that the new unit should be one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the Equator along the meridian through Paris. Jean-Baptiste Delambre and Pierre Méchain surveyed the meridian arc from Dunkirk to Barcelona (1792–1798), enduring the upheavals of the Revolutionary Wars; their result was embodied in a platinum bar, the Mètre des Archives, in 1799. The new unit's name came from the Greek metron, "a measure".

The international prototype

The Metre Convention of 1875 created the International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) and commissioned new standards: in 1889 the First General Conference on Weights and Measures sanctioned an International Prototype Metre, a bar of platinum-iridium alloy with an X-shaped cross-section, kept at Sèvres near Paris. National copies were distributed by lot. The prototype remained the definition for seventy years, even as interferometry allowed lengths to be compared to wavelengths of light with far greater reproducibility.

Wavelength definition

In 1960 the 11th General Conference redefined the metre as 1,650,763.73 wavelengths in vacuum of the orange-red radiation of the krypton-86 atom. This replaced the artifact with a natural standard any well-equipped laboratory could realise. Experiments in the 1970s measured the speed of light so precisely that length and time metrology merged.

The modern definition

In 1983 the 17th General Conference defined the metre as the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299,792,458 of a second, fixing the speed of light at exactly 299,792,458 metres per second. The wording was refined in the 2019 revision of the SI, but the definition's substance is unchanged. Practical realisations use stabilised lasers whose frequencies are known, giving the metre a reproducibility many orders of magnitude better than the meridian surveys of the 1790s.
