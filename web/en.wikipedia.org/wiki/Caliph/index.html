A caliph (Arabic: khalīfa, "successor" or "deputy") is the chief Muslim civic and religious leader of a state known as a caliphate, considered a successor (khalīfa) to the Islamic prophet Muhammad and a leader of the entire Muslim community (ummah).

History

After Muhammad's death in 632, Abu Bakr was chosen as the first caliph, taking the title Khalīfat Rasūl Allāh ("Successor of the Messenger of God"). He and the next three caliphs — Umar, Uthman and Ali — are known to Sunni Muslims as the Rashidun ("rightly guided") caliphs. Shia Muslims hold that leadership should have passed to Ali and the Prophet's family from the start, a dispute at the root of the Sunni–Shia split.

Under the Rashidun and their Umayyad successors (661–750), the caliphate expanded from Arabia across the Middle East, North Africa and into Iberia and Central Asia. The Abbasid caliphs (750–1258) ruled from Baghdad, though real power often lay with viziers, generals and regional dynasties. Rival caliphates arose in Cordoba and Cairo (the Fatimids). The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 killed the Abbasid caliph there; a shadow Abbasid line continued in Mamluk Cairo.

Later caliphates

After conquering Egypt in 1517, the Ottoman sultans took up the caliphal title, and the Ottoman caliphate lasted until the Turkish Republic abolished it on 3 March 1924. Sharif Hussein of Mecca briefly claimed the title afterwards. Subsequent claims, such as that of the Islamic State in 2014, have not gained recognition from the wider Muslim world.

Qualifications and theory

Classical Sunni jurists such as al-Mawardi set out qualifications for the office: the caliph should be a Muslim of sound mind and body, knowledgeable, just, and (in the dominant view) of Quraysh descent. The caliph's duties included upholding the sharia, defending the community and administering justice.
