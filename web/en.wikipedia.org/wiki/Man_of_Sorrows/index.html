Man of Sorrows

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Man of Sorrows is a pre-eminent Christian devotional image of Christ, and one of the most widely reproduced subjects in late medieval art. The phrase comes from Isaiah 53:3 in the King James Version: "He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief", understood in Christian tradition as a prophecy of the suffering Messiah.

Iconography

The image shows Christ, usually half-length and upright in his tomb or emerging from it, wearing the crown of thorns and displaying the wounds of the Passion — in the hands, feet and side — often flanked by the Arma Christi, the instruments of the Passion. Unlike narrative scenes of the Crucifixion, the Man of Sorrows presents Christ simultaneously dead and alive, directly confronting the viewer for meditation.

History

The type developed in Byzantine art by the 12th century, associated with the icon known as Akra Tapeinosis ("Utmost Humiliation"), and spread through Italy and northern Europe, where it was popularised by indulgenced prayers and artists including Geertgen tot Sint Jans and Albrecht Dürer.
