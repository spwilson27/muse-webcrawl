Samuel Loyd (January 30, 1841 – April 10, 1911) was an American chess player, chess composer, puzzle author, and recreational mathematician. He is regarded as America's greatest puzzlist and one of the most inventive puzzle creators of all time. From his teens until his death he composed chess problems and devised mathematical and mechanical puzzles that appeared in newspapers and magazines across the United States and Europe.

Sam Loyd, whose puzzles reached an enormous newspaper audience in the late nineteenth century.

Chess

Loyd began composing chess problems at the age of fourteen and was soon recognized as a prodigy; the "Excelsior" problem, composed when he was eighteen, remains one of the most famous chess compositions ever made. He was a strong practical player as well, competing in the Paris 1867 tournament, and served as problem editor for several periodicals. His compositions are noted for their wit, economy, and unexpected key moves.

Puzzles

Loyd popularized numerous puzzles, including the "14–15 puzzle" (the sliding-tile Fifteen puzzle), whose craze swept America and Europe in 1880 — although he almost certainly did not invent it, having embellished his claim in later years. He devised the trick donkeys puzzle, costing-account and dissection puzzles, and thousands of mathematical teasers. His son, Sam Loyd Jr., posthumously assembled much of his output in Sam Loyd's Cyclopedia of 5000 Puzzles, Tricks and Conundrums (1914), which remains in print and was a favourite source of Martin Gardner.

Legacy

Loyd was inducted into the United States Chess Hall of Fame in 1987. Many classic puzzles attributed to him in modern collections descend from the Cyclopedia, and his name remains attached to puzzle companies and annual puzzle design awards.
