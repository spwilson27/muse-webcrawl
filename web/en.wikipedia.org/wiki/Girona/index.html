Girona

Girona (Spanish: Gerona) is a city in northeastern Catalonia, Spain, at the confluence of the Ter, Onyar, Galligants and Güell rivers, about 100 km north-east of Barcelona and 60 km from the French border. Capital of its province, with a population of about 100,000, it is famous for its perfectly preserved medieval Jewish quarter (the Call), its cathedral — which has the widest Gothic nave in the world — and the pastel houses along the Onyar. In the Napoleonic Wars it endured three sieges, the last (1809) becoming a byword for heroic resistance.

History

Founded by the Romans as Gerunda on the Via Augusta, Girona passed through Visigothic and Moorish hands before becoming a Carolingian county town. Its Jewish community, present from the ninth century, produced the Kabbalist Nahmanides (the Ramban) and one of medieval Europe's best-preserved quarters, the Call, until the expulsion of 1492. Besieged many times — hence "city of a thousand sieges" — its most famous ordeal came in 1809, when General Álvarez de Castro's garrison and townspeople resisted a seven-month French siege during the Peninsular War until disease and starvation forced surrender.

The old town

The Barri Vell climbs from the Onyar through the cathedral square (reached by a great baroque staircase) to the Arab Baths, Sant Feliu church and the Carolingian walls, whose walkway (Passeig de la Muralla) offers the classic view over tiled roofs to the Pyrenees. In recent years the old town's atmosphere has made it a major filming location and visitor draw.
