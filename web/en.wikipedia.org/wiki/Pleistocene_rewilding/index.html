Pleistocene rewilding

Pleistocene rewilding is the advocacy of the reintroduction of extant Pleistocene megafauna, or the close ecological equivalents of extinct megafauna, to restore an ecosystem. It is a conservation method aimed at restoring natural processes and food webs by reintroducing apex predators and keystone species that were lost to human activity or to the changing climate at the end of the Pleistocene epoch, roughly 13,000 to 10,000 years ago, in what is termed the Quaternary extinction event.

Free-roaming herbivores are used to approximate the grazing ecology of the late Pleistocene mammoth steppe.

Background

By the close of the Pleistocene, the Americas, Australia, and much of Eurasia had lost most of their largest land animals — mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, glyptodonts, woolly rhinoceroses, giant deer, and many others. The relative contributions of human hunting and climate change to these extinctions remain debated, but the ecological consequences were profound: grazing lawns reverted to shrubland or forest, nutrient cycling slowed, and surviving plants retained defences and seed-dispersal adaptations directed at herbivores that no longer existed — so-called "ecological anachronisms", such as the oversized fruits of the Osage orange and avocado, once eaten by gomphotheres and ground sloths.

Proposals

The idea was articulated most prominently in a 2005 commentary in the journal Nature, "Re-wilding North America", by Josh Donlan and colleagues. The authors proposed that, over the coming century, North American reserves might again support free-roaming populations of horses, camels, cheetahs, elephants, and lions — using modern analogues (such as the endangered Bolson tortoise, Przewalski's horse, and African cheetahs) to substitute for extinct relatives including American cheetahs, American lions, and mammoths. They argued this would restore evolutionary potential, provide economic benefits through ecotourism, and offer conservation refuges for species endangered in their native ranges.

Projects

Pleistocene Park, Siberia

The most developed real-world experiment is Pleistocene Park in Yakutia, Russia, founded by Russian geophysicist Sergey Zimov and continued by his son Nikita Zimov. The project reintroduces grazers — Yakutian horses, reindeer, moose, bison, musk oxen, yaks, and others — to convert shrubby tundra back into grassland. The aim is both ecological and climatic: trampled snow in winter is less insulating, so permafrost stays colder and stores its carbon, while grassland reflects more solar radiation than dark shrubland or forest.

Europe

European rewilding projects, coordinated in part by Rewilding Europe, use back-bred or proxy animals: Heck and Tauros cattle stand in for the aurochs; Konik and Exmoor ponies for the tarpan; and introduced European bison (wisent) have been released in countries from which they were extirpated centuries ago. The Oostvaardersplassen reserve in the Netherlands became a famous, and controversial, example.

North America

In North America, the return of the American bison to large landscapes, the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park (1995), and proposals to restore jaguars to the U.S. Southwest are often discussed under the broader rewilding umbrella, though full Pleistocene-style assemblages of elephants and lions remain speculative.

Criticism

Critics argue that Pleistocene rewilding risks creating novel, poorly understood ecosystems, spreading disease, wasting scarce conservation funds on charismatic species, and ignoring social and political realities — for example, the danger of large predators near human communities. Others question whether climate has changed so much since the Pleistocene that restored ecosystems would be neither stable nor historically faithful. Supporters reply that all conservation involves choices among novel ecosystems, and that functional restoration matters more than taxonomic purity.
