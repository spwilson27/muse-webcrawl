Allied occupation of Germany

The Allied occupation of Germany began after the defeat of Nazi Germany in the Second World War in 1945. The victorious Allies — the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union and France — divided Germany and Berlin into four occupation zones, governed through the Allied Control Council.

The four zones

The division had been planned by the European Advisory Commission and confirmed at the Potsdam Conference in 1945. The Soviet zone lay in the east, the American in the south, the British in the northwest and the French in the southwest. Germany's eastern territories beyond the Oder–Neisse line were placed under Polish and Soviet administration, and millions of ethnic Germans were expelled from Eastern Europe.

Occupation policy

Early occupation policy followed the "Four Ds": demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation and decentralisation. The Western zones were merged economically — first the Anglo-American Bizone in 1947, then with the French zone — while relations with the Soviet Union deteriorated into the Cold War. The Berlin Blockade of 1948–49, in which the Soviets cut land access to West Berlin and the Western Allies supplied the city by airlift, marked the effective end of four-power cooperation.

Two German states

In 1949 the Western zones became the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany) and the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic (East Germany). Formal occupation statutes were progressively relaxed, and West Germany regained broad sovereignty in 1955, though Allied rights concerning Berlin and Germany as a whole persisted until reunification in 1990 under the Two Plus Four Treaty.
