Pseudepigrapha (singular pseudepigraphon or pseudepigraph) are works whose claimed author is not the real author: the text presents itself as written by a famous figure of the past—a patriarch, king, prophet or apostle—who did not in fact compose it. The term, from the Greek ψευδεπίγραφα, "writings with false superscriptions", is used most technically in biblical studies for a large body of Jewish and early Christian literature dating roughly from 300 BC to AD 300 that is attributed to figures of the Hebrew Bible and early church but was composed much later, and that stands outside the biblical canons of both Judaism and most Christian churches. In a broader sense the word can describe any falsely attributed writing, from the Hermetic corpus and the letters of Phalaris to forged medieval charters.

The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha

The standard corpus comprises some fifty-two or more Jewish works (several later reworked by Christians), including:

  • 1 Enoch — a composite of apocalyptic writings attributed to Enoch, including the Book of the Watchers, recounting the fall of angels; preserved fully only in Ge'ez (Ethiopic), with Aramaic fragments among the Dead Sea Scrolls.
  • Jubilees — a retelling of Genesis and Exodus as revelation given to Moses on Sinai, with a strict solar calendar; authoritative at Qumran and canonical in the Ethiopian church.
  • Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs — deathbed speeches of Jacob's sons, combining ethical exhortation with apocalyptic prophecy.
  • Psalms of Solomon — eighteen psalms responding to Pompey's conquest of Jerusalem (63 BC).
  • 4 Ezra and 2 Baruch — apocalypses written after Rome destroyed the Temple in AD 70, wrestling with the problem of evil and Israel's fate.
  • 2 Enoch, the Apocalypse of Abraham, the Letter of Aristeas (on the translation of the Septuagint), the Sibylline Oracles, the Life of Adam and Eve, the Assumption (Testament) of Moses, and the Martyrdom and Ascension of Isaiah.

Many of these works are also classed as apocalypses, and the boundary with the Apocrypha (deuterocanonical books such as Tobit and 2 Maccabees, which are mostly not pseudonymous) is a matter of canon rather than of kind. Several pseudepigrapha are quoted, echoed or presupposed in the New Testament—Jude cites 1 Enoch directly—and they are indispensable evidence for the diversity of Second Temple Judaism: its angelology and demonology, messianic expectations, resurrection beliefs, calendar disputes and scriptural interpretation all developed substantially in these texts.

New Testament and early Christian pseudepigrapha

Early Christianity produced its own pseudepigrapha: apocryphal gospels (such as the Gospel of Peter and the Infancy Gospel of Thomas), acts (the Acts of Paul, including the correspondence with Seneca), epistles and apocalypses (the Apocalypse of Peter was nearly canonical in some churches). Pseudepigraphy also touches the canon itself: critical scholarship widely holds that several New Testament letters attributed to Paul (the Pastoral Epistles most prominently) and to Peter (2 Peter) are pseudepigraphal, written by followers in the apostle's name and spirit—a conclusion most evangelical scholars contest. Ancient readers were not naïve about the practice: church writers such as Serapion of Antioch and Tertullian record the detection and rejection of forged apostolic writings, and a presbyter in Asia was deposed for composing the Acts of Paul "out of love for Paul".

Why write in another's name?

Motives varied. In apocalyptic literature, attributing revelations to an ancient seer (Enoch, Daniel, Ezra, Baruch) lent authority and allowed the author to present recent history as long-foretold prophecy (vaticinium ex eventu) and to address a present crisis under the veil of antiquity. Disciples wrote in a master's name to extend his teaching, as in the Pythagorean and Platonic schools and arguably in some biblical epistles. Some works are school exercises or literary fictions; others plainly intended to deceive for doctrine, money or prestige. Modern sensibilities about authorship and intellectual property differ from those of antiquity, and scholars debate how contemporaries judged each case.

Study

The modern scholarly corpus was defined by J. A. Fabricius's Codex pseudepigraphus Veteris Testamenti (1713), massively expanded by R. H. Charles's two-volume Oxford edition of 1913 and by James H. Charlesworth's The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha (1983–85). The discovery of the Dead Sea Scrolls transformed the field by providing early manuscripts of Enoch, Jubilees and related works in their original languages.
