Active radar homing

TypeMissile guidance system
SeekerMonopulse radar in missile nose
ExamplesAIM-120 AMRAAM, AIM-54 Phoenix, Exocet, R-77, PL-15

Active radar homing (ARH) is a missile guidance method in which the missile itself carries a radar transceiver in its nose. The missile emits radar signals and homes on the reflections from the target, allowing it to guide autonomously in the terminal phase of flight.

Operation

Most ARH missiles are launched toward a predicted interception point using inertial navigation, often with mid-course updates from the launch platform via a datalink. When the missile comes within range of its own seeker — typically a small monopulse radar — it switches on the transmitter, acquires the target, and homes in without further support. This "fire-and-forget" capability frees the launching aircraft or ship to manoeuvre or engage other targets.

Advantages and limitations

Because the missile closes on the target, the reflected radar energy grows stronger in the terminal phase, making active seekers progressively harder to jam as range decreases. Active guidance also gives the target less warning time than continuous illumination from a launch platform. However, fitting a transmitter into a missile seeker is expensive and limits range and power compared with semi-active radar homing, in which the launch platform provides the illumination.

History

The first widely deployed ARH weapon was the anti-ship AGM-84 Harpoon and the French Exocet, while the US Navy's long-range AIM-54 Phoenix introduced active terminal homing for air-to-air combat in the 1970s. The AIM-120 AMRAAM, entering service in 1991, made active radar homing the standard for beyond-visual-range air combat.
