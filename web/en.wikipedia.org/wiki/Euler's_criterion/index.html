Euler's criterion

In number theory, Euler's criterion is a formula for determining whether an integer is a quadratic residue modulo a prime. It was published by Leonhard Euler in 1748 and provides one of the foundational tests used in the study of quadratic residues and, today, in primality testing and cryptography.

Statement

Let p be an odd prime and a an integer coprime to p. Then a is a quadratic residue modulo p if and only if

a(p−1)/2 ≡ 1 (mod p),

and a is a quadratic non-residue if and only if

a(p−1)/2 ≡ −1 (mod p).

Equivalently, using the Legendre symbol, a(p−1)/2 ≡ (a/p) mod p. The criterion follows from Fermat's little theorem together with the fact that the polynomial x2 − a has at most two solutions modulo a prime: if a is a residue, say a ≡ x², then a(p−1)/2 ≡ xp−1 ≡ 1; there are exactly (p−1)/2 residues, each a root of X(p−1)/2 − 1, which forces every non-residue to give −1.

Applications

Euler's criterion makes it possible to compute the Legendre symbol efficiently by fast modular exponentiation, and hence, via the law of quadratic reciprocity, to decide quadratic residuosity for large moduli. Its probabilistic generalisation underlies the Solovay–Strassen primality test, in which numbers satisfying the criterion for many bases are very likely prime.
