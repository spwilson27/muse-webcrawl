Hick's law

Hick's law, or the Hick–Hyman law, named after British and American psychologists William Edmund Hick and Ray Hyman, describes the time it takes for a person to make a decision as a result of the possible choices: increasing the number of choices will increase the decision time logarithmically.

Formulation

The law states that reaction time RT increases with the logarithm of the number of equally likely alternatives: RT = a + b·log2(n + 1), where a and b are empirically determined constants. It is commonly expressed in terms of information entropy.

Applications

Hick's law is widely applied in human–computer interaction and interface design: menus, control layouts, and dashboards are designed with awareness that more choices slow responses, though grouping and familiarity can mitigate the effect.

Exceptions

The law does not apply when choices are highly familiar or when the user can search by reading, as with alphabetically ordered lists; it is closely related to Fitts's law, which concerns movement time rather than decision time.
