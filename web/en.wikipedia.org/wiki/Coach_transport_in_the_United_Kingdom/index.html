Coach transport in the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Coach transport in the United Kingdom covers long-distance, scheduled express coach services and private-hire and tour coaching. In British usage a "coach" is a comfortable, long-distance or touring bus, distinct from local buses. Coaches provide intercity travel that is generally slower but cheaper than rail, as well as school transport, holiday tours, and rail-replacement services.

History

Horse-drawn stagecoaches and mail coaches served British towns from the 17th century until the railway era. Motor coaches appeared in the early 20th century for excursions and then scheduled intercity work. The Road Traffic Act 1930 brought licensing, and after the Second World War much of the industry came under the state-owned National Bus Company. The Transport Act 1980 deregulated express coach services, allowing National Express to face competition, and the Transport Act 1985 deregulated local buses outside London.

Scheduled services

The dominant scheduled operator is National Express, whose network links hundreds of towns and major airports from hubs such as London's Victoria Coach Station (opened 1932). Competition intensified with low-cost entrants including Megabus (launched 2003 by Stagecoach) and, from 2019, the German operator FlixBus, alongside regional operators such as Scottish Citylink. Services compete with rail on price, and with the car on motorway journeys.

Private hire, tours and regulation

Hundreds of independent operators provide private hire, school contracts, football and concert transport, and escorted holiday tours (firms such as Shearings built a large market in coach holidays). Operators are licensed under the public service vehicle regime, with drivers subject to EU-derived drivers'-hours rules and vehicles to periodic testing; most coaches are now required to have seatbelts and accessibility features have steadily improved, with scheduled coaches offering wheelchair lifts or ramps.
