Tapenade

Tapenade (French pronunciation: [tapəˈnad]; Occitan: tapenada) is a Provençal dish consisting of puréed or finely chopped olives, capers, anchovies and olive oil. Its name comes from the Provençal word for capers, tapenas, reflecting that early versions were based on capers as much as on olives. Tapenade is usually served as an hors d'œuvre spread on bread or toast, or used as a stuffing and condiment.

Preparation

Black (or sometimes green) olives are pitted and blended with capers, anchovy fillets, garlic, lemon juice, herbs such as thyme, and olive oil to a coarse or smooth paste. Tapenade may also be flavoured with mustard, brandy or rum, or made with tuna. It is a staple of the cuisine of southern France and appears throughout Mediterranean cooking.

History

Olive pastes have ancient Mediterranean antecedents — the Roman author Columella recorded recipes for ground olives with herbs and oil. The modern tapenade is often credited to a Marseille chef named Meynier, who around 1880 combined olives with capers, anchovies and tuna at the Maison Dorée restaurant.
