Aufbau principle

The Aufbau principle (from German Aufbau, "building up, construction") is used in chemistry and atomic physics to determine the electron configuration of an atom, molecule or ion. The principle postulates a hypothetical process in which an atom is "built up" by progressively adding electrons, which assume the most stable configuration available, filling atomic orbitals in order of increasing energy.

The ordering of orbitals

Combined with the Pauli exclusion principle and Hund's rule, the Aufbau principle predicts that orbitals are filled in the order given by the Madelung (or Klechkowsky) rule: orbitals are occupied in increasing order of the sum n + l of the principal and azimuthal quantum numbers, and for equal sums, in order of increasing n. This yields the familiar sequence 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p, 7s, and so on, with each s, p, d and f subshell holding at most 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons respectively.

History

The principle grew out of Niels Bohr's "building-up" approach to the periodic table in the early 1920s, was refined with Wolfgang Pauli's exclusion principle of 1925, and received its ordering rule from Erwin Madelung in 1936. Bohr's Aufbauprinzip allowed the structure of the periodic table to be explained from the quantum numbers of electrons.

Exceptions

The Aufbau principle is a useful approximation but has many exceptions, especially among the transition metals and heavier elements. Chromium and copper, for instance, adopt configurations with half-filled and fully filled 3d subshells ([Ar] 3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹) rather than the predicted 3d⁴4s² and 3d⁹4s². In heavier elements, relativistic effects and the closeness of orbital energies make actual configurations increasingly irregular, and ions lose electrons from the highest-n shell first, so that transition-metal cations lose their s electrons before their d electrons.
