Taft–Hartley Act

Long titleLabor Management Relations Act, 1947
Enacted23 June 1947 (over presidential veto)
SponsorsSen. Robert A. Taft; Rep. Fred A. Hartley Jr.
AmendsNational Labor Relations Act of 1935 (Wagner Act)

The Labor Management Relations Act, 1947, better known as the Taft–Hartley Act, is a United States federal law that restricts the activities and power of labor unions. It was enacted on 23 June 1947 when Congress overrode the veto of President Harry S. Truman. The act amended the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 (the Wagner Act) and remains a cornerstone of U.S. labor law.

Provisions

The act prohibited certain union practices it defined as unfair labor practices, including jurisdictional strikes, secondary boycotts and picketing, and closed shops (workplaces where only union members could be hired). It permitted union shops only where a majority of employees voted for them, and allowed states to enact "right-to-work" laws banning even union shops (Section 14(b)). It outlawed union donations to federal political campaigns, required union leaders to sign non-communist affidavits (a provision later struck down), excluded supervisors from coverage, and empowered the President to obtain an 80-day injunction against strikes imperilling national health or safety. It also required unions to give advance notice before striking and to file financial reports.

Background and reaction

The law followed a wave of major strikes in 1945–46 and a Republican victory in the 1946 midterm elections. Labor organizations denounced it — AFL president William Green called it a "slave-labor act" — and repeal was a central labor goal for decades, though repeal efforts, including those backed by Presidents Truman and Johnson, failed. Truman himself, despite vetoing the bill, used its emergency injunction provisions against striking workers. Historians debate the act's long-term effect on union density, but it is widely credited with reshaping postwar American industrial relations, particularly by enabling the spread of right-to-work laws in the South and later across much of the country.
