Script (psychology)

In cognitive psychology, a script is a structured body of knowledge about the expected sequence of events in a familiar situation — essentially a mental representation of how a routine activity, such as eating in a restaurant, visiting a doctor, or attending a lecture, normally unfolds. Scripts are a kind of schema, and they guide both behaviour and the comprehension and memory of events.

Development of the theory

Script theory was developed in the 1970s by Roger Schank and Robert Abelson in their work on artificial intelligence and natural-language understanding, notably in Scripts, Plans, Goals and Understanding (1977). Their classic example was the "restaurant script": entering, being seated, reading the menu, ordering, eating, paying and leaving. In experiments by Bower, Black and Turner (1979), people confidently "remembered" script-typical actions that had not been mentioned in a story, showing that scripts fill in gaps in memory.

Features

Scripts specify the roles of participants, the props involved, entry conditions, the ordered scenes and actions, and the expected results. They allow rapid processing of routine events and quick inference, but can also produce stereotyped expectations and memory distortions when events deviate from the script. Developmental psychologists have studied how children acquire scripts for everyday routines, and the concept has been applied in social psychology, marketing and clinical work.
