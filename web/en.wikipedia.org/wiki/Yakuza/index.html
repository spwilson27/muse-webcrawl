Yakuza

Yakuza (Japanese: ヤクザ), also known as gokudō (極道, "the extreme path"), are members of transnational organized crime syndicates originating in Japan. The name derives from the card game oicho-kabu, in which the hand 8-9-3 (ya-ku-sa) is worthless. Yakuza groups are known for strict hierarchical codes, elaborate full-body tattoos (irezumi), and the ritual of yubitsume (severing a fingertip in apology).

History

The yakuza trace their origins to Edo-period groups: the tekiya (peddlers) and bakuto (gamblers), who were granted quasi-official status organizing fairs and gambling. After World War II, gangs expanded into black markets, construction, entertainment, and extortion, becoming semi-public organizations with offices and name cards. The largest syndicate, the Yamaguchi-gumi, was founded in Kobe in 1915; other major groups include the Sumiyoshi-kai and Inagawa-kai.

Organization and culture

Syndicates follow an oyabun–kobun (father–child) hierarchy sealed by a sake-sharing ceremony (sakazuki). Yakuza present themselves as ninkyō — chivalrous organizations aiding the weak — and have visibly provided disaster relief after the 1995 Kobe and 2011 Tōhoku earthquakes, while simultaneously running protection rackets, loan-sharking, and fraud.

Decline

The 1992 Anti-Bōryokudan Act and prefectural exclusion ordinances of 2010–2011, which punish businesses for transacting with gangs, have driven membership from a 1960s peak of over 180,000 to well under 25,000 in recent police estimates, with an aging membership and rising defections. Splits such as the 2015 Yamaguchi-gumi schism produced occasional gang violence, though overall yakuza social influence has sharply waned.
