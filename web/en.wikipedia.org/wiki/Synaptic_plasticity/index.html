Synaptic plasticity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In neuroscience, synaptic plasticity is the ability of synapses to strengthen or weaken over time, in response to increases or decreases in their activity. Since memories are postulated to be represented by vastly interconnected neural circuits in the brain, synaptic plasticity is one of the important neurochemical foundations of learning and memory (see the Hebbian theory).

Plastic change often results from the alteration of the number of neurotransmitter receptors located on a synapse. There are several underlying mechanisms that cooperate to achieve synaptic plasticity, including changes in the quantity of neurotransmitter released into a synapse and changes in how effectively cells respond to that neurotransmitter. Synaptic plasticity in both excitatory and inhibitory synapses has been found to be dependent upon postsynaptic calcium release.

History

The theory of synaptic plasticity was first proposed by the Polish neuroscientist Jerzy Konorski in 1948, and was later popularised by Donald Hebb's 1949 book The Organization of Behavior. Hebb's rule is often summarised as "cells that fire together wire together". Santiago Ramón y Cajal had earlier, in 1894, suggested that memories might be stored by strengthening connections between existing neurons.

Mechanisms

Long-term potentiation

Long-term potentiation (LTP) is a persistent strengthening of synapses based on recent patterns of activity. It was first characterised in the rabbit hippocampus by Terje Lømo in 1966 and has since become the most widely studied cellular model of learning. LTP is typically induced by high-frequency stimulation and depends critically on the NMDA receptor, which acts as a coincidence detector for presynaptic glutamate release and postsynaptic depolarisation.

Long-term depression

Long-term depression (LTD) is the activity-dependent reduction in the efficacy of neuronal synapses lasting hours or longer. It is the functional inverse of LTP and is thought to be important for the selective weakening of specific synapses so that new encoding can occur. Low-frequency stimulation commonly induces LTD, and AMPA receptor endocytosis is a key expression mechanism.

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity

Spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) refines Hebb's rule by making the direction and magnitude of plastic change depend on the relative timing of presynaptic and postsynaptic action potentials, on a timescale of tens of milliseconds. If a presynaptic spike precedes a postsynaptic spike, the synapse is potentiated; if the order is reversed, it is depressed.

Short-term plasticity

Short-term synaptic plasticity acts on a timescale of tens of milliseconds to a few minutes and includes synaptic facilitation, augmentation and depression. It is largely presynaptic in origin, reflecting changes in the probability of neurotransmitter release caused by residual calcium in the terminal.

Homeostatic plasticity

Homeostatic plasticity mechanisms, such as synaptic scaling, stabilise neuronal activity in the face of perturbations by adjusting all of a neuron's synaptic strengths up or down in a multiplicative fashion. This prevents runaway excitation or silencing while preserving the relative differences among synapse strengths that encode information.

Role in learning and memory

Blocking NMDA receptors in the hippocampus impairs spatial learning in rodents, and numerous genetic and pharmacological manipulations that alter LTP also alter learning. Human evidence is necessarily indirect, but the framework remains the dominant account of how experience physically reconfigures the brain.
