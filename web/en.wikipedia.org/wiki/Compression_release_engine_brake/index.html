Compression release engine brake

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trucks use compression release brakes to control speed on long descents

A compression release engine brake is a device that converts a diesel engine temporarily into an air compressor to slow a vehicle, supplementing the service brakes. It is best known under the trade name Jake Brake, after the Jacobs Vehicle Systems company that commercialized it; the generic term "engine brake" is also used, though it may refer to other retarding systems such as exhaust brakes. Compression release brakes are standard equipment on most heavy trucks in North America.

Operation

In a normal four-stroke diesel cycle, energy spent compressing air in the cylinder is largely returned to the piston during the power stroke. A compression release brake intervenes near the top of the compression stroke: it opens the exhaust valves just before top dead centre, venting the compressed air to the exhaust. The energy used for compression is thereby lost as heat and noise instead of being recovered, so the engine absorbs power from the drivetrain and slows the vehicle. Fuel injection is cut while the brake is active. The mechanism is typically a hydraulic add-on in the valve train, actuated by engine oil pressure under control of solenoids, and often offers two or three levels of retarding (for example, activating two, four, or all six cylinders of an inline-six engine).

History

The underlying principle was patented by Clessie Cummins in 1957 (US patent granted 1961) after Cummins's experience of a near-fatal truck descent in which the brakes overheated. The Jacobs Manufacturing Company, a maker of drill chucks, acquired the rights and began production in 1961; "Jake Brake" became a genericized trademark for the device.

Noise and regulation

The rapid venting of compressed air produces a distinctive loud staccato exhaust noise, especially on trucks with straight-through mufflers. Because of this, many municipalities post signs prohibiting the use of engine brakes within their limits, and some jurisdictions regulate or ban unmuffled compression braking. Modern systems and mufflers are considerably quieter than early installations, and enforcement of "no Jake Brake" ordinances varies.
