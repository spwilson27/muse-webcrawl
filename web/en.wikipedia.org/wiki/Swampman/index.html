Swampman

Swampman is a philosophical thought experiment devised by the American philosopher Donald Davidson. It appears in his 1987 essay "Knowing One's Own Mind" and is intended to challenge theories of meaning and mental content that appeal to a person's causal history — in particular, forms of externalism about the mental, including Davidson's own earlier views and related teleosemantic accounts.

Swampman, the molecule-for-molecule duplicate of Davidson created by a freak accident in a swamp.

The thought experiment

Davidson asks the reader to suppose that he is hiking in a swamp when he is struck and killed by lightning. At the same moment, purely by chance, another bolt of lightning rearranges a nearby dead tree (or the molecules of the swamp) into a physical replica of Davidson, molecule for molecule identical to him at that instant. This creature — Swampman — moves exactly as Davidson would, seems to recognise Davidson's friends, and apparently speaks English, producing sentences indistinguishable from Davidson's own. It eventually leaves the swamp, takes up residence in Davidson's house, and begins working on the same philosophical essays Davidson had been writing.

The problem

Davidson argues that, despite this perfect physical and behavioural similarity, Swampman does not actually mean anything by its words and has no thoughts at all. The reason is that Swampman's words and apparent beliefs lack the right causal history: its apparent word "house", for example, was never causally connected with any house, and its seeming memories are not memories of anything, since Swampman never encountered the people and places it seems to remember. On Davidson's view, meaning and thought depend constitutively on the history of interactions between a creature and its environment, so a being with no such history cannot have contentful mental states — at least not until it begins to interact with the world and acquire a history of its own.

Responses and significance

The example has been widely discussed in the philosophy of mind and philosophy of language. Critics have objected that Davidson's verdict is counterintuitive: if Swampman behaves exactly as Davidson would in every situation, it seems arbitrary to deny that it thinks and means. Defenders of teleological theories of content, such as those influenced by Ruth Millikan, have sometimes embraced the conclusion, since on such theories content depends on proper function grounded in a history of selection or learning, which Swampman lacks. Others use the case to argue for internalist or purely functional accounts of content. Swampman is also compared with other duplication cases in philosophy, such as teletransportation puzzles and the "Chinese room".
