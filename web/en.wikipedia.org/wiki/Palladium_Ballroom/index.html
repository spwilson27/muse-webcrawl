Palladium Ballroom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Palladium Ballroom was a dance hall located at 1698 Broadway at West 53rd Street in New York City, on the second floor above a Rexall drugstore. It rose to fame in the late 1940s and 1950s as the epicentre of mambo and Latin dance music in the United States, earning the nickname "the home of the mambo."

History

The venue opened in 1946 as a conventional ballroom catering to a white clientele. In 1948, promoter Federico Pagani persuaded the management to book the Afro-Cuban orchestra of Machito, and Sunday matinée Latin dances — promoted by figures such as Catalino Rolón — began drawing a racially and ethnically mixed crowd unusual for the era. The Palladium became famous for welcoming dancers of all backgrounds at a time when many Manhattan venues remained segregated.

The mambo era

The ballroom's resident orchestras — Machito and his Afro-Cubans, Tito Puente, and Tito Rodríguez, often billed as "the Big Three" — competed in celebrated Sunday-night battles. Dancers such as "Killer Joe" Piro taught the mambo there, and the dance floor became a laboratory for the merging of Cuban rhythms with American jazz and swing. Celebrities including Marlon Brando, George Shearing, and Eartha Kitt were regulars.

Decline and legacy

As rock and roll and changing economics eroded ballroom dancing, the Palladium's fortunes faded; it closed in May 1966 and the building was later demolished. Its influence endures in salsa music and dance: the "Palladium style" of dancing on the second beat ("on 2") descends directly from the mambo danced there, and the era is commemorated in music, film, and oral histories of New York Latin culture.
