The Cross of Lorraine (French: Croix de Lorraine), known in heraldry as the patriarchal cross, is a cross with two horizontal bars, the upper bar shorter than the lower. It originated as the emblem of the Dukes of Lorraine in eastern France and became world-famous as the symbol of Free France during the Second World War.

Origins

The two-barred cross came to Lorraine through the Angevin kings of Hungary and was adopted by René of Anjou in the 15th century; it passed into the arms of the Duchy of Lorraine. The form is related to the patriarchal crosses of Eastern Europe and to the cross borne by Joan of Arc, herself a native of Lorraine, on her banner.

Free France

In July 1940, Vice-Admiral Émile Muselier proposed the cross to General Charles de Gaulle as the emblem of the Free French forces, in deliberate contrast to the Nazi swastika. Lorraine, annexed by Germany after the Franco-Prussian War and again in 1940, symbolised occupied French soil to be liberated. The cross appeared on Free French flags, ships, aircraft, medals (notably the Order of Liberation), and Resistance monuments.

Later use

After the war the Cross of Lorraine remained the emblem of Gaullism and adorns de Gaulle's grave at Colombey-les-Deux-Églises and the great memorial cross there. It also survives in the arms of several Lorraine communes and, in international health campaigns, as a symbol of the fight against tuberculosis.
