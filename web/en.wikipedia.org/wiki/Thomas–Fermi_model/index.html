Thomas–Fermi model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electron density around a nucleus in the Thomas–Fermi picture

The Thomas–Fermi model is a statistical model for the electronic structure of atoms and matter, developed independently by L. H. Thomas and Enrico Fermi in 1927. Rather than tracking individual electron orbitals, it treats the electrons as a degenerate Fermi gas whose local density is determined by the electrostatic potential, which must be found self-consistently.

Theory

Combining the kinetic-energy density of a uniform electron gas with Poisson's equation for the potential leads to the Thomas–Fermi equation, a nonlinear differential equation for a universal screening function. The model predicts that atomic properties scale smoothly with atomic number, and it becomes exact in the limit of infinitely heavy atoms. It captures bulk features such as total energies to within a few percent but cannot reproduce shell structure, chemical binding, or the correct behaviour of the density far from the nucleus.

Legacy

The Thomas–Fermi model is the conceptual ancestor of modern density functional theory: it was the first demonstration that the ground-state energy of a many-electron system can be expressed in terms of the electron density alone. Extensions such as the Thomas–Fermi–Dirac model add exchange and are still used in high-pressure physics and the theory of matter under extreme conditions.
