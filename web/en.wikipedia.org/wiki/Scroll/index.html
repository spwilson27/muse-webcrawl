Scroll

A scroll (from Old French escroe or escroue) is a roll of papyrus, parchment, or paper for writing or drawing. Scrolls were the dominant form of book in the ancient world for over two thousand years, used in Egypt, the Near East, Greece and Rome, and they remained important in East Asia for painting and calligraphy long after the codex replaced them for texts in the West.

History

Papyrus scrolls were used in ancient Egypt by the third millennium BC. A typical classical scroll (volumen in Latin) was read by unrolling it horizontally with both hands, the text arranged in columns; a wooden roller (umbilicus) was often attached to the end. Jewish Torah scrolls, handwritten parchment rolls, remain in ritual use today. The Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered in the 1940s, are the most famous surviving ancient scrolls.

Scroll versus codex

From about the 1st to the 4th century AD, the codex — pages bound between covers, like a modern book — gradually displaced the scroll, first among Christians and then generally. The codex allowed random access, writing on both sides, and easier reference, though scrolls (rotuli) continued to be used for records such as the English Pipe Rolls and for liturgical and ceremonial purposes.

East Asian scrolls

In China, Japan and Korea, hanging scrolls and handscrolls became major formats for painting and calligraphy, mounted on silk with rollers, and remain prized art forms today.
