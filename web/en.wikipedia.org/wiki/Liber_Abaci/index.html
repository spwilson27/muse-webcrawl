The Liber Abaci ("The Book of Calculation") is a 1202 treatise on arithmetic by the Italian mathematician Leonardo of Pisa, known as Fibonacci. Drawing on the mathematics Fibonacci had encountered in North Africa and the eastern Mediterranean, it introduced the Hindu–Arabic numeral system — the nine digits and zero, with place value — to Latin Europe and demonstrated its superiority over Roman numerals for practical computation.

Contents

The book's fifteen chapters cover reading and writing the new numerals; addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of integers; fractions and their conversion; the rule of three and other methods for trade; currency conversion, barter and partnership problems; alloying; and recreational problems. One famous problem — about the growth of a rabbit population under idealised assumptions — generates the sequence 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…, now called the Fibonacci sequence. A revised edition appeared in 1228, dedicated to Michael Scot.

Influence

Adoption of the new numerals in Europe was gradual, resisted by abacists attached to traditional methods, but the Liber Abaci became the principal channel through which positional arithmetic entered European commerce and eventually universal practice.
