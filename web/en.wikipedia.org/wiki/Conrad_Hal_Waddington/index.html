Conrad Hal Waddington CH CBE FRS FRSE (8 November 1905 – 26 September 1975) was a British developmental biologist, palaeontologist, geneticist, embryologist and philosopher who laid much of the foundation for systems biology. He is best known for coining the term epigenetics and for his metaphor of the epigenetic landscape, depicting how a cell's developmental fate is progressively channelled during embryogenesis.

Work

Trained at Cambridge, Waddington worked on amphibian development and the "organiser" phenomenon before turning to the interface of genetics and embryology, notably in Organisers and Genes (1940) and An Introduction to Modern Genetics (1939). During the Second World War he applied operational research to anti-submarine warfare. In 1947 he became professor of animal genetics at the University of Edinburgh, where he remained for the rest of his career.

Two of his concepts remain central. Canalisation describes the buffering of development so that a trait is produced reliably despite genetic or environmental perturbation. Genetic assimilation, demonstrated experimentally in Drosophila (1953), showed how an environmentally induced trait — crossveinless wings produced by heat shock — could, after generations of selection, appear without the stimulus, offering a mechanism by which acquired responses could become genetically stabilised without Lamarckian inheritance. His image of the epigenetic landscape — a ball rolling down a branching, valleyed slope, the valleys carved by gene networks — has become iconic and is widely used in stem-cell and reprogramming biology, where Waddington's name is invoked to describe cell-fate transitions.

Other interests

Waddington wrote extensively on science, art and philosophy, collaborated with the artist Leslie Martin and others on design, and edited the journal Alumni-era interdisciplinary forums in Edinburgh; his later books included Behind Appearance (1969), on painting and science, and The Evolution of an Evolutionist (1975).
