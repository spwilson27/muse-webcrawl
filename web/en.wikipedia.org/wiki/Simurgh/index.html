Simurgh

The Simurgh (Persian: سیمرغ) is a benevolent, mythical bird in Persian mythology and literature, sometimes equated with other mythological birds such as the phoenix. Depicted in Iranian art as a winged creature in the shape of a bird, gigantic enough to carry off an elephant or a whale, the Simurgh is often shown with the head of a dog, the body and wings of a bird of brilliant plumage, and the claws of a lion.

Mythology

The Simurgh's most famous role is in Ferdowsi's epic Shahnameh (Book of Kings), where it dwells on Mount Alborz. It rescues and raises the infant Zāl, abandoned by his father Sām because of his white hair, and later aids Zāl's son, the hero Rostam — giving feathers that can summon it in need and advising on the caesarean birth of Rostam and on the healing of his wounds.

In Sufi literature, the Simurgh becomes a symbol of God and the soul's goal. In Farid ud-Din Attar's twelfth-century poem The Conference of the Birds, thirty birds undertake a perilous journey to find the Simurgh, only to discover that they themselves are the Simurgh — a pun in Persian, where si murgh means "thirty birds". The tale is a classic allegory of the Sufi path to enlightenment.

Origins and iconography

The Simurgh descends from the Avestan Saēna bird and the Middle Persian Sēnmurv, a composite creature prominent in Sasanian and Sogdian art, where it served as a royal and auspicious emblem on textiles, metalwork and stucco. In some traditions the Simurgh lives for 1,700 years, perches in the Tree of Life, and by beating its wings scatters the seeds of every plant across the world. It has remained a potent symbol in Iranian culture and has been adopted in the iconography of neighbouring peoples of the Caucasus and Central Asia.
