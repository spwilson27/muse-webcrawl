Signor–Lipps effect

The Signor–Lipps effect is a paleontological principle proposed by Philip W. Signor and Jere H. Lipps stating that, since the fossil record of organisms is never complete, neither the first nor the last organism in a given taxon will be recorded as a fossil. In particular, the last recorded occurrence of a species almost always predates its true extinction, because the probability of the very last individual being fossilised and recovered is negligible. The effect therefore makes even an abrupt, simultaneous mass extinction appear gradual and smeared out in the fossil record.

Description

One famous example used by Signor and Lipps is the coelacanth, long thought to have gone extinct at the end of the Cretaceous until a living specimen was caught off South Africa in 1938. More generally, the effect means that the observed stratigraphic ranges of taxa underestimate their true ranges: first appearances postdate true originations and last appearances predate true extinctions, with the magnitude of the error depending on the completeness of sampling.

Implications

The Signor–Lipps effect has been central to debates over mass extinctions, above all the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction: an apparently gradual decline of dinosaurs or ammonites below the boundary could be an artefact of incomplete sampling rather than evidence against a sudden catastrophe. Statistical methods, including confidence intervals on stratigraphic ranges, have been developed to correct for the effect.
