Shenandoah Valley

The Shenandoah Valley is a geographic valley and cultural region of western Virginia and the Eastern Panhandle of West Virginia in the United States. The valley is bounded to the east by the Blue Ridge Mountains, to the west by the eastern front of the Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians (excluding Massanutten Mountain), to the north by the Potomac River at Harpers Ferry, and to the south by the James River.

The valley is drained by the Shenandoah River, which flows northeastward to join the Potomac at Harpers Ferry. It is predominantly rural and agricultural, historically known as a breadbasket of the Confederacy during the American Civil War.

Geography

The valley stretches roughly 140 miles (225 km) from the vicinity of Harpers Ferry in the north to the Lexington–Staunton area in the south. Massanutten Mountain divides the valley for much of its length. Major towns include Winchester, Harrisonburg, Staunton, Lexington, and Front Royal.

History

Native American peoples used the valley as a hunting ground and travel corridor long before European settlement. In the 18th century, Scots-Irish and German settlers moved south from Pennsylvania along the Great Wagon Road, establishing farms on the fertile limestone soils. During the Civil War the valley was fiercely contested: Stonewall Jackson's Valley Campaign of 1862 and Jubal Early's 1864 campaign, answered by Philip Sheridan's devastating "Burning" of the valley's farms and mills, made it one of the war's most famous theatres.

Economy and culture

Agriculture — including orchards, poultry, cattle and vineyards — remains central to the valley economy, alongside tourism drawn by Shenandoah National Park, the Skyline Drive, Luray Caverns, and Civil War battlefields. The name "Shenandoah" is popularly said to derive from a Native American expression meaning "daughter of the stars."
