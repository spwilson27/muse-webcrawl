United Nations General Assembly Resolution 377


The General Assembly, empowered under Uniting for Peace to act when the Security Council is deadlocked.
UN General Assembly Resolution 377
Common nameUniting for Peace
Adopted3 November 1950
Vote52–5, with 2 abstentions
SponsorUnited States (Secretary of State Dean Acheson)
SubjectGeneral Assembly action on threats to the peace when the Security Council fails to act

United Nations General Assembly Resolution 377, also known as the "Uniting for Peace" resolution, was adopted on 3 November 1950 at the initiative of U.S. Secretary of State Dean Acheson. It provides that if the UN Security Council, because of a lack of unanimity among its permanent members, fails to exercise its primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security, the General Assembly shall consider the matter immediately and may issue recommendations to UN members for collective measures, including the use of armed force.

Background

The resolution grew out of the Korean War and the Soviet Union's return to the Security Council, ending its boycott, in August 1950 — which meant future Security Council authorizations could be blocked by the Soviet veto. Uniting for Peace was designed to route around the veto by shifting deliberation to the General Assembly, where no state has a veto. It also created machinery for emergency special sessions, convenable within 24 hours at the request of the Security Council (on any procedural vote) or of a majority of member states.

Use

Emergency special sessions under the resolution have addressed the Suez Crisis (1956), the Hungarian uprising (1956), the Six-Day War (1967), and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1980). The Suez session led to the creation of the first UN Emergency Force (UNEF), the prototype peacekeeping force. The eleventh emergency special session, on the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, was convened by a Security Council procedural vote that Russia could not veto.

Legal status

General Assembly resolutions are recommendatory rather than binding, so Uniting for Peace does not transfer the Security Council's enforcement powers; its legality was debated during the Cold War, but the International Court of Justice's Certain Expenses advisory opinion (1962) supported a broad reading of the Assembly's competence in peace and security matters short of enforcement action.
