Philadelphia chromosome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Philadelphia chromosome or Philadelphia translocation (Ph) is a specific genetic abnormality in chromosome 22 of leukemia cancer cells: a reciprocal translocation, t(9;22)(q34;q11), in which parts of chromosomes 9 and 22 swap places. This creates the BCR::ABL1 fusion gene, joining the BCR gene of chromosome 22 to the ABL1 tyrosine kinase gene of chromosome 9. The resulting oncoprotein has constitutively active kinase activity that drives uncontrolled cell division.

History

The abnormality was described in 1960 by Peter Nowell of the University of Pennsylvania and David Hungerford of the Fox Chase Cancer Center in Philadelphia — hence the name — and was the first consistent chromosomal abnormality linked to a specific cancer. In 1973 Janet Rowley showed it was a translocation, not a simple deletion, the first such demonstration in cancer genetics.

Associated diseases

The Philadelphia chromosome is the hallmark of chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), present in about 95% of cases. It is also found in a significant minority of acute lymphoblastic leukemias — where it historically conferred a poor prognosis — and in some acute myeloid leukemias.

Treatment

The fusion protein is the target of the tyrosine kinase inhibitors beginning with imatinib (Gleevec), approved in 2001, which transformed CML from a fatal disease into a chronic, manageable condition for most patients; later-generation inhibitors (dasatinib, nilotinib, ponatinib) address resistance mutations. Detection relies on karyotyping, FISH and quantitative PCR monitoring of BCR::ABL1 transcripts.
