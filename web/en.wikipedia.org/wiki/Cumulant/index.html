Cumulant

In probability theory and statistics, the cumulants κn of a probability distribution are a set of quantities that provide an alternative to the moments of the distribution. Any two distributions whose moments are identical will have identical cumulants and vice versa, but cumulants often behave more simply — most notably, the cumulants of a sum of independent random variables are the sums of their individual cumulants, and cumulants above the second order vanish only for the normal distribution.

Definition

The cumulant-generating function g(t) is defined as the natural logarithm of the moment-generating function:

g(t) = log E[etX] = Σn≥1 κn tn / n!

The first few cumulants in terms of the central moments μi and mean μ are: κ1 = μ (the mean), κ2 = μ2 (the variance), κ3 = μ3 (related to skewness), and κ4 = μ4 − 3μ22 (related to excess kurtosis).

History

Cumulants were introduced by the Danish astronomer and statistician Thorvald N. Thiele in 1889, under the name "semi-invariants". The term "cumulant" was coined by Ronald Fisher and John Wishart in 1932, and cumulants became standard tools through the work of Fisher and later textbook treatments of mathematical statistics.

Properties

  • Additivity: for independent random variables, κn(X + Y) = κn(X) + κn(Y) for every n — the property that makes cumulants central to the study of sums of random variables and the central limit theorem.
  • Homogeneity and translation: κn(cX) = cnκn(X); adding a constant shifts only κ1.
  • Normal distribution: all cumulants above the second are zero, and the normal is the only distribution with finitely many non-zero cumulants beyond κ2.

Applications

Cumulants underlie Edgeworth expansions of distributions, saddlepoint approximations, and higher-order statistics in signal processing, where third- and fourth-order cumulants are used because they suppress Gaussian noise. In statistical physics, cumulants (such as the Binder cumulant) are used to locate phase transitions, and connected correlation functions in quantum field theory are expressible through cumulant-like constructions.
