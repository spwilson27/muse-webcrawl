Frequency-hopping spread spectrum

Frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) is a method of transmitting radio signals by rapidly changing the carrier frequency among many distinct frequencies occupying a large spectral band. The changes in frequency are controlled by a code known to both the transmitter and the receiver. FHSS is used as a multiple access method in the frequency-hopping code division multiple access (FH-CDMA) scheme.

A spread-spectrum signal offers three main advantages over a fixed-frequency signal: it is highly resistant to narrowband interference, it is difficult to intercept because an eavesdropper unaware of the hopping sequence hears only momentary blips, and it can share a band with many types of conventional transmissions with minimal interference.

History

Early mentions of frequency hopping appear in Nikola Tesla's patents of 1903 and in Jonathan Zenneck's 1908 book Wireless Telegraphy. The Polish engineer Leonard Danilewicz proposed the idea in 1929. During the Second World War the US Army Signal Corps used a secret communication system called SIGSALY that employed spread-spectrum techniques.

The most celebrated invention of frequency hopping in this period was the patent granted in 1942 to the actress Hedy Lamarr and the composer George Antheil, who proposed guiding torpedoes by radio signals that jumped between 88 frequencies, synchronised by perforated paper rolls like those of a player piano. Their patent was not implemented at the time but was recognised decades later; Lamarr and Antheil were inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2014.

Technical details

The overall bandwidth required for frequency hopping is much wider than that needed to transmit the same information using only one carrier frequency. The transmitter operates essentially as a conventional narrowband transmitter but changes its carrier frequency many times per second according to a pseudorandom sequence. Because only a small part of any interference affects the signal at a given moment, FHSS signals are considered highly resistant to narrowband interference.

The time the signal spends on each frequency is called the dwell time. Systems hopping more than once per symbol period are called fast frequency hopping; those hopping more slowly are slow frequency hopping.

Applications

Military radios such as the SINCGARS family use frequency hopping for secure voice communication. In civilian technology, Bluetooth uses adaptive frequency-hopping spread spectrum in the 2.4 GHz band, hopping among 79 channels up to 1600 times per second. FHSS is also used in some cordless telephones, wireless local area networks, model aircraft radio control, and automatic meter reading systems.
