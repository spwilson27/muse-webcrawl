Particular

Particulars are concrete individual entities, contrasted with abstract universals.

In metaphysics, particulars are defined as concrete, spatiotemporal entities — individual objects that exist at a particular place and time. They stand in contrast to universals, which are properties or types that can be instantiated by many particulars. Socrates, for example, is a particular; "humanity", the property he instantiates, is a universal.

The problem of universals

The distinction between particulars and universals is central to the problem of universals, one of the oldest debates in philosophy. Realists hold that universals exist and are instantiated by particulars; nominalists deny that universals exist as entities and treat only particulars as real, explaining resemblance between particulars in other ways.

Bundles and substrata

Philosophers disagree about what makes a particular the individual it is. Bundle theories hold that a particular is nothing over and above a bundle of properties held together by some relation. Substratum (or "bare particular") theories hold that beneath the properties lies a propertyless bearer that individuates the object. Aristotle's distinction between primary substance (the individual) and secondary substance (the species and genus) is an early form of the particular–universal distinction.

Abstract particulars

Some philosophers admit "abstract particulars", also called tropes: individualised properties, such as the particular redness of this apple, which are particular occurrences rather than repeatable universals. Trope theory uses such entities to build a metaphysics without universals.
