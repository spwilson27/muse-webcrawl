Malayo-Polynesian languages

The Malayo-Polynesian languages are a major subgroup of the Austronesian language family, comprising roughly 1,200 languages spoken by some 386 million people. They include nearly all Austronesian languages spoken outside Taiwan — where the family's deepest diversity is found among the Formosan languages — and extend from Madagascar in the west to Easter Island (Rapa Nui) in the east, and from Hawaiʻi and Taiwan in the north to New Zealand in the south.

Subgroups

Major branches commonly recognised include:

  • Philippine languages — including Tagalog (Filipino), Cebuano, and Ilocano.
  • Western Malayo-Polynesian groupings of Borneo, Sumatra, Java, and the Malay Peninsula — including Malay/Indonesian, Javanese, Sundanese, and Malagasy of Madagascar.
  • Central–Eastern Malayo-Polynesian — including the languages of the Lesser Sundas, Maluku, and the vast Oceanic branch, itself containing the Polynesian languages (Māori, Hawaiian, Samoan, Tahitian, Tongan) and the many languages of Melanesia and Micronesia.

Characteristics

Malayo-Polynesian languages typically derive from Proto-Austronesian through a reduced phoneme inventory; many have a (C)V syllable structure, verb-initial or verb-medial word order, and the distinctive Austronesian voice system in Philippine-type languages. Reduplication is widely used for plurality, intensity, and derivation. The spread of these languages represents one of the greatest maritime expansions of the pre-modern world, carried by outrigger-canoe voyaging peoples across two oceans.
