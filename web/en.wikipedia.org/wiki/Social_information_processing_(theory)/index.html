Social information processing (theory)

Social information processing theory (SIP) is a communication theory developed by Joseph Walther in 1992 to explain how people form impressions and develop relationships through computer-mediated communication (CMC) despite the absence of nonverbal cues. Contrary to earlier "cues filtered out" approaches that treated text-based communication as inherently impersonal, Walther argued that users adapt: given sufficient time and message exchanges, online relationships can become as intimate as face-to-face ones, and may even become "hyperpersonal".

Key ideas

In text-only media, communicators use language, typography, emoticons and timing to convey social information. Because channels transmit fewer cues per unit of time, impression formation is slower but not impossible. The hyperpersonal model (1996) adds that senders can selectively self-present, receivers may idealise partners, asynchronous channels allow message editing, and feedback can intensify the interaction, producing intimacy exceeding that of comparable offline contact.

Applications and criticism

SIP has been widely applied to online dating, virtual teams, social media and online communities. Critics note that modern media are rich in images and video, changing the conditions Walther described, and that the theory says less about deception and identity play, which Walther addressed through related work on warranting.
