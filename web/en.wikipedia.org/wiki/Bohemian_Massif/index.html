Bohemian Massif

The Bohemian Massif (Czech: Česká vysočina or Český masiv; German: Böhmische Masse) is a large crystalline massif in Central Europe, spanning most of the Czech Republic and parts of Austria, Germany and Poland. Formed during the Variscan (Hercynian) orogeny in the late Paleozoic, it is one of the oldest and most stable geological structures in Europe, consisting mainly of gneisses, granites and other metamorphic and igneous rocks. Its rim is defined by mountain ranges including the Ore Mountains, the Sudetes, the Bohemian Forest and the Moravian-Silesian Beskids.

Geology

Geologists divide the massif into several terranes — including the Moldanubian, Teplá-Barrandian, Saxothuringian and Moravo-Silesian zones — assembled during the Variscan collision of Gondwana with Laurussia roughly 380–300 million years ago. The core Moldanubian zone contains high-grade metamorphic rocks and large granite batholiths rich in uranium, tin and other ores; the Barrandian preserves famous fossiliferous Paleozoic sediments studied since Joachim Barrande. After the orogeny the massif was planed down, buried in the Mesozoic in part, and re-uplifted in blocks during the Alpine orogeny.

Geography

The massif's interior is a rolling plateau drained by the Vltava and Elbe, while the border ranges rise above 1,000 m, culminating in Sněžka (1,603 m) in the Giant Mountains (Krkonoše). Historically important mining districts in the Ore Mountains produced silver (Jáchymov) and later uranium.
