Pitru Paksha (Sanskrit: पितृ पक्ष, "fortnight of the ancestors") is a 16-lunar-day period in the Hindu calendar in which Hindus pay homage to their ancestors (pitrs), especially through food offerings. The period is also known as Pitru Pokkho in Bengal, Sola Shraddha ("sixteen shraddhas"), Jitya, and Mahalaya Paksha. It falls in the Hindu lunar month of Bhadrapada (or Ashvin), beginning with the full moon day (Purnima) or the day after, and ending with the all-important new moon day known as Sarvapitri Amavasya or Mahalaya Amavasya — usually in September or October.

Significance

According to Hindu tradition, the souls of three preceding generations dwell in Pitṛloka, the realm of the ancestors between heaven and earth, ruled by Yama. During Pitru Paksha the ancestors are believed to descend to earth and bless their descendants. Performing śrāddha during this fortnight is considered especially meritorious: the offerings are believed to free the ancestors from suffering and help them attain liberation (moksha), while neglect is said to incur the ancestors' displeasure. The legend of Karna — who in life gave gold but no food in charity, and was therefore served gold in the afterlife until allowed to return to earth for sixteen days to make good the omission — is the popular myth explaining the institution of the fortnight.

Rituals

On the tithi (lunar day) corresponding to the death anniversary of a particular ancestor, the eldest son or a descendant performs śrāddha: offering piṇḍas of cooked rice and barley flour mixed with ghee and black sesame seeds, and water libations (tarpaṇa), often on riverbanks such as those of the Ganges at Varanasi and Gaya. Food is also offered to crows, believed to be messengers or embodiments of the ancestors, and to cows, dogs, and the poor. Brahmins are fed and given gifts (dāna) in the ancestors' names. Auspicious activities such as weddings and new ventures are avoided during the fortnight, as are purchases of new clothes and haircuts in stricter observance.

End of the fortnight

Pitru Paksha ends with Mahalaya, the last day, on which the goddess Durga is believed to begin her descent to earth; in West Bengal it marks the start of the Durga Puja season, traditionally inaugurated by listening to the radio programme Mahishasura Mardini.
