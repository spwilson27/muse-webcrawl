Suspension (chemistry)

In chemistry, a suspension is a heterogeneous mixture in which solid particles are dispersed in a liquid or gas but are not dissolved. The suspended particles are typically larger than about one micrometre, big enough to be visible under a microscope and to settle out over time under gravity (sedimentation), unlike the much finer particles of a colloid, which remain dispersed.

Properties

Suspensions are unstable in the thermodynamic sense: given time, the dispersed phase separates, and many commercial suspension products bear instructions to "shake well before use" to redisperse the particles. Suspended particles can be removed by filtration, whereas colloids and true solutions pass through ordinary filter paper. Suspensions scatter light and appear cloudy or opaque.

Examples

Common examples include muddy river water (silt in water), chalk in water, flour stirred into water, many liquid medicines (such as some antibiotics and antacids formulated as oral suspensions), paints, and blood — which can be regarded as a suspension of cells in plasma. Aerosols, such as dust or sea-salt particles in air, are suspensions in a gas.

Stabilisation

Settling rates follow Stokes' law, decreasing with smaller particle size and with a more viscous medium. Formulators slow sedimentation by milling particles finer, increasing the viscosity of the vehicle, matching densities, or adding suspending and flocculating agents; controlled, loose aggregation (flocculation) can produce sediments that redisperse easily.
