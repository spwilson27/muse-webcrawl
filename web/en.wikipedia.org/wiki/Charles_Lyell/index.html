Charles Lyell

Sir Charles Lyell, 1st Baronet (14 November 1797 – 22 February 1875) was a Scottish geologist who popularised the revolutionary work of James Hutton and was the leading proponent of uniformitarianism — the idea that the Earth was shaped by slow processes still in operation today.

Principles of Geology

Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–1833), published in three volumes, argued that geological features should be explained by processes observable in the present, operating over immense spans of time. It became the most influential geology text of the 19th century and introduced the terms Eocene, Miocene, and Pliocene for subdivisions of the Tertiary.

Influence on Darwin

Charles Darwin took the first volume of Principles aboard HMS Beagle and credited Lyell's gradualism with shaping his thinking about gradual change in biology, which culminated in On the Origin of Species (1859). Lyell later cautiously endorsed evolution in The Geological Evidences of the Antiquity of Man (1863).

Later life

Lyell was knighted in 1848 and made a baronet in 1864. He died in 1875 and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
