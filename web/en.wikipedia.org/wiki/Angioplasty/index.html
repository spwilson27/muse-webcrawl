Angioplasty

Angioplasty, also known as balloon angioplasty and percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA), is a minimally invasive endovascular procedure used to widen narrowed or obstructed arteries or veins, typically to treat arterial atherosclerosis. During the procedure, an empty, collapsed balloon on a guide wire — known as a balloon catheter — is passed into the narrowed vessel and then inflated to a fixed size. The balloon forces expansion of the blood vessel and the surrounding muscular wall, allowing improved blood flow. A stent may be inserted at the time of ballooning to ensure the vessel remains open; the balloon is then deflated and withdrawn.

Angioplasty has come to be the standard treatment for a number of conditions, most notably coronary artery disease, where it is known as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). It is generally less invasive and has a shorter recovery time than bypass surgery, though for some patterns of disease — such as severe multi-vessel coronary disease in people with diabetes — coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) may offer better long-term outcomes.

History

Angioplasty was first described by the American interventional radiologist Charles Dotter in 1964. Dotter pioneered modern medicine with the invention of angioplasty and the catheter-delivered stent, and he used a series of coaxial catheters of increasing diameter to dilate a narrowed femoral artery — a technique now known as the Dotter technique. The procedure was refined by Andreas Gruentzig, a German cardiologist, who in 1977 performed the first successful balloon dilation of a coronary artery in an awake human patient in Zurich, using a balloon-tipped catheter he had developed. Gruentzig's innovation made it possible to treat coronary stenoses without open-heart surgery and launched the field of interventional cardiology.

Uses

Coronary angioplasty

Coronary angioplasty, or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), is used to open narrowed coronary arteries in patients with coronary artery disease. It is the treatment of choice for ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) when it can be performed promptly — "primary PCI" — because it restores blood flow to the heart muscle more effectively than thrombolytic (clot-dissolving) therapy. It is also used to relieve symptoms of stable angina caused by flow-limiting stenoses.

Peripheral angioplasty

Outside the heart, angioplasty is used to treat narrowing of arteries in the legs (peripheral artery disease), the kidneys (renal artery stenosis), the carotid arteries supplying the brain, and other vessels. It is also used to maintain the patency of arteriovenous fistulas and grafts created for haemodialysis access in patients with kidney failure.

Venous angioplasty

Balloon dilation is likewise applied to narrowed veins, for example central venous stenoses in dialysis patients, and may be combined with thrombolysis or stent placement in selected cases of venous thrombosis.

Procedure

Access to the blood vessel is most commonly obtained by puncturing the femoral artery in the groin or the radial artery in the wrist, using the Seldinger technique. Under X-ray fluoroscopic guidance, a guide wire is advanced across the narrowed segment, followed by the balloon catheter. The balloon is inflated with a mixture of saline and contrast medium to several atmospheres of pressure for tens of seconds, compressing the plaque against the vessel wall and stretching the artery open. Angiographic images taken before and after confirm the result.

In most coronary procedures, a stent — a small wire-mesh tube — is deployed at the dilation site. Modern practice predominantly uses drug-eluting stents, which release medication that reduces the risk of the vessel narrowing again (restenosis). Patients typically receive dual antiplatelet therapy, commonly aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor such as clopidogrel, for a period after stent implantation to prevent stent thrombosis.

Risks and complications

Angioplasty is generally safe, but complications can include bleeding or haematoma at the access site, damage or dissection of the vessel, allergic reaction to contrast dye, kidney injury from contrast, heart attack, stroke, and, rarely, emergency bypass surgery or death. Restenosis — gradual re-narrowing of the treated segment — was common after plain balloon angioplasty, occurring in a substantial fraction of patients within six months; bare-metal stents reduced this rate, and drug-eluting stents reduced it further. Late stent thrombosis, though uncommon, is a serious complication that prolonged antiplatelet therapy helps to prevent.

Recovery

Most patients remain in hospital for one night or less after an uncomplicated elective procedure, and radial (wrist) access often permits same-day discharge and rapid mobilisation. Heavy lifting is generally avoided for a few days, and patients are usually enrolled in cardiac rehabilitation and prescribed long-term secondary-prevention medications such as statins and antiplatelet agents.
