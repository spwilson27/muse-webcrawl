Cornucopia

The cornucopia (from Latin cornu copiae, "horn of plenty") is a symbol of abundance and nourishment, commonly a large horn-shaped container overflowing with produce, flowers or nuts. It has been used since classical antiquity and remains a common motif, especially in North America in association with harvest festivals and Thanksgiving.

Mythology

In Greek mythology the cornucopia was created when Heracles wrestled with the river god Achelous, who had taken the form of a bull, and broke off one of his horns; the horn was filled with fruit and flowers by the naiads and thereafter supplied endless nourishment. Another tradition associates the horn with Amalthea, the goat who suckled the infant Zeus, whose horn likewise poured forth unending abundance.

Symbolism

The cornucopia became an attribute of numerous deities associated with the harvest, prosperity and wine, including Demeter, Gaia, Fortuna and Plutus, and appears on coins and state emblems. It features in the coats of arms and flags of several Latin American countries and of U.S. states including Idaho, and is the centrepiece of North Carolina's state flag motif and of Peru's national coat of arms.

Modern usage

Wicker or ceramic horn-shaped baskets filled with fruit, gourds and autumn leaves are traditional Thanksgiving centrepieces in the United States and Canada. The word is also used figuratively for any great abundance, as in "a cornucopia of ideas."
