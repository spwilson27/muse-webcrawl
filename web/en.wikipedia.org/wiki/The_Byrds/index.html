The Byrds were an American rock band formed in Los Angeles in 1964. Fronted by Roger McGuinn, the band pioneered folk rock by blending the vocal harmonies and lyrical concerns of folk music with the instrumentation of British Invasion rock, and went on to help invent psychedelic rock and country rock.

History

The founding members — McGuinn, Gene Clark, David Crosby, Chris Hillman, and Michael Clarke — scored an international number-one hit in 1965 with their jangling twelve-string arrangement of Bob Dylan's "Mr. Tambourine Man", followed by "Turn! Turn! Turn!", adapted from Ecclesiastes by Pete Seeger. Their 1966 single "Eight Miles High" was an early landmark of psychedelic rock.

Personnel changes were frequent: Clark left in 1966, and Crosby was fired in 1967. With Gram Parsons briefly in the line-up, the band recorded Sweetheart of the Rodeo (1968), widely regarded as the foundational country-rock album. McGuinn continued the band with new members until 1973, when the original quintet briefly reunited for an album on Asylum Records.

Legacy

The Byrds were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1991. McGuinn's compressed Rickenbacker twelve-string sound influenced generations of guitarists, and the band's genre-hopping path — folk rock, raga rock, space rock, country rock — made them one of the most consequential American bands of the 1960s.
