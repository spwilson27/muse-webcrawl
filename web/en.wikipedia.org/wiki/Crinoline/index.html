Crinoline

A steel cage crinoline, c. 1860s, supporting a wide bell-shaped skirt.

A crinoline is a stiffened or structured petticoat designed to hold out a woman's skirt, popular at various times since the mid-19th century. Originally, crinoline described a stiff fabric of horsehair (crin) and cotton or linen, used by the 1850s to make underskirts that held wide skirts in shape. By 1856 the term had come to refer to the light cage of spring steel hoops that replaced layers of petticoats, allowing skirts to reach their maximum width around 1860.

History

The cage crinoline, patented in Paris in 1856 and soon manufactured in huge numbers in Britain and the United States, freed wearers from the weight of numerous petticoats while creating the fashionable dome-shaped silhouette of the late 1850s and 1860s. Contemporary commentators satirised the garment's size and the hazards it created: the highly flammable fabrics worn over crinolines caused many deaths by fire, a danger widely reported in the press of the period.

By the late 1860s the silhouette had shifted, the fullness moving to the back in the form of the crinolette and later the bustle. Crinolines have since been revived in various forms, notably in the full skirts of Christian Dior's post-war "New Look" of 1947 and in bridal and formal wear.
