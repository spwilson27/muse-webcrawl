History of the Catholic Church in Japan

The history of the Catholic Church in Japan began in 1549 with the arrival of the Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier at Kagoshima. Over the following centuries, Christianity in Japan experienced rapid early growth, severe persecution under the Tokugawa shogunate, a period of clandestine survival by the Kakure Kirishitan ("Hidden Christians"), and eventual legalisation during the Meiji Restoration.

Arrival and early missions (1549–1587)

Francis Xavier landed at Kagoshima on 15 August 1549, accompanied by the Japanese convert Anjirō. Jesuit missionaries, followed by Franciscans, Dominicans and Augustinians, established missions in Kyushu and Honshu. With the support of converts such as Ōmura Sumitada, the first baptised daimyō, and the tolerance of Oda Nobunaga, the number of Japanese Christians grew to perhaps 300,000 by the end of the sixteenth century. The city of Nagasaki, founded as a Christian port, became the centre of the mission.

Persecution (1587–1639)

In 1587 Toyotomi Hideyoshi issued an edict expelling missionaries, and in 1597 twenty-six Christians — the Twenty-six Martyrs of Japan — were crucified at Nagasaki. Persecution intensified under the Tokugawa shogunate: Christianity was formally banned in 1614, churches were destroyed, and suspected Christians were required to trample on sacred images (fumi-e) to prove they were not believers. The Shimabara Rebellion of 1637–38, in which many Christian peasants took part, led to the final expulsion of the Portuguese in 1639 and Japan's period of isolation (sakoku).

Hidden Christians

Deprived of priests, many Japanese Christians preserved their faith in secret for over two centuries. The Kakure Kirishitan disguised statues of the Virgin Mary as the Buddhist bodhisattva Kannon and transmitted prayers orally. In 1865, after Japan reopened, villagers from Urakami revealed themselves to Father Bernard Petitjean at the newly built Ōura Cathedral in Nagasaki — an event sometimes called the "discovery of the Christians".

Meiji era to the present

The ban on Christianity was lifted in 1873. Missionaries returned, schools and hospitals were founded, and the Catholic hierarchy was re-established. The atomic bombing of Nagasaki on 9 August 1945 destroyed Urakami Cathedral and killed thousands of Catholics. Pope John Paul II visited Japan in 1981, and Pope Francis visited in 2019. Today Catholics number roughly 450,000 in Japan, a small minority of the population, alongside a larger number of other Christians.
