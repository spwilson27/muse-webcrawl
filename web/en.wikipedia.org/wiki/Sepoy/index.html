Sepoy (/ˈsiːpɔɪ/) is a term denoting a soldier in the service of various powers in South Asia, ultimately derived from the Persian word sepāhī (سپاهی) meaning "infantryman". The term was used by the Mughal Empire and, most famously, by the British and French East India Companies for Indian soldiers recruited into their armies. In the modern Indian Army, Pakistani Army, and Bangladeshi Army, sepoy (or sipahi) remains the lowest enlisted rank, equivalent to a private.

History

The French East India Company under Joseph François Dupleix was among the first European powers to recruit and train Indian infantry to European standards in the 1740s. The British East India Company followed, raising the Bengal, Madras and Bombay armies, which by the early nineteenth century numbered over 300,000 sepoys — a force larger than many European national armies, officered by Britons but manned overwhelmingly by Indians.

Indian Rebellion of 1857

Sepoys of the Bengal Army rose in revolt in 1857, in the uprising British sources called the "Sepoy Mutiny" and Indian sources the First War of Independence. A proximate cause was the rumour that cartridges for the new Enfield rifle were greased with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindu and Muslim soldiers alike. After the rebellion was suppressed, the East India Company was dissolved and its armies passed to the British Crown as the British Indian Army.

Later use

Sepoys served in large numbers in both world wars. The term also spread to other colonial forces, including the Portuguese in India and Africa, and to Sikh policemen recruited in British Malaya and Hong Kong.
