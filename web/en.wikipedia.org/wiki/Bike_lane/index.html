A bike lane or cycle lane is a portion of a roadway that has been designated by striping, signage, and pavement markings for the preferential or exclusive use of bicyclists. Bike lanes are among the most common forms of cycling infrastructure worldwide, intended to organise traffic, give cyclists a predictable space in the roadway, and encourage cycling by increasing perceived and actual safety.

Types

Conventional bike lanes are separated from motor traffic by a painted line. Buffered lanes add a marked buffer zone, sometimes with diagonal hatching. Protected bike lanes (also called cycle tracks when at sidewalk level) use physical separation — curbs, bollards, planters, or parked cars — and are associated in research with the largest safety gains and increases in ridership. Contraflow lanes permit two-way cycling on one-way streets, and advisory lanes (edge-line lanes) use dashed lines that motorists may cross when necessary. In many countries lanes may be painted green or red at conflict points such as junctions.

Safety debate

The safety of bike lanes has been debated since the 1970s, when "vehicular cycling" advocates argued that cyclists are safer behaving as vehicle drivers in general traffic. More recent empirical studies generally find that marked lanes, and especially physically protected lanes, reduce cyclist injuries and increase cycling volumes, though design at intersections — where most serious collisions occur — is critical. Standards vary by country: Dutch CROW guidelines, widely regarded as a benchmark, emphasise separation from motor traffic wherever speeds or volumes are significant.
