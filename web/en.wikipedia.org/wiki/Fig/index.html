Fig

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Ripe figs on a plate, one split open to show the pink-red flower-filled interior]

The fig (Ficus carica) is among humanity's very first domesticated fruits — and one of its strangest: what we eat is not a fruit but a syconium, a hollow stem lined inside with hundreds of tiny flowers. Many fig types depend on a partnership with a pinhead-sized wasp, Blastophaga psenes, which pollinates the enclosed flowers and usually dies inside the fig it fertilises. Native to the Mediterranean and western Asia, the fig nourished ancient Egypt, Greece and Rome, travelled with Spanish missions to California, and is now produced chiefly in Turkey, the leading exporter of dried figs.

An ancient fruit

Parthenocarpic (seedless) fig remains from Gilgal in the Jordan Valley, dated to around 9400 BCE, suggest figs were cultivated before grain farming — possibly the first plant humans ever farmed. Figs appear in Genesis (the fig leaf of modesty), in the Buddha's family tree (he was enlightened under a relative, Ficus religiosa), in Rome's foundation myth (Romulus and Remus rested under a fig), and in Greek athletics, where figs were a staple energy food. Dried figs kept armies and caravans alive across deserts.

Types and use

Figs divide into common figs (which set fruit without pollination), Smyrna types (which need the wasp — California's Calimyrna is a Smyrna fig), and San Pedro intermediates. Fresh figs are ruinously perishable and mostly eaten near the tree; the world mostly knows figs dried, in rolls and biscuits, or as jam. In the kitchen they pair with prosciutto, blue cheese, honey and walnuts, star in Christmas figgy pudding, and turn into the Anatolian and Greek desserts of a dozen cuisines. "Not worth a fig" preserves their old cheapness in English — they were once too common to value.
