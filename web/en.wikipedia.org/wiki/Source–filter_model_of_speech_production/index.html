Source–filter model of speech production

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The source–filter model of speech production models the human voice as the combination of a sound source — typically the vibration of the vocal folds — and a linear acoustic filter formed by the vocal tract. It is the standard framework underlying speech analysis, synthesis, and coding, systematically developed by Gunnar Fant in his 1960 book Acoustic Theory of Speech Production.

The model

Source

During voiced speech, the vocal folds open and close periodically, chopping the airflow from the lungs into a sequence of puffs. This glottal source has a harmonic spectrum whose amplitude falls off at roughly 12 dB per octave. For voiceless sounds, the source is instead turbulent noise generated at a constriction.

Filter

The throat, mouth, and nasal cavity form a tube whose resonances — the formants — selectively amplify some frequencies of the source and suppress others. Changing the shape of the vocal tract by moving the tongue, lips, and jaw shifts the formant frequencies, producing different vowels; by the model, vowel quality is largely determined by the first two or three formants (F1, F2, F3).

Applications and limitations

The model underpins linear predictive coding (LPC), vocoders, and much of speech synthesis and speech recognition. It assumes the source and filter are independent, which is an approximation: in reality the glottal flow and vocal tract interact somewhat, and the model is extended for nasalised sounds (which add anti-resonances) and for fricatives, whose noise source lies within the tract itself.
