Blemmyes

The Blemmyes were an Eastern Desert people who lived in Lower Nubia between the Nile and the Red Sea, in what is now southern Egypt and northern Sudan. They appear in Egyptian, Greek and Roman sources from antiquity and remained a significant frontier power into late antiquity, when they controlled much of the region between the third and sixth centuries AD.

History

The Blemmyes are generally identified with the Beja peoples of the Eastern Desert. From the third century AD they raided Roman Egypt and, after Diocletian withdrew the frontier to Aswan around 298 AD, occupied much of Lower Nubia. They fought prolonged wars with the neighbouring Nobades and maintained the cult of Isis at Philae under agreements with Rome and later Constantinople that allowed them periodic access to the temple. A letter of the Blemmyan king Phonen, written in Greek on papyrus, survives from the fifth century. By the sixth century the region's peoples were converted to Christianity and the Blemmyes fade from the record as a distinct polity.

The headless Blemmyes of legend

Separately, classical and medieval writers described fabulous "Blemmyae" as a race of headless men with faces on their chests. This monstrous-race tradition, transmitted through Pliny the Elder and medieval mappaemundi, became attached to the name — Shakespeare's Othello alludes to "men whose heads do grow beneath their shoulders" — but it has no basis in the historical people.
