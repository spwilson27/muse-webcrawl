Chromosome abnormality

A chromosome abnormality (or chromosomal aberration) is a change in the normal number or structure of chromosomes in a cell. Such abnormalities arise from errors in cell division — principally nondisjunction during meiosis or mitosis, or from breakage and faulty repair of chromosomes. They are a major cause of miscarriage, congenital disorders, and infertility, and some are characteristic of cancers.

Numerical abnormalities

Changes in chromosome number are called aneuploidy when individual chromosomes are gained or lost, and polyploidy when whole sets are added. Well-known examples include trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome), trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome), monosomy X (Turner syndrome), and 47,XXY (Klinefelter syndrome). Most autosomal monosomies and many trisomies are lethal before birth; aneuploidy is detected in a large fraction of spontaneous miscarriages.

Structural abnormalities

Structural changes include deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, insertions, and ring chromosomes. A reciprocal translocation exchanges segments between chromosomes; a Robertsonian translocation fuses two acrocentric chromosomes. Balanced rearrangements may cause no symptoms in a carrier but can produce unbalanced gametes. Specific structural abnormalities cause recognized syndromes, such as the 5p deletion in cri-du-chat syndrome and the 22q11.2 deletion in DiGeorge syndrome.

Detection

Chromosome abnormalities are diagnosed by karyotyping, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), chromosomal microarray analysis, and DNA sequencing methods. Prenatal screening and diagnostic testing (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, and cell-free DNA screening) are widely used to detect fetal aneuploidy.

In cancer

Acquired chromosomal abnormalities are hallmarks of many cancers; the Philadelphia chromosome, a translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 creating the BCR–ABL1 fusion gene, is the classic example, driving chronic myeloid leukemia and targeted by the drug imatinib.
