Balustrade


A stone balustrade with vase-shaped balusters.

A balustrade is a railing supported by a row of small columns or posts called balusters, topped by a coping or handrail. Balustrades occur along staircases, balconies, terraces, parapets, and bridges, serving both as a safety barrier and as a conspicuous element of classical architectural ornament. The word derives from the Italian balaustra, from balaustro, the pomegranate flower, whose double-curved shape the classic baluster profile resembles.

Components

  • Balusters — the individual turned, moulded, or cut posts. The classic form swells near the bottom like a vase, a profile popularised in Renaissance Italy; simpler square or "stick" balusters and barley-twist forms are also common.
  • Handrail (coping) — the continuous rail along the top, grasped on stairs.
  • Base rail (plinth) — the rail into which the feet of the balusters are set.
  • Newel posts or piers — larger posts, often crowned with balls or finials, that punctuate the run at intervals and ends.

History

Rows of baluster-like supports appear in Assyrian bas-reliefs and in early Asian architecture. The form was systematised in Renaissance Italy: balustrades crown the palaces of Florence and Rome, were theorised by Palladio and Vignola, and spread across Europe in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, gracing the Louvre, Versailles, and St Paul's Cathedral in London. Shakespeare's "balcony scene" architecture and later Georgian and Federal houses in Britain and America used balustrades in wood, stone, and cast iron; modern versions are made in metal, glass, and precast concrete.

In contemporary building codes, balustrades are regulated as guard rails: minimum heights (commonly around 1.1 m externally, and 900 mm on stairs) and maximum gaps between balusters (often a 100 mm sphere must not pass through) are specified to prevent falls and to stop small children slipping through.
