Mortar (weapon)

Mortar
TypeIndirect-fire infantry support weapon
Typical calibres51 mm, 60 mm, 81/82 mm, 120 mm (heavy mortars up to 240 mm)

A mortar is a short-barrelled artillery weapon that fires explosive projectiles — mortar bombs — at low velocities, along short ranges, and in high, arcing trajectories. Mortars are usually simple, portable infantry weapons consisting of a smooth-bore metal tube on a baseplate, supported by a bipod. A bomb is dropped down the muzzle; it slides down the tube until its propelling charge strikes a fixed firing pin, launching it toward the target. Because of their high trajectory, mortars can hit targets behind hills, buildings, or inside trenches that flat-trajectory weapons cannot reach.

Characteristics

Modern mortars are generally classed as light (51–60 mm), medium (81–82 mm), and heavy (107–120 mm and above). Compared with conventional artillery they are far lighter and cheaper, can be carried by infantry, and can fire very rapidly — a trained crew can loose 20–30 bombs per minute for short periods. Their chief limitations are short range and modest accuracy compared with guns and howitzers. Most mortar bombs are fin-stabilized and fired from smooth-bore tubes, though some designs, such as the French 120 mm MO-120 RT, use rifled barrels. Ammunition types include high explosive, smoke, illumination, and precision-guided rounds.

History

Weapons named mortars — after the short, stout apothecary's vessel they resembled — have existed since the Middle Ages; huge bombards such as the Pumhart von Steyr fired stone balls in high arcs against fortifications. Siege mortars remained important through the early modern period, including the coehorn, a light mortar invented in the 17th century by the Dutch engineer Menno van Coehoorn. The modern infantry mortar was created during the trench warfare of the First World War, when the British Stokes mortar of 1915 established the classic pattern — tube, baseplate, bipod, drop-fired finned bomb — that persists today. The French Brandt mle 27/31 design became the template for the 81 mm mortars used by most armies in the Second World War.

Use

Mortars provide infantry commanders with their own organic indirect fire support at company and battalion level. Larger mortars are mounted in armoured vehicles, and self-propelled mortar systems combine a breech-loaded mortar with modern fire control. Gun-mortars, such as turreted 120 mm systems, can also fire in a direct-fire role.
