Wildlife conservation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wildlife conservation refers to the practice of protecting wild species and their habitats in order to maintain healthy wildlife species or populations and to restore, protect or enhance natural ecosystems. Major threats to wildlife include habitat destruction, degradation and fragmentation, overexploitation, poaching, pollution, and climate change. The IUCN estimates that tens of thousands of assessed species are threatened with extinction.

Conservation efforts range from the establishment of protected areas such as national parks and wildlife refuges, to legislation such as the United States Endangered Species Act of 1973, international treaties such as CITES, captive breeding and reintroduction programmes, and community-based conservation that involves local people in stewardship of wildlife.

Threats to wildlife

Habitat loss

Habitat destruction through deforestation, agricultural expansion, urbanisation and infrastructure development is widely regarded as the single greatest threat to terrestrial biodiversity. Fragmentation isolates populations, reducing gene flow and increasing vulnerability to local extinction.

Overexploitation and poaching

Unsustainable hunting, fishing and harvesting, together with the illegal wildlife trade in products such as ivory, rhino horn and pangolin scales, have driven severe declines in many species.

Climate change and pollution

Shifting temperatures and rainfall patterns alter habitats faster than many species can adapt, while pesticides, plastics and other pollutants accumulate in food webs.

History

Modern wildlife conservation emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, spurred by the near-extermination of the American bison and the extinction of the passenger pigeon in 1914. Figures such as Theodore Roosevelt, Aldo Leopold and Rachel Carson shaped the movement, alongside organisations including the Sierra Club, the World Wildlife Fund (founded 1961) and the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN, founded 1948), whose Red List remains the standard inventory of species' extinction risk.

Methods

Conservation biologists employ habitat restoration, wildlife corridors, ex-situ conservation in zoos and seed banks, assisted migration, invasive-species control, and anti-poaching patrols. Increasing emphasis is placed on "30 by 30" — the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework target of protecting 30 percent of land and sea by 2030.

Protected areas

Protected areas, from strict nature reserves to multiple-use landscapes, cover roughly a sixth of the world's land surface. Iconic examples include Yellowstone (established 1872, the world's first national park), the Serengeti, and vast marine protected areas such as Papahānaumokuākea.

Legislation and treaties

Key instruments include the Ramsar Convention on wetlands (1971), CITES (1973), the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992), and national laws such as India's Wildlife Protection Act of 1972.
