Algonquin Round Table

The Algonquin Round Table was a group of New York City writers, critics, actors, and wits who met for lunch at the Algonquin Hotel in Manhattan from 1919 into the early 1930s. Their daily gatherings became famous for wordplay, practical jokes, and razor-sharp repartee, much of it reported in newspaper columns around the country; members referred to themselves as the "Vicious Circle".

Origins and members

The lunches began in June 1919 as a welcome-home gathering for New York Times drama critic Alexander Woollcott, recently returned from the First World War, arranged by press agent John Peter Toohey and others. The group took a regular table in the hotel's Rose Room (later the Round Table Restaurant) and grew to include Dorothy Parker, Robert Benchley, Robert E. Sherwood, Heywood Broun, Franklin Pierce Adams, Harold Ross (founder of The New Yorker in 1925), George S. Kaufman, Edna Ferber, Marc Connelly, Jane Grant, Neysa McMein, and Harpo Marx, among others.

Reputation

The circle's wit was amplified by members' own columns — Adams's "The Conning Tower" and others quoted the table's jokes nationally — helping shape the sophisticated tone of 1920s American humour writing and The New Yorker's early voice. Parker summarised their reputation by saying the group was famous mostly for saying so itself. The regular lunches faded by the early 1930s as members drifted to Hollywood and elsewhere.
