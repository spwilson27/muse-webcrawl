Video compression (or video coding) is the process of reducing the amount of data needed to represent digital video, exploiting redundancy within and between frames to make storage and transmission practical. Uncompressed high-definition video can require gigabits per second; modern codecs routinely achieve compression ratios of 100:1 or more with visually acceptable quality, making digital television, streaming and video calling feasible.

Principles

Video compression exploits several kinds of redundancy. Spatial (intra-frame) compression removes repetition within a single picture, much as image codecs such as JPEG do, typically by transforming blocks of pixels with the discrete cosine transform (DCT) and quantising the coefficients. Temporal (inter-frame) compression exploits the fact that consecutive frames are usually very similar: motion estimation divides frames into blocks and records only motion vectors plus a small residual difference, stored in predicted frames (P-frames) and bidirectionally predicted frames (B-frames) that reference self-contained key frames (I-frames). Chroma subsampling reduces colour resolution, to which the eye is less sensitive, and entropy coding (such as CABAC or Huffman coding) squeezes the remaining symbols losslessly.

Lossy and lossless

Almost all widely deployed video compression is lossy: quantisation discards detail judged perceptually unimportant, and raising compression increases artefacts such as blocking, banding and ringing. Lossless video codecs exist for archiving and production work but achieve much smaller reductions, typically only 2:1 to 3:1.

Standards and applications

Successive generations of standards — MPEG-2 (DVD and early digital TV), H.264/AVC, H.265/HEVC, AV1 and H.266/VVC — have each roughly halved the bit rate needed for a given quality, at the cost of greatly increased computational complexity. Video compression underpins broadcast television, optical discs, web streaming services, video conferencing, surveillance and smartphone recording.
