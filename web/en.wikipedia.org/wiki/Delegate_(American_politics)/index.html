Delegate (American politics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In American politics, a delegate is a person chosen to represent others at a meeting or convention — most prominently the delegates to the Democratic and Republican national conventions who formally nominate the parties' presidential candidates, allocated by state primaries and caucuses and (for Democrats) supplemented historically by unpledged “superdelegates”.

The term also names a theory of representation: the delegate model, in which the representative should vote as their constituents would wish, in contrast to the trustee model, in which representatives exercise independent judgement. American practice mixes the models: members of Congress respond to constituency opinion (and fear primary challenges) while claiming trustee discretion on complex or conscience questions. In a different usage, “delegate” is the title of the non-voting representatives of US territories and the District of Columbia in the House of Representatives, and of members of some state lower houses (Virginia, Maryland, West Virginia).
