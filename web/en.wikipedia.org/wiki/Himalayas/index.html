Himalayas

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Himalayas, or Himalaya, is a mountain range in Asia separating the plains of the Indian subcontinent from the Tibetan Plateau. The range contains many of Earth's highest peaks, including the highest, Mount Everest, and more than a hundred peaks exceeding 7,200 metres. Its name derives from Sanskrit words meaning "abode of the snow".

The Himalayas arc for roughly 2,400 kilometres across five countries. They are the source of some of the world's major river systems — including the Indus, the Ganges and the Brahmaputra — whose combined drainage basins are home to hundreds of millions of people. The range profoundly shapes South Asian climate, intercepting the summer monsoon and keeping the subcontinent far warmer than equivalent latitudes elsewhere.

Geography

The range runs northwest to southeast, from the Indus bend in the west to the Brahmaputra bend in the east, whose great syntaxial gorges mark either end. Parallel sub-ranges include the Greater Himalayas (Himadri), with the highest summits; the Lesser Himalayas; and the outer Siwalik hills. Major peaks besides Everest include K2 in the Karakoram (often discussed alongside the Himalayas), Kangchenjunga, Lhotse, Makalu and Annapurna.

Geology

The Himalayas began forming roughly 50 million years ago, when the Indian plate collided with the Eurasian plate after the closing of the ancient Tethys Ocean — an application of plate tectonics on a continental scale. India still moves north by several centimetres a year, so the range continues to rise and the region is seismically active; the 2015 Nepal earthquake, centred near Gorkha, killed around 9,000 people.

Hydrology

Himalayan glaciers — such as the Siachen, Gangotri and Khumbu glaciers — store more ice than anywhere outside the polar regions, earning the region the nickname "the Third Pole". Meltwater feeds rivers that sustain farming, drinking water and hydropower across South and Southeast Asia, which is why accelerated glacial retreat under climate change is a major regional concern.

Ecology

Altitude creates stacked climate zones, from subtropical jungle at the foothills to alpine tundra and permanent snow. Wildlife includes the snow leopard, red panda, Himalayan tahr and Bengal tigers in the lowland Terai forests. Protected areas include Sagarmatha National Park in Nepal and Great Himalayan National Park in India.

Peoples and religions

The mountains are sacred in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism and Bon. Mount Kailash in Tibet is revered by several faiths, and pilgrimage routes thread the range, as do trade and trekking routes through high passes. Peoples of the Himalayas include the Sherpa of the Everest region, famed for mountaineering skill at altitude.

Mountaineering

European surveyors and climbers probed the range in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The "golden age" of Himalayan climbing followed the 1950 ascent of Annapurna I by a French expedition — the first 8,000-metre peak climbed — and the 1953 ascent of Everest by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay. Trekking and commercial expeditions are now central to the economies of places such as Kathmandu and the Khumbu.
