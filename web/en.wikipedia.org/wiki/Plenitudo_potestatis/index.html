Plenitudo potestatis

Papal authority in the high Middle Ages

Plenitudo potestatis (Latin for "fullness of power") is a term used by medieval canon lawyers to describe the pope's supreme jurisdiction within the Catholic Church. Adapted from Roman law, where it described an official acting with full delegated authority, the phrase came to express the claim that the pope, as successor of St Peter and vicar of Christ, held the fullness of ecclesiastical power, of which other bishops possessed only a share.

Development

Pope Leo I in the fifth century asserted a form of the idea, but it was developed systematically during the Gregorian reform of the eleventh century and elaborated by canonists such as Huguccio and by popes including Innocent III, who used the term frequently. Innocent argued that the pope could intervene in any church, depose bishops, and judge all while being judged by none. The doctrine reached its boldest statement in Boniface VIII's bull Unam sanctam (1302).

Scope and legacy

Plenitudo potestatis concerned primarily the internal government of the Church — the pope's authority as supreme legislator and judge — though medieval writers disputed how far it extended into temporal affairs, feeding conflicts between papacy and empire. The concept survives in canon law's description of the pope's "supreme, full, immediate and universal ordinary power" in the Church (1983 Code of Canon Law, canon 331).
