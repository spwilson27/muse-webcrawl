Leptin

Leptin (from the Greek leptos, "thin") is a 16 kDa protein hormone produced predominantly by adipose tissue (fat cells). It plays a central role in regulating energy balance by suppressing hunger and signalling satiety to the brain — in effect reporting the size of the body's fat stores to the hypothalamus.

Discovery

Leptin was discovered in 1994 by Jeffrey Friedman and colleagues at Rockefeller University, who identified the product of the mouse ob gene (human LEP gene on chromosome 7). Mice lacking the gene become massively obese; administering leptin reverses their obesity. The discovery transformed obesity research by demonstrating that body weight is under active hormonal regulation rather than determined solely by behaviour.

Function and clinical significance

Circulating leptin levels are proportional to total body fat. Acting on receptors in the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, leptin inhibits orexigenic neurons (including those expressing neuropeptide Y and AgRP) and stimulates anorexigenic POMC neurons, reducing food intake and increasing energy expenditure. It also has roles in reproduction, immunity, and bone metabolism.

Humans with rare congenital leptin deficiency develop severe early-onset obesity that responds dramatically to leptin replacement (metreleptin, approved for lipodystrophy and congenital deficiency). In common obesity, however, leptin levels are high but the brain becomes resistant to its signal — leptin resistance — which is why leptin therapy has not proved a general cure for obesity.
