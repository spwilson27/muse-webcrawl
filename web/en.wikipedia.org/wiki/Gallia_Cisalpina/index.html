Gallia Cisalpina

Gallia Cisalpina ("Gaul on this side of the Alps"), also called Gallia Citerior or Gallia Togata, was the Roman name for the part of Italy inhabited by Celts (Gauls) during the 4th and 3rd centuries BC, roughly the Po valley of modern northern Italy. Its name contrasts with Gallia Transalpina — Gaul "across the Alps", in modern southern France.

History

Celtic tribes — including the Insubres, Boii, Senones and Cenomani — settled the Po valley from around 400 BC, displacing or absorbing Etruscan and other Italic peoples. The Gauls famously sacked Rome itself after the Battle of the Allia in 390 BC. Rome gradually conquered the region during the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC, a process completed after the defeat of the Boii in 191 BC, and organised it as a province around 81 BC under Sulla.

Romanisation

The province was criss-crossed by Roman roads such as the Via Aemilia and Via Flaminia and dotted with colonies including Placentia (Piacenza), Bononia (Bologna), Mutina (Modena) and Mediolanum (Milan). Its inhabitants, many of them granted Latin rights by the Lex Pompeia in 89 BC, received full Roman citizenship in 49 BC under Julius Caesar, who had governed the province together with Illyricum and Transalpine Gaul during his Gallic Wars.

Abolition

Around 42 BC, during the Second Triumvirate, Cisalpine Gaul was formally incorporated into Italy proper, ceasing to be a separate province. The region went on to form the Augustan regiones of Transpadana, Venetia et Histria, Aemilia and Liguria.

The name was revived nearly two millennia later by Napoleon, whose Cisalpine Republic (1797–1802) was created in the same territory.
