Portuguese Inquisition

Portuguese: Inquisição Portuguesa
Established: 1536
Abolished: 1821
Headquarters: Lisbon

The Portuguese Inquisition (Portuguese: Inquisição Portuguesa), officially the General Council of the Holy Office of the Inquisition in Portugal, was formally established in 1536 at the request of King John III and operated until its abolition in 1821. Like the Spanish Inquisition, it was a tribunal of the Catholic Church under strong royal control, charged with policing heresy.

Origins

Its establishment followed the forced conversion of Portuguese Jews in 1497, when King Manuel I, seeking a marriage alliance with Spain, ordered all Jews in Portugal to convert to Christianity or leave, then prevented most from leaving. The resulting population of "New Christians" (cristãos-novos) was widely suspected of secretly practising Judaism. The Lisbon massacre of 1506, in which thousands of New Christians were killed, illustrated the tensions. After years of negotiation and bribery at the papal court, Pope Paul III authorised the Inquisition in 1536, and the first auto-da-fé was held in Lisbon in 1540.

Operation

Tribunals sat in Lisbon, Évora and Coimbra, and later in Goa in India (the Goa Inquisition, established 1560). The Inquisition prosecuted alleged Judaizers, Protestants, suspected witches, bigamists, sodomites and others accused of offences against orthodoxy, including the scientist and theologians suspected of heterodox ideas. Penalties ranged from penances and confiscation of property to imprisonment and, in a minority of cases, execution: historians estimate roughly 1,200–1,800 people were executed over its nearly 300 years, with tens of thousands tried.

Abolition

The power of the Inquisition declined in the 18th century under the Marquis of Pombal, who ended discrimination against New Christians in 1773. The tribunal was formally abolished by the liberal Cortes on 31 March 1821.
