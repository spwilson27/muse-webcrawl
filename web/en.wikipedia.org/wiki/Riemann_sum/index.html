Riemann sum

In mathematics, a Riemann sum is a method for approximating the total area underneath a curve on a graph, otherwise known as an integral. It may also be used to define the integration operation. The method is named after the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, who presented it in the 19th century as part of the rigorous foundation of calculus.

Definition

To approximate the area under a function f on an interval [a, b], the interval is divided into n subintervals of width Δx. On each subinterval a sample point is chosen, and the heights f(xi*) are multiplied by Δx to give rectangle areas; the Riemann sum is the total Σ f(xi*) Δx. As the number of rectangles increases, the sum converges to the definite integral for integrable functions.

Types

Common choices of sample point give the left Riemann sum (left endpoints), the right Riemann sum (right endpoints) and the midpoint rule (midpoints). Using maxima or minima on each subinterval gives the upper and lower Darboux sums, whose agreement determines Riemann integrability.

Related methods

The trapezoidal rule and Simpson's rule can be viewed as refinements that use trapezoids or parabolic arcs instead of flat-topped rectangles, often converging much faster for smooth functions. Riemann sums remain the standard route by which definite integrals are introduced in calculus courses.
