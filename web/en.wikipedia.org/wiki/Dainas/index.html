Dainas

Dainas are traditional Latvian folk songs and one of the defining elements of Latvian culture. A daina is typically a very short poem — most often a single quatrain of four lines, though two-line and six-line forms exist — written in a trochaic or dactylic metre and sung to simple, often archaic melodies. More than 1.2 million daina texts, together with some 30,000 melodies, have been recorded, making the corpus one of the largest collections of folk poetry in the world.

Content and themes

Dainas address every part of traditional life: the cycle of the year and agricultural work, birth, courtship, marriage and death, and the pre-Christian Baltic mythology of figures such as Dievs (God), the sun goddess Saule, her daughters the Saules meitas, the fate goddess Laima, and Māra. Many are associated with the midsummer festival of Jāņi. Their style is laconic and metaphorical, favouring diminutives and parallels drawn from nature; a person’s sorrows may be voiced through an orphaned bird or a windswept tree.

Collection and the Cabinet of Folksongs

The systematic collection of dainas was the life’s work of Krišjānis Barons (1835–1923), the "father of the dainas". Between 1894 and 1915 he published the six-volume Latvju dainas, containing 217,996 song texts gathered from singers across Latvia. The slips of paper on which the songs were catalogued were kept in a purpose-built cabinet, the Dainu skapis (Cabinet of Folksongs), now preserved at the Archives of Latvian Folklore in the National Library of Latvia and inscribed in UNESCO’s Memory of the World register in 2001. Dainas were central to the Latvian national awakening and to the "Singing Revolution" of the late 1980s, when mass song festivals became vehicles of peaceful protest.
