Westphalia (German: Westfalen; Low German: Westfalen) is a region of northwestern Germany, forming the eastern part of the state of North Rhine-Westphalia. It is roughly bounded by the rivers Rhine and Weser and by the Teutoburg Forest, and encompasses the eastern Ruhr area and the cities of Dortmund, Bielefeld, Münster, and Paderborn.

History

Westphalia was originally the western territory of the Saxons, conquered by Charlemagne in the late 8th century. After the breakup of the Duchy of Saxony in 1180, the region fragmented into numerous ecclesiastical and secular territories, including the Prince-Bishoprics of Münster, Paderborn, and Osnabrück and the counties of Mark and Ravensberg.

The name became internationally famous through the Peace of Westphalia of 1648, the treaties concluded in Münster and Osnabrück that ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic. The settlement is regarded as a foundation of the modern state system — "Westphalian sovereignty" remains a core concept in international relations.

Napoleon created a Kingdom of Westphalia (1807–1813) with Kassel as capital, though it lay mostly outside the historic region. After 1815 most of Westphalia became a province of Prussia, with Münster as capital. Industrialisation made its eastern Ruhr districts a centre of coal and steel. In 1946 the province was merged with the northern Rhineland to form North Rhine-Westphalia.

Geography and culture

The region ranges from the flat Münsterland plain to the Sauerland highlands in the south (including the Rothaar Mountains) and the Wiehen Hills. Westphalian culture retains a distinct identity in dialect (Westphalian Low German), cuisine — pumpernickel, Westphalian ham, and hearty stews — and in the red-brick and half-timbered architecture of its towns.
