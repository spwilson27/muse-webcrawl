Shear zone

A shear zone is a tabular zone of rock in the Earth's crust or mantle that has undergone intense shear strain relative to the surrounding, less deformed rock. Shear zones range from millimetres wide to hundreds of kilometres long, and they are fundamental structures accommodating relative motion between crustal blocks.

Brittle and ductile types

In the upper, cooler crust shear is typically brittle, producing faults and fault breccias. Deeper down, where temperature and pressure promote crystal-plastic flow, shear is ductile: the rock deforms by flowing, developing strong foliation and lineation and, at high strain, fine-grained mylonite. Many large shear zones pass downward from brittle faults into ductile zones, reflecting the brittle–ductile transition of the crust. The displacement across a major ductile shear zone can reach tens or hundreds of kilometres.

Geometry and kinematics

Geologists determine the sense of shear from asymmetric indicators in the deformed rock: S–C fabrics, rotated porphyroclasts ("sigma" and "delta" clasts), mica fish, and asymmetric folds. Shear zones may be strike-slip, thrust (compressional) or extensional (detachment) in character, and their networks localise later deformation, metamorphic fluids — many gold deposits are shear-zone-hosted — and sometimes magma ascent.

Examples

Well-known shear zones include the Great Glen Fault of Scotland, the Periadriatic lineament of the Alps, the South Armorican shear zone of Brittany, the Main Central Thrust of the Himalayas, and the Red River shear zone of Southeast Asia, whose displacement is tied to the collision of India with Asia.
