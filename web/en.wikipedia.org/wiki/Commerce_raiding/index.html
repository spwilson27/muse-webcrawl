Commerce raiding

Commerce raiding is a form of naval warfare whose aim is to attack and destroy the merchant shipping of an enemy nation, disrupting its trade and war economy rather than engaging its battle fleet. In French it is known as guerre de course ("war of the chase"). Historically it has been the strategy of weaker naval powers confronting stronger ones.

Age of sail

In the age of sail, commerce raiding was carried out by warships, privateers sailing under letters of marque, and pirates. French corsairs such as Jean Bart and Robert Surcouf preyed on English and Dutch trade; during the American Revolutionary War and the War of 1812, American privateers seized hundreds of British merchantmen. The Declaration of Paris (1856) abolished privateering among its signatories.

Steam era

The Confederate States Navy's raiders, above all CSS Alabama, devastated Union shipping during the American Civil War, driving up insurance rates and driving the U.S. merchant marine into foreign flags — grievances settled in the Alabama Claims. In the First World War, German surface raiders and disguised auxiliary cruisers operated worldwide before unrestricted submarine warfare became the principal threat to commerce.

Second World War and after

German surface raiders such as Admiral Graf Spee and the disguised Atlantis repeated the strategy in 1939–41, but air power, radar and convoy escorts reduced its effectiveness; the tonnage war was waged mainly by U-boats and, in the Pacific, by American submarines against Japan. Modern anti-access weapons and commerce warfare remain elements of naval planning, but the classic surface raider has vanished.
