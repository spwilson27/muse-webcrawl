Shoshoni, also written Shoshone, is a Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family spoken by the Shoshone people, traditionally across a large area of the Great Basin and northwestern Plains in what is now Wyoming, Idaho, Utah, and Nevada. It forms a dialect chain with Timbisha (Panamint) and Comanche, which split off historically from Shoshoni.

Dialects and distribution

Major regional varieties include Western Shoshoni in Nevada and eastern California's border area, Northern Shoshoni in Idaho, Gosiute in western Utah, and Eastern Shoshoni in Wyoming's Wind River country. The dialects are largely mutually intelligible, with differences mainly in vocabulary and sound correspondences.

Features

Shoshoni is an agglutinative language with complex verb morphology, a characteristic Numic consonant system involving gemination and spirantization of consonants in predictable environments, and vowel devoicing in certain positions that can make final vowels nearly silent. Nouns can incorporate into verbs, and the language distinguishes inclusive and exclusive first-person plural pronouns.

Status and revitalization

The number of fluent speakers has fallen sharply; most first-language speakers are elderly, and UNESCO and Ethnologue classify the language as endangered. Revitalization efforts include tribal language programs, immersion and master-apprentice teaching, university courses at Idaho State University and the University of Utah, and the Shoshoni Language Project, which has produced dictionaries, teaching materials, and recordings of elders.
