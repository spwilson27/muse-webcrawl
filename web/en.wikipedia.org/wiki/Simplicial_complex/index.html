Simplicial complex

In mathematics, a simplicial complex is a set composed of points, line segments, triangles, and their n-dimensional counterparts (see simplex). Simplicial complexes should not be confused with the more abstract notion of a simplicial set appearing in modern simplicial homotopy theory. The purely combinatorial form of a simplicial complex is an abstract simplicial complex.

Definitions

A (geometric) simplicial complex is a set K of simplices satisfying two conditions: every face of a simplex in K is also in K, and the intersection of any two simplices in K is either empty or a face of both. The union of all simplices of K, with the subspace topology, is called the polyhedron (or underlying space) of the complex; a topological space that is the polyhedron of some simplicial complex is called triangulable. The dimension of a complex is the largest dimension of any of its simplices.

An abstract simplicial complex is a purely set-theoretic object: a family of nonempty finite sets, called simplices, closed under taking nonempty subsets. This combinatorial data suffices for computing the homology of the complex without reference to geometry, and the two notions are equivalent up to geometric realisation.

Homology

Simplicial homology assigns to a simplicial complex a sequence of abelian groups by forming chain groups of oriented simplices and computing the cycles modulo boundaries. Its great advantage over singular homology is that it is fully computable: simplicial homology is one of the working tools of computational topology and topological data analysis, where complexes such as the Vietoris–Rips and Čech complexes are built from point-cloud data and their homology is tracked across scales in persistent homology.

Combinatorial notions

Standard constructions on complexes include the star and link of a simplex, barycentric subdivision, simplicial maps between complexes, and the simplicial approximation theorem, which guarantees that any continuous map between polyhedra can be approximated by a simplicial map after sufficient subdivision. The f-vector of a complex counts its simplices by dimension, and the Euler characteristic can be computed as an alternating sum of these numbers.
