Armature (electrical)

The armature of a DC motor, showing the laminated iron core and copper windings.

In electrical engineering, the armature is the component of an electric machine that carries alternating current, or, in many DC machines, the rotating part carrying the windings in which the main electromotive force is induced. The armature windings conduct AC even on DC machines, due to the commutator action, or because of the electronic control.

Description

The armature generally consists of a laminated soft-iron core with slots in which insulated copper conductors are wound. Lamination reduces eddy-current losses. In a typical DC machine the armature is the rotor; it is mounted on a shaft together with a commutator, and brushes transfer current between the rotating commutator and the external circuit.

In most alternators and many synchronous machines the roles are reversed: the armature winding is on the stator while the field winding rotates. This arrangement avoids passing large output currents through sliding contacts; only the relatively small field current goes through slip rings.

Armature reaction

The magnetic field produced by armature currents distorts the main field — an effect called armature reaction. It can shift the neutral plane, cause sparking at the brushes, and reduce machine efficiency. Designers counter it with compensating windings, interpoles (commutating poles), and skewed slots.

Applications

Armatures are found in DC motors and generators, universal motors in household appliances, automotive starter motors and alternators, and in electromagnetic devices such as relays and solenoids, where "armature" also denotes the movable iron part attracted by a coil.
