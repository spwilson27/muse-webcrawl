Four Sons of Horus

The four sons of Horus were a group of four deities in ancient Egyptian religion, who were essentially the personifications of the four canopic jars, which accompanied the mummified remains of the deceased. Since the heart was thought to embody the soul, it was left inside the body; the brain was thought only to be the origin of mucus, so it was reduced to liquid, removed with hooks and discarded. The stomach and large intestines were stored in one jar, the small intestines in another, and the lungs and liver in separate jars.

The four gods

  • Hapy — baboon-headed god who protected the lungs; himself protected by the goddess Nephthys; associated with the north.
  • Imsety — human-headed god who protected the liver; protected by Isis; associated with the south.
  • Duamutef — jackal-headed god who protected the stomach; protected by Neith; associated with the east.
  • Qebehsenuef — falcon-headed god who protected the intestines; protected by Serqet; associated with the west.

Role in funerary religion

The four sons were depicted on the sides of canopic chests and coffins, and in the Book of the Dead they stand on a lotus in the presence of Osiris in the hall of judgment. They were also linked to the four pillars of the sky and the four cardinal points, embodying the completeness of the deceased's protection in the afterlife.
