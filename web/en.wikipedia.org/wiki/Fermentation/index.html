Fermentation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fermentation is a metabolic process that produces chemical changes in organic substances through the action of enzymes from microorganisms — and, in biochemistry, the anaerobic breakdown of glucose by cells themselves when oxygen is absent (see cellular respiration).

In food and drink

Humanity has fermented since the Neolithic: yeasts convert sugars to ethanol and CO₂ in beer, wine and bread; lactic-acid bacteria sour milk into yogurt and cheese, cabbage into sauerkraut and kimchi, and preserve vegetables everywhere; acetic bacteria turn wine to vinegar; moulds and mixed cultures make soy sauce, miso and tempeh. Fermentation preserves food, creates flavour, and sometimes detoxifies (cassava). Pasteur proved it a work of living microbes — "fermentation is life without air" — founding microbiology on it and rescuing French wine and beer in the process (see germ theory).

In cells and industry

In muscle cells starved of oxygen, pyruvate is reduced to lactate to regenerate NAD⁺, buying time at low efficiency — the burn of exertion and the biochemistry behind athletic limits (see marathon). Industrially, fermentation in great vats (bioreactors) now produces insulin and other biologics, enzymes, biofuels and the antigens of some vaccines — the oldest biotechnology, still expanding.
