Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) is a psychotherapy developed by Francine Shapiro starting in 1987 for the treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). During EMDR sessions, the client recalls distressing memories while receiving bilateral stimulation — most commonly side-to-side eye movements guided by the therapist — which is intended to help the brain reprocess the memories so they become less vivid and emotionally charged.

Procedure

Standard EMDR follows eight phases: history-taking, preparation, assessment of the target memory, desensitization (during which the client holds the memory in mind while tracking the therapist's moving fingers or another bilateral stimulus), installation of a preferred positive belief, body scan, closure and re-evaluation. Sessions typically last 60 to 90 minutes, and treatment for a single traumatic event may take only a few sessions.

Evidence

Numerous randomised controlled trials and meta-analyses find EMDR effective for PTSD, and it is recommended in treatment guidelines of organisations including the World Health Organization and the American Psychological Association. Its efficacy appears comparable to trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy. Debate continues over how it works: proposed mechanisms include working-memory taxation (holding a memory while performing a dual task reduces its vividness), a relaxation or orienting response, and effects similar to REM sleep. Studies differ on whether the eye movements themselves add benefit beyond exposure and other common therapy elements.

History and reception

Shapiro reported discovering the effect in 1987 while walking in a park and noticing that spontaneous eye movements reduced the disturbance of her own negative thoughts; she published the first controlled study in 1989. Early scepticism — EMDR was at first marketed through closed trainings and described by some critics as pseudoscientific in its promotion — gave way as controlled evidence accumulated, though debate over its mechanism and the necessity of eye movements persists.
