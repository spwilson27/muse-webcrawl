The Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom which instituted a legislative framework for the powers of police officers in England and Wales to combat crime, and provided codes of practice for the exercise of those powers. It balances police powers of stop and search, arrest, detention, investigation and identification against safeguards for suspects, including rights to legal advice and limits on detention.

PACE and its Codes of Practice govern the treatment of arrested persons in police custody in England and Wales.

Background

PACE followed the report of the Royal Commission on Criminal Procedure (the Philips Commission, 1981), which was prompted by concern over miscarriages of justice and by the outdated and inconsistent common-law rules then governing police powers. The Act largely came into force on 1 January 1986.

Provisions

Key provisions regulate: powers to stop and search persons and vehicles (Part I); search of premises and seizure of property (Part II); arrest (Part III); detention, treatment and questioning of suspects (Parts IV–V); and the tape-recording of interviews (Part VI). Section 24, as amended by the Serious Organised Crime and Police Act 2005, governs arrest without warrant. Section 78 gives courts a discretion to exclude unfair evidence, and section 76 excludes confessions obtained by oppression or in circumstances likely to render them unreliable.

Codes of Practice

The Act requires the Home Secretary to issue Codes of Practice (Codes A–H) covering stop and search, search and seizure, arrest, detention, identification, interviewing, tape and visual recording, and the detention of terrorism suspects. Breaches of the codes do not themselves create criminal liability but are admissible in evidence and relevant to the exclusion of evidence.
