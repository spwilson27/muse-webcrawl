A triode is an electronic amplifying vacuum tube (valve) consisting of three electrodes inside an evacuated glass or metal envelope: a heated cathode, which emits electrons; an anode (plate), held positive, which collects them; and a control grid between the two, by which a small voltage can modulate the much larger cathode-to-anode current. It was the first device capable of electronic amplification and made possible radio broadcasting, long-distance telephony, sound film, and early computers.

History

The triode grew out of John Ambrose Fleming's two-electrode diode (1904). In 1906 the American inventor Lee de Forest added the grid, patenting his "Audion"; the Austrian Robert von Lieben independently pursued similar ideas. De Forest initially did not fully understand his device's physics, but by about 1912 researchers at AT&T and elsewhere understood its amplifying action and improved the vacuum, enabling reliable repeaters for transcontinental telephone lines and, soon, radio transmitters and receivers.

Operation

Electrons boiled off the heated cathode are drawn toward the positive plate. Because the grid is close to the cathode, small changes in its (usually negative) voltage strongly influence the electron flow, so a weak signal at the grid produces a much stronger copy in the plate circuit. Key parameters are the amplification factor (μ), plate resistance, and transconductance (gm). Limitations—including interelectrode capacitance that caused instability at high frequencies (the Miller effect)—led to the tetrode and pentode, which added screen and suppressor grids.

Uses and legacy

Triodes dominated electronics from the 1920s until transistors displaced them in the 1960s. Classic types include the 300B (1938) and 12AX7/ECC83 twin triode. Today triodes survive in guitar and high-end audio amplifiers prized for their soft clipping and harmonic character, in high-power radio transmitters, and in niche applications. In semiconductor form, the triode region of a field-effect transistor's operation is named after the tube's behaviour.
