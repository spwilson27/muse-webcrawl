Brownian motion

Brownian motion is the random motion of particles suspended in a medium (a liquid or a gas). It has been observed in many kinds of natural phenomena and is named after the botanist Robert Brown, who in 1827 described the erratic movement of pollen grains in water under a microscope. The mathematical model of Brownian motion — the Wiener process — has applications in physics, chemistry, biology and financial mathematics.

History

The Roman poet Lucretius described the dancing of dust motes as evidence for invisible atoms. Robert Brown's 1827 observations ruled out life as the cause, since inorganic particles moved the same way. In 1905 Albert Einstein published a quantitative theory: the motion results from collisions with the molecules of the fluid, and the mean squared displacement of a particle grows linearly with time, ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt, where D is the diffusion coefficient. Jean Perrin's experiments (1908) confirmed Einstein's predictions and measured Avogadro's number, convincing remaining sceptics of the reality of atoms; Perrin received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1926.

Mathematical modelling

Norbert Wiener gave a rigorous construction of Brownian motion as a stochastic process in 1923. The Wiener process W(t) has independent, normally distributed increments, continuous paths that are almost surely nowhere differentiable, and satisfies ⟨W(t)²⟩ = t. Brownian motion is the scaling limit of a simple random walk, by Donsker's invariance principle.

D = RT / (6πηrNA) (Stokes–Einstein relation)

Applications

Beyond diffusion in physics and chemistry, Brownian motion underlies the Black–Scholes model of option pricing (via geometric Brownian motion), models of polymer dynamics, population genetics (genetic drift), and search algorithms. The collisional physics of particles and fluids is also central to astrophysics.
