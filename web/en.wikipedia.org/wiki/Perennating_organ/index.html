Perennating organ
Field: Botany
Function: Survival through adverse seasons; vegetative reproduction
Examples: Bulbs, corms, tubers, rhizomes, turions

A bulb is a classic perennating organ

In botany, a perennating organ is a structure by which a perennial plant survives periods of unfavourable conditions — winter cold, summer drought, or fire — and from which new growth resumes when conditions improve. Such organs typically store food reserves (starch, sugars, proteins) and contain buds protected below ground or under water, while the plant's above-ground parts die back.

Types

The principal kinds of perennating organ include:

  • Bulbs — short stems surrounded by fleshy scale leaves (onion, daffodil, tulip).
  • Corms — swollen solid stem bases covered in dry scale leaves (crocus, gladiolus).
  • Stem tubers — swollen underground stems bearing buds or "eyes" (potato).
  • Root tubers — swollen storage roots (dahlia, sweet potato).
  • Rhizomes — horizontal underground stems (iris, ginger, many grasses).
  • Turions — compact overwintering buds produced by some aquatic plants, which sink to the bottom and rise again in spring.

Perennating organs also function in vegetative (asexual) reproduction: offsets, bulbils, and divided tubers or rhizomes produce genetically identical new plants, a property widely exploited in horticulture and agriculture. The Danish botanist Christen Raunkiær used the position of perennating buds during the unfavourable season as the basis of his influential life-form classification of plants.
