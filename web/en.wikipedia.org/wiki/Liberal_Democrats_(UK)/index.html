The Liberal Democrats (often referred to as the Lib Dems) are a liberal political party in the United Kingdom. Ideologically, they draw on both the classical liberal and social liberal traditions. The party was formed in 1988 by the merger of the Liberal Party and the Social Democratic Party (SDP), which had contested the 1983 and 1987 elections as the SDP–Liberal Alliance.

History

The party's roots lie in the historic Liberal Party — the party of Gladstone, Lloyd George and Keynes — which had been one of Britain's two governing parties until the rise of Labour in the 1920s. After early struggles, including a disastrous 1989 European election, the party under Paddy Ashdown (1988–99) rebuilt, and under Charles Kennedy it won 62 seats in 2005, its best result since 1923. Under Nick Clegg, following the 2010 general election's hung parliament, the party entered a coalition government with the Conservatives, with Clegg as Deputy Prime Minister — the first UK coalition since 1945.

The coalition proved electorally devastating: the party's U-turn on tuition fees badly damaged its credibility, and at the 2015 election it fell from 57 to 8 seats. It campaigned prominently for Remain in the 2016 EU referendum and for a second referendum under Jo Swinson in 2019, but won only 11 seats that year. Under Ed Davey, the party rebuilt through by-election successes (Chesham and Amersham, North Shropshire, Tiverton and Honiton, Somerton and Frome) and at the 2024 general election won 72 seats — its highest ever number of MPs — becoming the third-largest party in the Commons, mainly at Conservative expense in southern England.

Ideology and policies

The party's constitution commits it to building "a fair, free and open society" balancing liberty, equality and community. Signature positions include constitutional reform (proportional representation, an elected House of Lords, votes at 16), strong civil liberties, environmentalism and climate action, a close relationship with Europe, and support for devolution and federalism within the UK. Its membership sits in the tens of thousands, and its internal structure is federal, with semi-autonomous English, Scottish and Welsh parties.
