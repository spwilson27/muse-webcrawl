Ordnance Survey

Ordnance Survey

Founded: 1791

Headquarters: Southampton, England

Type: Government-owned company (from 2015)

Coverage: Great Britain

Ordnance Survey (OS) is the national mapping agency for Great Britain. It produces and maintains the definitive geographic database of the country — covering England, Scotland and Wales — and the paper and digital maps derived from it. Its name derives from its original military purpose under the Board of Ordnance: in the aftermath of the Jacobite rising of 1745, and with invasion feared from France, accurate maps of the terrain were needed for defence.

History

The agency's origins lie in the military survey of Scotland under William Roy after the 1745 rising, and the subsequent Principal Triangulation of Great Britain. In 1791 the Board of Ordnance purchased a great theodolite built by Jesse Ramsden, and the first one-inch-to-the-mile map, of Kent, was published in 1801. County series maps at six inches and then 25 inches to the mile followed through the 19th century. In the 1930s the Retriangulation of Great Britain under Martin Hotine established the National Grid reference system still used on OS maps, based on the OSGB36 datum.

Products

For leisure users the best-known products are the paper Explorer (1:25,000) and Landranger (1:50,000) series, instantly recognisable to generations of British walkers. Professional users licence the OS MasterMap database, which records over 450 million geographic features, as well as address, road-network and imagery products. Since 2010 a range of data has been released free as OS OpenData, and the agency now offers digital mapping via its OS Maps service and APIs. In 2015 Ordnance Survey became a government-owned company wholly owned by the Secretary of State.

Symbols and benchmarks

OS maps are famed for their rich symbology and for the cut benchmark arrows carved into buildings and survey pillars, part of the levelling network established to give heights above the Newlyn datum. Triangulation pillars ("trig points") on hilltops remain a cultural fixture for walkers.
