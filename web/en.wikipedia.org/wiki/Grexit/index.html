Grexit

Grexit is a portmanteau of "Greek exit" coined by Citigroup economists in February 2012 to describe the possibility of Greece withdrawing from the eurozone, the group of European Union countries using the euro as their common currency. The term came to prominence during the Greek government-debt crisis, when Greece's enormous public debt and successive bailouts raised the prospect that it might be forced — or choose — to leave the single currency and restore the drachma.

Background

Greece joined the euro in 2001. Following the global financial crisis, its debt was revealed to be far larger than officially reported, and from 2010 it received rescue loans from the eurozone and the International Monetary Fund in exchange for austerity measures. Speculation about exit peaked in 2012 and again in 2015, when the Syriza government of Alexis Tsipras called a referendum on creditors' bailout terms; although voters rejected the terms, the government accepted a third bailout and Greece remained in the euro, imposing capital controls for a time. Analysts warned that a Grexit could trigger bank runs, a collapse in the value of a new currency, and contagion to other indebted eurozone members. The word inspired a family of similar coinages, most famously "Brexit" for the United Kingdom's departure from the European Union.
