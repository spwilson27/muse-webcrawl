Dry toilet

A dry toilet (or non-flush toilet, no-flush toilet or toilet without a flush) is a toilet that operates without flush water, unlike a flush toilet. The dry toilet may have a raised pedestal on which the user can sit, or a squat pan over which the user squats. Urine and feces may be collected separately or together, depending on the type of dry toilet and the design of the system.

Types

Several types of toilets can be described as dry toilets:

  • Urine-diverting dry toilets (UDDTs) separate urine from feces at the pedestal, keeping the feces dry so they can dehydrate and be handled more safely.
  • Composting toilets treat excreta together, often with a carbon-rich bulking material such as sawdust, to produce a compost-like output.
  • Pit latrines collect excreta in a hole in the ground and are the most common dry toilets worldwide.
  • Container-based toilets collect excreta in removable containers that are collected and treated off-site; bucket toilets are a simple form.
  • Chemical toilets and incinerating toilets use chemicals or combustion instead of water.

Advantages

Dry toilets save water, do not require connection to a sewer system, and can recover nutrients — nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in urine and feces — for use as fertilizer in agriculture, a central idea of ecological sanitation (ecosan). They can be built locally from inexpensive materials and are well suited to areas with water scarcity, rocky ground, high water tables, or no sewerage.

Challenges

Dry toilets may require more user involvement in maintenance than flush toilets; poorly managed systems can produce odors and fly problems. Where excreta are reused, adequate treatment is necessary to inactivate pathogens such as Ascaris eggs. Cultural acceptance of handling excreta varies, and building codes in many countries assume flush toilets.
