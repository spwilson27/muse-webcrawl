In geology, a rift is a linear zone where the lithosphere is being pulled apart, an example of extensional tectonics. Typical rift features are a central linear downfaulted depression, called a graben, or more commonly a half-graben with normal faulting and tilting along one main boundary fault. Rifts are the first stage in the separation of continental plates and, if rifting continues, can lead to the formation of a new ocean basin.

Formation

Rifting begins when tensional stresses stretch the crust and lithosphere. "Active" rifting is driven from below by mantle upwelling (plumes) that thins and domes the lithosphere; "passive" rifting is driven by plate-boundary forces transmitted into the continent. As extension proceeds, the crust thins, faults multiply, magma rises into the weakened zone, and subsidence creates basins that fill with sediment and volcanic rocks. Continued extension eventually ruptures the continent, and the rift transitions into a mid-ocean ridge spreading centre.

Structure

Rifts are bounded by normal faults organised into segments tens of kilometres long. The axial depression is typically an asymmetric half-graben; flanking "rift shoulders" may be uplifted, generating dramatic escarpments. Rifting is commonly accompanied by alkaline volcanism, hot springs and seismicity, and rift lakes collect in the deepest grabens (e.g. Tanganyika, Baikal, Malawi).

Examples

  • East African Rift — the classic active continental rift, splitting the African Plate into the Nubian and Somali plates; associated with Afar triple junction volcanism and early hominin sites.
  • Baikal Rift Zone — contains Lake Baikal, the world's deepest lake.
  • Rhine Graben — part of the European Cenozoic Rift System.
  • Basin and Range Province — broad extension in the western United States.
  • Red Sea and Gulf of Aden — young ocean basins formed by rifting of Arabia from Africa; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge is the mature, submarine continuation of rifting between the Americas and Europe/Africa.

Failed rifts

Many rifts stop extending before breakup, leaving sediment-filled troughs called aulacogens — such as the Midcontinent Rift of North America (around Lake Superior) and the Benue Trough of Nigeria. These basins can be reactivated later and often host significant hydrocarbon and mineral deposits.
