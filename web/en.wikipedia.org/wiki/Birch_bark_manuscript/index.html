Birch bark manuscript

Birch bark manuscripts are documents written on pieces of the inner bark of birch trees, a writing material used for many centuries in several cultures. The most famous examples are the medieval birch bark letters of Novgorod in Russia and the ancient Buddhist manuscripts from Gandhara in Central Asia.

Gandharan manuscripts

The oldest surviving Indian manuscripts are birch bark scrolls from the ancient region of Gandhara (in modern Pakistan and Afghanistan), dating from around the 1st century CE. Written in the Kharoshthi script in the Gandhari language, they contain Buddhist texts and were preserved inside clay pots. The British Library's collection of these scrolls is among the most significant finds in Buddhist studies.

Slavic birch bark letters

In northwestern Russia, more than a thousand birch bark documents have been excavated, beginning in 1951 in Novgorod under archaeologist Artemiy Artsikhovsky. Mostly dating from the 11th to 15th centuries, they record everyday matters — business dealings, household instructions, personal letters and a child's school exercises (those of the boy Onfim) — rather than official chronicles, providing unique evidence of widespread literacy in medieval Rus'. Letters have also been found in Smolensk, Pskov, Moscow and other cities, typically preserved in waterlogged clay soil.

Other traditions

In India, birch bark (Sanskrit bhurja) was used for centuries for religious and literary texts, particularly in Kashmir and the Himalayan region. Birch bark was also used for writing by some Indigenous peoples of North America, including the Ojibwe, whose sacred scrolls (wiigwaasabak) record migration histories and ceremonial knowledge.
