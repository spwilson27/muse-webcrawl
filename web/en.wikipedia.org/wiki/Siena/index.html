Siena (Italian: [ˈsjɛːna]) is a city in Tuscany, Italy, and the capital of the province of Siena. It is famed for its medieval cityscape, its cathedral, and the Palio, a horse race held twice each summer in the shell-shaped Piazza del Campo. The historic centre of Siena is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city sits on three hills connected by winding alleys and was, in the Middle Ages, one of Italy's great banking and trading centres and the chief rival of Florence.

History

According to legend, Siena was founded by Senius and Aschius, sons of Remus, who fled Rome after their father's murder by Romulus; the city's emblem, the she-wolf suckling twins, reflects this myth. In reality the settlement has Etruscan and Roman roots. Siena flourished in the 12th and 13th centuries as a republic on the Via Francigena pilgrimage route; its bankers lent across Europe, and the Monte dei Paschi, founded in 1472, is the world's oldest surviving bank. The Black Death of 1348 and long rivalry with Florence checked Siena's growth. In 1559, after a brutal siege, the republic fell and Siena was absorbed into the Florentine state of Cosimo I de' Medici.

Main sights

The Piazza del Campo is the city's civic heart, paved in brick and divided into nine sectors recalling the Government of the Nine. On it stand the Palazzo Pubblico, with Lorenzetti's frescoes of Good and Bad Government, and the slender Torre del Mangia. Siena Cathedral (the Duomo), built in black-and-white marble, contains works by Nicola Pisano, Donatello, Michelangelo and Pinturicchio. The city's seventeen contrade (districts) compete in the Palio, run on 2 July and 16 August.

Culture and economy

Siena is home to the University of Siena, founded in 1240, one of Italy's oldest. Local specialities include panforte, a dense fruit-and-nut cake, and ricciarelli almond biscuits. Tourism, banking and the university dominate the modern economy.
