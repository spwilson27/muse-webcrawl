Taconic orogeny

The Taconic orogeny was a mountain-building episode that ended around 440 million years ago (Ma) and affected most of modern-day northern Appalachia. It was the first of three major orogenies that built the Appalachian Mountains in eastern North America, followed by the Acadian and Alleghanian orogenies.

Cause

During the Middle and Late Ordovician, the eastern margin of the continent of Laurentia collided with a volcanic island arc as the Iapetus Ocean began to close. As the arc approached, deep-water sediments deposited on the continental slope were scraped off, folded and thrust westward over the continental shelf in enormous sheets known as the Taconic allochthon. The collision raised a mountain range roughly where the Taconic and Berkshire mountains of New York and New England stand today, though the original range was far higher and has since been eroded.

Effects

The weight of the thrust sheets depressed the crust to the west, forming a foreland basin in which the black shales of the Utica Shale and later the red beds of the Queenston Delta accumulated — sediments shed from the rising mountains. Volcanic ash beds (bentonites) from the arc are preserved across eastern North America and serve as important time markers in Ordovician stratigraphy. The orogeny also coincides broadly with a major extinction pulse and the end-Ordovician glaciation.
