Genetic drift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic drift is the change in the frequency of an existing gene variant (allele) in a population due to random chance. Drift acts in all populations but is powerful in small ones, where sampling accidents — which individuals happen to breed — can drive alleles to loss or fixation regardless of their effects on fitness. Together with mutation, selection and gene flow, drift is one of the basic forces of evolution.

Two situations amplify it: the bottleneck effect, when a population crashes to a few survivors (cheetahs and northern elephant seals show its genetic scars), and the founder effect, when a few colonists start a new population — one reason isolated island and religious communities can have unusually high rates of particular inherited diseases.

Neutral theory

Motoo Kimura's neutral theory (1968) proposed that most evolutionary change at the molecular level is the drift of neutral mutations, not selection — a claim that reframed molecular evolution and gave biology the molecular clock for dating divergences. The modern view assigns both drift and selection large roles, their balance depending on population size.

Drift vs selection

An allele behaves as effectively neutral when selection on it is weaker than about the inverse of population size. Drift explains much genomic 'noise' — pseudogene decay, intron variation — and why small endangered populations lose the diversity they need to adapt, a core concern of conservation genetics.
