Southern soul

Southern soul, also known as deep soul, is a type of soul and R&B music that emerged in the Southern United States in the 1960s, centred on the studios of Memphis, Tennessee, and Muscle Shoals, Alabama. Compared with the polished sound of Motown, southern soul was rawer and more emotive, driven by punchy horn sections, greasy Hammond organ, and gospel-inflected vocals. Its principal labels were Stax/Volt in Memphis, Atlantic's southern recordings, Goldwax, Hi Records, and the FAME studio operation in Muscle Shoals.

Characteristics and key artists

Southern soul records typically feature a tight rhythm section — exemplified by Booker T. & the M.G.'s and the Muscle Shoals Rhythm Section ("The Swampers") — interacting with unison horn riffs and impassioned lead singing. Defining artists include Otis Redding, Sam & Dave, Wilson Pickett, Aretha Franklin (whose Atlantic breakthrough was recorded at FAME), Percy Sledge ("When a Man Loves a Woman", 1966), James Carr, Solomon Burke, Eddie Floyd, William Bell and, in its smoother 1970s Memphis form, Al Green and Ann Peebles at Hi Records.

History

The style crystallised around 1961–65 as Stax built a national profile through its distribution deal with Atlantic. The assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. in Memphis in 1968 and Stax's troubled 1970s are often cited in the genre's decline, as disco and smoother Philadelphia soul took over the charts. Southern soul's legacy persists in soul, blues and Americana music, and in the "soul blues" circuit of the American South.
