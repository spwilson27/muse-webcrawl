Proto-Germanic language

Proto-Germanic (abbreviated PGmc; also called Common Germanic) is the reconstructed common ancestor of all Germanic languages, spoken in Iron Age northern Europe, roughly between 500 BCE and 500 CE. Because no written records survive, the language has been reconstructed by the comparative method from its descendants: the North Germanic languages (Old Norse and its descendants such as Icelandic and Norwegian), the West Germanic languages (including English, German and Dutch), and the extinct East Germanic branch attested mainly by Gothic.

Development from Indo-European

Proto-Germanic descends from Proto-Indo-European and is distinguished by several characteristic innovations. Grimm's law describes the systematic shift of the Indo-European stop consonants: voiceless stops became fricatives, voiced stops became voiceless, and voiced aspirated stops became plain voiced stops or fricatives. Verner's law explains exceptions in which voiceless fricatives were voiced when the preceding syllable was unstressed. Other hallmarks include a fixed stress accent on the first syllable of the word, the merger of the vowels a and o, and a reduction of the rich Indo-European tense system to two tenses, present and past.

Phonology

The consonant inventory included the fricatives *f, *þ (as in English thin), *s, *h and the voiced fricatives *ƀ, *ð, *ɣ, *z alongside nasals, liquids and the stops *p, *t, *k, *kʷ. The vowel system had short and long vowels *i, *e, *a, *o, *u and long *ī, *ē, *ā, *ō, *ū, plus diphthongs. Grammatical vowel alternation (ablaut), inherited from Indo-European, remained central to the strong verb system.

Morphology

Proto-Germanic nouns were inflected for three genders (masculine, feminine, neuter), two numbers and four cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, with an instrumental surviving in some branches), organised into stem classes such as a-stems, ō-stems and n-stems. Adjectives had both strong and weak declensions — an innovation linked to the development of a definite article-like demonstrative. Verbs divided into strong verbs forming the past tense by ablaut, weak verbs using a dental suffix (the ancestor of English -ed), and a small class of preterite-presents that produced the modern modal auxiliaries.

Legacy

Reconstructions of Proto-Germanic draw on runic inscriptions (the earliest Germanic writing, from about the 2nd century CE), the 4th-century Gothic Bible of Wulfila, and the earliest Old English, Old High German and Old Norse texts. See also Germanic languages, Grimm's law and comparative method.
