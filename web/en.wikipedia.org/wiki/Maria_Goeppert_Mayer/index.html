Maria Goeppert Mayer

Maria Goeppert Mayer

Died20 February 1972 (aged 65), San Diego, California, US
Known forNuclear shell model; two-photon absorption
AwardsNobel Prize in Physics (1963)
[Image: Photograph of Maria Goeppert Mayer at a blackboard of nuclear diagrams]
Goeppert Mayer in the 1960s.

Maria Goeppert Mayer (28 June 1906 – 20 February 1972) was a German-American theoretical physicist and Nobel laureate in Physics for her proposal of the nuclear shell model of the atomic nucleus — the second woman, after Marie Curie, to win the Physics Nobel (1963, shared with J. Hans D. Jensen and Eugene Wigner).

Working for much of her career without a paid position — anti-nepotism rules at the universities employing her husband confined her to 'volunteer' posts — she explained two-photon absorption in her 1931 Göttingen thesis (the unit of two-photon cross-section, the Goeppert Mayer, honours her), worked on isotope separation theory for the Manhattan Project, and in 1948–49, at Chicago and Argonne, recognised that nucleons fill shells like electrons, with strong spin–orbit coupling explaining the 'magic numbers' of especially stable nuclei (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126). Jensen reached the same model independently in Germany; they wrote the standard book together. She finished her career as a full professor at UC San Diego from 1960.

The shell model

The insight was to take the nuclear mean field seriously and add a large spin–orbit term — suggested, legend says, when Fermi casually asked whether there was evidence for spin–orbit coupling in nuclei. The magic numbers fell out at once, converting a catalogue of nuclear facts into a structure as legible as the periodic table Goeppert Mayer had been contemplating as the analogy.
