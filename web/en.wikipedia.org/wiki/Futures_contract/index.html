In finance, a futures contract (sometimes called futures) is a standardised legal contract to buy or sell something at a predetermined price for delivery at a specified time in the future, between parties not yet known to each other. The asset transacted is usually a commodity or financial instrument. The predetermined price at which the contract is agreed is called the futures price.

How futures work

Unlike a customised forward contract, futures are standardised by quantity, quality, and delivery date and are traded on an exchange, with a clearing house guaranteeing performance. Positions are marked to market daily: gains and losses are settled each day, and traders must post and maintain margin. Most positions are closed out before delivery, so futures serve mainly for hedging and speculation rather than physical exchange.

Uses

Producers and consumers of commodities — farmers, miners, airlines — hedge price risk, while speculators provide liquidity and take on that risk for profit. Financial futures on stock indices, currencies, and interest rates are among the most heavily traded contracts in the world.

History

Precursors include rice futures on Japan's Dōjima exchange in the 18th century. Modern futures trading grew from the Chicago Board of Trade (founded 1848) and the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, whose currency futures in 1972 launched the financial futures era.
