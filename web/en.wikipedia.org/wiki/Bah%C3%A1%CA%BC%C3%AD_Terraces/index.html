The Baháʼí Terraces, or the Hanging Gardens of Haifa, are garden terraces on Mount Carmel in Haifa, Israel, surrounding the Shrine of the Báb, one of the holiest sites of the Baháʼí Faith. The nineteen terraces — nine above the shrine and nine below, plus the shrine's own level — extend nearly a kilometre up the mountain and were designed by Iranian-born architect Fariborz Sahba. They were opened to the public in June 2001.

Design

The terraces were conceived as a giant necklace framing the golden-domed Shrine of the Báb, the resting place of the Báb, the faith's herald. Their flowing geometry combines stone and metal work with elaborate plantings, water features and gravel paths; irrigation is computer-controlled and uses recycled water. The design incorporates principles of light and shadow, with decorative lighting for the evening hours. Stones were cut in Italy, and the gardens' plantings were chosen for year-round colour against the buff-coloured Jerusalem stone.

World heritage

In July 2008 the Baháʼí Holy Places in Haifa and the Western Galilee, including the terraces and the Shrine of the Báb, were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List for their outstanding universal value as places of pilgrimage and for their architectural significance. The terraces are among Haifa's most visited landmarks and a symbol of the city.
