Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) is a method of digital modulation in which a high-rate data stream is split across many closely spaced, mutually orthogonal subcarriers, each modulated at a low symbol rate. Because the subcarriers are orthogonal, their spectra can overlap without interference, giving high spectral efficiency. A cyclic prefix copied from the end of each symbol guards against inter-symbol interference caused by multipath propagation.

Implementation and applications

OFDM is implemented efficiently with the fast Fourier transform: the transmitter uses an inverse FFT and the receiver an FFT. It underlies most modern broadband systems, including DSL, Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11a/g/n/ac/ax), WiMAX, LTE and 5G NR mobile networks, digital television (DVB-T), digital radio (DAB) and power-line communications. Its main drawbacks are a high peak-to-average power ratio and sensitivity to frequency offset and phase noise.

History

The concept originated in the 1960s with work by Robert Chang at Bell Labs, and the use of the discrete Fourier transform was described by Weinstein and Ebert in 1971. OFDM became practical with cheap DSP hardware in the 1990s and was popularised through DSL and early wireless LAN standards.
