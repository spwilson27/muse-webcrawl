Patio process

The patio process is a process for extracting silver from ore using mercury amalgamation. Invented by Bartolomé de Medina in Pachuca, Mexico, in 1554–1555, it was the first large-scale method for refining silver ore in the Americas and remained in use for over 300 years, underpinning the silver economy of colonial Spanish America.

Method

Crushed silver ore was spread in shallow layers on a paved courtyard (the patio), mixed with water, salt, copper sulfate (magistral) and mercury, and trodden — often by mules — for weeks. Chemical reactions converted silver minerals to metallic silver, which amalgamated with the mercury. The amalgam was washed, strained and heated to distil off the mercury, leaving silver. The process worked at ambient temperature but slowly, taking weeks to months depending on climate.

History and impact

The patio process made the great silver mines of Potosí, Zacatecas and Guanajuato profitable despite low-grade ores and scarce fuel. It consumed vast quantities of mercury from Huancavelica and Almadén, with severe health and environmental costs for workers and surrounding land. Variants included the cazo (hot amalgamation) process. The patio process was largely displaced in the late 19th century by cyanidation.
