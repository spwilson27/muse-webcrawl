Preterm birth, also known as premature birth, is the birth of a baby at fewer than 37 weeks gestational age, as opposed to full-term delivery at approximately 40 weeks. Extreme preterm is less than 28 weeks, very preterm is 28 to <32 weeks, early preterm is 32 to <34 weeks, late preterm is 34 to <37 weeks. Every year, about 15 million babies are born preterm — more than 1 in 10 of all births — and although survival has improved greatly, prematurity remains the leading cause of death in children under five.

Signs and complications

Preterm infants are at greater risk of cerebral palsy, delays in development, hearing problems, and problems with vision. The earlier a baby is born, the greater these risks. Complications include respiratory distress syndrome (from immature lungs lacking surfactant), intraventricular haemorrhage, necrotising enterocolitis, patent ductus arteriosus, retinopathy of prematurity, and sepsis. Signs of preterm labour include regular uterine contractions before 37 weeks, pelvic pressure, and cervical change.

Causes and risk factors

Often the cause of preterm birth is unknown. Risk factors include diabetes, high blood pressure, multiple gestation (twins or more), obesity or underweight, vaginal infections, tobacco smoking, and psychological stress, among others. A previous preterm birth is one of the strongest predictors. Preterm labour may follow preterm prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM), intrauterine infection, placental abruption, or be medically indicated (iatrogenic) because of conditions such as pre-eclampsia.

Prevention

In those at risk, the hormone progesterone, if taken during pregnancy, may prevent preterm birth, and a cervical cerclage (stitch) may help women with a short cervix. Bed rest, however, is not useful for preventing preterm birth and may have harms. Smoking cessation and treatment of infections reduce risk.

Treatment

Tocolytic drugs may delay delivery long enough for antenatal corticosteroids to mature the fetal lungs — one of the most effective interventions for improving outcomes. After birth, care includes keeping the baby warm (including kangaroo mother care), supporting breathing with CPAP or ventilation, administering surfactant, supporting feeding, and careful attention to infection. Care in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) has pushed the limit of viability down to around 22–24 weeks in high-resource settings.

Epidemiology

Globally, preterm birth affects between 5% and 18% of births depending on the country, with the highest rates in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Of the roughly one million annual deaths from preterm complications, most occur in low- and middle-income countries where simple measures — warmth, feeding support, infection treatment, and kangaroo care — could prevent many of them.
