Mixed strategy

In game theory, a mixed strategy is a strategy in which a player chooses among their pure strategies according to a probability distribution. It contrasts with a pure strategy, in which a single action is chosen with certainty.

Rationale

Mixing makes a player unpredictable and can make opponents indifferent between their own options. John Nash showed that every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium, possibly in mixed strategies. Classic examples are matching pennies and rock-paper-scissors, whose only equilibria are mixed, and penalty kicks in football, where kickers and goalkeepers randomise direction.

Interpretation

Mixed strategies can be interpreted literally as randomisation, as beliefs held by opponents, or — following John Harsanyi — as purification by small payoff perturbations.
