The pulmonary veins are the veins that carry oxygenated blood from the lungs back to the heart, an unusual role since most veins in the body carry deoxygenated blood toward the heart. They are large blood vessels that empty into the left atrium, completing the pulmonary circulation. Unlike most systemic veins, the pulmonary veins contain no valves.

Structure

There are normally four pulmonary veins, two arising from each lung: a superior and an inferior vein on each side. The right superior pulmonary vein drains the right upper and middle lobes, the right inferior vein drains the right lower lobe, the left superior vein drains the left upper lobe and lingula, and the left inferior vein drains the left lower lobe. Anatomical variation is common; in some individuals the veins share a common trunk, and accessory veins, particularly from the right middle lobe, may open separately into the left atrium.

The pulmonary veins begin as networks in the walls of the alveoli, coalesce into larger vessels within the lung parenchyma, and exit at the hilum of the lung. From there they pass medially, piercing the fibrous pericardium, and open into the upper posterior part of the left atrium. Their ostia on the posterior wall of the atrium are a frequent site of origin of ectopic electrical activity.

Function

Having picked up oxygen and released carbon dioxide in the pulmonary capillaries, blood travels through the pulmonary veins into the left atrium. From there it passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle, which pumps it into the aorta for distribution around the body. Together with the pulmonary artery, which carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs, the pulmonary veins form the two great vessel pairs of the pulmonary circuit.

Clinical significance

The myocardial sleeves extending into the pulmonary veins are the most common source of the abnormal electrical impulses that trigger atrial fibrillation. Catheter ablation procedures for this arrhythmia typically isolate the pulmonary veins electrically from the rest of the atrium. Pulmonary vein stenosis, a narrowing of the veins, can be a complication of such ablation or can occur congenitally. Anomalous pulmonary venous return is a congenital defect in which some or all of the pulmonary veins drain into the right side of the circulation instead of the left atrium; the total form (TAPVR) is a surgical emergency in newborns.
