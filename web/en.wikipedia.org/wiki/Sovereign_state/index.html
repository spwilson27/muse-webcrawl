Sovereign state

A sovereign state is a political entity with a permanent population, a defined territory, an effective government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states, exercising supreme authority within its territory free from external control. Sovereignty is the defining principle of the modern international system, conventionally traced to the Peace of Westphalia of 1648.

Criteria and recognition

The Montevideo Convention of 1933 codified the declarative criteria of statehood: permanent population, defined territory, government, and capacity for foreign relations. Under the rival constitutive theory, statehood depends on recognition by other states. In practice the two interact: entities such as Somaliland function as states without broad recognition, while widely recognised states may lack full control of their territory. Membership of the United Nations, currently numbering 193 member states plus two observer states, is the most general marker of sovereign status.

Sovereignty in practice

States may pool or limit their sovereignty by treaty — as European Union members do — without ceasing to be sovereign. Debates continue over humanitarian intervention, the responsibility to protect, and the status of microstates, dependencies and governments-in-exile.
