Martian calendar

A Martian calendar is any proposed system for tracking time on the planet Mars. Because Mars's solar day (the sol, about 24 hours 39 minutes) and year (about 668.6 sols, or 687 Earth days) differ from Earth's, colonists and scientists have devised various calendars adapted to Martian conditions. No single system has been officially adopted, though the Darian calendar is the most widely discussed proposal.

The sol and the Martian year

Mission teams at NASA simply count sols from landing, and Mars timekeeping for landed spacecraft uses local solar time divided into 24 "Mars hours" of slightly longer minutes and seconds. A Martian year lasts nearly twice as long as an Earth year, and because Mars's orbit is more eccentric, its seasons vary greatly in length, with northern spring the longest season.

The Darian calendar

The Darian calendar, proposed by aerospace engineer Thomas Gangale in 1985, divides the Martian year into 24 months of 28 or 27 sols, with six-day weeks that skip a weekday at the end of each month to stay aligned. Month names draw on Latin and Sanskrit names for the zodiacal constellations.

Other proposals

Other schemes include scientific systems based on areosynchronous units, the Mars Standard Time proposal, and simply extending the Gregorian calendar with adjusted units. Fictional calendars appear in the works of Edgar Rice Burroughs, Robert Zubrin, and Kim Stanley Robinson, whose Mars trilogy popularized timekeeping "stopping the clock" at midnight to absorb the extra 39 minutes.
