Calotype

The calotype, also called the Talbotype, was an early photographic process introduced by William Henry Fox Talbot in 1841. It used paper coated with silver iodide to create a negative image from which multiple positive prints could be made.

Process

Writing paper was iodized with potassium iodide and sensitized with silver nitrate ("gallic acid" development brought out a latent image). The paper negative was then contact-printed onto another sheet of salted paper to produce a positive. The fibers of the paper gave calotypes a soft, painterly quality distinct from the sharp daguerreotype.

Significance

Although Talbot's patents restricted its use in England, the calotype was practiced by photographers such as David Octavius Hill and Robert Adamson in Scotland. The negative–positive principle it established became the foundation of most photography until the digital era.
