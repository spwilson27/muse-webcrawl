Plaid

A plaid is a pattern of crossed horizontal and vertical bands in two or more colours, formed by weaving threads of different colours at right angles so that the bands overlap to produce new shades. In Scotland the pattern is properly called tartan, and each sett is associated with a clan, regiment, or district; in North America "plaid" is the everyday term for such patterned cloth.

Terminology

Historically, the plaid (Gaelic plaide) was the garment itself — a long length of woollen cloth worn belted around the body and thrown over the shoulder, as in the Highland great plaid (feileadh mòr). In modern usage outside Scotland, the word has transferred from the garment to the check pattern.

History

Checked woollen cloth is ancient in Scotland; the Falkirk tartan fragment dates to about the 3rd century CE. After the Jacobite rising of 1745, the Dress Act banned Highland dress until 1782. In the nineteenth century tartan was romanticized — notably during George IV's visit to Edinburgh in 1822 and under Queen Victoria — and clan setts multiplied, some of them documented, others invented by enterprising weavers and writers such as the Sobieski Stuarts.

Modern use

Plaid patterns are staples of flannel shirts, school uniforms, and fashion generally; famous commercial patterns include Burberry's camel check. Madder and windowpane are related but differently constructed patterns.
