Cork (material)

Cork is an impermeable, buoyant material harvested from the phellem layer of bark tissue of the cork oak (Quercus suber), a tree native to the western Mediterranean. It is composed of suberin, a hydrophobic substance, and its honeycomb structure of air-filled cells — some 40 million cells per cubic centimetre — gives it low density, elasticity, thermal and acoustic insulation, and resistance to moisture and decay. Cork has been used since antiquity for bottle stoppers, floats, footwear, and insulation.

Harvesting

Cork is harvested by stripping the outer bark from living trees without felling them. The first stripping (virgin cork) takes place when the tree is about 25 years old; thereafter the bark regrows and can be stripped roughly every nine years, with the best quality "reproduction cork" coming from the third and later harvests. A harvested tree may live and produce for 150–200 years. Portugal accounts for roughly half of world production, followed by Spain; the montado and dehesa agroforestry systems in which cork oaks grow are also important wildlife habitats, including for the Iberian lynx and imperial eagle.

Uses

The highest-value use is natural wine stoppers, punched from thick bark planks; pierced, lower-grade, and waste cork is granulated and moulded into agglomerated products including technical stoppers, flooring, wall tiles, shoe soles and wedges, fishing floats, gaskets, bulletin boards, and insulation board. Cork's elasticity and heat resistance led to its use as nose-cone and ablative material on spacecraft, and cork-composite materials appear in sports equipment (table-tennis blades, badminton shuttlecocks, baseball cores) and musical instruments (pads and joints on woodwinds). Wine closures remain contested territory: natural cork is associated with a small incidence of "corked" wine from the compound 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), which spurred the adoption of synthetic stoppers and screw caps, but cork producers have greatly reduced TCA incidence and emphasize cork's carbon-retention and sustainability credentials.

Properties

Cork's closed cells make it nearly impermeable to liquids and gases, very light (density about 0.12–0.20 g/cm³), and highly compressible with near-complete elastic recovery — the last two properties explain its excellence as a bottle stopper, sealing by expansion against the neck. It is also a slow conductor of heat and a poor transmitter of sound and vibration, and it does not support combustion easily, charring rather than flaming.
