Tent rock

A tent rock is a conical or tent-shaped rock formation produced by differential erosion, in which a cap of harder, more resistant rock protects a column or cone of softer material beneath it. Tent rocks are closely related to hoodoos and earth pyramids, differing mainly in their smooth, cone-like form, which often resembles a Native American tipi or army tent.

Formation

Tent rocks typically form in layered volcanic or sedimentary deposits of contrasting hardness. Rain wash and wind erode the softer layers — for example, weakly cemented pumice and tuff — while boulders or layers of harder rock armour the material directly below them, leaving a cone that tapers as erosion proceeds. When the protective capstone finally falls, the exposed cone erodes rapidly.

Examples

The type examples in the United States are found at Kasha-Katuwe Tent Rocks National Monument in north-central New Mexico, where cone-shaped formations up to about 90 feet (27 m) high were carved into pumice and tuff deposits from eruptions of the Jemez volcanic field roughly 6 to 7 million years ago; the monument lies on the Pueblo de Cochiti reservation and includes a slot canyon trail. Similar tent-like cones occur among the fairy chimneys of Cappadocia in Turkey and the earth pyramids of northern Italy.
