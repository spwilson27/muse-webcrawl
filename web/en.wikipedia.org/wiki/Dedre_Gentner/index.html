Dedre Gentner (born 1944) is an American cognitive psychologist and cognitive scientist. She is the Alice Gabrielle Twight Professor of Psychology Emerita at Northwestern University. Gentner is best known for developing structure-mapping theory, an influential account of analogy and similarity, and for her research on language acquisition, especially how children learn verbs and relational words.

Career

Gentner earned her bachelor's degree in physics from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1967 and her PhD in psychology from the University of California, San Diego, in 1974. She worked at Bolt, Beranek and Newman before joining Northwestern University, where she helped build its cognitive science program. She has been elected to the National Academy of Sciences and the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, and received the Rumelhart Prize for contributions to the theoretical foundations of human cognition.

Structure-mapping theory

Structure-mapping theory (introduced in 1983) holds that analogy works by aligning the relational structure of two representations: an analogy maps relations between objects in a base domain onto a target domain, while largely discarding the objects' surface attributes. The theory emphasizes systematicity — people prefer mappings that form deeply interconnected relational systems. With colleagues, Gentner implemented the theory computationally in the Structure-Mapping Engine (SME), developed with Ken Forbus. The framework reshaped research on analogy, metaphor, and conceptual change in science education.

Other research

Gentner's work on language includes the "noun bias" debate — why children learn nouns earlier than verbs — arguing that verbs are relationally complex and vary more across languages. Her research on analogical encoding (comparing examples) shows that side-by-side comparison helps learners abstract schemas and transfer knowledge. She has also studied spatial language, mental models of physical systems, and how metaphor shapes thought.
