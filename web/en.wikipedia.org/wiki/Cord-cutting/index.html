Cord-cutting

In broadcast television, cord-cutting refers to the pattern of viewers, referred to as cord-cutters, cancelling their subscriptions to multichannel television services available over cable or satellite, dropping pay television in favor of alternate content delivered over the public Internet via streaming video services, or via free over-the-air antenna reception. The related phenomenon of cord-shaving describes subscribers who downgrade to cheaper, smaller channel packages, while cord-nevers are consumers — disproportionately younger adults — who have never subscribed to pay television at all.

Causes

Analysts attribute cord-cutting chiefly to the rising cost of cable and satellite bundles, whose monthly bills in the United States climbed past an average of $100 in the 2010s, alongside the growth of subscription video-on-demand services such as Netflix, Hulu, Amazon Prime Video, and later Disney+ and HBO Max. High-speed broadband made streaming a plausible substitute, and competition among streaming services temporarily kept their combined cost below that of a traditional bundle for many households, particularly those uninterested in live sports — historically the content category most resistant to cord-cutting because of long-term rights contracts with regional sports networks and leagues.

Industry response

Distributors responded with "skinny bundles" of fewer channels at lower prices, internet-delivered virtual MVPD services such as Sling TV, YouTube TV, and Hulu + Live TV, and consolidation of media companies to control both content and distribution. Programmers also launched their own direct-to-consumer platforms. As streaming prices rose and content fragmented among many services in the early 2020s, commentators noted signs of "cord-cutting fatigue", churn among subscribers cycling between services, and the re-bundling of streaming offers — though the long-term decline of traditional pay-TV subscriptions continued, with U.S. pay-TV households falling from a peak of roughly 100 million around 2012 to well under 70 million a decade later.

Impact

Cord-cutting has pressured the economics of cable networks dependent on per-subscriber carriage fees and advertising simultaneously, accelerated the collapse of regional sports networks into bankruptcy in some markets, and pushed sports leagues to sell streaming rights as a complement to broadcast deals. Local television news and broadcast networks have been partially insulated because antennas and streaming bundles still carry them in many markets.
