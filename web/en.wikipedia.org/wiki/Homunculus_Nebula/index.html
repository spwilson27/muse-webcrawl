Homunculus Nebula


The Homunculus Nebula surrounding Eta Carinae

The Homunculus Nebula is a bipolar emission and reflection nebula surrounding the massive star system Eta Carinae, in the constellation Carina. The nebula was created during an outburst of Eta Carinae known as the Great Eruption, which occurred between 1841 and 1847, when material ejected from the star formed two expanding polar lobes and a thin equatorial disk.

Description

The word homunculus is Latin for "little man," a name given because of the nebula's shape, which resembles a small human figure with a head, body, and legs. The nebula is roughly 0.11 light-years (about 6,700 astronomical units) across and is expanding at speeds of up to 650 km/s. Its total mass is estimated at 10 to 40 solar masses. Nearly all of the material was ejected during the Great Eruption of the 1840s, making the Homunculus one of the best-studied products of a luminous blue variable outburst.

Structure

The nebula consists of two polar lobes of gas and dust that expand outward from the central star, plus an equatorial "skirt" of material. The lobes contain dust that reflects the light of the central star, whose direct view is largely obscured by the equatorial disk. Embedded within the larger nebula is a smaller structure nicknamed the "Little Homunculus," thought to have been produced by a later, smaller eruption around 1890.

Observation

Because Eta Carinae lies about 7,500 light-years away, the Homunculus appears small in telescopes, but its lobes can be distinguished in good seeing. Images from the Hubble Space Telescope revealed fine filamentary structure in the lobes and knots of material moving at high velocity. Spectroscopic studies show the lobes are rich in nitrogen, a product of CNO-cycle processing in the massive star.
