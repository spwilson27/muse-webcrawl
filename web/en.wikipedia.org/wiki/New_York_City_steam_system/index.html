New York City steam system

The New York City steam system is a district heating network that distributes steam through about 105 miles (169 km) of mains beneath the streets of Manhattan, serving more than 1,500 buildings — hospitals, museums, office towers and apartment houses — for heating, cooling, sterilization and other uses. Operated by Consolidated Edison, it is the largest district steam system in the United States.

History

The system traces to 1882, when the New York Steam Company began supplying steam in lower Manhattan, a few years after birds-eye experiments with central steam in other American cities. Con Edison acquired the network in the 1930s. Steam is generated at several large plants, including the East River Station, largely through cogeneration that also produces electricity, improving overall efficiency.

Features and incidents

The orange-and-white funnels placed over manholes on city streets vent steam that naturally condenses in the system. The network has suffered notable failures, including a 1989 explosion in Gramercy Park and the 2007 steam pipe explosion near Grand Central Terminal, which scattered debris and asbestos-containing material. Despite its age, the system avoids the need for individual boilers in many of the city's largest buildings.
