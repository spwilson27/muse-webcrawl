The Plain of Jars is a megalithic archaeological landscape in central Laos, in Xiangkhouang Province, where thousands of huge stone jars are scattered across more than ninety sites on the plateau around Phonsavan. The jars, carved from sandstone, granite and other rock, date to the Iron Age and are among Southeast Asia's most enigmatic monuments.

Description

The jars range from one to three metres in height and up to a metre or more in diameter; the largest weighs many tonnes. Some sites have only a few jars; others, such as Site 1 (Ban Ang), have several hundred. A cylindrical stone quarry at Phu Salato has been identified as a source. Most jars are plain, though some bear carved discs, figures or lids.

Purpose

Local legend attributes the jars to a race of giants, or to a victorious king who had them made as vessels for rice wine. Archaeological research, beginning with Madeleine Colani of the École française d'Extrême-Orient in the 1930s, established an association with burials: human remains, burial goods, beads and bronze and iron objects have been found around the jars, and they are generally interpreted as funerary urns — possibly used to expose or store remains before secondary burial. Research published in recent years has shown the sites were used over many centuries.

Modern history

The region was heavily bombed during the Vietnam War-era "Secret War" in Laos; unexploded ordnance still litters many sites and only a few have been cleared and opened to visitors. In 2019 the "Megalithic Jar Sites in Xiengkhuang — Plain of Jars" were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
