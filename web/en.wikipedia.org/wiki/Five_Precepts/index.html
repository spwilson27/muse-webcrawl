Five Precepts

The Five Precepts (pañca-sīla)
1Refrain from taking life
2Refrain from taking what is not given
3Refrain from sexual misconduct
4Refrain from false speech
5Refrain from intoxicants that cloud the mind
TraditionBuddhism (lay practice)

The Five Precepts (Sanskrit: pañcaśīla; Pali: pañcasīla) are the most important system of morality for Buddhist lay people. They constitute the basic code of ethics undertaken by lay followers of Buddhism, usually recited as voluntary undertakings — "I undertake the training rule to refrain from…" — rather than as commandments imposed by a deity. Taking the precepts is a regular part of lay Buddhist practice in both Theravada and Mahayana traditions, often in the same ceremony as taking refuge in the Three Jewels.


Lay devotees receiving the precepts

Content

The precepts prohibit killing living beings, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying and consuming intoxicating substances that cause heedlessness. In modern interpretations they are also understood positively — as commitments to protect life, practise generosity, respect relationships, speak truthfully and kindly, and consume mindfully. On observance days (uposatha), devout laypeople may undertake the stricter Eight Precepts, and monastics follow the much larger Vinaya codes of 227 or more rules.

Role in Buddhist ethics

The precepts are understood as practical training rules that protect others from harm and support the mental clarity needed for meditation and the development of wisdom. Breaking a precept is not punished by a god but is held to produce unwholesome karma. Scholars compare the Five Precepts to similar ethical lists in Jainism and Hinduism, with which early Buddhism shared the central value of ahimsa (non-violence), and to the moral codes of other world religions.
