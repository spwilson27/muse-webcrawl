Perovskite is a crystal structure type with the general formula ABX3, first described for the mineral calcium titanate (CaTiO3), discovered in the Ural Mountains in 1839 and named after Russian mineralogist Lev Perovski. A vast family of compounds adopts this structure or distorted versions of it, giving rise to many technologically important materials.

Structure

In the ideal cubic structure, the larger A cation sits at the cube corners (12-fold coordinated), the smaller B cation at the body centre, and the X anions (often oxygen or halides) at the face centres, forming corner-sharing BX6 octahedra. Whether a given composition fits the structure is estimated by the Goldschmidt tolerance factor; deviations produce tilting of the octahedra and lower-symmetry (orthorhombic, tetragonal, rhombohedral) variants, which strongly affect physical properties.

Properties and applications

Perovskite oxides display ferroelectricity (BaTiO3), superconductivity (the cuprate high-temperature superconductors derive from layered perovskite structures), colossal magnetoresistance (manganites), piezoelectricity (PZT) and ionic conductivity. In Earth's interior, bridgmanite — a silicate perovskite — is the most abundant mineral in the lower mantle. Since 2009, lead-halide perovskites such as methylammonium lead iodide have become leading materials for low-cost, high-efficiency solar cells, with certified efficiencies rivalling silicon, though stability and lead content remain challenges.
