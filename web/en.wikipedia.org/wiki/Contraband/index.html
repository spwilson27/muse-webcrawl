Contraband

Contraband (from Medieval Latin contrabandum, from contra, "against", and bando, "proclamation") refers to any goods that are illegal to possess, trade, import or export under the laws of the relevant jurisdiction. Contraband includes goods that are inherently illegal — such as illicit narcotics or unlicensed weapons — and goods that are lawful in themselves but are being moved in violation of customs duties, embargoes, quotas, sanctions or wartime restrictions. Trade in contraband is smuggling.

In wartime

In international law, contraband traditionally meant goods carried by neutral shipping that a belligerent may seize because they assist the enemy's war effort. The Declaration of London (1909), though never ratified, distinguished absolute contraband (arms and munitions, seizable whatever their destination) from conditional contraband (dual-use goods such as food and fuel, seizable only when destined for the enemy's armed forces). The doctrine shaped naval blockades and prize law in both world wars; the corresponding enforcement practice included visit-and-search of neutral ships and, controversially, long-distance blockades. The 1994 San Remo Manual restated the law of armed conflict at sea, limiting contraband to goods ultimately destined for enemy-controlled territory that are usable in armed conflict.

Other uses

In the American Civil War, "contrabands" was the term applied by Union general Benjamin Butler in 1861 to enslaved people who escaped to Union lines at Fort Monroe; reasoning that the Confederacy treated them as property used for war work, Butler refused to return them as "contraband of war", a legal fiction that encouraged thousands to seek refuge and pushed federal policy toward emancipation. In modern usage the word also denotes prohibited items in prisons, schools and other controlled environments, from drugs and phones to weapons.
