Invention of the telephone

The invention of the telephone was the culmination of work done by many individuals, and was the subject of a long series of patent disputes. The first telephone patent, United States patent 174,465, was awarded to Alexander Graham Bell in March 1876; days later Bell transmitted the first intelligible sentence — "Mr. Watson — come here — I want to see you" — to his assistant Thomas Watson.

Contributors and claimants

Precursors included Innocenzo Manzetti, Charles Bourseul, and Johann Philipp Reis, whose "Reis telephone" of 1861 transmitted tones and some speech-like sounds. Antonio Meucci demonstrated voice communication devices in the 1850s and filed a patent caveat in 1871 that he could not afford to renew; in 2002 the U.S. House of Representatives passed a resolution recognising his work. Elisha Gray filed a caveat for a telephone design on the same day Bell's application was filed in February 1876, a coincidence that fuelled decades of litigation and claims of improper influence at the patent office.

Priority disputes

Bell's patents were challenged in hundreds of lawsuits, but the Bell Telephone Company ultimately prevailed, including in the U.S. Supreme Court's Telephone Cases of 1888. Historians continue to debate the contributions of each claimant, noting that practical telephony also depended on later improvements such as Thomas Edison's carbon microphone and the development of exchanges and switching.
