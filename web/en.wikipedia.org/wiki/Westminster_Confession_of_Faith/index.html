The Westminster Confession of Faith is a Reformed confession of faith drawn up in 1646 by the Westminster Assembly, a council of English and Scottish theologians and laymen convened by the Long Parliament during the English Civil War. Together with the Westminster Larger and Shorter Catechisms, it forms the Westminster Standards, the doctrinal standard of Presbyterian churches worldwide, subordinate only to the Bible.

History

The Assembly met at Westminster Abbey from 1643, initially to reform the Church of England. Scottish commissioners, admitted under the Solemn League and Covenant, pressed the Assembly toward Presbyterian church government. The Confession was completed in December 1646 and approved by the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland in 1647. Although the restoration of Charles II prevented its permanent establishment in England, it was adopted by Presbyterians in Scotland, Ireland, and the American colonies, where the Synod of Philadelphia adopted it in 1729.

Contents

Its 33 chapters treat the Holy Scripture, God and the Trinity, God's eternal decree (including a strong doctrine of predestination), creation, providence, the Fall, God's covenant, Christ the Mediator, justification, sanctification, the church, the sacraments, church government and discipline, and the last judgment. Distinctive teachings include the sole authority of Scripture, covenant theology, the regulative principle of worship, and a strict Sabbatarian view of the Lord's Day — the last softened in the 1788 American revision, which also removed the identification of the Pope as Antichrist and revised the chapters on the civil magistrate.

Influence

The Confession shaped later statements of faith, including the Congregationalist Savoy Declaration (1658) and the Baptist London Confession of 1689, and remains the confessional basis of denominations such as the Church of Scotland, the Presbyterian Church in America, and the Orthodox Presbyterian Church.
