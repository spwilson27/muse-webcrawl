Paternity leave

Paternity leave is leave from employment granted to a father (or the partner of a mother) around the birth or adoption of a child, allowing time to care for the newborn and support the other parent. It is a component of parental leave policies and differs from maternity leave, which applies to the mother and includes recovery from childbirth.

Provision worldwide

Provision varies greatly between countries. The Nordic countries pioneered "father's quotas" — non-transferable portions of parental leave reserved for the second parent — beginning with Norway in 1993 and Sweden in 1995, policies credited with greatly increasing fathers' take-up of leave. Sweden's system provides 480 days of parental leave per child, of which 90 days are reserved for each parent. In contrast, the United States has no federal paid paternity leave, though the Family and Medical Leave Act provides up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave for eligible workers, and several states operate paid family leave schemes.

Effects and debate

Research associates paternity leave with increased father involvement in child care, improved outcomes for maternal health, and more equitable sharing of domestic labor, though cultural norms and workplace stigma can suppress uptake. Critics of generous mandates raise employer costs, while advocates argue that reserved, well-paid leave is essential to changing norms. International organizations such as the OECD and ILO track and encourage provision.
