Dutch colonization of the Americas

The Dutch colonization of the Americas began in the early 17th century, principally under the Dutch West India Company (chartered 1621). Unlike the mass settler colonies of some rivals, Dutch ventures were primarily commercial, built around trade in furs, sugar, salt and enslaved Africans.

New Netherland

The colony of New Netherland occupied parts of the present-day US East Coast, centred on New Amsterdam at the tip of Manhattan, founded in the 1620s. It traded furs from the Hudson Valley but remained sparsely settled. England seized the colony in 1664, briefly lost it in 1673, and retained it permanently by the Treaty of Westminster (1674), in exchange for which the Dutch kept Suriname.

The Caribbean and South America

The Dutch captured or settled several Caribbean islands — Curaçao, Aruba, Bonaire, Sint Eustatius, Saba and part of Sint Maarten — which remained Dutch. Between 1630 and 1654 the West India Company held a sugar-producing colony in northeastern Brazil (Dutch Brazil) before being expelled by the Portuguese. On the Guiana coast the Dutch founded colonies that developed into Suriname, which became independent in 1975.

Legacy

Dutch influence survives in Caribbean territories of the Kingdom, in Suriname's official language and legal traditions, and in New York-area place names and cultural traces of New Netherland such as Harlem, Brooklyn and the Bronx, derived from Dutch names.
