Stereotypic movement disorder

Stereotypic movement disorder is a motor disorder characterised by repetitive, seemingly driven, and apparently purposeless motor behaviour. Examples include hand shaking or waving, body rocking, head banging, self-biting, hitting one's own body, and picking at skin or body orifices. The behaviour interferes with normal activities or results in self-inflicted bodily injury that requires medical treatment (or would result in injury if preventive measures were not used).

Diagnosis

In the DSM-5, stereotypic movement disorder is classified among the neurodevelopmental motor disorders, alongside coordination disorder and tic disorders. Diagnosis requires that the repetitive movements interfere with social, academic, or other activities, or cause self-injury; that onset occurs in the early developmental period; and that the behaviour is not better explained by another neurological or medical condition, a substance, or another mental disorder such as trichotillomania or obsessive-compulsive disorder. Specifiers indicate whether the behaviour is self-injurious and whether it is associated with another medical or neurodevelopmental condition, such as intellectual disability or Lesch–Nyhan syndrome.

Causes and associations

Stereotypies occur in typically developing children — primary motor stereotypies such as thumb-sucking and nail-biting are common and usually resolve — but persistent, impairing stereotypies are most often seen in association with intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, and genetic syndromes including Rett syndrome and Lesch–Nyhan syndrome. In adults with intellectual disability, prevalence estimates range from a few percent to as high as 40% in institutional settings. Neurochemical hypotheses implicate dopaminergic pathways in the basal ganglia, and stereotypies can also be induced or worsened by stimulant medications and dopamine agonists.

Treatment

There is no universally effective medication for stereotypic movement disorder. Behavioural interventions — including habit-reversal training, differential reinforcement of other behaviour, and environmental enrichment — have the strongest evidence base. Where movements are self-injurious, protective equipment and, in severe cases, pharmacotherapy with agents such as atypical antipsychotics, clonidine, or naltrexone may be considered, although evidence is limited. Treatment of comorbid conditions (such as ADHD or anxiety) can also reduce the behaviour.

Differential diagnosis

Stereotypies must be distinguished from tics, which are typically preceded by a premonitory urge and are briefly suppressible; from compulsions, which are performed in response to an obsession or rigid rules; and from paroxysmal dyskinesias and seizure activity. Stereotypies usually begin before age three, are rhythmic and fixed in form, last longer than tics, and characteristically stop when the child's attention is engaged.
