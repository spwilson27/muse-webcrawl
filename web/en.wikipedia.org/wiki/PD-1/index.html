Programmed cell death protein 1, also known as PD-1 and CD279 (cluster of differentiation 279), is a protein on the surface of T and B cells that has a role in regulating the immune system's response to the cells of the human body by down-regulating the immune system and promoting self-tolerance by suppressing T cell inflammatory activity. It is an immune checkpoint and guards against autoimmunity through two mechanisms, including promoting apoptosis of antigen-specific T cells in lymph nodes and reducing apoptosis of regulatory T cells.

Discovery

PD-1 was discovered in 1992 by Tasuku Honjo and colleagues at Kyoto University, who cloned the gene while screening for molecules involved in programmed cell death — the origin of its name, although its principal role proved to be immune inhibition rather than cell death itself. Honjo's group later showed that PD-1-deficient mice develop lupus-like autoimmune disease, and identified its ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2. For the discovery of cancer therapy by inhibition of negative immune regulation, Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with James P. Allison.

Function

PD-1 is expressed on activated T cells, B cells and myeloid cells. When it binds PD-L1 or PD-L2, its cytoplasmic ITIM and ITSM motifs recruit the phosphatase SHP-2, which attenuates T-cell receptor and CD28 signaling, reducing T-cell proliferation, cytokine production and cytotoxic activity. Many tumors exploit this pathway by overexpressing PD-L1, thereby "switching off" anti-tumor T cells in their microenvironment; chronic infection similarly drives PD-1-high "exhausted" T-cell states.

Clinical significance

Monoclonal antibodies that block PD-1 — notably nivolumab and pembrolizumab — or its ligand PD-L1 (atezolizumab, durvalumab, avelumab) release this brake and have transformed the treatment of melanoma, non-small-cell lung cancer, renal cell carcinoma, Hodgkin lymphoma, head and neck cancer and many other malignancies, producing durable responses in a subset of patients. Because they unleash immune activity, these checkpoint inhibitors can also cause immune-related adverse events such as colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis and endocrinopathies. PD-L1 expression on tumors and other biomarkers are used, imperfectly, to predict response.
