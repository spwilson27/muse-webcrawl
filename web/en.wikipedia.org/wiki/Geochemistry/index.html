Geochemistry is the science that uses the tools and principles of chemistry to explain the mechanisms behind major geological systems such as the Earth's crust and its oceans. It studies the abundance, distribution and migration of chemical elements and their isotopes in rocks, minerals, waters, the atmosphere and living matter, and applies this knowledge to the origin and evolution of the Earth and other planets.

Scope and subfields

Major branches include isotope geochemistry, which uses radiogenic isotopes for geochronology (dating rocks and events) and stable isotopes as tracers of processes such as climate change and biological metabolism; igneous and metamorphic geochemistry, which uses trace-element and isotope signatures to trace magmas to their sources; aqueous and environmental geochemistry, covering water chemistry, weathering and pollution; organic geochemistry, studying the fate of organic compounds in sediments and petroleum formation; biogeochemistry, on element cycles through living systems; and cosmochemistry, the study of the chemical composition of the universe and meteorites.

History

The term was coined by Christian Friedrich Schönbein in 1838. The discipline matured with Frank Wigglesworth Clarke's compilations of average crustal composition (The Data of Geochemistry, 1908) and was transformed by Victor Moritz Goldschmidt, whose work in the 1920s–30s on element distribution between mineral phases founded modern crystal chemistry and geochemistry. Vladimir Vernadsky pioneered biogeochemistry, and Clair Patterson's lead-isotope work gave the age of the Earth (about 4.55 billion years) while exposing global lead contamination.
