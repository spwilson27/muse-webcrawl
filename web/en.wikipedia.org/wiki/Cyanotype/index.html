Cyanotype

Cyanotype
InventorJohn Herschel (1842)

The cyanotype (from Ancient Greek κυάνεος, kyáneos "dark blue" and τύπος, týpos "mark, impression") is a slow-reacting, economical photographic printing formulation sensitive to a limited near-ultraviolet and blue light spectrum. The process, invented by Sir John Herschel in 1842, produces a cyan-blue print through the formation of the pigment Prussian blue.

Cyanotype is often used for contact printing and photograms. For over a century it was the standard way of reproducing technical drawings — the origin of the term blueprint.

History

Polymath John Herschel devised the process in 1842 while searching for a way to copy his notes. The botanist Anna Atkins adopted it almost immediately, producing Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions (1843), generally regarded as the first book illustrated with photographs. Herschel also coined the photographic terms "positive" and "negative".

Process

A solution of ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide is coated onto paper or cloth and dried in the dark. Objects or a negative are placed on the sensitized surface and exposed to ultraviolet light (traditionally sunlight). Exposed areas turn deep blue as ferric iron is reduced and reacts to form Prussian blue; washing in water removes the unexposed, soluble chemicals, leaving a fixed blue image. Exposure and development take minutes, and prints can be toned with substances such as tannic acid or tea to alter the blue.

Blueprints

Engineers adopted cyanotype for reprography because it was cheap, large-format, and produced exact copies of drawings on translucent paper. "Blueprint" became a generic word for a technical plan long after diazo and xerographic processes displaced the original chemistry.

Artistic use

Contemporary artists and printmakers value cyanotype for its simplicity, safety, and capacity for large-scale and fabric work. It remains one of the most accessible alternative photographic processes, frequently taught in schools.
