Transient equilibrium

In nuclear physics, transient equilibrium is a situation in which equilibrium is reached by a parent–daughter radioactive decay chain, but the amount of daughter activity exceeds that of the parent. It contrasts with secular equilibrium, in which the parent is so long-lived that the activities of parent and daughter become equal.

Conditions

Transient equilibrium occurs when the half-life of the parent nuclide is longer than that of the daughter, but not so much longer that the parent's decay is negligible over the timescale of interest. After a sufficient number of daughter half-lives have elapsed, the daughter activity grows to a maximum, passes it, and thereafter both parent and daughter decay together with the half-life of the parent, maintaining a constant ratio in which the daughter activity is somewhat greater than the parent's.

Examples

A widely used example is the molybdenum-99 / technetium-99m generator used in nuclear medicine: molybdenum-99, with a half-life of about 66 hours, decays to technetium-99m, with a half-life of about 6 hours. After roughly a day, the pair reach transient equilibrium, allowing the daughter to be repeatedly eluted ("milked") from the generator as the parent continues to produce it.
