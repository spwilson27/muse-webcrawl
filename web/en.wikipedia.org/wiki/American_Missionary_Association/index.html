The American Missionary Association (AMA) was a Protestant missionary society founded in 1846 in Albany, New York, by abolitionists. It grew out of the movement that had defended the Africans of the slave ship Amistad, and it combined evangelical missions with a firm commitment to the abolition of slavery, refusing contributions from slaveholders.

Reconstruction work

After the American Civil War, the AMA became one of the most important organisations educating formerly enslaved people in the South. It sent hundreds of teachers into the region and founded or supported schools and colleges that endure today, including Fisk University, Hampton University, Atlanta University, Howard University (with the Freedmen's Bureau), Talladega College and Tougaloo College.

Other fields

Beyond the South, the AMA sponsored missions among Native Americans, in China, and in other overseas fields, and it supported civil rights causes into the twentieth century. Its work among freedpeople made it a central institution of Reconstruction-era education.

Legacy

The AMA eventually became part of the organisational lineage of the United Church of Christ, into which its functions were absorbed during the twentieth century. The colleges it founded remain among the most prominent historically black colleges and universities in the United States.
