Syncopation

Syncopation places rhythmic emphasis where a listener does not expect it.

In music, syncopation is a variety of rhythms played together to make a piece of music, making part or all of a tune or piece of music off-beat. More simply, syncopation is "a disturbance or interruption of the regular flow of rhythm": a "placement of rhythmic stresses or accents where they wouldn't normally occur".

Syncopation can be understood as the art of dividing the beats in a measure in such a way that it contradicts the regular grouping of the beat. It involves stressing normally unaccented beats, omitting expected strong beats, or prolonging notes over bar lines so that accents fall in unexpected places.

Types

Common forms include suspension, in which a note tied over a bar line delays resolution; off-beat accentuation, in which weak beats or subdivisions receive emphasis; anticipated bass, a pattern common in Latin American dance music where the bass note sounds slightly before the beat; and missed-beat syncopation, where a rest replaces an expected strong accent.

History and use

Syncopation has been an element of European music since the Middle Ages, but it became central to many African-American musical traditions and the genres derived from them, including ragtime, jazz, blues, funk, reggae, and hip hop. Ragtime's characteristic "ragged time" piano style is built on heavily syncopated melody against a steady march-like bass.
