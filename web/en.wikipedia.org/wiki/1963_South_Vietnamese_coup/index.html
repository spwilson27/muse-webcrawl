The 1963 South Vietnamese coup was a coup d'état carried out by generals of the Army of the Republic of Vietnam (ARVN) on 1 November 1963, overthrowing President Ngô Đình Diệm, the first president of the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam). The coup culminated in the assassination of Diệm and his younger brother and chief adviser, Ngô Đình Nhu, on 2 November, and installed a military junta headed by General Dương Văn Minh.

Background

Diệm's rule had become increasingly unpopular by 1963. His regime favoured the country's Catholic minority in a majority-Buddhist nation, and the Buddhist crisis of 1963 — sparked by the shooting of nine unarmed Buddhist civilians in Huế and the self-immolation of the monk Thích Quảng Đức in Saigon — drew international condemnation. The violent Xá Lợi Pagoda raids of August 1963, carried out by Nhu's Special Forces, convinced the United States that Diệm could not be persuaded to reform. American officials, including Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., signalled through contacts such as the CIA's Lucien Conein that Washington would not thwart a coup by dissident generals.

The coup

On the afternoon of 1 November 1963, rebel units loyal to the generals seized key installations in Saigon, including the radio station, Tan Son Nhut Air Base and police headquarters. Fighting centred on the Gia Long Palace, defended by the Presidential Guard. Diệm and Nhu escaped through a secret tunnel to a safe house in Cholon, but were captured the following morning after attending Mass at St. Francis Xavier Church. While being transported in an M113 armoured personnel carrier, the brothers were shot and bayoneted by Captain Nguyễn Văn Nhung, an aide to General Minh.

Aftermath

A Military Revolutionary Council under Dương Văn Minh took power, dissolving the National Assembly and suspending the 1956 constitution. The coup was celebrated in the streets of Saigon, but the junta proved short-lived: Minh was himself overthrown by General Nguyễn Khánh in January 1964, beginning a period of revolving-door military governments. The United States, under President John F. Kennedy — himself assassinated three weeks later — deepened its involvement in the Vietnam War in the coup's wake.
