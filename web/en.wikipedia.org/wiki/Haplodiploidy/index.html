Haplodiploidy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In haplodiploid species, fertilised eggs become diploid females and unfertilised eggs become haploid males.

Haplodiploidy is a sex-determination system in which males develop from unfertilised eggs and are haploid, while females develop from fertilised eggs and are diploid. The system is characteristic of the insect order Hymenoptera — ants, bees and wasps — and occurs in roughly 20 percent of animal species, including some beetles, thrips, whiteflies, scale insects, mites and rotifers.

Mechanism

A female haplodiploid produces eggs by meiosis, as in other animals. If an egg is fertilised by sperm stored in her spermatheca, it restores the diploid chromosome number and develops into a female. If it is not fertilised, it develops parthenogenetically into a male carrying only the maternal genome. Males therefore have no father and cannot have sons, though they can have grandsons through their daughters. Because a male is haploid, his sperm are produced by a modified mitosis rather than meiosis, so all his sperm are genetically identical.

In honey bees, sex is determined at the csd (complementary sex determiner) locus: heterozygotes develop as females, hemizygotes as males, and rare diploid homozygotes as diploid males, which workers usually destroy.

Relatedness and social evolution

Haplodiploidy produces striking asymmetries of genetic relatedness. A female shares on average three-quarters of her genes with her full sisters (r = 0.75), because sisters inherit an identical paternal genome plus half of their maternal genome, whereas she would share only half her genes with her own daughters. W. D. Hamilton proposed in 1964 that this elevated sister–sister relatedness helps explain the repeated evolution of eusociality and sterile worker castes in the Hymenoptera, through kin selection.

The haplodiploidy hypothesis has since been qualified: many haplodiploid species are solitary, some eusocial lineages (such as termites) are diploid, and lifetime monogamy in ancestral hymenopterans may have been equally important. Haplodiploidy also allows mothers precise control over offspring sex ratios by choosing whether to fertilise each egg, and it exposes recessive deleterious alleles to selection in haploid males, purging them from the population.
