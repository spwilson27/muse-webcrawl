Leaf spring

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A leaf spring is a simple form of spring commonly used for suspension in wheeled vehicles. Originally called a laminated or carriage spring, and sometimes referred to as a semi-elliptical spring, elliptical spring, or cart spring, it is one of the oldest forms of vehicle suspension. A leaf spring is one or more narrow, arc-shaped, thin plates attached to the axle and chassis in a way that allows the leaf spring to flex vertically in response to irregularities in the road surface.

Design

A leaf spring takes the form of a slender arc-shaped length of spring steel of rectangular cross-section. In the most common configuration, the centre of the arc provides location for the axle, while tie holes at either end attach to the vehicle chassis. Multiple leaves may be stacked together into a multi-leaf spring, with progressively shorter leaves beneath the main leaf; this gives a progressively stiffening response and shares the load between leaves. Single-leaf (mono-leaf) springs, often of tapered thickness, are also used.

Leaf springs locate the axle as well as suspend it, eliminating the need for separate trailing arms in a simple Hotchkiss drive layout. They are still widely used on trucks, trailers, and railway vehicles, though most modern passenger cars use coil springs or other systems. The Chevrolet Corvette famously used a transverse composite leaf spring from 1963 onwards.

History

Elliptical springs were used on horse-drawn carriages from the 17th century, and one of the earliest patents for a leaf spring was granted to Obadiah Elliott in London in 1804. Leaf springs were standard on early automobiles and remained dominant in American cars into the 1970s through the use of the Hotchkiss drive.
