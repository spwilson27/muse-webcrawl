The Pullman Strike was a nationwide railroad strike in the United States that lasted from May 11 to July 20, 1894, and marked a turning point in US labour law. It pitted the American Railway Union (ARU), led by Eugene V. Debs, against the Pullman Palace Car Company, the main railroads, and the federal government under President Grover Cleveland.

Background

During the depression following the Panic of 1893, the Pullman Company cut wages by roughly a quarter but refused to lower rents in its company town of Pullman, Illinois, where most workers lived. On May 11, 1894, about 4,000 Pullman workers walked out. In June the ARU voted to boycott all trains hauling Pullman cars, paralysing rail traffic west of Chicago; at its peak the boycott involved some 250,000 workers in 27 states.

Federal intervention and collapse

Railroad managers obtained a sweeping federal injunction against the union, and Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago over the objections of Governor John Peter Altgeld. Violence on July 6–7 left dozens dead and rail property burned. Debs and other leaders were arrested; Debs was jailed for six months for contempt of court, and the strike collapsed. The ARU was dissolved, and Debs emerged from prison a committed socialist.

Legacy

In a gesture of reconciliation, Cleveland signed legislation making Labor Day a federal holiday in 1894. A federal commission later condemned the Pullman Company's paternalism, and Illinois courts ordered the company to divest its residential properties.
