Greek Cypriots

Greek Cypriots (Greek: Ελληνοκύπριοι) are the ethnic Greek population of Cyprus, forming the island's largest community. They speak Cypriot Greek, adhere predominantly to the Orthodox Church of Cyprus, and number roughly 660,000 in the government-controlled area of the Republic of Cyprus, with a large diaspora, especially in the United Kingdom, Greece, Australia, and the United States.

History

Greeks have been present on Cyprus since the Mycenaean settlements of the Late Bronze Age, and the island was thoroughly Hellenised under successive Greek, Hellenistic, Roman, and Byzantine rule. Later centuries brought Lusignan, Venetian, Ottoman, and British rule. Under the Ottomans (1571–1878), the Orthodox Church became the recognised representative of the Greek population. During British rule, Greek Cypriots increasingly demanded enosis (union with Greece), a movement that culminated in the EOKA campaign of 1955–59 and, ultimately, independence in 1960 under a power-sharing constitution with the Turkish Cypriot community.

1974 and division

Intercommunal violence in the 1960s was followed in 1974 by a coup sponsored by the Greek military junta aimed at enosis, which triggered a Turkish military intervention. Cyprus has remained divided since: around 200,000 Greek Cypriots were displaced from the north of the island, and their descendants form a politically significant refugee community. The internationally recognised Republic of Cyprus, dominated by Greek Cypriots, joined the European Union in 2004.

Culture

Greek Cypriot culture blends Greek traditions with local and Levantine influences, visible in its dialect, cuisine (such as halloumi and the meze), folk music, and the strong institutional role of the autocephalous Church of Cyprus, whose archbishop Ethnarch also plays a national-symbolic role.
