Dark pool

In finance, a dark pool is a private forum (an alternative trading system) for trading securities, derivatives and other financial instruments. Unlike public exchanges ("lit" markets), dark pools do not display pre-trade bids and offers; participants trade without revealing their intentions to the wider market, though trades are reported after execution. Liquidity in these venues is called dark liquidity.

Dark pools allow institutional investors to trade large blocks of shares with reduced market impact and information leakage. In the United States they account for a substantial share of equity volume — roughly 15–18% in recent years, with total off-exchange trading around 40% or more.

History

Dark pools originated in the 1980s, when US rules permitted after-hours crossing of orders; firms such as Instinet (1986) pioneered anonymous matching. Growth accelerated in the 2000s with Regulation NMS (2005), decimalisation and algorithmic trading, which fragmented orders across venues. Banks and independent operators launched pools such as Liquidnet (2001, for institutions), ITG's POSIT, Credit Suisse's CrossFinder and Goldman's Sigma X.

Types

Pools are commonly grouped into broker-dealer-owned pools (internalising client order flow, including payment-for-order-flow wholesalers' systems), agency or exchange-owned pools, and electronic market-maker pools. Matching typically occurs at the midpoint of the national best bid and offer.

Criticism and regulation

Critics argue dark pools harm price discovery, create two-tiered markets, and can disadvantage uninformed traders; high-frequency traders have been accused of "pinging" pools to detect large orders. Regulators have fined several operators for misleading subscribers — including Barclays (2016, $70 million settlement) and Credit Suisse (2016) — and rules such as the EU's MiFID II imposed volume caps on dark trading. Michael Lewis's book Flash Boys (2014) brought dark pools to wide public attention.
