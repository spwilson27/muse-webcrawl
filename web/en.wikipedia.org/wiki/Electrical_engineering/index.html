Electrical engineering

[Image: high-voltage transmission towers and power lines crossing a landscape at dusk]

Electrical engineering is an engineering discipline concerned with the study, design and application of equipment, devices and systems that use Electricity, electronics and electromagnetism. It emerged as an identifiable occupation in the late nineteenth century with the commercialisation of the electric telegraph, the telephone and electrical power generation, distribution and use.

Electrical engineers design systems from the gigawatt scale — national grids and power stations — to the nanometre scale of integrated circuits, in subfields including power, electronics, telecommunications, control systems, signal processing and photonics.

History

The science was created by experimenters from Volta, Ampère and Faraday to Maxwell, whose equations (1860s) unified electricity, magnetism and light. Practical electrical engineering followed: Edison's and Swan's lamps and power systems, Tesla's and Westinghouse's alternating current, and the telegraph and telephone networks that wired the world.

Electronics grew from the vacuum tube to the transistor (1947) and the integrated circuit (1958), shrinking electrical engineering into every device; digital signal processing and embedded systems merged it increasingly with Computer science.

Practice

Power engineers manage generation (now in rapid transition to wind, solar and storage), transmission and distribution, and the stability of grids. Telecommunications engineers build the radio, fibre and satellite networks carrying the Internet; control engineers design the feedback systems of aircraft, robots and industry.

Professional practice is licensed or chartered in most countries; the IEEE is the field's largest professional society, publishing standards — from Wi-Fi's 802.11 family to power-system codes — that shape global practice.
