Land reclamation in the Netherlands

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Netherlands has a long tradition of land reclamation: roughly one fifth of the country's land area has been reclaimed from the sea, lakes, marshes and swamps, and about a quarter of the country lies below sea level. The Dutch saying "God created the world, but the Dutch created the Netherlands" captures the scale of this achievement. The reclaimed land, much of it organised as polders — tracts enclosed by dykes and drained by pumps — is used for agriculture, housing, industry and nature.

History

Early works

From around the year 1000, inhabitants of the low coastal regions built dwelling mounds (terpen) and then dykes to protect farmland from flooding. Windmills, from the fifteenth century onward, provided the power to drain enclosed land, and in the seventeenth century wealthy merchants financed the drainage of lakes such as the Beemster (1612), now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Steam, diesel and electric pumping

The nineteenth century brought large steam-powered projects, most famously the draining of the Haarlemmermeer (1849–1852), which removed a lake that had threatened Amsterdam, Leiden and Haarlem; its bed now hosts Schiphol Airport. The Cruquius pumping station, with the largest steam-cylinder engine ever built, is preserved as a museum.

Zuiderzee Works

After the flood of 1916, parliament passed the Zuiderzee Act (1918), championed by engineer Cornelis Lely. The Afsluitdijk, completed in 1932, closed the Zuiderzee from the North Sea, creating the freshwater IJsselmeer. The surrounding polders — Wieringermeer (1930), Noordoostpolder (1942), Eastern Flevoland (1957) and Southern Flevoland (1968) — together form most of the modern province of Flevoland, the largest artificial island by reclamation in the world. The city of Almere and the provincial capital Lelystad stand on this new land.

Delta Works

The catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953, which killed 1,836 people in the Netherlands, prompted the Delta Works: a series of dams, sluices, locks and storm-surge barriers — including the Maeslantkering and the Oosterscheldekering — designed to shorten the coastline and protect the southwestern delta to a very high safety standard.

Technique

A polder is created by building a ring dyke, then pumping out the water. Groundwater levels are thereafter maintained artificially: rain and seepage are collected in canals and lifted into the surrounding water by pumping stations (historically windmills, now diesel or electric gemalen). Peat polders subside over time as drained peat oxidises, requiring ever-deeper drainage — a growing concern for emissions and flood risk.

Recent projects and debate

Modern additions include Maasvlakte 2, the expansion of the Port of Rotterdam into the North Sea, and the Marker Wadden, an archipelago built in the Markermeer primarily as a nature reserve. Since the late twentieth century, policy has shifted from reclaiming ever more land towards "Room for the River" — giving water more space — in response to climate change and sea-level rise.
