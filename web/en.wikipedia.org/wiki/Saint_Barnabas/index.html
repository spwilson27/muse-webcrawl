Saint Barnabas

Barnabas (died c. 61 AD), born Joseph, was an early Christian missionary and companion of Paul the Apostle, counted among the first disciples in Jerusalem. The name Barnabas, given him by the apostles, is traditionally interpreted as "son of encouragement" (Acts 4:36). A Levite from Cyprus, he sold a field and laid the proceeds at the apostles' feet.

Ministry

Barnabas vouched for the newly converted Paul before the Jerusalem apostles, who feared him, and was later sent to the growing church at Antioch, where he brought Paul from Tarsus to teach. From Antioch the two were sent out on what is called Paul's first missionary journey (c. 46–48), evangelising Cyprus — where Barnabas's cousin John Mark served as assistant — and southern Galatia, enduring expulsion and, at Lystra, being mistaken for the god Zeus. At the Council of Jerusalem (c. 49) Barnabas and Paul successfully argued that Gentile converts need not be circumcised. The two later parted over a dispute about taking John Mark on a second journey; Barnabas sailed with Mark to Cyprus and disappears from the Acts narrative, though Paul mentions him warmly in later letters.

Veneration

Barnabas is traditionally regarded as the founder of the Church of Cyprus and its first archbishop, martyred at Salamis. His feast day is 11 June in both Eastern and Western churches. The later Epistle of Barnabas and the apocryphal Acts of Barnabas bear his name but are not considered his work by most scholars.
