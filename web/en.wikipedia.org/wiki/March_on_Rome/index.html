March on Rome

The March on Rome (Italian: Marcia su Roma) was an organized mass demonstration in October 1922 which resulted in Benito Mussolini's National Fascist Party (Partito Nazionale Fascista) ascending to power in the Kingdom of Italy. In late October 1922, Fascist Party leaders planned an insurrection to take place on 28 October. When fascist troops entered Rome, Prime Minister Luigi Facta wished to declare a state of siege, but this was overruled by King Victor Emmanuel III. The following day, the king appointed Mussolini as prime minister, thereby transferring political power to the fascists without armed conflict.

Background

Following the First World War, Italy faced economic turmoil, inflation, and social unrest. The Biennio Rosso ("Two Red Years", 1919–1920) saw widespread strikes and factory occupations by socialist and communist workers. Landowners and industrialists, fearing a socialist revolution, increasingly funded Mussolini's fascist movement and its paramilitary squads, the Blackshirts (squadre), which violently attacked socialist organizations.

Mussolini had founded the Fasci Italiani di Combattimento in Milan in March 1919. By 1921 the movement had been reorganized as the National Fascist Party, and fascists held 35 seats in parliament. Through 1922, fascist squads seized control of many local governments in northern and central Italy, often with the tacit support of police and army officers.

The march

On 24 October 1922, at a fascist congress in Naples, Mussolini declared that the fascists intended to rule Italy. Four leaders, known as the Quadrumvirs — Italo Balbo, Michele Bianchi, Emilio De Bono, and Cesare Maria De Vecchi — organized the march. On 27–28 October, tens of thousands of Blackshirts converged on Rome from several directions. Their numbers and equipment were modest compared with the forces available to the state: the army in Rome could likely have dispersed them had the order been given.

Prime Minister Luigi Facta asked King Victor Emmanuel III to sign a decree declaring a state of siege, which would have authorized the army to stop the march. The king refused, fearing civil war, doubting the army's loyalty, and hoping to absorb the fascists into government. Facta resigned in protest.

Aftermath

On 29 October 1922, the king invited Mussolini, who had remained in Milan, to come to Rome and form a government. Mussolini arrived by train on 30 October and was appointed prime minister, at age 39 the youngest in Italian history. A celebratory march of Blackshirts through Rome followed.

Although the transfer of power was technically legal, the March on Rome was later mythologized by the fascist regime as a revolutionary seizure of power, and its anniversary was celebrated as the beginning of the Fascist era. Mussolini's government gradually dismantled Italy's democratic institutions, and by 1925–1926 he had established a dictatorship.
