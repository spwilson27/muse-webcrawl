Phase noise

In signal processing, phase noise is the frequency-domain representation of random fluctuations in the phase of a waveform, corresponding in the time domain to jitter. An ideal oscillator would produce a pure sine wave; in practice, noise processes modulate the phase, spreading the signal's power across neighbouring frequencies as sidebands around the carrier.

Measurement

Phase noise is conventionally measured as the single-sideband noise power in a 1 Hz bandwidth at a specified offset from the carrier, expressed in dBc/Hz (decibels relative to the carrier). Its spectral shape typically follows power-law regions — white phase noise, flicker phase noise, white frequency noise and flicker frequency noise — depending on the physical noise sources in the oscillator, as formalised in Leeson's model for feedback oscillators.

Effects

Phase noise degrades the performance of radio systems: in receivers it causes reciprocal mixing, in which a strong adjacent signal mixes with the local oscillator's noise skirt to mask a weak wanted signal; in transmitters it raises adjacent-channel interference; and in digital systems it contributes to error vector magnitude and bit errors. Radar, communications and precision timing systems therefore specify stringent phase noise limits, and low-phase-noise oscillators — such as oven-controlled crystal oscillators and high-Q dielectric resonator designs — are chosen for demanding applications.
