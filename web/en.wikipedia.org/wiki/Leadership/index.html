Leadership

Leadership, both as a research area and as a practical skill, encompasses the ability of an individual, group, or organization to "lead", influence, or guide other individuals, teams, or entire organizations. The study of leadership has produced many theories concerning the traits, behaviours, power, and situational factors that make leadership effective. "Leadership" is a contested term; specialist literature debates various viewpoints on the concept, sometimes contrasting Eastern and Western approaches and (within the West) North American versus European approaches.

Theories

Early theories focused on the qualities that distinguish leaders from non-leaders. Trait theory sought stable characteristics such as intelligence, self-confidence, and integrity, echoing the older "great man" view associated with Thomas Carlyle. Because no universal trait profile emerged, researchers turned to behavioural theories, which classify what leaders do — for example, the distinction between task-oriented and relationship-oriented behaviour, and Kurt Lewin's autocratic, democratic, and laissez-faire styles.

Contingency theories hold that effectiveness depends on the situation: Fiedler's contingency model matches leader style to situational favourableness, while situational leadership prescribes adapting one's style to follower maturity. Later approaches include transactional leadership, based on exchanges of rewards and punishments, and transformational leadership, in which leaders inspire followers through vision, charisma, intellectual stimulation, and individual consideration. Other frameworks include servant leadership, authentic leadership, and distributed or shared leadership.

Emergence and selection

Leader emergence — who attains leadership positions — is only loosely related to leader effectiveness. Meta-analyses associate emergence with extraversion, conscientiousness, openness, and low neuroticism among the Big Five personality traits. Leadership is also exercised in groups without formal leaders, where it may rotate among members according to the task at hand.

Contexts

Leadership is studied in political, military, business, educational, and community settings. Management literature often distinguishes leadership (setting direction and motivating change) from management (planning, organising, and controlling), though the functions overlap in practice.

Criticism

Critics note that leadership research can romanticise leaders, attributing organisational outcomes to individuals while neglecting followers, context, and luck; this bias is termed the "romance of leadership".
