Seed fern

Seed ferns, or pteridosperms, are an extinct group of seed plants that bore fern-like foliage but reproduced with true seeds rather than spores. They flourished from the Devonian through the Cretaceous, and were among the dominant plants of Carboniferous coal forests. Despite their name, seed ferns were not ferns at all, but early relatives of modern seed plants.

Discovery and classification

For decades, palaeobotanists classified the fern-like fossils as true ferns until seeds were found attached to the fronds — dramatically demonstrated in 1903–1904 when Lyginopteris foliage was shown to bear Lagenostoma seeds. The group is now regarded as a grade of several distinct orders, including the Lyginopteridales, Medullosales, Callistophytales, Caytoniales, and Glossopteridales, rather than a single natural lineage.

Description

Seed ferns ranged from scrambling and climbing plants such as Lyginopteris to substantial trees such as Medullosa, whose stems had multiple vascular cylinders. Their ovules were often borne in cupules, and pollen organs produced large prepollen grains, suggesting wind pollination and, in some groups, possibly pollination drop mechanisms similar to those of living gymnosperms. The glossopterids, with tongue-shaped leaves, dominated the Permian floras of Gondwana and became key evidence for continental drift.

Evolutionary significance

The earliest seed plants, such as Elkinsia from the Late Devonian, were pteridosperms, showing that the seed habit evolved among plants with fern-like foliage. Some Caytoniales produced enclosed, angiosperm-like fruits and have been discussed as possible relatives of flowering plants. The group declined through the Mesozoic and disappeared by the end of the Cretaceous.
