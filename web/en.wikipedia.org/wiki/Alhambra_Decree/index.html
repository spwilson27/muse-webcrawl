Alhambra Decree

The Alhambra Decree (also known as the Edict of Expulsion) was an edict issued on 31 March 1492 by the joint Catholic Monarchs of Spain, Isabella I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon, ordering the expulsion of practising Jews from the Crowns of Castile and Aragon and their territories by 31 July of that year. Jews who did not leave or convert to Christianity faced death. The decree was the culmination of rising anti-Jewish measures following the establishment of the Spanish Inquisition in 1478.

Background

Spain's Jewish community was one of Europe's largest, but had been devastated by the pogroms of 1391, which drove many Jews to convert. Their converts (conversos) were suspected by the Inquisition of secretly practising Judaism. The monarchs' confessor Tomás de Torquemada pressed for removal of the Jewish community as a supposed corrupting influence on conversos. The fall of Granada in January 1492 completed the Reconquista and sharpened the drive for religious uniformity.

Terms and exodus

Jews were given four months to leave and forbidden to take gold, silver or minted money, though they could sell property and take bills of exchange — often at ruinous losses. Estimates of those expelled range widely, from about 40,000 to over 150,000; many others converted and remained. The exiles — the Sephardic Jews — dispersed to Portugal (from which they were expelled again in 1497), North Africa, Italy, and the Ottoman Empire, where Sultan Bayezid II famously welcomed them. Sephardic communities preserved the Ladino (Judeo-Spanish) language for centuries.

Aftermath

The decree was not formally revoked until 1968, and in 1992 King Juan Carlos I attended a Madrid synagogue ceremony of reconciliation on the 500th anniversary. In 2015 Spain passed a law offering citizenship to descendants of Sephardic Jews expelled in 1492.
