Wola massacre

The Wola massacre (Polish: Rzeź Woli, "Wola slaughter") was the systematic murder of between 40,000 and 50,000 Polish civilians by German forces in the Wola district of Warsaw during the opening phase of the Warsaw Uprising, principally between 5 and 12 August 1944. It was one of the largest single massacres of civilians in the Second World War and formed part of Adolf Hitler's order that Warsaw be razed and its population annihilated in reprisal for the uprising.

Background

The Warsaw Uprising began on 1 August 1944. Hitler ordered that the city be destroyed and its inhabitants killed; SS chief Heinrich Himmler stated that Warsaw was to be "pacified" and cease to exist. The suppression was entrusted to SS-Gruppenführer Heinz Reinefarth, whose forces included the notorious Dirlewanger Brigade of convicted criminals and the Kaminski (RONA) brigade.

The massacre

Beginning on 5 August — "Black Saturday" — German units went house to house through Wola, shooting residents regardless of age or sex, including hospital patients and staff: the staff and several hundred patients of the Wola Hospital were murdered, as were patients of St Lazarus Hospital. Groups were assembled in factories, parks and open ground and machine-gunned; corpses were stacked and burned by "burning detachments" (Verbrennungskommando) of Polish prisoners, who were themselves later killed. In the first days alone an estimated 20,000–30,000 people died; mass killing continued at a reducing tempo until about 12 August, and executions of smaller groups went on for weeks afterwards.

Aftermath and accountability

Reinefarth received the Knight's Cross with Oak Leaves for his role in suppressing the uprising. After the war he was never extradited to Poland; he lived in West Germany, serving as mayor of Westerland and a member of the Schleswig-Holstein Landtag, and died in 1979 without facing trial. Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski, overall commander of the anti-uprising forces, testified at Nuremberg but was never tried for Warsaw. The massacre is commemorated in Warsaw at the execution sites along Górczewska and Wolska streets, including the monument at the site of the "Ursus" factory. Modern estimates commonly cite 40,000–50,000 victims, making Wola one of the largest single atrocities of the war in Europe.
