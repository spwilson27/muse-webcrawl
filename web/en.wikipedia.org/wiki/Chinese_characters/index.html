Overview

Chinese characters are logographs used to write the Chinese languages and, historically, other languages of East and Southeast Asia. They are among the oldest continuously used writing systems in the world, with origins in inscriptions of the late Shang dynasty. Each character generally corresponds to a syllable and a morpheme, and the standard repertoire numbers in the tens of thousands, though a few thousand cover most everyday text.

History and structure

The earliest surviving Chinese writing is the oracle bone script carved on bones for royal divination, followed by bronze script, seal script — standardised under the Qin dynasty — clerical script and the regular script (kaishu) still used today. Traditional analysis classifies characters into pictographs, simple and compound ideographs, phono-semantic compounds (the large majority), rebus loans and derivative cognates. In the twentieth century the People's Republic of China promulgated simplified characters, while traditional forms remain standard in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau.

Use in other languages

Chinese characters were adapted as Japanese kanji, Korean hanja (now largely replaced by hangul) and Vietnamese chữ Hán and chữ Nôm (now replaced by the Latin-based quốc ngữ). Characters are encoded in Unicode's CJK Unified Ideographs blocks, and input methods convert phonetic typing into characters on modern devices.
