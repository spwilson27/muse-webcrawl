The Junggar Basin (Chinese: 準噶爾盆地; pinyin: Zhǔngé'ěr Péndì) is one of the largest sedimentary basins in northwestern China, located in northern Xinjiang between the Tian Shan mountains to the south and the Altai Mountains to the north. Named after the Dzungar (Junggar) Mongols who once ruled it, the triangular basin is one of the two great basins of Xinjiang, the other being the Tarim Basin south of the Tian Shan. Geography

The centre of the basin is occupied by the Gurbantünggüt Desert, the second-largest desert in China, at elevations of roughly 500–1,000 metres. Unlike the Tarim's wholly internal drainage, the Junggar Basin drains in part to the Arctic Ocean: the Irtysh River flows northwestward across the basin's northern rim into Kazakhstan and Siberia. The Dzungarian Gate, a mountain pass at the basin's western edge, is a historic corridor between Central Asia and China. Oases and cities — including Karamay, Shihezi, and the fringes of the regional capital Ürümqi — line the mountain foothills, where snowmelt-fed streams reach the plain.

Climate and ecology

The basin has a cold desert to semi-arid continental climate, moderated slightly by moist westerly winds admitted through the Dzungarian Gate, which make it wetter than the Tarim Basin. The desert supports saxaul shrubs and diverse desert fauna; the Kalamaili reserve in the east is a reintroduction site for Przewalski's horse. The basin lies within the historical range of the Dzungarian hamster and other Central Asian steppe species.

Geology and resources

The Junggar Basin is a long-lived foreland and intracratonic basin with thick Permian-to-Cenozoic sedimentary fill, celebrated among geologists for its rich Permian source rocks and fossiliferous Mesozoic strata — including dinosaur-bearing formations of the Shishugou and Wucaiwan areas that have yielded theropods such as Shishugousaurus and early tyrannosauroids. It is a major hydrocarbon province: the Karamay oilfield, discovered in 1955, was one of the first large oilfields of the People's Republic of China, and the basin remains important for petroleum, natural gas, and coal.

History

The basin was home to nomadic peoples including the Xiongnu, Wusun, and Turkic groups, and from the 17th century it formed the core of the Dzungar Khanate, the last great nomadic empire of the steppe. The Qing dynasty destroyed the khanate in the Dzungar–Qing Wars of the 1750s, an event accompanied by massive loss of life, and subsequently incorporated the region into Xinjiang. The name "Dzungaria" remains in use for the broader region around the basin.
