Conviction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A conviction is the determination, in a criminal trial, that the defendant is guilty of the offence charged — by a jury's (or judge's) verdict after trial, or by acceptance of a guilty plea. Conviction triggers the sentencing stage and a cascade of legal consequences beyond the sentence itself: criminal record, and "collateral consequences" (disfranchisement, deportation for non-citizens, employment and housing bars, licence losses) whose scale has become a major reform topic.

After conviction

Convictions may be challenged by appeal (errors of law), habeas corpus or post-conviction review (constitutional violations, newly discovered evidence), and by clemency (pardon or commutation). Wrongful convictions — several thousand documented exonerations in the United States since 1989 in the National Registry of Exonerations, with mistaken eyewitness identification, false confessions, official misconduct and flawed forensics as leading contributors — have driven conviction-integrity units, recording-of-interrogation laws and compensation statutes. Conviction rates, like acquittal rates, resist simple interpretation: systems built on guilty pleas (the US) display high "conviction" figures partly because contested cases are filtered out of trials, while inquisitorial systems' rates reflect judicial investigation upstream.
