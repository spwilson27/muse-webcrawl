Cholera toxin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cholera toxin (also known as choleragen and sometimes abbreviated to CTX, Ctx or CT) is an AB5 multimeric protein complex secreted by the bacterium Vibrio cholerae. CTX is responsible for the massive, watery diarrhea characteristic of cholera infection. It is a member of the heat-labile enterotoxin family.

Structure

The cholera toxin consists of a single A subunit surrounded by five B subunits. The A subunit contains the enzymatic activity, while the ring of B subunits binds to ganglioside GM1 receptors on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells, allowing the toxin to enter the cell.

Mechanism of action

Once inside the cell, the A1 fragment ADP-ribosylates the Gs alpha subunit of the heterotrimeric G protein, locking it in its active state. This causes constitutive activation of adenylate cyclase, raising intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) levels. Elevated cAMP opens chloride channels (CFTR), and the resulting efflux of chloride, sodium and water into the intestinal lumen produces the profuse watery diarrhea of cholera.

Discovery and uses

The toxin was identified in the late 1950s by Sambhu Nath De in Calcutta. Because of its potent ability to raise cAMP, purified cholera toxin is widely used in cell biology research, and its B subunit is used as a neuronal tracer and as a vaccine adjuvant component.
