Cyrano de Bergerac (play)

Constant Coquelin as Cyrano in the original 1897 production.

Cyrano de Bergerac is a play written in 1897 by Edmond Rostand. It is a fictionalisation of the life of the real Cyrano de Bergerac, following the broad outline of his career as a duelist, soldier, and writer in seventeenth-century France. The play, written entirely in verse in rhyming couplets of twelve syllables, premiered in Paris on 28 December 1897 and was an immediate triumph, with the actor Constant Coquelin in the title role.

Plot

Cyrano, a brilliant swordsman and poet afflicted with an enormous nose, loves his cousin Roxane but believes himself unlovable. When Roxane falls for the handsome but tongue-tied cadet Christian, Cyrano agrees to supply Christian with the words to woo her — letters and balcony speeches of Cyrano's own passionate composition. At the siege of Arras, Christian is killed; Roxane retires to a convent, and only fifteen years later, as Cyrano lies dying from a head wound, does she learn that the soul she loved in the letters was his. Cyrano dies proclaiming his "panache".

Reception and adaptations

The play revived heroic romantic drama at the end of the nineteenth century and has been translated and adapted many times — notably Brian Hooker's English version — and filmed repeatedly, including the 1950 film with José Ferrer and the 1990 French film with Gérard Depardieu. It has also inspired operas, musicals (including Cyrano: The Musical), and the modern film Roxanne (1987).
