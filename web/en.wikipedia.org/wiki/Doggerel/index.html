Doggerel is a derogatory term for poetry considered of low quality, characterised by irregular or clumsy rhythm and rhyme, forced or predictable rhymes, comic or trivial subject matter, and a sing-song regularity that becomes monotonous. The word has been used since the late Middle Ages; Geoffrey Chaucer applied a form of it to his own tale of Sir Thopas, a burlesque in which the Host interrupts the pilgrim Chaucer to complain of his "drasty rymyng" — often read as a deliberate parody of bad popular romance verse.

Characteristics

Doggerel typically relies on thumping metre, rhymes achieved by distorting word order or pronunciation, stock phrases, and bathos — an abrupt descent from the elevated to the ridiculous. Unlike genuinely artless verse, however, doggerel effects can be achieved deliberately: comic poets deploy irregular metre, puns and outrageous rhymes for humour, so "doggerel" describes a quality critics perceive rather than a fixed form. Much nursery rhyme, broadside balladry, advertising jingle and light verse sits near the boundary, and judgement is notoriously subjective.

Deliberate doggerelists

Several writers have made careers of artful doggerel. John Skelton's tumbling "Skeltonics" influenced later comic verse. In the nineteenth century the Scottish poet William McGonagall became celebrated as possibly the worst poet in the English language for earnest, catastrophically rhymed poems such as "The Tay Bridge Disaster." The German poet Christian Morgenstern and the English humorist Ogden Nash — famous for couplets like "Candy / Is dandy / But liquor / Is quicker" — exploited deliberate metrical clumsiness. Spike Milligan and the writers of comic song continued the tradition, and the limerick is arguably institutionalised doggerel.

"I never saw a purple cow,
I never hope to see one;
But I can tell you, anyhow,
I'd rather see than be one."
— Gelett Burgess, 1895

Etymology

The origin is uncertain: it may derive from "dog" in its disparaging sense (as in "dog Latin"), implying verse fit only for dogs, perhaps influenced by Italian dogheria or the Middle English word for a worthless cur. The adjective "doggerel" appears in the fourteenth century, and the noun sense — a loose, irregular measure — followed.
