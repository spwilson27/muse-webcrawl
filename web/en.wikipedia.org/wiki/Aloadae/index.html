The Aloadae (Ancient Greek: Ἀλωάδαι) were, in Greek mythology, the twin giants Otus (Ὦτος) and Ephialtes (Ἐφιάλτης), sons of Poseidon and Iphimedia, and stepsons of Aloeus, from whom they took their collective name. Famous for their immense size and strength, they are best known for attempting to storm Mount Olympus by piling mountains on top of one another.

Mythology

According to Homer's Odyssey, the brothers grew a cubit in breadth and a fathom in height every year, so that by the age of nine they were roughly nine cubits wide and nine fathoms tall. In their hubris they threatened the gods themselves: they would pile Mount Ossa on Olympus, and Mount Pelion on Ossa, to make a stairway to the sky, fill the sea with mountains to make it dry land, and make the land into sea.

They also bound Ares, the god of war, in a bronze jar (or chains), where he remained for thirteen months until Hermes freed him, weakened and nearly dead. Ephialtes desired Hera and Otus desired Artemis; their pursuit of Artemis led to their deaths.

Death

In the best-known version, told by Apollodorus, Artemis transformed herself into a deer and leapt between the brothers on the island of Naxos; as each hurled his spear at the animal, he struck and killed the other. In Homer's account, Apollo killed them both before their beards had grown. In the underworld, according to later authors, they were bound back-to-back with serpents to a column, tormented by an owl's screeching.

Cultural references

The brothers appear in Virgil's Aeneid among the punished in Tartarus, and Dante places Ephialtes among the giants in the Inferno. Their assault on heaven became a standard classical emblem of hubris. Ephialtes' name, meaning "one who leaps upon," later became the Greek word for a nightmare — and, confusingly, also the name of the historical Ephialtes who betrayed the Greeks at Thermopylae, a different figure entirely.
