Ignaz Semmelweis

Ignaz Philipp Semmelweis (1 July 1818 – 13 August 1865) was a Hungarian physician and scientist, known as an early pioneer of antiseptic procedures, described as the "saviour of mothers". He discovered that the incidence of puerperal fever (childbed fever) in the maternity clinic of Vienna General Hospital could be drastically reduced by requiring physicians to disinfect their hands with chlorinated lime solution before examining patients.

Discovery

Working in Vienna from 1846, Semmelweis noticed that a clinic staffed by doctors and medical students — who came directly from performing autopsies — had far higher maternal mortality than a neighbouring clinic staffed by midwives. After the death of his colleague Jakob Kolletschka from a scalpel wound acquired during an autopsy, with symptoms resembling puerperal fever, Semmelweis concluded that "cadaverous particles" carried on hands caused the disease. In 1847 he introduced handwashing with chlorinated lime, and mortality fell dramatically, from around 18% to below 2%.

Rejection and death

Despite the results, Semmelweis's ideas conflicted with the established medical opinion of the time, and his refusal or delay in publishing and explaining his theory — he published his main work, The Etiology, Concept, and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever, only in 1861 — led to rejection. He was dismissed from Vienna, returned to Budapest, and, after a mental breakdown, was committed to an asylum in 1865, where he died within two weeks, probably from sepsis after being beaten by guards or from an infected wound.

Legacy

Semmelweis's findings were vindicated after the germ theory of disease was established by Louis Pasteur and antiseptic surgery was promoted by Joseph Lister. The "Semmelweis reflex" — the tendency to reject new evidence that contradicts established norms — is named after him. Semmelweis University in Budapest bears his name.
