Phaeton (carriage)

Phaeton
TypeHorse-drawn carriage
WheelsFour, very large
PeriodLate 18th–19th century
Named afterPhaëthon of Greek mythology

A phaeton is a form of sporty open carriage popular in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Drawn by one or two horses, a phaeton typically featured a minimal, lightly sprung body on four extravagantly large wheels, with an open seat and a high perch that gave the driver a commanding view — and made the vehicle fast but somewhat dangerous to ride in.

History

The phaeton took its name from Phaëthon, the mythological son of Helios who lost control of the sun chariot. It became fashionable in Britain in the 1780s and 1790s; high-perch phaetons were driven for sport by wealthy young men, including the Prince Regent. Variants included the mail phaeton, with a heavier body on mail-coach-style suspension for carrying a groom or luggage, and lighter spider phaetons.

Legacy

The name survived into the automobile era: early open touring cars were called phaetons, a usage adopted by manufacturers including Ford and by Volkswagen for its luxury Phaeton saloon. The term also persists in literature of the period, in which phaeton driving signals fashion and daring.
