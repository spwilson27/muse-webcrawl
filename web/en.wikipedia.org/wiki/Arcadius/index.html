Arcadius (Latin: Flavius Arcadius; Greek: Ἀρκάδιος; c. 377 – 1 May 408) was Roman emperor from 383 to his death in 408. He was the eldest son of the emperor Theodosius I, and on his father's death in January 395 he became ruler of the eastern half of the Roman Empire, while his younger brother Honorius ruled the west. Arcadius thus became the first emperor of what historians later called the Byzantine Empire.

Early life and accession

Arcadius was born in Hispania around 377, the son of Theodosius and his first wife Aelia Flaccilla. He was declared augustus by his father in January 383, at about the age of five. His education was entrusted to tutors including the rhetorician Themistius and the monk Arsenius, while his religious instruction was guided by Theophilus, patriarch of Alexandria.

Reign

Throughout his reign Arcadius was dominated by a succession of powerful ministers and courtiers. Initially the praetorian prefect Rufinus held sway, but he was murdered in November 395 by Gothic foederati amid suspicions involving the western general Stilicho. Power then passed to the eunuch chamberlain Eutropius, who arranged Arcadius's marriage to Aelia Eudoxia, daughter of the Frankish general Bauto. Eutropius's ascendancy ended in 399, when military discontent and the empress's hostility brought about his exile and execution.

The reign saw persistent tension between the eastern and western courts, the revolt of the Gothic general Gainas in 400, and the anti-Gothic reaction in Constantinople that followed. Relations with the Church were marked by the conflict between the empress Eudoxia and the patriarch John Chrysostom, who was deposed and exiled in 404. After Eudoxia's death that year, the able praetorian prefect Anthemius became the effective governor of the east, overseeing the construction of the great land walls of Constantinople. Arcadius died on 1 May 408 and was succeeded by his seven-year-old son, Theodosius II.

Legacy

Arcadius was a devout Christian who issued laws suppressing pagan sacrifice and closing temples. Although later historians judged him a weak ruler overshadowed by his ministers and his wife, the eastern empire under his reign preserved its territorial integrity and accumulated the administrative stability that would allow it to outlast the west. The Forum of Arcadius in Constantinople, with its triumphal column, commemorated his reign.
