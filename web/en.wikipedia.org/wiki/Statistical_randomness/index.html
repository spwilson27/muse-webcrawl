Statistical randomness

A numeric sequence is said to be statistically random when it contains no recognizable patterns or regularities; sequences such as the results of an ideal die roll, or the digits of π, exhibit statistical randomness. Statistical randomness does not necessarily imply "true" randomness — pseudorandom sequences generated by deterministic algorithms can pass statistical tests while being entirely predictable from their seed.

Concepts

Statistical randomness is assessed relative to a set of tests: a sequence is judged by its frequency distribution, correlations between successive values, the distribution of runs and gaps, and compressibility. A stronger notion, algorithmic (Kolmogorov) randomness, holds a sequence random if it cannot be produced by any computer program shorter than the sequence itself. Measures of randomness also include the distribution of subsequences of all lengths, as in the concept of a normal number.

Tests for randomness

Several standard test suites evaluate random number generators, including the Diehard tests developed by George Marsaglia, the NIST Statistical Test Suite, and TestU01. Common individual tests include the frequency (monobit) test, runs test, poker test, chi-squared test, and spectral test. Passing such tests is necessary but not sufficient for cryptographic security, which requires unpredictability against adversaries rather than mere patternlessness.

Philosophical issues

Whether any physical process is truly random, or merely unpredictable, remains debated. In statistics and everyday usage, however, a sample is "random" if every member of a population had a known (usually equal) chance of selection — a practical definition that underpins sampling theory, randomized experiments, and Monte Carlo methods.
