The Battle of Myriokephalon was a battle between the Byzantine Empire and the Seljuk Turks in Phrygia in the vicinity of the pass of Tzibritze, south of Myriokephalon, on 17 September 1176. It was a setback for the imperial ambitions of Emperor Manuel I Komnenos to recover the interior of Anatolia from the Sultanate of Rum, and it is often compared in significance, probably with exaggeration, to the disaster at Manzikert a century earlier.

Background

Manuel assembled one of the largest armies fielded by the empire in the twelfth century, accompanied by a vast baggage train including siege engines intended for the conquest of Iconium, the Seljuk capital. Sultan Kilij Arslan II sought peace, but Manuel, confident in his strength, refused and advanced through hostile territory toward the pass near the ruined fortress of Myriokephalon.

The battle

The Byzantine army, strung out over many miles in the narrow defile, was ambushed by Seljuk forces occupying the heights. The vanguard and right wing pushed through, but the centre, including the baggage and siege train, was attacked and thrown into confusion. A sudden rainstorm of dust added to the chaos. Manuel eventually forced his way through the pass with the surviving troops, but the siege equipment was destroyed and the army's animals largely lost.

Aftermath

Despite the defeat, the Byzantine army was not destroyed, and Manuel was able to negotiate a withdrawal in exchange for demolishing the frontier fortresses of Dorylaeum and Sublaeum. Manuel himself compared the defeat to Manzikert in a letter to Henry II of England, but modern historians regard its long-term effects as more limited; nevertheless, the dream of reconquering the Anatolian plateau died with Manuel in 1180.
