Citadel of Aleppo

Citadel of Aleppo

Location: Aleppo, Syria
Type: Medieval fortified palace
Part of: Ancient City of Aleppo (UNESCO World Heritage Site)

The Citadel of Aleppo (Arabic: قلعة حلب, romanized: Qalʿat Ḥalab) is a large medieval fortified palace in the centre of the old city of Aleppo, northern Syria. It is considered to be one of the oldest and largest castles in the world. Usage of the Citadel hill dates back at least to the middle of the 3rd millennium BC. Occupied by many civilizations over time — including the Greeks, Byzantines, Ayyubids and Mamluks — the majority of the construction as it stands today is thought to originate from the Ayyubid period. An extensive conservation work took place in the 2000s by the Aga Khan Trust for Culture in collaboration with the Aleppo Archeological Society. Dominating the city, the Citadel is part of the Ancient City of Aleppo, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986. During the 2010s, the Citadel received significant damage during the Battle of Aleppo in the Syrian Civil War, and was reopened to the public in early 2018 with repairs to damaged parts underway. The Citadel received further damage during the 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquake.

History

Recent studies have indicated the earliest occupation of the tell on which the Citadel stands dates to around 3000 BC. A temple dedicated to the storm god Hadad stood on the hill in ancient times, attested by cuneiform inscriptions and a relief discovered on the site. The hill gained the advantages of a citadel during the Seleucid period and was further fortified under the Romans and Byzantines.

After the Muslim conquest in 636, the fortress was repaired, and under the Hamdanid ruler Sayf al-Dawla in the 10th century it became a fortified palace. The Citadel withstood Crusader sieges in 1098 and 1124. Under the Ayyubids, particularly al-Zahir Ghazi, son of Saladin, who ruled Aleppo from 1186 to 1216, the Citadel was comprehensively rebuilt: the great entrance bridge and gatehouse, the deep moat, the palace complex, the mosque, and the defensive walls largely date from his reign. The Mongols damaged it in 1260, and it was rebuilt by the Mamluks; Timur damaged it again in 1400. The Ottomans used it as a garrison and residence, and it remained militarily occupied into the 20th century.

Layout

The Citadel occupies an elliptical mound roughly 450 by 325 metres, rising some 50 metres above the surrounding city. A wide moat, still partly water-filled in places, surrounds the base. Entry is via a steep stone bridge leading to a massive bent-entrance gatehouse designed so attackers would be exposed to fire from multiple directions. Inside are the remains of palaces, baths, mosques — including the Great Mosque of the Citadel and the smaller Mosque of Abraham — an amphitheatre built in the modern period, cisterns, and storerooms.
