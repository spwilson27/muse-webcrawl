Dynamic light scattering

Dynamic light scattering (DLS), also known as photon correlation spectroscopy (PCS) or quasi-elastic light scattering (QELS), is a technique in physics that can be used to determine the size distribution profile of small particles in suspension or polymers in solution.

Principle

When light hits moving particles undergoing Brownian motion, the scattered light undergoes a Doppler shift and its intensity fluctuates over time. A detector records these fluctuations and an autocorrelator computes the intensity autocorrelation function. The decay of this function gives the translational diffusion coefficient, from which the hydrodynamic radius is obtained via the Stokes–Einstein equation.

Applications

DLS is widely used to characterize nanoparticles, proteins, micelles, emulsions, and colloids, measuring sizes from below one nanometre to several micrometres. Analysis methods such as the cumulants method yield a z-average size and polydispersity index, while distribution algorithms such as CONTIN resolve multimodal populations. DLS assumes roughly spherical, non-interacting particles and is highly sensitive to small amounts of large aggregates or dust.
