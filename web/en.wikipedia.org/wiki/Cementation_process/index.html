Cementation process

Iron bars were packed with charcoal in sealed chests and heated for days.

The cementation process is an obsolete technique for making steel from wrought iron, used in Europe from the early 17th century until the mid-19th century. Bars of wrought iron were packed in powdered charcoal inside sealed stone or clay chests and heated in a furnace for about a week, allowing carbon to diffuse into the surface and gradually through the bars.

Process

The chests were kept at high temperature—around 1,100 °C—for five to fourteen days depending on the grade desired. The resulting product was known as blister steel because of the blistered appearance of its surface, caused by slag inclusions reacting with the absorbed carbon. Blister steel could be forged into shear steel, or melted in crucibles to produce the homogeneous crucible steel developed by Benjamin Huntsman in the 1740s.

History

The process was practised particularly in Sweden (using high-quality Swedish bar iron), in Newcastle and Sheffield in England, and in parts of Germany and France. It was capital-intensive and slow, and was superseded for bulk steelmaking by Henry Cort's puddling process and, decisively, by the Bessemer process after 1856.
