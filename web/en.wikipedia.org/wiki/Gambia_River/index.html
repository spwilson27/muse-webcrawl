The Gambia River is one of West Africa's major rivers, flowing about 1,120 km from the Fouta Djallon plateau in north Guinea westward through Senegal and the Gambia to the Atlantic Ocean at Banjul. It is unusual among great rivers for being navigable by ocean-going vessels for roughly half its length — about 390 km to the barrages of its tidal reach and far upstream for river craft — which made it a highway into the African interior for European traders from the 15th century and determined the bizarre geography of the country it names: the Gambia is essentially the river's lower valley, a strip some 30–50 km wide and 320 km long enclosed by Senegal.

History and ecology

Portuguese explorers reached the river in 1455; English, French, Dutch and Courlander (Latvian) forts followed, competing for gold, ivory and — from the 17th to early 19th centuries — enslaved people shipped from posts such as James Island (now Kunta Kinteh Island, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, made famous by Alex Haley's Roots) and Juffureh. Britain's river colony (1816–1965) became independent Gambia in 1965. The river traverses savanna and gallery forest into a wide, mangrove-fringed estuary rich in fish, oysters and birdlife; hippos and crocodiles survive in its middle reaches (River Gambia National Park protects chimpanzee islands), while a long-debated bridge-or-barrage at the estuary was effectively settled when the Senegambia Bridge (2019) finally spanned the river upstream at Farafenni — transforming the Dakar–Casamance road journey that once queued for its notorious ferries.
