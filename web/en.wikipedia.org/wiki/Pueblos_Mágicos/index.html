Pueblos Mágicos

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Pueblos Mágicos are recognised for their preserved colonial architecture, traditions, and natural settings.

The Pueblos Mágicos ("Magical Towns") programme is an initiative led by Mexico's Secretariat of Tourism (SECTUR), created in 2001, that recognises towns and villages across Mexico for their natural beauty, cultural richness, historical relevance, and preserved traditions. Designated localities receive federal support and promotion for tourism development in exchange for maintaining the qualities—architecture, festivals, gastronomy, crafts, and legends—that earned the designation.

History and criteria

The first town designated was Huasca de Ocampo in Hidalgo (2001), followed by Real de Catorce and Tepoztlán. To qualify, a locality—normally with a population of at least 20,000 in its area, though exceptions occur—must demonstrate symbolic attributes, legends, history, and distinctive everyday life, form a local tourism committee, and commit to preserving its heritage. The number of designated towns grew steadily, past 120 by the early 2020s and to 177 following the large 2023 round of designations. Towns can lose the title if they fail periodic reviews, as happened to some localities in 2009.

Examples

Well-known Pueblos Mágicos include Valle de Bravo, famous for its lake and monarch-butterfly sanctuaries nearby; Taxco, the silver-mining town in Guerrero; Pátzcuaro in Michoacán, known for Day of the Dead celebrations on Janitzio island; San Cristóbal de las Casas in Chiapas; Bacalar, on the "Lagoon of Seven Colours" in Quintana Roo; and Tequila in Jalisco, the birthplace of the distilled spirit. Smaller designated places, such as Real de Catorce in San Luis Potosí, attract visitors for pilgrimage, mining history, and desert landscapes.

Impact and criticism

Supporters credit the programme with diversifying Mexican tourism beyond beach resorts, creating employment, and funding restoration. Critics argue that benefits are unevenly distributed, that designation can drive gentrification and rising prices, and that some towns receive the label without adequate infrastructure or conservation enforcement.
