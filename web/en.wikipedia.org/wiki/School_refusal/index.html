School refusal

School refusal is characterised by emotional distress about attending school

School refusal is a term describing the behaviour of children and adolescents who have difficulty attending school, or who refuse to attend entirely, because of emotional distress. It is not a formal diagnosis in itself; rather, it is associated with anxiety disorders (such as separation anxiety and social anxiety), depression, and other difficulties. School refusal is distinguished from truancy, which typically involves concealment from parents and conduct problems rather than anxiety.

Presentation

Children with school refusal often complain of physical symptoms — headaches, stomach aches, nausea — that appear on school mornings and remit on weekends and holidays. They may plead to stay home, have tantrums, or experience panic at the prospect of school. Onset commonly peaks at school transitions: ages 5–6, 10–11, and around 14.

Causes

Contributing factors include separation anxiety, fear of social evaluation or academic failure, bullying, difficult transitions, and family stress. Functional analyses identify four common functions of the behaviour: avoiding school-related negative affect, escaping aversive social or evaluative situations, gaining attention from caregivers, and obtaining tangible rewards at home.

Treatment

Evidence-based treatment is usually cognitive behavioural therapy, combining gradual re-exposure to school, relaxation and coping skills, and parent and school involvement. Prompt return to school is generally recommended, since prolonged absence tends to entrench the problem and harm academic and social development.
