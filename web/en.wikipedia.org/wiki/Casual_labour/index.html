Casual labour

Casual labour is an employment classification in which workers are engaged on a temporary, irregular or on-demand basis rather than under a permanent contract. Casual workers are typically paid by the hour or day, may be hired and released at short notice, and in many jurisdictions receive a loading (a higher hourly rate) in place of entitlements such as paid leave and job security that accompany permanent employment.

Characteristics

Casual work is common in hospitality, retail, agriculture, construction, warehousing and dock work, where demand fluctuates daily or seasonally. Historically, casual hiring at ports — where dockers assembled daily to be selected for work — was notorious for insecurity and exploitation, prompting "decasualisation" reforms in many countries during the 20th century, such as registration schemes guaranteeing minimum payments. In Australia, casual employment is defined by the absence of a firm advance commitment to continuing and indefinite work, and federal law provides pathways for eligible casuals to convert to permanent employment. In the United Kingdom and the United States, related arrangements include zero-hour contracts and day labour.

Debate

Supporters argue casual labour provides flexibility for employers and for workers who prefer irregular hours, while critics associate it with income insecurity, exclusion from benefits and difficulty obtaining credit or housing. The growth of platform-based gig work has renewed debate about whether on-demand workers should be classified as employees or independent contractors.
