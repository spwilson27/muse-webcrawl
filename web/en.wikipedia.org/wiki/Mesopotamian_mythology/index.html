Mesopotamian mythology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A relief of the god Marduk with his dragon, from Babylonian art]
A relief of the god Marduk with his dragon, from Babylonian art

Mesopotamian mythology comprises the myths, epics and religious beliefs of the peoples of ancient Mesopotamia — Sumerians, Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians — the earliest written mythology in the world, preserved on cuneiform tablets from the third millennium BC. Its major works include the creation epic Enuma Elish, the Epic of Gilgamesh, and the myths of Inanna's descent to the underworld.

Pantheon

The gods formed a divine assembly headed successively by Anu (sky), Enlil (air, executive power) and, in Babylonian theology, Marduk. Major deities included Enki/Ea (fresh water, wisdom, crafts), Inanna/Ishtar (love and war), Utu/Shamash (sun, justice), Nanna/Sin (moon) and Nergal (plague, underworld). Each city had its patron god, housed in a temple on a Ziggurat such as Babylon's Etemenanki — a plausible inspiration for the Tower of Babel.

Creation and flood

In the Enuma Elish, Marduk defeats the primordial salt-water goddess Tiamat, splits her body to form heaven and earth, and creates humanity from the blood of the rebel god Kingu to serve the gods. The flood myth — told in the Atrahasis epic and within Gilgamesh — has the gods send a deluge to silence noisy humanity, thwarted by Enki's warning to the wise survivor; these stories deeply influenced the biblical Genesis traditions.

Worldview and legacy

Mesopotamian religion was civic and pessimistic: humans were made to labour for the gods, the afterlife (Kur) was a dusty shadow-existence for all, and fate was read through omens — astrology, extispicy — that seeded later Western astronomy and divination. Its myths survive through the chance preservation of library tablets, especially Ashurbanipal's at Nineveh.
