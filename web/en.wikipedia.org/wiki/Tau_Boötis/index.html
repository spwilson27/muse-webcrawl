Tau Boötis (τ Boo) is a binary star system in the constellation Boötes, about 51 light-years from the Sun. The primary is a yellow-white subgiant of spectral type F6 IV, slightly hotter and more massive than the Sun, orbited by a red dwarf companion at a wide separation. It is notable as one of the first stars found to host an exoplanet.

The planet

In 1996 a team led by Geoffrey Marcy and R. Paul Butler announced a planet, Tau Boötis b, detected by the radial velocity method. It is a "hot Jupiter" of at least about four Jupiter masses orbiting only 0.046 AU from the star with a period of about 3.3 days. Because the star is bright and the planet massive and close, Tau Boötis became a favourite target for characterisation: in 2012 astronomers using the CRIRES spectrograph at the Very Large Telescope detected carbon monoxide in the planet's atmosphere directly and measured its orbital inclination (about 44°), yielding a true mass near six Jupiter masses. Water vapour has also been reported. Unusually, the star's rotation appears synchronised with the planet's orbit, and the system has been a key case for studying star–planet magnetic interaction, since the planet orbits inside the star's magnetosphere and modulates its chromospheric activity.

Properties

Tau Boötis A is about 1.3 solar masses with an estimated age of roughly 1–2 billion years; it has a shallow convective envelope and an unusually short magnetic activity cycle of about 120 days, among the shortest known for any star. The companion, Tau Boötis B, is an M2 red dwarf separated by about 220 AU.
