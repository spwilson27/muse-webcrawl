Wealth inequality in the United States

Wealth inequality in the United States, also known as the wealth gap, is the unequal distribution of assets among the population of the United States. Wealth includes the value of homes, automobiles, personal valuables, businesses, savings, and investments. By some accounts, wealth is more unevenly distributed than income: Federal Reserve data from the Survey of Consumer Finances have shown the top 1% of households holding roughly a third of all household wealth, and the top 10% holding around two-thirds.

Statistics

According to Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts, in the early 2020s the top 1% of Americans held about 30% of total household wealth while the bottom 50% held roughly 2.5%. Wealth concentration rose substantially from 1989 onward, driven by equity and business ownership gains accruing mainly to the top of the distribution. The Gini coefficient for wealth in the United States has been estimated at around 0.85, among the highest in the developed world.

Causes

Researchers attribute wealth inequality to differences in income and savings rates, homeownership, access to retirement plans and stock ownership, inheritances, and tax policy. Racial and ethnic wealth gaps are substantial: median white household wealth has been measured at several times that of Black and Hispanic households, reflecting historical policies including redlining and differential access to credit and education.

Policy debate

Proposals debated in response include changes to capital gains taxation, estate taxes, expanded retirement savings programs, baby bonds, and in some proposals an annual wealth tax on very large fortunes. Critics of intervention argue that wealth concentration reflects entrepreneurship and investment returns; supporters of intervention point to effects on equality of opportunity and political influence.
