Alexander S. Wiener

Alexander Solomon Wiener (1907 – 1976) was an American immunohematologist who, with Karl Landsteiner, co-discovered the Rh blood group system in 1940, a breakthrough that transformed blood transfusion medicine and the prevention of hemolytic disease of the newborn.

Biography

Wiener was born in Brooklyn, New York, the son of an attorney. He studied at Cornell University and earned his medical degree from the Long Island College of Medicine (now SUNY Downstate). He worked for decades in the serology laboratory of the New York City Chief Medical Examiner's office and maintained a private practice in Brooklyn.

The Rh factor

Working with Landsteiner at the Rockefeller Institute, Wiener helped identify a new blood antigen in rhesus macaque blood in 1937; by 1940 the team had shown that the corresponding human antigen — the Rh factor — was responsible for dangerous transfusion reactions and for erythroblastosis fetalis (hemolytic disease of the newborn). Wiener became a vigorous, sometimes combative defender of his own nomenclature (the Rh–Hr system) against the rival Fisher–Race CDE notation, a dispute that lasted for decades.

Other work

Beyond transfusion science, Wiener applied blood-group serology to forensic problems, including disputed paternity testing and crime-scene blood analysis, and wrote extensively on the genetics and anthropology of blood groups. He also championed the clinical use of exchange transfusion for newborns affected by Rh disease.
