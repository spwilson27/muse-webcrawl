The Maximato was a period in Mexican history between 1928 and 1934 in which former president Plutarco Elías Calles remained the country's dominant political figure — styled Jefe Máximo de la Revolución ("Supreme Chief of the Revolution"), from which the period takes its name — while three other men formally held the presidency.

Background

Calles served as president from 1924 to 1928. His chosen successor, Álvaro Obregón, was re-elected in July 1928 but assassinated days later by a Catholic militant, José de León Toral, amid the religious conflict of the Cristero War. Barred by the constitution's no-re-election principle from simply continuing, Calles announced that Mexico should pass from a regime of caudillos to a "nation of institutions and laws", and in March 1929 founded the National Revolutionary Party (PNR) — the institutional ancestor of the PRI — while continuing to exercise power from behind the scenes.

The three presidents

  • Emilio Portes Gil (1928–1930), provisional president; negotiated the 1929 settlement that ended the Cristero War.
  • Pascual Ortiz Rubio (1930–1932), won the disputed 1929 election against José Vasconcelos; his dependence on Calles was so notorious that he was nicknamed the nopalito ("little cactus", for being easy to handle); he resigned in 1932.
  • Abelardo L. Rodríguez (1932–1934), substitute president, a wealthy general and businessman who nonetheless governed with somewhat greater autonomy.

End of the Maximato

The Maximato ended when Lázaro Cárdenas, the PNR candidate hand-picked by Calles for the 1934 election, turned on his patron after taking office. Exploiting a split within the regime, Cárdenas arrested and deported Calles and his leading supporters to the United States in April 1936, consolidating presidential authority and beginning the six-year Cardenismo period of land reform, labour mobilisation and the 1938 oil expropriation.
