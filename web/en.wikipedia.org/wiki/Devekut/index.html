Devekut

Devekut describes a state of devotional cleaving to God in prayer and daily life.

Devekut (Hebrew: דְּבֵקוּת, "cleaving" or "clinging"), also spelled deveikut or devekus, is a Jewish concept referring to closeness to God. It may refer to a deep, meditative state attained during prayer, Torah study, or the performance of the mitzvot (commandments), and is considered a central goal of Jewish religious life, especially in Hasidic Judaism and Kabbalah.

Biblical and rabbinic roots

The Torah repeatedly commands Israel to "cleave" to God (e.g., Deuteronomy 10:20, 11:22, 13:5, 30:20). Rabbinic interpreters debated how one could cleave to a non-corporeal God; the Talmud suggests cleaving to God's attributes — imitating divine mercy and kindness — and associating with Torah scholars.

In Kabbalah and Hasidism

Kabbalistic tradition understood devekut as an intense, contemplative union of the soul with God, attainable by the spiritually elite through meditation on the sefirot. Hasidism, founded by the Baal Shem Tov in the 18th century, democratised devekut, teaching that every Jew could cleave to God in prayer and even in mundane activities, by recognising the divine presence in all things. Hasidic prayer emphasised fervour, joy, and attachment of thought to God, and the tzaddik (righteous leader) served as a channel of devekut for his followers.

Practice

Practices associated with devekut include fervent prayer (sometimes with melody and movement), contemplation of God's unity, ethical conduct, and performing commandments with intention (kavanah). Some traditions also practice hitbodedut, solitary meditation and personal prayer.
