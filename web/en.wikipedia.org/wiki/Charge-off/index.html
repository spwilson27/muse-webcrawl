Charge-off

Charge-off
FieldAccounting, consumer credit
Typical timing120–180 days delinquent
EffectAsset written down; debt remains legally owed

A charge-off or chargeoff is a declaration by a creditor (usually a credit card or loan company) that a debt is unlikely to be collected. The creditor writes the account down to zero (or to its expected recovery value) on its books and treats it as a loss. Traditionally, this occurs when a consumer becomes severely delinquent — credit card accounts in the United States are typically charged off after 180 days of non-payment, and instalment loans after 120 days.

Consequences

A charge-off does not cancel the debt: the borrower still legally owes the amount, and the creditor may continue collection efforts, sell the debt to a collection agency, or sue. A charge-off is one of the most damaging entries on a credit report and generally remains visible for seven years from the date of first delinquency. If a creditor forgives or cancels more than a threshold amount of debt, the amount may be treated as taxable income to the borrower in the United States.

Accounting treatment

Charged-off amounts reduce a bank's loan-loss reserves; subsequent recoveries offset charge-offs, giving "net charge-offs", a closely watched measure of credit quality in banking statistics published by regulators such as the Federal Reserve.
