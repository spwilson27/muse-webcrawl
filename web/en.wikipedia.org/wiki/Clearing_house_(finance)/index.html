Clearing house (finance)

Clearing house
FunctionClearing and settlement of trades; central counterparty
ExamplesDTCC, LCH, CME Clearing, Eurex Clearing, The Clearing House (payments)

A clearing house is a financial institution that provides clearing and settlement services for financial trades — in exchanges and over-the-counter markets for securities, derivatives, and payments. By interposing itself between buyers and sellers as a central counterparty (CCP), the clearing house becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer, thereby mutualizing and managing counterparty risk.

Functions

After a trade is executed, the clearing house nets members' obligations (so only net amounts change hands), collects margin and default-fund contributions to cover potential losses, and guarantees performance of contracts. If a member defaults, the clearing house uses the defaulter's margin and a pre-funded "waterfall" of resources to complete settlement. Historically, bank clearing houses also exchanged cheques and settled interbank balances; The Clearing House in New York, founded in 1853, is the oldest such association in the United States and now operates major payment systems such as CHIPS and the RTP network.

Systemic importance

Because clearing houses concentrate risk, they are designated as systemically important financial market utilities in many jurisdictions and are subject to heightened regulation, including the CPMI-IOSCO Principles for Financial Market Infrastructures. Post-2008 reforms (the Dodd–Frank Act in the US and EMIR in the EU) mandated central clearing for most standardized derivatives, greatly expanding clearing houses' role in global finance.
