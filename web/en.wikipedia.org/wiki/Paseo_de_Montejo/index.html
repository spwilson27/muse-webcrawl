Paseo de Montejo is a grand, tree-lined avenue in Mérida, Yucatán, Mexico, famous for the opulent Beaux-Arts and Neoclassical mansions built along it during the region's henequen (sisal) boom at the turn of the 20th century. Named after Francisco de Montejo, the Spanish conquistador who founded Mérida in 1542, the boulevard was laid out beginning in 1888 on the model of Mexico City's Paseo de la Reforma and Paris's Champs-Élysées, extending north from the historic centre. Today it is the city's principal ceremonial and commercial corridor, home to museums, hotels, consulates, cafés and monuments.

History

During the Porfiriato, Yucatán's henequen hacendados became some of the richest families in Mexico, and they built European-style palaces on the new avenue to display their wealth. Construction proceeded between 1888 and 1905. After the decline of the henequen industry in the mid-20th century, many mansions were abandoned or converted; from the 1990s onward, restoration efforts — led by institutions such as the Museo Regional de Antropología in the Palacio Cantón — revived the avenue as the cultural face of modern Mérida.

Landmarks

  • Palacio Cantón (1911) — the largest of the mansions, built for former governor Francisco Cantón Rosado; now the regional anthropology museum.
  • Monumento a la Patria (1956) — Rómulo Rozo's carved-stone monument narrating Mexican history, at the avenue's northern roundabout.
  • Casa Vales and Quinta Montes Molina — preserved house-museums showing Porfirian domestic life.
  • Remate de Paseo de Montejo — the avenue's southern entrance plaza, a venue for weekend street closures and bicycle rides (the Sunday Biciruta).

The avenue today

Paseo de Montejo and its extension (Prolongación) now carry Mérida's main concentration of upscale hotels, banks, restaurants and shopping centres, while the historic stretch is protected for its architectural value and shaded by flamboyant (flamboyan) trees.
