The Cenotaph is a war memorial on Whitehall in London, England. Designed by Sir Edwin Lutyens, it is the United Kingdom's national memorial to the British and Commonwealth dead of the two world wars and subsequent conflicts. The word cenotaph derives from the Greek for "empty tomb".

History

Lutyens designed a temporary wood-and-plaster cenotaph for the London Peace Day parade of 19 July 1919. Public demand for it to be kept led to its replacement in Portland stone, unveiled by King George V on 11 November 1920, Armistice Day, as the funeral procession of the Unknown Warrior passed on its way to Westminster Abbey. After the Second World War the dates were extended to include that conflict's dead.

Design

The memorial is a subtly tapering pylon of Portland stone with no decoration other than carved laurel wreaths and the words "The Glorious Dead". Its apparently straight lines incorporate delicate entasis — slight curves calculated by Lutyens so that the vertical lines would meet at a point far above the monument. Flags of the armed services and the national flags flank the memorial.

Remembrance Sunday

The Cenotaph is the focus of the National Service of Remembrance, held at 11 a.m. on Remembrance Sunday, the Sunday nearest 11 November. The sovereign lays a wreath of poppies, followed by other members of the Royal Family, political leaders, and representatives of the armed forces and Commonwealth nations, while a two-minute silence is observed.
