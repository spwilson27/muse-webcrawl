Amazonian Craton

The Amazonian Craton is a geologic province located in South America that occupies a large portion of the central, north and eastern part of the continent. The Guyana Shield and the Central Brazil Shield (Guaporé Shield) constitute respectively the northern and southern exhumed parts of the craton. Between the two shields lies the Amazon Rift, a zone of weakness within the craton, where the Amazon River flows.

Extent and provinces

The Amazonian Craton covers much of Brazil, and parts of Bolivia, Peru, Colombia, Venezuela, Guyana, Suriname, and French Guiana. It is one of the largest and best-exposed Precambrian cratons in the world. Geochronological studies have divided it into several tectonic provinces of different ages, including the Central Amazonian Province (the ancient nucleus, older than about 2.3 billion years), and the Maroni-Itacaiúnas, Ventuari-Tapajós, Rio Negro-Juruena, Rondonian-San Ignacio, and Sunsás provinces, accreted progressively during the Paleoproterozoic and Mesoproterozoic.

Geological evolution

The craton grew by the accretion of magmatic arcs and microcontinents between about 2.2 and 1.0 billion years ago, during the Trans-Amazonian and later orogenies. It formed part of the supercontinents Columbia (Nuna) and Rodinia; some reconstructions place the Amazonian Craton adjacent to Laurentia (North America), with the Sunsás belt correlated with the Grenville orogeny. During the assembly of Gondwana in the late Neoproterozoic and early Paleozoic, the craton was welded to the West African Craton and the São Francisco Craton.

Significance

The craton hosts major mineral deposits, including the giant iron ore deposits of Carajás (Pará, Brazil), bauxite, manganese, gold, and tin. Its basement is largely covered by the Phanerozoic sedimentary basins of the Amazon, Solimões, and Paraná systems.
