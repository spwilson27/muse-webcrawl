Minh Mạng (chữ Hán: 明命; 25 May 1791 – 20 January 1841), born Nguyễn Phúc Đảm, was the second emperor of the Nguyễn dynasty of Vietnam, reigning from 1820 until his death in 1841. The fourth son of Emperor Gia Long, he is remembered as an energetic and conservative administrator who centralised and standardised the Vietnamese state on Confucian lines, renamed the country Đại Nam, expanded its territory in Cambodia and Laos, and resisted Western political and religious influence — policies that included the persecution of Christian missionaries.

The tomb of Minh Mạng outside Huế, begun in 1840 and completed under his successor.

Reign

Administration and reforms

Minh Mạng reorganised the empire's administration, replacing hereditary regional viceroys with a uniform system of provinces governed by appointed officials, extending the mandarin examination system, revising law codes, censuses, land registers and weights and measures. In 1839 he formally renamed the country Đại Nam ("Great South"). His reign is also associated with inventions and scholarship patronised by the court, and with strict Confucian orthodoxy in ritual and education.

Territorial expansion and revolts

Vietnamese influence deepened in Cambodia, where a Vietnamese protectorate was imposed in the 1830s, provoking revolts; wars with Siam (1831–1834 and 1841–1845) contested control of Cambodia and Laos. At home, Minh Mạng faced major rebellions, including the Lê Văn Khôi revolt in the south (1833–1835) and highland and minority uprisings.

Foreign policy and religion

Suspicious of French intentions after the involvement of French officers in his father's campaigns, Minh Mạng restricted foreign trade to designated ports and issued edicts (1833, 1836, 1838) prohibiting Christianity and ordering the arrest of missionaries; several French and Spanish missionaries and many Vietnamese converts were executed. These persecutions later furnished France with a pretext for intervention under his successors.

Legacy

Minh Mạng fathered 142 children and is also famous for the aphrodisiac wine named after him. Historians credit him with completing the administrative unification of Vietnam but criticise his rigidity toward the West and his religious persecution. His tomb, near Huế, is noted for its harmonious integration of architecture and landscape and forms part of the UNESCO-listed Complex of Huế Monuments.
