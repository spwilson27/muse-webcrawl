Thaler

The thaler (German: Thaler, also spelled taler) was a large silver coin minted in various European states between the 15th and 19th centuries. The name is short for Joachimsthaler, after Joachimsthal (Jáchymov) in Bohemia, where rich silver deposits were discovered and coins were first struck in 1518 by the Counts of Schlick. The word travelled widely: it is the origin of the English word dollar, as well as the Dutch daalder, the Ethiopian talari and the Samoan tālā.

History

The exhaustion of earlier silver mines and the demand for a large coin matching the value of the gold gulden led the Archduke Sigismund of Tyrol to strike the first large silver "guldengroschen" in 1486. The Bohemian Joachimsthalers, produced in enormous quantities from 1519 onward, became the standard of the type: roughly 27–28 grams of nearly pure silver, about 40 mm in diameter. The Holy Roman Empire's Imperial Minting Ordinance of 1566 fixed the Reichsthaler at a standard that endured for centuries.

Thalers circulated throughout Europe and the wider world. The Spanish peso de ocho reales — the "piece of eight" — was modelled on the thaler, and its descendants include the United States dollar (defined in 1792 by reference to the Spanish milled dollar), the Maria Theresa thaler, which continued to circulate in Arabia and East Africa into the 20th century, and the lev, leu and other Balkan currencies whose names recall the lion (Dutch leeuwendaalder) stamped on related coins.

Later issues

In the 19th century the thaler remained the principal coin of northern Germany, standardized under the Dresden and Vienna monetary conventions. Prussia and its neighbours adopted the Vereinsthaler in 1857. When the German Empire introduced the gold mark in 1873, the thaler was retained at a value of three marks until it was demonetized in 1908. Slovenia revived the old name in 1991 when it named its first post-independence currency the tolar.
