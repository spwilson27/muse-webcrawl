T-glottalization

T-glottalization (or T-glottaling) is a sound change in English in which the voiceless alveolar plosive /t/ is replaced by, or accompanied by, a glottal stop [ʔ] — as in pronunciations of button as "bu'on" [ˈbʌʔn̩] or water with a glottalised medial consonant. It is one of the most studied ongoing changes in English phonetics.

Distribution

Glottal replacement of /t/ before a consonant or pause (in words like football or at the end of what) has long been widespread in British regional accents, including Cockney and Glaswegian, and has spread through much of urban Britain; it is also common in many American accents before syllabic /n/ (button, mountain). Historically stigmatised as sloppy, intervocalic glottaling — between vowels, as in water — has spread rapidly among younger British speakers, including into Received Pronunciation, where it is now common word-finally and increasingly word-internally.

Related processes

T-glottalization is related to glottal reinforcement (pre-glottalisation), in which a glottal stop precedes an oral /t/, /p/, or /k/, and to the North American flapping of /t/ to [ɾ], a different lenition of the same consonant. Sociolinguists treat the spread of T-glottaling as a textbook example of dialect levelling and change from below.
