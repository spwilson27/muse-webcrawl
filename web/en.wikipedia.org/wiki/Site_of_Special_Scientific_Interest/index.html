A Site of Special Scientific Interest (SSSI) is a conservation designation denoting a protected area in Great Britain. SSSIs are the basic building blocks of site-based nature conservation legislation in the United Kingdom, selected for their flora, fauna, geological or physiographical features. They range from tiny sites — a single meadow, quarry or fossil bed — to vast areas such as the Wash and the Cairngorms, and underpin designations including National Nature Reserves, Ramsar sites and Special Areas of Conservation.

Legal framework

The concept was created by the National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949 and greatly strengthened by the Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000. When a statutory conservation body notifies an SSSI, it specifies the features of special interest and a list of "operations likely to damage" the site; owners and occupiers must obtain consent before carrying out such operations, and public bodies have duties to protect and enhance SSSIs. Damaging an SSSI can be a criminal offence, with prominent prosecutions following damage to sites by development or off-road vehicles.

Selection and monitoring

Sites are chosen according to published guidelines on representativeness, rarity and naturalness, within a framework of "areas of search". Conservation bodies assess each notified feature's condition as favourable, recovering, unfavourable or destroyed. Government targets (formerly the Public Service Agreement target for England) aimed to bring 95% of SSSI land into favourable or recovering condition.

Examples and coverage

There are more than 4,100 SSSIs in England covering about 8% of the land area, over 1,400 in Scotland (about 12.7%) and over 1,000 in Wales (about 12%). Famous examples include Lundy Island, the Lizard Peninsula, Wicken Fen and the fossil cliffs of Lyme Regis. Northern Ireland uses the parallel designation of Area of Special Scientific Interest (ASSI).
