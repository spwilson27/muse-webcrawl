The Estado da Índia (officially the Estado Português da Índia, Portuguese State of India) was the Portuguese colonial administration and network of territories in Asia governed from the Indian subcontinent. Founded in 1505, six years after Vasco da Gama's sea route to India was discovered, it began as an empire of fortified trading posts stretching from East Africa to Japan and shrank over the centuries until only the Indian districts of Goa, Daman and Diu remained, which India annexed in 1961.

History

Francisco de Almeida was appointed the first viceroy in 1505, based at Cochin. His successor Afonso de Albuquerque conquered Goa in 1510, along with Malacca (1511) and Hormuz (1515), creating a maritime empire controlling the main spice-trade chokepoints. At its height the Estado encompassed possessions including Mozambique, Mombasa, Muscat, Ceylon, Malacca, Macau and Timor.

Decline

Competition from the Dutch and English East India Companies eroded Portuguese power in the 17th century; by the 19th century the Estado consisted mainly of the Indian enclaves. After Indian independence in 1947, Portugal refused to cede them, and in December 1961 Indian forces occupied Goa, Daman and Diu in Operation Vijay, ending over 450 years of Portuguese rule. Portugal recognised Indian sovereignty only after the Carnation Revolution of 1974.
