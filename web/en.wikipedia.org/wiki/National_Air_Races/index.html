National Air Races

The National Air Races were a series of pylon and cross-country races that took place in the United States from 1920 to 1949. The National Air Races were a primary developer of popular aviation, drawing huge crowds — hundreds of thousands at their peak — and featured the most famous racing pilots and aircraft of the era. For many years the event was held in Cleveland, Ohio (1929–1949, interrupted by World War II), and became known as the Cleveland National Air Races.

Events

The programme combined closed-course pylon racing with long-distance races:

  • The Thompson Trophy — a closed-course pylon race for the fastest aircraft, first held in 1930; winners included Jimmy Doolittle (1932, in the Gee Bee R-1) and Roscoe Turner (1938, 1939).
  • The Bendix Trophy — a transcontinental point-to-point race (usually from the West Coast to Cleveland, then on to New York), begun in 1931 and won by pilots including Doolittle, Turner, Amelia Earhart (fifth, 1935), and Louise Thaden and Blanche Noyes (1936).
  • The Greve Trophy and other races for smaller-displacement aircraft.

End of the races

Racing resumed after the war with surplus fighter aircraft such as modified P-51 Mustangs and F2G Corsairs. The 1949 event ended in tragedy when pilot Bill Odom crashed his P-51 into a home in Berea, Ohio, killing himself and two people on the ground. The National Air Races were discontinued, and large-scale pylon racing in the United States did not return until the Reno Air Races began in 1964.
