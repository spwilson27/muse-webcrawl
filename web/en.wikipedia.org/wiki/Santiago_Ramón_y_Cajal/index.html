Santiago Ramón y Cajal (1 May 1852 – 17 October 1934) was a Spanish neuroscientist, pathologist, and histologist specializing in neuroanatomy and the central nervous system. He is widely regarded as the father of modern neuroscience. His pioneering investigations into the microscopic structure of the brain made him a central figure in the development of the neuron doctrine, the idea that the nervous system is composed of individual, discrete cells rather than a continuous network.

Ramón y Cajal shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1906 with the Italian scientist Camillo Golgi "in recognition of their work on the structure of the nervous system". Ironically, the two men held opposing views: Golgi defended the reticular theory, while Ramón y Cajal's meticulous drawings demonstrated that neurons are separate cells that communicate at specialized junctions.

Early life

Ramón y Cajal was born in Petilla de Aragón, the son of a surgeon. As a youth he was rebellious and showed little interest in formal schooling, preferring drawing and nature. He eventually studied medicine at the University of Zaragoza, graduating in 1873, and served as an army medic in Cuba during the Ten Years' War, an experience that damaged his health.

Scientific work

In 1887 Ramón y Cajal encountered Golgi's staining method, which used silver chromate to reveal the structure of individual neurons. He refined the technique and applied it to the brains of birds and young mammals, producing extraordinarily detailed drawings of neural circuits in the cerebellum, retina, spinal cord, and hippocampus. His work demonstrated the existence of dendrites and axons as distinct structures and supported the law of dynamic polarization, according to which signals flow through a neuron in one direction.

His monumental work Textura del sistema nervioso del hombre y de los vertebrados (1899–1904) remains a classic of neuroanatomy. He also proposed that learning involves the growth of new connections between neurons, an idea that anticipated the modern concept of synaptic plasticity.

Legacy

Ramón y Cajal founded the Spanish school of neurology and trained a generation of researchers. His drawings, many preserved at the Cajal Institute in Madrid, are still used in neuroscience teaching today. He is considered the first person from Spain to win a Nobel Prize in science.
