Agustín de Iturbide

Agustín de Iturbide (27 September 1783 – 19 July 1824), in full Agustín Cosme Damián de Iturbide y Arámburu, was a Mexican army general and politician. During the Mexican War of Independence he built a successful political and military coalition that achieved independence from Spain in 1821, and he then became the first emperor of Mexico, reigning as Agustín I in 1822–1823.

Independence

Iturbide began his career as a royalist officer fighting the insurgents of Hidalgo and Morelos. In 1820, alarmed by liberal reforms in Spain, he changed course and proclaimed the Plan of Iguala (February 1821), promising independence, Catholicism as the state religion, and equality (the "Three Guarantees"). Allying with the insurgent leader Vicente Guerrero, he led the Army of the Three Guarantees into Mexico City on 27 September 1821; the Treaty of Córdoba confirmed independence.

Empire and downfall

When no European prince accepted the Mexican crown, Iturbide was proclaimed emperor in May 1822. His reign was plagued by financial crisis and conflict with Congress, which he dissolved. The Plan of Casa Mata (February 1823), backed by Antonio López de Santa Anna and Guerrero, forced his abdication in March 1823. He went into exile in Europe; unaware that Congress had declared him a traitor, he returned in July 1824 and was arrested and shot at Padilla. His remains were later transferred to Mexico City Cathedral. Historians remain divided between his image as liberator and as opportunist.
