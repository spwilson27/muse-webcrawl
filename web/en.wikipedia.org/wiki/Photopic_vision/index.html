Photopic vision

Photopic vision is the vision of the eye under well-lit conditions (luminance levels from about 10 to 108 cd/m²). In humans it is mediated primarily by the cone cells of the retina, which provide high visual acuity and colour perception. Photopic vision contrasts with scotopic vision, the rod-mediated vision of very low light, and mesopic vision, the intermediate regime in which both rods and cones contribute.

Spectral sensitivity

The sensitivity of photopic vision to different wavelengths is described by the photopic luminosity function, V(λ), standardised by the CIE in 1924 (and revised since). It peaks at about 555 nm (green-yellow) and falls to near zero in the deep violet and far red. This curve is the basis of photometric quantities such as the lumen: radiant power is weighted by V(λ) to give luminous flux. Under scotopic conditions the peak shifts to about 507 nm — the Purkinje effect — so that in dim light blues appear relatively brighter and reds darker.

Characteristics

Because cones are concentrated in the fovea and adapt quickly, photopic vision offers sharp central detail, rapid response, and full trichromatic colour. It requires much more light than scotopic vision, and the eye takes minutes to switch fully between the two regimes (dark adaptation).
