Sacred groves of India

Sacred groves of India are fragments of forest or stands of trees that have religious, cultural, or communal significance for the local people who protect them. In and around these groves, hunting, logging, and other extractive activities are typically forbidden or strictly limited by tradition and religious belief, and many groves preserve vegetation resembling the natural forest that once covered the surrounding landscape. Estimates of their number range from 100,000 to 150,000 across India, and they are found in almost every state.

Regional names

Sacred groves are known by many local names, including:

  • Kavu in Kerala, often associated with serpent deities (sarpa kavu) and the goddess Bhagavati
  • Devarakadu in Kodagu, Karnataka
  • Devrai or devban in Maharashtra, especially in the Western Ghats
  • Khlaw kyntang among the Khasi people of Meghalaya, of which the Mawphlang sacred forest is the best known
  • Sarna in Jharkhand and Chhattisgarh
  • Oran in Rajasthan
  • Kovil kadu (temple forest) in Tamil Nadu

Ecological significance

Because extraction has been prohibited for generations — in some groves even dead wood and fallen leaves may not be removed — sacred groves often contain old-growth trees, rare and endemic plants, and relict animal populations absent from surrounding agricultural land. They function as genetic reservoirs, protect watersheds and springs, and stabilise soils. The pioneering ecological studies of Madhav Gadgil and V. D. Vartak in the 1970s drew scientific attention to the Western Ghats groves as refuges for species such as the lion-tailed macaque habitat trees and rare Myristica swamp communities.

Beliefs and management

Groves are usually dedicated to a deity — a village god or goddess, a serpent deity, or local spirits — and maintained by village communities, temple trusts, or particular families through customary rules enforced by social sanction and fear of divine displeasure. Some groves permit limited collection of medicinal plants or fallen fruit; others are entirely inviolate. Annual festivals, offerings, and rituals renew the bond between community and grove.

Threats

Sacred groves are declining under pressure from urbanisation, road-building, quarrying, encroachment, and the weakening of traditional taboos as belief systems change. Changes in land ownership, temple administration reforms, and the commercialisation of temple lands have eroded community control in some regions. Conservationists argue that groves offer a model of community-based conservation, and various state governments and NGOs have begun documenting and supporting them; the Wildlife (Protection) Amendment Act of 2002 allows "community reserves", under which some groves have gained formal protection.
