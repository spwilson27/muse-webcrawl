Spectral theorem

In mathematics, particularly linear algebra and functional analysis, a spectral theorem is a result about when a linear operator or matrix can be diagonalized — that is, represented as a diagonal matrix in some basis. For finite-dimensional spaces, the spectral theorem states that a self-adjoint (Hermitian) matrix can be diagonalised by a unitary transformation, with real eigenvalues; equivalently, a real symmetric matrix is diagonalisable by an orthogonal transformation.

A symmetric matrix maps the unit circle to an ellipse whose axes are its eigenvectors.

Finite-dimensional case

The theorem extends to normal operators (those commuting with their adjoint, AA* = A*A): exactly the normal operators on a finite-dimensional inner product space are unitarily diagonalisable. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal, so the operator is a sum of scalar multiples of mutually orthogonal projections — its spectral decomposition. Consequences include the classification of quadratic forms, principal component analysis, and the fact that Hermitian matrices have real spectra (as observables in quantum mechanics).

Infinite-dimensional case

For bounded self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space, eigenvalues need not exist; the spectrum can be continuous. The spectral theorem then represents the operator as an integral over its spectrum against a projection-valued measure: A = ∫ λ dE(λ). This is the mathematical foundation of the quantum-mechanical formalism of observables and measurement. John von Neumann's work in the 1920s–30s made the theorem rigorous, and unbounded self-adjoint operators (such as position and momentum) are handled through their resolvents or through multiplication-operator forms of the theorem.

Generalisations

Related results include the singular value decomposition for arbitrary matrices, the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators (discrete spectrum accumulating only at 0), and Gelfand–Naimark theory for commutative C*-algebras, which recasts the theorem as the identification of an algebra with functions on its spectrum.
