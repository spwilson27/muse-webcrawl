Garden city movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The garden city movement is a method of urban planning initiated in 1898 by Sir Ebenezer Howard in the United Kingdom, in his book To-morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform (reissued as Garden Cities of To-morrow). Influenced by the model communities of industrial philanthropists and by Edward Bellamy's utopian novel Looking Backward, Howard proposed self-contained communities surrounded by "greenbelts", containing proportionate areas of residences, industry, and agriculture.

Howard's model

Howard's famous "Three Magnets" diagram contrasted the drawbacks of Town and Country with the attractions of a combined "Town-Country". A garden city would house about 32,000 people on 6,000 acres, its land held in trust so that rising land values benefited the community. When full, another city would be founded beyond its greenbelt, forming a cluster linked by rail.

Built examples and legacy

Howard founded Letchworth Garden City (1903) and Welwyn Garden City (1920) in Hertfordshire to prove the model. The movement shaped the British New Towns programme after 1946, suburb design worldwide, and — in diluted form — countless "garden suburbs". Though criticized for low density and car dependence in its suburban descendants, its core ideas of greenbelts, community land ownership and balanced development remain central to planning debates.
