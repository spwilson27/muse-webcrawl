Whitewash (beauty)

Placeholder image: traditional skin-lightening cosmetics.

Whitewash in the context of beauty refers to the cultural and cosmetic preference for lighter or paler skin, and to practices of skin whitening (also called skin lightening or skin bleaching). In many societies, fair skin has historically been associated with wealth, status, and beauty, because it suggested a life indoors away from manual outdoor labour. The phenomenon is discussed in relation to colourism — discrimination based on skin tone within and across ethnic groups.

History

Practices of whitening the skin are ancient. In Elizabethan England, ceruse — a lead-based white cosmetic — was fashionable despite its toxicity. In East Asia, pale skin was prized in China and Japan for centuries, expressed in the saying that "a white complexion can hide many flaws". In South Asia, fairness became strongly linked to caste, class, and marriageability, a preference reinforced under British colonial rule.

Modern industry

Skin-lightening products form a large global industry, sold widely in Asia, Africa, the Caribbean, and elsewhere, with ingredients such as hydroquinone, corticosteroids, glutathione, vitamin C, and niacinamide; some products illegally contain mercury. Health authorities have warned about skin damage, irritation, ochronosis, and mercury poisoning from unregulated products. Several countries have banned or restricted hydroquinone and mercury-based creams.

Criticism and movements

Campaigns such as "Dark is Beautiful" in India and movements celebrating natural skin tones have criticised fairness marketing. Under pressure, major brands have renamed or repositioned fairness products — for example, Unilever renamed Fair & Lovely to Glow & Lovely in 2020. Critics link the beauty whitewash to colonialism and media representation, noting that advertising and casting have historically favoured lighter skin.
