Baccalauréat

The baccalauréat (French pronunciation: [bakaloʁea]), often known in France colloquially as the bac, is a French national academic qualification that students achieve at the end of their secondary education (at a lycée), fulfilling the requirements to enter university. It was introduced under Napoleon in 1808 and is broadly comparable to A-levels in England or the high-school diploma in the United States.

History

The baccalauréat was created by a decree of Napoleon Bonaparte in 1808, with the first examinations held in 1809. Originally examined orally before university professors, it has evolved through many reforms. Women were first admitted in the 1860s, and Julie-Victoire Daubié became the first woman to obtain the degree in 1861. Pass rates have risen substantially over the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

Types

There are three main types: the baccalauréat général (general), the baccalauréat technologique (technological), and the baccalauréat professionnel (vocational). Reforms implemented from 2021 replaced the old L, ES and S series of the general bac with a common core plus chosen speciality subjects (spécialités), with a significant portion of the final grade based on continuous assessment alongside final examinations.

Examinations

The examination season takes place each June and famously begins with the philosophy examination, whose essay questions are a national talking point. Students are graded on a scale of 20; a mark of 10 or above is a pass, with honours (mentions) awarded at higher thresholds: assez bien, bien and très bien.

International equivalents

The baccalauréat is awarded in France and in French-administered territories and schools abroad. It is distinct from, though historically related to, the International Baccalaureate.
