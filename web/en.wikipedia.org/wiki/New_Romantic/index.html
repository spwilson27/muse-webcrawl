New Romantic

New Romantic was an underground subculture and pop music movement that originated in the United Kingdom in the late 1970s and peaked in the early 1980s. Emerging from London nightclubs such as Billy's and the Blitz club in Covent Garden, the movement was characterized by flamboyant, theatrical fashion — drawing on glam rock, punk, historical costume, and androgynous styling — and by synthesizer-driven pop music. The club's regulars were dubbed the "Blitz Kids," and Blitz host and door-picker Steve Strange became the movement's emblematic figure, fronting the band Visage.

Music

New Romantic music blended the electronic experimentation of Kraftwerk, Roxy Music, and David Bowie with dance rhythms and polished pop production. Leading acts included Duran Duran, Spandau Ballet, Visage, Ultravox, Japan, ABC, the Human League, Culture Club, and Adam and the Ants. Duran Duran and Spandau Ballet, initially rivals marketed in the press as figureheads of the movement, achieved major international success as part of the "Second British Invasion" of the United States in the early MTV era.

Fashion

The look combined frilled shirts, pirate and dandy costumes, dramatic makeup for men and women, and designer or DIY outfits inspired by historical periods and futuristic imagery. Magazines such as The Face and i-D, launched in 1980, chronicled and spread the style. New Romantic fashion influenced mainstream 1980s pop aesthetics and later revivals in fashion and music.

Decline and legacy

By the mid-1980s the movement had been absorbed into mainstream chart pop, and its leading bands pursued more conventional rock or soul directions. New Romanticism is remembered as one of the defining pop-cultural phenomena of early-1980s Britain, notable for blurring gender presentation in fashion and for marrying club culture to chart success.
