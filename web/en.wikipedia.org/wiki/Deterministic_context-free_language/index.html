Deterministic context-free language

The deterministic context-free languages form a proper subset of the context-free languages.

In formal language theory, deterministic context-free languages (DCFL) are the set of languages that can be recognized by a deterministic pushdown automaton. DCFLs are an important subset of the context-free languages, and they underpin the theory of parsing for programming languages, since most practical programming language grammars can be designed to be deterministic.

Definition

A context-free language is deterministic if there exists a deterministic pushdown automaton (DPDA) that accepts it. Equivalently, by a theorem of Knuth (1965), the deterministic context-free languages are exactly the languages that have an LR(1) grammar, and also exactly those with an LR(k) grammar for some k.

Properties

DCFLs are closed under complementation, a striking contrast with general context-free languages, which are not. They are also closed under union with regular languages, intersection with regular languages, and inverse homomorphism. They are not closed under union, intersection, concatenation, or Kleene star. Emptiness, finiteness, and equivalence of DCFLs are decidable; equivalence was long an open problem until Sénizergues proved it decidable in 1997.

Examples

The language of balanced parentheses and the language {anbn : n ≥ 0} are deterministic context-free. The language {anbncm} ∪ {anbmcm} is context-free but inherently nondeterministic in a way that makes it non-DCFL; more standardly, even-length palindromes over a binary alphabet form a context-free language that is not deterministic context-free.

Applications

Because deterministic languages admit efficient linear-time parsing with LR parsers, they are the theoretical basis of parser generators such as yacc and bison, and of the syntax of most programming languages.
