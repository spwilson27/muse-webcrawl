William Playfair

William Playfair (22 September 1759 – 11 February 1823) was a Scottish engineer and political economist, remembered chiefly as the inventor of statistical graphics: the line graph, bar chart, pie chart and circle graph.

William Playfair (22 September 1759 – 11 February 1823), a Scottish engineer and political economist, is remembered chiefly as the inventor of statistical graphs. In 1786 he introduced the line graph and bar chart of economic data, and in 1801 the pie chart and circle graph to show part-whole relationships.

Early life

Playfair was born in Benvie, Forfarshire, Scotland, the fifth child of a minister. Orphaned young, he was apprenticed to the millwright Andrew Meikle, inventor of the threshing machine, and later worked as a draughtsman and engineer at Boulton and Watt's Soho works in Birmingham, where he served as assistant to James Watt.

Statistical graphics

His Commercial and Political Atlas (1786) presented 43 time-series plots of British trade data — the first major work of data visualisation — using what we now call line charts and one bar chart (of Scottish imports and exports). In The Elements of Geography (1801) he introduced the first known pie chart, using divided circles to compare the populations and revenues of European states.

Later life and adventures

Playfair lived in Paris during the French Revolution, took part in the storming of the Bastille in 1789, and became involved in various ventures, including a scheme to settle lands in America, silver-plating businesses, journalism, and secret-agent work for the British government. His later years were marred by business failures and a libel sentence. He died in London in 1823.

Legacy

Although his graphical innovations were largely ignored by his contemporaries, Playfair is today recognised as a founder of information design, and his charts are reproduced in virtually every history of statistics.
