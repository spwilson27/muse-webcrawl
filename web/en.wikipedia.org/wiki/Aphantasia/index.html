Aphantasia

Aphantasia is the inability to voluntarily create mental images in one's mind. The phenomenon was first described by Francis Galton in 1880 and named in 2015 by a team led by Adam Zeman.

Aphantasia is a phenomenon in which people are unable to visualize imagery. While most people are able to evoke images in their mind, people with aphantasia cannot voluntarily conjure a visual image in their "mind's eye". People with the condition may still dream visually, recognise faces, and describe objects from factual knowledge, and they are often unaware that their experience differs from that of others until it is pointed out.

History

The phenomenon was first noted by Francis Galton in his 1880 statistical study of mental imagery, in which some respondents reported having "no power of visualising". It attracted little attention for over a century until a widely reported case study in 2010 of a patient ("MX") who lost mental imagery after surgery. The term itself was coined in 2015 by Adam Zeman and colleagues at the University of Exeter, from the Greek phantasia (imagination) with the privative prefix a-.

Research

Assessment commonly uses the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire (VVIQ). Brain imaging studies suggest that aphantasia may involve weaker connectivity between visual areas and prefrontal regions involved in voluntary imagery. Its opposite, extremely vivid imagery, is termed hyperphantasia. Estimates of prevalence vary, but roughly 1–4% of the population may experience aphantasia. It is not classified as a disorder, and research into its causes and cognitive consequences is ongoing.
