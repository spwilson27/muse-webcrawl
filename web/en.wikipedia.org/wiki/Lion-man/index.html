Lion-man

The Lion-man of Hohlenstein-Stadel (German: Löwenmensch) is a prehistoric ivory figurine discovered in the Hohlenstein-Stadel cave in the Swabian Jura of southwestern Germany. Carved from mammoth ivory with flint tools, it depicts a standing figure with a human-like body and the head of a cave lion. Dated to the Aurignacian culture of the Upper Palaeolithic, about 40,000 years old, it is one of the oldest known examples of figurative art and the oldest known depiction of a creature that does not exist in nature — evidence, archaeologists argue, of imagination, myth or religion in Ice Age humans. It ranks among the most famous objects of the UNESCO-listed Caves and Ice Age Art in the Swabian Jura.

Discovery and reconstruction

Fragments of the figurine were recovered on 25 August 1939 by geologist Otto Völzing, working under Robert Wetzel, in the Stadel cave near Asselfingen — days before the outbreak of the Second World War suspended the excavations. The pieces lay unrecognised in a box for thirty years until archaeologist Joachim Hahn began assembling them in 1969, recognising a human-animal figure but lacking the head and many fragments. Further excavations in the cave in 1997–2008, directed by Claus-Joachim Kind, recovered around 600 additional fragments from the original find layer, including parts of the head and back. A major restoration in 2012–13 by the Baden-Württemberg state conservation office reassembled more than 300 pieces, nearly doubling the known substance and revealing details such as a carved mouth, ears, incised marks on the left arm and triangular patches suggesting the figure may be male.

Description

The figure stands 31.1 cm tall, 7 cm wide and 5.9 cm deep. Its body is humanoid, with legs, feet, a navel and arms held slightly away from the body; the head has the muzzle, ears and rounded profile of a cave lion (Panthera spelaea). Experimental archaeology suggests it took over 400 hours to carve — a major investment for an Ice Age group — using flint burins and knives on a section of mammoth tusk. The figurine was found in a small inner chamber of the cave, together with pierced arctic fox teeth and reindeer antler, a context some researchers interpret as a storage or ritual deposit.

Interpretation

The Lion-man combines features of human and animal in a single body, making it the earliest secure evidence of therianthropic (part-human, part-animal) representation — a category central to later mythology and religion worldwide. Interpretations range from a deity or mythic ancestor, to a shaman in transformation, to a figure of story or teaching. Together with the slightly younger animal figurines and flutes from the neighbouring Swabian caves (Hohle Fels, Vogelherd, Geissenklösterle), it shows that the first modern humans in Europe possessed a rich symbolic culture.
