Baker v. Carr

Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962)

Argued19 April 1961; reargued 9 October 1961
Decided26 March 1962
MajorityBrennan, joined by Warren, Black, Douglas, Clark, Stewart
DissentFrankfurter, joined by Harlan
HoldingChallenges to legislative apportionment under the Equal Protection Clause are justiciable; the political question doctrine does not bar them

Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962), was a landmark United States Supreme Court case holding that federal courts may hear claims that state legislative apportionment violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The decision opened the courthouse door to the reapportionment revolution of the 1960s, culminating in the "one person, one vote" standard of Reynolds v. Sims (1964).

Background

Charles W. Baker and other Tennessee voters sued Joe C. Carr, the Tennessee secretary of state, alleging that the state's 1901 apportionment statute had never been updated despite massive population shifts: by 1960 some urban districts had many times the population of rural districts, grossly diluting city voters' ballots. The federal district court dismissed the case as a non-justiciable "political question", following Colegrove v. Green (1946).

Decision

Justice William J. Brennan Jr., writing for a 6–2 majority, held the claim justiciable. The opinion carefully confined the political question doctrine, listing six formulations — most famously, a "textually demonstrable constitutional commitment of the issue to a coordinate political department", or "a lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards for resolving it" — and held that an equal-protection challenge to apportionment fell into none of them. The Court did not itself order reapportionment; it remanded for trial on the merits.

Dissents and aftermath

Justice Felix Frankfurter, joined by Justice John Marshall Harlan II, filed a celebrated dissent warning the Court away from the "political thicket" and against becoming an arbiter of electoral districting. Frankfurter retired months later, and the decision is often said to have hastened his departure.

Significance

Baker transformed American politics. Within two years the Court decided Wesberry v. Sanders (1964), requiring equal-population congressional districts, and Reynolds v. Sims, applying "one person, one vote" to both houses of state legislatures. Chief Justice Earl Warren considered the reapportionment cases the most important work of his Court — more consequential even than Brown v. Board of Education — because they made all other rights enforceable through representative government. Legislative districts across the United States were redrawn, shifting power from rural to urban and suburban voters.
