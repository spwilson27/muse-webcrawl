Nematic

In the nematic phase, rod-shaped molecules align along a common direction (the director) but have no positional order.

A nematic is a phase of liquid crystals in which the elongated, rod-like molecules have no positional order, but they self-align to have long-range directional order with their long axes roughly parallel. Thus, the molecules are free to flow and their centre-of-mass positions are randomly distributed as in a liquid, but they maintain their long-range directional order. The word "nematic" comes from the Greek nēma (νῆμα), meaning "thread," referring to the thread-like topological defects observed under a polarizing microscope.

Director and order parameter

The local orientation of nematic molecules is described by a unit vector called the director, n. The degree of order is quantified by the scalar order parameter S, which measures the average alignment of molecular axes with the director: S = 1 for perfect order and S = 0 in the isotropic phase. In practice, typical values for nematics lie between 0.3 and 0.8.

Defects

Nematics exhibit characteristic line defects called disclinations, classified by half-integer or integer strengths. Thread-like textures of these defects give the phase its name and are readily visible between crossed polarizers as the classic Schlieren texture.

Applications

Nematic liquid crystals are the basis of most liquid-crystal displays (LCDs). In the twisted nematic field effect, a voltage applied across a thin nematic layer rotates the director, changing the polarization of transmitted light and enabling pixels to switch between bright and dark states. Nematics also respond strongly to electric and magnetic fields because of their anisotropic dielectric and diamagnetic properties, and they exhibit large birefringence.

Related phases

Other liquid-crystal phases include the smectic phases, in which molecules additionally organize into layers, and the cholesteric (chiral nematic) phase, in which the director twists helically along an axis perpendicular to it.
