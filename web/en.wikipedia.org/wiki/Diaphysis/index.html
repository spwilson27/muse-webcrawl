Diaphysis

The diaphysis is the main or midsection (shaft) of a long bone. It is made up of cortical (compact) bone and usually contains bone marrow and adipose tissue (fat). It is a middle tubular part composed of compact bone which surrounds a central marrow cavity which contains red or yellow marrow. In diaphysis, primary ossification occurs.

Structure

The diaphysis is a hollow tube of compact bone whose wall thickness is greatest near the middle of the shaft. Its inner surface is lined by endosteum and its outer surface by periosteum, through which muscles, tendons and ligaments attach. At each end the shaft widens into the metaphysis and then the epiphysis; during growth, the epiphyseal (growth) plate of cartilage between metaphysis and epiphysis allows the bone to lengthen from the diaphyseal side.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the diaphysis ("shaft fractures") of the femur, tibia, humerus and forearm bones are common injuries, often treated with intramedullary nails or plates. Ewing sarcoma typically arises in the diaphysis of long bones in adolescents, whereas osteosarcoma more often involves the metaphysis. Conditions such as diaphyseal aclasis (hereditary multiple exostoses) and progressive diaphyseal dysplasia (Engelmann disease) specifically affect the shaft.
