Bridgewater Canal

LocationNorth West England
Length65 km (41 miles)
Opened1761
EngineerJames Brindley

The Bridgewater Canal connects Runcorn, Manchester and Leigh in North West England. It was commissioned by Francis Egerton, 3rd Duke of Bridgewater, to transport coal from his mines at Worsley to the growing industrial city of Manchester. Opened in 1761, it is often regarded as the first true canal of the modern era and a landmark of the Industrial Revolution.

History

The canal was engineered by James Brindley. At Worsley the canal ran directly into the collieries through underground navigable levels, and at Barton it crossed the River Irwell on the Barton Aqueduct — a celebrated feat that astonished contemporaries as boats sailed high above a river. Because the canal was built on a level contour with no locks on its original section, transport was cheap and efficient: the price of coal in Manchester halved almost overnight.

Route

From Worsley the canal runs east to Manchester, where it meets the Rochdale Canal at Castlefield, and west through Sale and Altrincham to Runcorn, where it once connected with the Mersey. A branch from Worsley leads north-west to Leigh, joining the Leeds and Liverpool Canal. The Barton aqueduct was replaced in 1893 by the Barton Swing Aqueduct when the Manchester Ship Canal was built.

Legacy

The canal's success triggered "canal mania", the decades-long boom in canal building that transformed British transport. It is still navigable today and is used mainly by leisure craft; unusually for a British canal, it remains in private ownership.
