Poynting–Robertson effect

Poynting–Robertson effect
Named afterJohn Henry Poynting; Howard P. Robertson
First described1903 (Poynting); 1937 (Robertson)
FieldAstrophysics

The Poynting–Robertson effect, also known as Poynting–Robertson drag, is a process by which solar radiation causes a dust grain orbiting a star to lose angular momentum relative to its orbit, spiralling slowly inward toward the star.

Explanation

A grain absorbs sunlight and re-radiates it isotropically in its own rest frame; in the star's frame the re-emitted radiation is beamed slightly forward (aberration), producing a small force opposing the grain's motion. The drag matters most for micron-sized particles and clears dust from planetary systems over astronomical timescales.

Significance

The effect explains the inward drift of zodiacal dust in the Solar System and helps set the lifetimes of dust in debris disks around other stars. Robertson's 1937 relativistic treatment placed the earlier classical argument of Poynting (1903) on a firm footing.
