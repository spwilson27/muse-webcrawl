Binding of Isaac

Hero image: Rembrandt van Rijn, The Angel Preventing the Sacrifice of Abraham (1635).

The Binding of Isaac (Hebrew: עֲקֵידַת יִצְחָק, ʿAqēḏaṯ Yīṣḥāq), or simply the Akedah, is a story from the Book of Genesis in which God tests Abraham by commanding him to sacrifice his son Isaac on Mount Moriah. As Abraham raises the knife, an angel of the Lord stops him, and a ram caught in a thicket is offered in Isaac's place. The narrative, found in Genesis 22, is one of the most discussed and depicted episodes in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

The narrative

God commands Abraham: "Take your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering." Abraham rises early, saddles his donkey, takes two servants and Isaac, and travels three days to the place. Isaac carries the wood and asks where the lamb for the offering is; Abraham answers, "God will provide the lamb." At the site Abraham builds the altar, binds Isaac, lays him on the wood, and takes the knife — at which point the angel calls out from heaven and stays his hand: "Do not lay your hand on the boy... for now I know that you fear God." God reaffirms the covenant, promising Abraham descendants as numerous as the stars.

Interpretation

In Jewish tradition the Akedah is read at Rosh Hashanah and is a central symbol of faith and merit; the shofar recalls the ram substituted for Isaac. Rabbinic midrash expands the story, portraying Isaac as a willing adult participant. In Christian interpretation, the episode is read as a type prefiguring the sacrifice of Jesus: a father offering his beloved son at (traditionally) the same mountain. In Islam, the story appears in the Qur'an (37:99–113); most Muslim tradition holds that the son was Ishmael rather than Isaac, and the event is commemorated at Eid al-Adha with the ritual sacrifice of an animal.

Philosophers have long debated the story's ethics. Søren Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling (1843) treated Abraham's willingness as a paradoxical "teleological suspension of the ethical," while Immanuel Kant argued that a command to murder one's child could not genuinely come from God. Modern scholarship debates the story's composition and its relation to ancient Near Eastern practices of child sacrifice.

In art

The Binding of Isaac is a staple of Western art, famously rendered by Caravaggio, Rembrandt, Titian, and Donatello's contemporary Brunelleschi, whose competition panel of the scene (1401) is a landmark of Renaissance art.
