Michif

Michif (also Mitchif, Mechif, Michif-Cree, Métif, Métchif, French Cree) is a mixed language spoken by the Métis people of Canada and the northern United States, who are the descendants of Indigenous women and French/Scottish fur traders. It combines Plains Cree (verbs and grammar) with French (nouns, adjectives and articles), the two components retaining largely intact phonology and structure — a combination linguists regard as one of the most striking examples of a mixed language anywhere in the world.

Structure

In Michif the noun phrase is typically French — nouns keep their gender and French articles and adjective agreement — while the verb phrase is Cree, with the full polysynthetic complexity of Plains Cree: animate/inanimate distinctions, obviation, and elaborate inflection. Thus a sentence takes its nouns from French and its verbal machinery from Cree, and speakers must command both systems. The language emerged in the early 19th century in the Red River and Saskatchewan country as the Métis nation formed around the fur trade and the buffalo hunt.

Decline and revitalisation

After the defeat of the Métis resistances of 1869–70 and 1885, and through the residential school era, Michif transmission collapsed; most Métis shifted to English or to regional French. Today the number of fluent speakers is estimated in the hundreds, mainly elders in Manitoba and Saskatchewan, and the language is classed as critically endangered. Revitalisation efforts include dictionaries and grammars (building on the work of linguist Peter Bakker, whose 1997 A Language of Our Own remains the standard description), language nests, online courses and Métis Nation cultural programmes.

Related varieties

Michif should not be confused with Bungi (a Scots-influenced English of the Red River Settlement) or with Métis French, the distinct French dialect also spoken in Métis communities.
