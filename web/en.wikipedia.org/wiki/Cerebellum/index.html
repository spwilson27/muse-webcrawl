The cerebellum at the base of the brain

The cerebellum (Latin for "little brain") is a major feature of the hindbrain of all vertebrates. Although usually smaller than the cerebrum, in some animals such as the mormyrid fishes it may be as large as, or larger than, the cerebrum. In humans the cerebellum plays an important role in motor control, coordination, precision, and accurate timing, and it is involved in motor learning; evidence also implicates it in cognitive and emotional functions.

Anatomy

The cerebellum sits at the back of the skull, beneath the occipital lobes and behind the pons and medulla, from which the fourth ventricle separates it. It consists of two hemispheres joined by the midline vermis, and its surface is folded into fine parallel fissures (folia). Inside a thin layer of grey matter (the cerebellar cortex) lie white matter and four pairs of deep cerebellar nuclei — the dentate, emboliform, globose, and fastigial nuclei. Remarkably, the cerebellum contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined — roughly 69 billion of the human brain's ~86 billion neurons — mostly tiny granule cells; its characteristic circuit elements include Purkinje cells, which provide the cortex's sole output.

Function and clinical significance

The cerebellum does not initiate movement but refines it, comparing intended movement with proprioceptive feedback and correcting errors. Damage produces ataxia: unsteady gait, intention tremor, slurred speech (dysarthria), and impaired eye movements (nystagmus). Causes of cerebellar dysfunction include stroke, tumours, alcohol-related degeneration, multiple sclerosis, and hereditary spinocerebellar ataxias.
