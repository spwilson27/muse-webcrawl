Martian meteorite

A Martian meteorite is a rock that formed on Mars, was ejected from the planet by the impact of an asteroid or comet, and then landed on Earth. Of the more than 60,000 meteorites known to science, a few hundred have been identified as Martian. They are typically grouped into the "SNC" classes — shergottites, nakhlites, and chassignites — together with a few ungrouped specimens. The largest individual Martian meteorite found is Zagami (about 18 kg), which fell in Nigeria in 1962.

Identification rests on several lines of evidence: the rocks are igneous and much younger than most meteorites; their oxygen isotope ratios differ from Earth's and the Moon's but are mutually consistent; and some contain trapped gases whose composition matches the Martian atmosphere as measured by the Viking landers in 1976 — a match widely regarded as decisive proof of origin.

Groups

Shergottites

The most abundant group, named for the Shergotty meteorite that fell in India in 1865; mostly basaltic volcanic rocks, many crystallizing only a few hundred million years ago.

Nakhlites and chassignites

Nakhlites, named for the 1911 Nakhla fall in Egypt, are clinopyroxene-rich cumulates about 1.3 billion years old, often showing alteration by liquid water on Mars. The chassignites are rare olivine-rich rocks; the 1815 Chassigny fall is the type example. The orthopyroxenite ALH 84001, found in Antarctica, is an ancient (~4.1 billion years) outlier famous for the 1996 claim — now generally rejected — that it contained fossil evidence of Martian life.

Significance

Martian meteorites are the only samples of Mars available for laboratory study, since no sample-return mission has yet brought material back. They provide data on Martian volcanism, crustal composition, atmospheric evolution, and past water activity that complement orbiter and rover observations.
