Image stabilization

Image stabilization (IS) is a family of techniques that reduce blurring associated with the motion of a camera or other imaging device during exposure. Generally, it compensates for pan and tilt (angular movement, equivalent to yaw and pitch) of the imaging device, though electronic image stabilization can also compensate for rotation. It is used in image-stabilized binoculars, still and video cameras, astronomical telescopes, and smartphones.

With still cameras, camera shake is a particular problem at slow shutter speeds or with long focal-length lenses (telephoto or zoom). With video cameras, camera shake causes visible frame-to-frame jitter in the recorded video. In astronomy, the problem of lens shake is added to by variation in the atmosphere over time, which changes the apparent positions of objects.

Optical image stabilization

Optical image stabilization (OIS) uses hardware to physically counteract motion. In lens-based systems, a floating lens element is moved by electromagnets under the control of gyroscopic sensors that detect angular movement; shifting the element redirects the image so it stays stationary on the sensor. In in-body image stabilization (IBIS), the image sensor itself moves to compensate. Modern systems can correct along five axes: pitch, yaw, roll, and horizontal and vertical shift, and are commonly rated as providing several stops of shutter-speed advantage, allowing sharp handheld exposures far slower than the reciprocal rule would suggest.

Nikon introduced the first consumer OIS lens in 1994, and Canon followed with its Image Stabilizer system in 1995. Minolta pioneered sensor-shift stabilization in 2003, an approach later adopted by Pentax, Olympus, Sony, and eventually most mirrorless camera makers.

Digital (electronic) image stabilization

Electronic image stabilization (EIS) uses software: successive frames are aligned by detecting the motion of features in the image, then cropped and shifted so the subject appears steady. It requires a small margin of the frame to be discarded, reducing the field of view, and works only for video or for stacking still frames. Smartphones typically combine OIS with EIS for video. Digital stabilization cannot remove motion blur within a single exposure, only between frames.

Orthogonal transfer CCD

In astronomy, an orthogonal transfer CCD can shift accumulated charge across the sensor during an exposure to track the apparent motion of a guide star, compensating both for telescope shake and atmospheric seeing without moving any optics.

Limitations

Stabilization corrects only camera motion, not subject motion; a moving subject will still blur at slow shutter speeds. It also does not replace a tripod for very long exposures, though modern IBIS systems have narrowed the gap considerably.
