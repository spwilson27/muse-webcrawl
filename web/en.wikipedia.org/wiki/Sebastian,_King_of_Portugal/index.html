Sebastian (Portuguese: Sebastião; 20 January 1554 – 4 August 1578), often called the Desired (o Desejado), was King of Portugal from 1557 until his death. The son of Prince John Manuel and Joanna of Austria, he was born after his father's death and succeeded his grandfather John III at the age of three, under the regencies of his grandmother Catherine of Austria and then his great-uncle Cardinal Henry. Educated by Jesuits, Sebastian grew into a fervently religious and warlike young king, obsessed with crusading against the Muslims of North Africa.

Reign

Sebastian took personal control of the government in 1568. His reign saw administrative and judicial reforms — including the extension of the Ordenações legal codes — an attempt to standardise weights and measures, and growing fiscal strain as the costs of the Portuguese empire mounted. In 1574 he made a first expedition to Tangier, and he repeatedly sought allies for a full-scale crusade in Morocco, meeting Philip II of Spain at Guadalupe in 1576 but receiving only limited support.

The Moroccan campaign and Alcácer Quibir

In 1578 Sebastian intervened in a Moroccan succession dispute on behalf of the deposed sultan Abu Abdallah Mohammed II. Against the advice of most of his counsellors, he marched inland from Asilah with an army of roughly 17,000 men, including foreign mercenaries. On 4 August 1578, at Alcácer Quibir (Ksar el-Kebir), the Portuguese army was annihilated by the forces of Abd al-Malik at the Battle of the Three Kings — so called because Sebastian, Abd al-Malik, and Abu Abdallah all died in or around the engagement. Sebastian's body was never conclusively identified.

Aftermath and Sebastianism

Because Sebastian died childless, the crown passed to his great-uncle Cardinal Henry, whose death in 1580 triggered a succession crisis resolved by Philip II of Spain, inaugurating the Iberian Union (1580–1640). The disaster at Alcácer Quibir destroyed Portugal's military strength and independence for two generations. In Portuguese folklore the king became the centre of Sebastianism: a messianic belief that Sebastian had not died and would return on a foggy morning to restore Portugal's fortunes. Several impostors appeared claiming to be the lost king, and the myth later travelled to Brazil, where it animated millenarian movements.
