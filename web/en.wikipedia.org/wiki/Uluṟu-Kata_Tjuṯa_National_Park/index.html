Uluṟu-Kata Tjuṯa National Park is a protected area in the southern Northern Territory of Australia, about 335 km south-west of Alice Springs. It contains two of Australia's most famous natural landmarks: Uluṟu (formerly Ayers Rock), a massive sandstone monolith rising 348 m above the plain, and Kata Tjuṯa (the Olgas), a group of 36 domed rock formations. The park is jointly managed by its Aṉangu traditional owners and Parks Australia.

Geology

Uluṟu is an inselberg of arkose sandstone, the tilted remnant of an ancient alluvial fan; its iron-rich surface oxidises to the famous rust-red that shifts in colour through the day. Kata Tjuṯa is formed of a conglomerate of boulders cemented in a sandy matrix. Both formations are around 500–550 million years old in origin, products of the same mountain-building episode that created the Petermann Ranges.

Aṉangu ownership and management

The land is sacred to the Pitjantjatjara and Yankunytjatjara Aṉangu people, whose Tjukurpa (law and creation stories) is inscribed in the features of both sites, including in ancient rock art at Uluṟu's base. Title was handed back to the traditional owners in 1985, who lease the park to the Commonwealth for joint management. In October 2019, after decades of Aṉangu requests, the climb up Uluṟu was permanently closed.

Tourism and ecology

The park receives several hundred thousand visitors a year, centred on the resort town of Yulara just outside its boundary. Its desert ecosystem supports spinifex grasslands, mulga woodland and animals including red kangaroos, dingoes and the great desert skink, with cultural tours, base walks and the sunset viewing areas drawing visitors to experience the landscape under Aṉangu guidance.
