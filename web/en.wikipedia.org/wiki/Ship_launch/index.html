Ship launching

Ship launching is the process of transferring a vessel to the water. It is a naval tradition in many cultures, often conducted as a public ceremony, dating back thousands of years. Launching marks the point at which the hull, built on land in a shipyard, first floats; outfitting, trials and commissioning follow.

Methods

There are three principal methods of getting a new ship into the water:

  • Longitudinal (stern-first) launching — the ship slides down a slipway, usually stern first, into the water. This is the oldest and most common method, used since the introduction of slipways in the 19th century.
  • Sideways launching — the ship is dropped sideways into the water, used where the waterway is too narrow for longitudinal launching, notably on the North American Great Lakes.
  • Float-out — the ship is built in a dry dock or building basin, which is then flooded so the vessel floats and can be towed out. This is standard for very large vessels.

Air bags are also increasingly used to launch smaller ships, rolling the hull into the water on inflated rollers.

Ceremony

Launching ceremonies typically include the naming of the ship and its christening by a sponsor, traditionally involving breaking a bottle of champagne across the bow. Superstition holds that a bottle that fails to break is a bad omen. Naval launches may be accompanied by speeches, blessings, and the attendance of dignitaries. In some traditions the launch is accompanied by rituals intended to invoke the protection of the sea gods, a custom with roots in ancient Mediterranean practice.

Risks

A launch is one of the most dangerous moments in a ship's life: the stresses on the hull as it enters the water are considerable, and ships have capsized, collided with the far bank, or overwhelmed spectators with their wash. Drag chains and other braking arrangements are used to check a vessel's momentum once afloat.
