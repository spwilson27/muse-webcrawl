Herbal medicine

Herbal medicine (also called herbalism, phytomedicine or phytotherapy) is the study of pharmacognosy and the use of medicinal plants, which are a basis of traditional medicine. With worldwide research into pharmacology, some herbal medicines have been translated into modern remedies, such as the anti-malarial group of drugs called artemisinin, isolated from Artemisia annua, a herb that was known in Chinese traditional medicine to treat fever.

History

Archaeological evidence indicates that humans used medicinal plants during the Paleolithic period, approximately 60,000 years ago. Written records survive from Sumer (the Nippur tablets, c. 2000 BC), Egypt (the Ebers Papyrus, c. 1550 BC), India (Ayurvedic texts) and China (the Shennong Ben Cao Jing). Dioscorides' De Materia Medica (1st century AD) remained a standard reference for 1,500 years.

Modern pharmacology

Many modern pharmaceuticals derive from plant compounds: aspirin from willow bark salicylates, morphine from the opium poppy, quinine from cinchona bark, digoxin from foxglove, and the cancer drug paclitaxel from the Pacific yew. The scientific discipline studying these is pharmacognosy. Artemisinin, discovered by Tu Youyou in the 1970s from a traditional Chinese text, earned a share of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Efficacy and safety

There is limited scientific evidence for the safety and efficacy of many herbal products, and quality varies widely; products may be contaminated, adulterated, or contain inconsistent doses, and some herbs interact dangerously with prescription drugs — St John's wort, for example, induces drug-metabolising enzymes. Herbal remedies are regulated differently across jurisdictions, in the United States generally as dietary supplements rather than drugs.
