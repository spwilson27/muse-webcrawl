The Cretaceous is a geological period that lasted from about 145 to 66 million years ago. It is the third and final period of the Mesozoic Era, following the Jurassic and preceding the Paleogene of the Cenozoic Era. The Cretaceous is the longest period of the Phanerozoic Eon, spanning 79 million years. Its name derives from the Latin word for chalk, creta, reflecting the extensive chalk deposits laid down in its shallow seas; it was first defined by Belgian geologist Jean d'Omalius d'Halloy in 1822.

Geology and climate

During the Cretaceous the supercontinent Pangaea completed its breakup: the Atlantic Ocean widened, and Gondwana fragmented into the southern continents. Sea levels were among the highest in Earth history, flooding continental interiors with shallow epicontinental seas such as the Western Interior Seaway of North America and depositing thick chalk beds across Europe. The climate was warm, with little or no polar ice, and high atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Life

Flowering plants (angiosperms) appeared and diversified explosively, transforming terrestrial ecosystems together with pollinating insects. Dinosaurs remained the dominant land vertebrates, including Tyrannosaurus, Triceratops, hadrosaurs and enormous sauropods; birds evolved from small feathered theropods, and early mammals diversified in the undergrowth. In the seas swam mosasaurs, plesiosaurs and ammonites.

End of the Cretaceous

The period ended 66 million years ago with the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event, caused primarily by the impact of a large asteroid at Chicxulub on the Yucatán Peninsula, possibly compounded by Deccan Traps volcanism. The event eliminated the non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, mosasaurs and ammonites — about three-quarters of all species.
