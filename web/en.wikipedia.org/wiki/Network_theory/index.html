Network theory is the study of graphs as a representation of either symmetric or asymmetric relations between discrete objects. In computer science and network science, network theory is the part of graph theory: a network can be defined as a graph in which nodes and/or edges have attributes (e.g. names), and it is applied across disciplines including physics, biology, computer science, operations research, sociology, and economics.

History

Graph theory begins with Leonhard Euler's 1736 solution of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem. In the 20th century, network analysis grew out of sociometry (Jacob Moreno's sociograms), electrical circuit theory, and random graph theory founded by Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi (1959). Stanley Milgram's small-world experiment (1967) and Mark Granovetter's "strength of weak ties" (1973) were landmarks in social network analysis. A modern synthesis — often called network science — emerged in the late 1990s with the Watts–Strogatz model of small-world networks (1998) and the Barabási–Albert model of scale-free networks (1999).

Basic measures

Key analytical concepts include degree (number of connections of a node) and the degree distribution; paths and shortest paths, giving the network's diameter and average path length; centrality measures identifying important nodes (degree, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector/PageRank centrality); clustering coefficients measuring how interconnected a node's neighbours are; and community structure — groups of nodes densely connected internally but sparsely between groups, detected by algorithms such as modularity maximisation.

Network models

The Erdős–Rényi model connects node pairs with fixed probability, producing Poisson degree distributions. The Watts–Strogatz model interpolates between regular lattices and random graphs, capturing high clustering with short path lengths (small-world networks). The Barabási–Albert model grows networks by preferential attachment, generating power-law degree distributions (scale-free networks) with prominent hubs — a pattern observed in the World Wide Web, citation networks, and many biological networks.

Applications

Network theory underpins internet and transport routing, epidemiology (disease spread over contact networks), neuroscience (connectomics), systems biology (protein interaction networks), finance (interbank exposure), and social media analysis. Its methods also inform resilience studies: scale-free networks are robust to random failures but vulnerable to targeted attacks on hubs.
