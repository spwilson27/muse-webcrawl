Flexography

Flexography (often abbreviated to flexo) is a form of printing which utilizes a flexible relief plate. It is essentially a modern version of letterpress, evolved with high-speed rotary functionality, and is used for printing on almost any type of substrate, including plastic, metallic films, cellophane, and paper. It is widely used for printing on the non-porous substrates required for various types of food packaging, and is also well suited for printing large areas of solid color.

Process

A flexographic press transfers ink to the substrate from a flexible plate wrapped around a rotating cylinder. Ink is supplied to the plate by an anilox roll, a cylinder engraved with millions of tiny cells whose volume precisely meters the ink film; a doctor blade wipes the roll surface so only the cells carry ink. Fast-drying, low-viscosity inks — water-based, solvent-based, or UV-curable — allow very high press speeds. Plates are made from rubber or photopolymer, exposed through a negative or imaged directly by laser.

Applications

Flexography dominates flexible packaging — bags, pouches, labels, shrink sleeves, and corrugated boxes (often printed post-print on board) — and is also used for newspapers inserts, envelopes, gift wrap, wallpaper, and disposable paper products. Its ability to print on uneven and non-porous surfaces with quick-drying inks at high speed, with relatively inexpensive plates, makes it economical for medium and long runs.

History

The process developed from nineteenth-century rubber-stamp and aniline printing; early "aniline presses" used rubber plates and aniline dye inks. The name flexography was adopted in 1952 after an industry vote to replace the term "aniline printing," which had acquired negative connotations. Improvements in photopolymer plates, anilox technology, and servo-driven presses steadily raised print quality through the late twentieth century, allowing flexo to compete with gravure and offset lithography in packaging work.
