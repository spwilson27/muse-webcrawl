Biblical archaeology

[Image placeholder: Excavation in the southern Levant]

Biblical archaeology is an academic school within archaeology and biblical studies that involves the recovery and scientific investigation of the material remains of past cultures that can illuminate the periods and descriptions in the Bible, whether the Old Testament (Tanakh) or the New Testament, as well as the history and cosmogony of the Abrahamic religions.

The field developed in the 19th and early 20th centuries with figures such as Edward Robinson and William F. Albright, and landmark discoveries including the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Tel Dan stele, the Merneptah Stele and the Siloam Inscription. Modern practice is often framed more broadly as Syro-Palestinian or Levantine archaeology.

Debates

Scholars debate how closely the material record matches biblical narratives: "maximalists" give the text substantial historical weight, while "minimalists" argue that much of the Hebrew Bible was composed late and should not be read as straightforward history. Mainstream consensus holds a middle position for many periods, accepting, for example, the existence of the kingdoms of Israel and Judah while questioning the scale of the united monarchy.
