Part ofDiencephalon
SystemCentral nervous system
LocationDorsal diencephalon, either side of the third ventricle

The thalamus (from Greek θάλαμος, "chamber") is a large mass of gray matter in the dorsal diencephalon of the brain, situated between the cerebral cortex and the midbrain. It has several functions, the best known being the relay of sensory and motor signals to the cerebral cortex; it also plays roles in consciousness, sleep, and alertness.

Anatomy

The thalamus is a paired structure, one on each side of the third ventricle, the two often joined by the interthalamic adhesion. It is composed of many nuclei, each with reciprocal connections to specific cortical areas. Major nuclei include the lateral geniculate nucleus (vision), the medial geniculate nucleus (hearing), the ventral posterior nuclei (somatosensation), the mediodorsal nucleus (connected to the prefrontal cortex), and the pulvinar.

Function

Almost all sensory pathways — visual, auditory, somatosensory, and gustatory — synapse in the thalamus before reaching the cortex; smell is the notable exception. The thalamus also relays motor signals from the basal ganglia and cerebellum, and its intralaminar and reticular nuclei help regulate arousal and the sleep–wake cycle. The thalamic reticular nucleus gates the flow of information through the thalamus, for example during sleep spindles.

Clinical significance

Damage to the thalamus, often from stroke, can produce sensory loss, thalamic pain syndrome (Dejerine–Roussy syndrome), disorders of consciousness, and memory impairment when the mediodorsal or anterior nuclei are affected. Bilateral paramedian thalamic infarcts can cause coma. Thalamotomy and deep brain stimulation of thalamic targets are used in the treatment of tremor.

Development and evolution

The thalamus develops from the embryonic diencephalon. A conserved thalamocortical organisation is found across mammals, while analogous relay structures exist in other vertebrates.
