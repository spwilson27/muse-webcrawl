Mahmud of Ghazni (Persian: محمود غزنوی; 2 November 971 – 30 April 1030), fully Yamīn ad-Dawla Abul-Qāsim Maḥmūd ibn Sebüktegīn, was the founder of the Ghaznavid Empire and the first ruler to bear the title of Sultan. He ruled from 998 to 1030, building an empire that stretched from eastern Iran through Afghanistan to the Punjab in northwestern India. A brilliant military commander, he conducted some seventeen expeditions into India, plundering wealthy cities and temples — most famously Somnath in 1025–26 — and used the wealth to make his capital, Ghazna, a celebrated centre of Persian culture.

Rise and campaigns

Mahmud was the son of Sebuktigin, a Turkic slave commander who had taken control of Ghazna under the Samanids. After a succession struggle with his brother Ismail, Mahmud seized the throne in 998 and received investiture from the Abbasid caliph al-Qadir, who conferred the title Yamīn ad-Dawla ("Right Hand of the State"). He defeated the Hindu Shahi kingdom in a series of battles (1001–1013), annexing the Punjab, and raided deep into India: Thanesar, Mathura, Kannauj, Kalinjar, and finally the temple city of Somnath in Gujarat, whose shrine he sacked in 1026. In the west he annexed Khwarazm and much of eastern Persia, though his late campaigns against the Buyids overextended his state.

Patronage of culture

The plunder of India financed a court that attracted some of the greatest figures of the Persianate world: the poet Ferdowsi, who presented his epic Shahnameh to Mahmud (and was famously under-rewarded, according to legend); the polymath al-Biruni, brought to Ghazna after the conquest of Khwarazm, whose India remains a masterpiece of comparative scholarship; and the court poets ʿUnsuri and Farrukhi. The chronicler al-Utbi wrote the official history of the reign.

Legacy

Mahmud's memory is deeply contested: in Muslim tradition he is honoured as a champion of Islam and patron of Persian letters; in Indian memory he is the archetypal temple-plundering invader. He died in 1030, and his empire, weakened by succession disputes, soon lost its western territories to the Seljuks after the Battle of Dandanaqan (1040), though the Ghaznavids survived in the Punjab until 1186.
