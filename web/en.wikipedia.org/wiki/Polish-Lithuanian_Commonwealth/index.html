The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, formally the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, was a bi-confederal state formed by the Union of Lublin in 1569 between the Kingdom of Poland and the Grand Duchy of Lithuania. For over two centuries it was one of the largest and most populous countries of Europe, stretching at its greatest extent after the Truce of Deulino (1618) to nearly one million square kilometres, from the Baltic Sea almost to the Black Sea, with a multi-ethnic, multi-confessional population of 11–12 million.

Political system

The Commonwealth was a unique polity: an elective monarchy in which the king was chosen by the nobility (szlachta) — some 8–10% of the population, an unusually broad political nation for the era — assembled in a free election. The parliament, the Sejm, held legislative power, and from 1573 each elected king signed the Henrician Articles and his pacta conventa, limiting royal power. The Warsaw Confederation of 1573 guaranteed an unusual degree of religious tolerance. However, the liberum veto — the practice allowing any single deputy to dissolve a Sejm session and nullify its acts — became a symbol of paralysis in the 18th century and was exploited by neighbouring powers.

Golden age and wars

The Commonwealth reached its height in the early 17th century. Its army, famous for the winged hussars, defeated the Swedes at Kircholm (1605), the combined Muscovite-Tatar forces at Klushino (1610), and the Ottomans at Khotyn (1621 and 1673); in 1683, King John III Sobieski led the relief of Vienna against the Ottoman siege. From the mid-17th century, the Khmelnytsky Uprising (1648), the Swedish invasion known as the Deluge (1655–1660), and wars with Russia devastated the country, killing perhaps a third of the population.

Decline and partitions

Through the 18th century the Commonwealth fell under Russian domination and reform efforts came late: the Constitution of 3 May 1791 — Europe's first modern written constitution — abolished the liberum veto and strengthened the state, but provoked intervention. Russia, Prussia, and Austria partitioned the Commonwealth in three stages (1772, 1793, 1795), erasing it from the map until 1918.

Legacy

The Commonwealth is remembered for its republican traditions of the nobility, its religious toleration, its cultural flowering (the astronomer Copernicus pre-dated it, but its universities, literature in Polish and Latin, and multi-confessional society flourished under it), and as the shared heritage of modern Poland, Lithuania, Belarus, and Ukraine.
