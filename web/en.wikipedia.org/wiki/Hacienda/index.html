Hacienda

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The central house and courtyard of a Mexican hacienda.

A hacienda (Spanish: [aˈsjenda] or [aˈθjenda]) is an estate (or finca) in Spain and the former Spanish Empire. In Spanish America, haciendas were typically large landed estates that combined agriculture, livestock ranching, and sometimes mining or manufacturing, worked by a resident labour force. The word derives from the Spanish hacer ("to make" or "to do"), referring to the estate as a productive enterprise, and shares its root with the Portuguese fazenda.

Origins and development

Haciendas emerged in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as Spanish colonial land-grant systems such as the encomienda and repartimiento declined. Granted or purchased enormous tracts of land, hacendados built estates that became largely self-sufficient economic units, with their own chapels, stores (tiendas de raya), workshops, and quarters for workers. In Mexico, haciendas grew to dominate the countryside during the Porfiriato (1876–1911), when a small number of families and corporations controlled a majority of the country's arable land.

Labour system

Hacienda labour was typically supplied by peones, resident workers bound to the estate through debt peonage: wages paid in scrip redeemable only at the estate store kept workers in permanent debt. In the Andes the system took forms such as huasipungo and yanaconaje; in Yucatán, henequen haciendas relied on near-slavery conditions that became a scandal of the Porfirian era.

Decline

The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) was driven in part by land hunger, and the 1917 Constitution provided for the break-up of haciendas and redistribution into communal ejidos. Similar agrarian reforms reduced haciendas across Latin America in the twentieth century. Many great houses survive today as hotels, museums, and event venues, and "hacienda" has become a general architectural term for the Spanish colonial revival style.
