Pink noise

Pink noise (or 1/ f noise) is a signal or process with a frequency spectrum such that the power spectral density is inversely proportional to the frequency. In other words, pink noise carries equal energy per octave, so each octave carries the same amount of power. It increases in amplitude at lower frequencies and is intermediate between white noise (equal power per hertz) and red noise (power proportional to 1/f²).

Properties

Because every octave contains the same energy, pink noise sounds balanced to human hearing, which perceives frequencies logarithmically. White noise, by contrast, sounds hissy and dominated by high frequencies. Pink noise is widely used as a test signal in audio engineering and acoustics, and as a soothing background sound for sleep and masking.

Occurrence

1/f-like spectra occur in an astonishing variety of systems: electronic devices (where it is known as flicker noise), the loudness and pitch fluctuations of music, heartbeats, neural activity, traffic flow, and the brightness of some astronomical sources. No single mechanism explains all cases; proposed causes include systems of many relaxation processes with a wide distribution of time constants.

Generation

Pink noise can be generated by filtering white noise with a −3 dB-per-octave filter, or by algorithms such as the Voss–McCartney algorithm, which sums random values updated at octave-related rates.
