Bishōjo (Japanese: 美少女, literally "beautiful girl") is a Japanese term used to refer to young and pretty girls, most frequently in the context of anime and manga character design. The term is distinct from bijin, which generally describes adult women, and from bishōnen, its male counterpart denoting beautiful boys.

Characteristics

Bishōjo characters are typically drawn with large eyes, slender builds, long hair and youthful facial features, conventions that developed through the shōjo manga tradition and were popularised by artists such as Osamu Tezuka and later character designers of the 1980s and 1990s. The aesthetic is closely associated with the concept of moe, an affective response evoked by fictional characters, and with the broader kawaii (cute) culture in Japan.

History

The stylistic roots of the bishōjo lie in early twentieth-century Japanese girls' magazine illustration, particularly the lyrical art of illustrators such as Yumeji Takehiko and Jun'ichi Nakahara. In manga, the large-eyed style was codified by Tezuka and the Year 24 Group of women shōjo artists. During the 1980s, the bishōjo became central to genres aimed at male audiences, including dating simulation games and "bishōjo games", as well as model kits and figurines of female characters.

In media and criticism

Bishōjo-style artwork is a major element of Japanese popular culture industries, appearing in advertising, video games and collectible merchandise. Scholars such as Hiroki Azuma have analysed the consumption of such characters in terms of "database consumption", while critics have debated the sexualisation of young-looking characters and its regulation, both in Japan and internationally.
