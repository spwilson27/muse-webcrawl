The Pentarchy (from the Greek Pentarchia, "rule of five") is a model of Church organisation formulated in the laws of Emperor Justinian I (527–565) of the Roman Empire. In this model, the Christian Church is governed by the heads (patriarchs) of the five major episcopal sees of the Roman Empire: Rome, Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem.

The idea came about because of the political and ecclesiastical prominence of these five sees, but the concept of their universal and exclusive authority was attached to the councils that recognised their precedence. The Pentarchy was first legally expressed in Justinian's legislation, in particular in Novella 131 of 545, which granted ecclesiastical rank to the five sees and declared that a synod of the five patriarchs governed the Church.

Development of the concept

The Council of Nicaea in 325 recognised the special authority of Rome, Alexandria and Antioch. The First Council of Constantinople (381) raised Constantinople to second place after Rome because it was "New Rome". The Council of Chalcedon (451) elevated Jerusalem to a patriarchate and, in its controversial 28th canon, confirmed Constantinople's privileges. The Quinisext Council (691–692) in canon 36 gave the definitive ordering: Rome first, then Constantinople, Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem.

Challenges and decline

The theory was never fully accepted in the West, where the Bishop of Rome claimed a unique primacy by divine right rather than by conciliar or imperial grant. The Muslim conquests of the seventh century left Alexandria, Antioch and Jerusalem under Islamic rule, greatly weakening the system in practice. The East–West Schism of 1054 then split Rome from the other four sees, effectively ending the Pentarchy as a functioning model, although it remained influential in Eastern Orthodox canon law and ecclesiology.

Modern usage

In modern Eastern Orthodox theology, the term is sometimes revived to describe the five ancient patriarchates, with the Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople acting as first among equals. Some theologians have proposed a renewed "pentarchy" of the heads of autocephalous churches, though this has no formal standing.
