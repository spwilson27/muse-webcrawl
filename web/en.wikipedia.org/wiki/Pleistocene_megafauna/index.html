Pleistocene megafauna

Pleistocene megafauna are the large animals that lived during the Pleistocene epoch (about 2.58 million to 11,700 years ago) and, in many regions, went extinct during the Quaternary extinction event at the end of the epoch. "Megafauna" is generally taken to mean animals with an adult body weight of at least 44 kg (97 lb), with some definitions using thresholds of several hundred kilograms or a tonne.

Representative animals

The Pleistocene world supported a far richer assemblage of giant mammals than today's. In the Americas these included woolly and Columbian mammoths, mastodons, giant ground sloths such as Megatherium, glyptodonts (giant armadillo relatives), saber-toothed cats (Smilodon), the American lion, dire wolves, short-faced bears, giant beavers, and native horses and camels. Eurasia had woolly rhinoceroses, cave lions, cave bears, cave hyenas, giant deer (Irish elk), aurochs, and steppe bison. Australia had Diprotodon (a rhinoceros-sized wombat relative), giant kangaroos, the marsupial lion Thylacoleo, and huge flightless birds such as Genyornis; Madagascar and New Zealand had giant lemurs, elephant birds, and moa surviving into the late Holocene.

Extinction

Between roughly 50,000 and 10,000 years ago, most large terrestrial mammals outside Africa disappeared. The timing differed by continent: Australia lost its megafauna around 50,000–40,000 years ago, the Americas around 13,000–11,000 years ago, and islands such as Wrangel Island retained mammoths until about 4,000 years ago. Two main explanations have been debated: climate change associated with the last glacial cycle and the warming that ended it, and human impacts—hunting ("overkill," proposed by Paul S. Martin), habitat alteration, and fire. Many researchers now favour a combination, in which climate-stressed populations were pushed to extinction by even modest human hunting, with effects magnified by the animals' slow reproduction.

Ecological consequences

The loss of megafauna reshaped ecosystems: vegetation structure changed without giant browsers, nutrient transport declined, some plants lost their seed dispersers (the "anachronistic fruits" such as avocado relatives), and predator and scavenger communities collapsed. These ideas underlie proposals for Pleistocene rewilding—introducing modern relatives of extinct species to restore lost ecological functions.
