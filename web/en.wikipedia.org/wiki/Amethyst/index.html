Amethyst

Amethyst is a violet variety of quartz. The name comes from the Koine Greek ἀμέθυστος amethystos from ἀ- a-, "not" and μεθύσκω methysko / μεθώ metho, "intoxicate", a reference to the belief that the stone protected its owner from drunkenness. Ancient Greeks wore amethyst and carved drinking vessels from it in the belief that it would prevent intoxication.

Structure and colour

Amethyst is a variety of silicon dioxide (SiO₂) with the hardness of quartz, 7 on the Mohs scale. Its violet colour is due to irradiation, impurities of iron (Fe⁴⁺) and in some cases other transition metals, and the presence of trace elements, which result in complex crystal lattice substitutions. The colour ranges from pale lilac-pink to deep purple; the most prized hues, historically from Siberian deposits, are deep purple with red flashes. Exposure to heat can change amethyst's colour: much commercial citrine is produced by heating amethyst, and heating can also produce a green variety sold as prasiolite.

Occurrence

Until the 19th century amethyst was mainly obtained from Sri Lanka, India, and the Urals, and it ranked among the precious cardinal gems. Large deposits discovered in Brazil, where amethyst occurs in volcanic geodes, made it abundant and much cheaper. Major producers today include Brazil, Uruguay, Zambia, and South Korea; geodes with amethyst crystals line many basalt flows in the Paraná traps of South America.

History and use

Amethyst has been used in jewellery since ancient Egyptian times; it appears in royal collections and bishops' rings, and was among the gems in the British Crown Jewels. It is the traditional birthstone for February and is associated with the astrological sign Pisces. In the Christian tradition, amethyst was linked to piety and celibacy, and in feng shui and modern crystal healing it is popularly associated with calm and clarity.
