White Lotus Rebellion
Date:1794/1796 – 1804
Location:Central China (Hubei, Sichuan, Shaanxi)
Belligerents:Qing dynasty vs. White Lotus sect rebels
Result:Qing victory at enormous fiscal cost
Casualties:Estimated ~100,000 or more (some estimates far higher)

The White Lotus Rebellion (1794/1796–1804) was an uprising against the Qing dynasty of China, led by adherents of the White Lotus, a millenarian religious movement venerating the Eternal Venerable Mother and expecting the coming of the Maitreya Buddha. Centred in the mountainous border region of Hubei, Sichuan and Shaanxi, it took the Qing nearly a decade and ruinous expense to suppress.

Background

White Lotus societies had periodically rebelled since the Yuan dynasty. In the 1790s Qing officials, extorting money by prosecuting alleged sectarians, provoked the very revolt they claimed to prevent. When the leader Liu Zhixie was arrested, followers rose in 1796; a first flashpoint had come in 1794.

Course of the rebellion

Rebel bands, loosely inspired by leaders such as Wang Cong'er — a charismatic woman commander — used guerrilla tactics in terrain where the Qing Banner and Green Standard armies proved slow, corrupt and ineffective. At its height the rebellion tied down forces across five provinces.

Suppression and militias

The court turned to local gentry-led militias (tuanlian) and a fortification strategy: walled villages, scorched earth and population concentration, a model later reused against the Taiping. The rebellion was finally extinguished in 1804.

Consequences

The war cost the treasury an estimated 200 million taels — several years of revenue — depleting the reserves accumulated under the Qianlong Emperor. It exposed the decay of the hereditary armies, expanded the role of regional militias (a precedent with long consequences), and is often taken as a turning point toward the Qing dynasty's 19th-century decline.
