Epistasis

Epistasis is the phenomenon in genetics in which the effect of a gene mutation is dependent on the presence or absence of mutations in one or more other genes — in other words, the effect of one gene is masked or modified by another. A gene whose phenotype is expressed is called epistatic, while one whose phenotype is altered or suppressed is called hypostatic.

History

The term was coined by William Bateson in 1909, adapting a concept from his earlier work with Reginald Punnett on comb shape in chickens. Classical examples include coat colour in Labrador retrievers, where a locus determining pigment deposition (the E locus) masks the black/brown locus: dogs homozygous recessive at E are yellow regardless of the other locus.

Types

  • Recessive epistasis — a recessive allele at one locus masks both alleles at another (9:3:4 ratio in a dihybrid cross).
  • Dominant epistasis — a dominant allele masks another locus (12:3:1 ratio).
  • Duplicate gene action / complementary gene action — interactions giving 15:1 or 9:7 ratios.

Quantitative genetics and evolution

In quantitative genetics, epistasis refers statistically to deviations from additivity of genetic effects. R. A. Fisher and Sewall Wright debated its evolutionary importance: Wright's shifting balance theory gave epistasis a central role, arguing that populations could cross fitness valleys through interacting gene combinations, whereas Fisher saw selection acting mainly on additive effects. Sign epistasis — where a mutation is beneficial on one genetic background and deleterious on another — constrains the paths evolution can take through fitness landscapes, and is important in the evolution of antibiotic resistance and protein function.
