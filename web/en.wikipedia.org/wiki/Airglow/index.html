Airglow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Green airglow band seen from the International Space Station

Airglow (also called nightglow) is the faint emission of light by a planetary atmosphere. In Earth's case, it makes the night sky never completely dark, even far from cities: a permanent, roughly uniform glow about 10 % as bright as a starlit sky.

Mechanism

Airglow results from several processes in the upper atmosphere. During the day, solar ultraviolet radiation excites and dissociates molecules; at night, the stored energy is released through chemiluminescence as atoms recombine — for example, atomic oxygen recombining to form O2, and reactions involving hydroxyl (OH) radicals producing emission in the near infrared. The strongest visible feature is the green oxygen line at 557.7 nm, emitted around 90–100 km altitude, with a weaker red line at 630 nm higher up.

Observation

Airglow was identified as an atmospheric phenomenon by Anders Ångström in 1868. Its wavelike structures reveal gravity waves propagating through the mesosphere. For astronomers, airglow is a significant source of sky background; for astronauts it forms a thin glowing band along the limb of the Earth.

Effect on astronomy

Even at the darkest sites, airglow limits ground-based optical observations; near-infrared telescopes are strongly affected by OH emission, which varies over the night.
