Vote splitting

Vote splitting is an electoral effect in which the presence of two or more similar candidates (or parties) divides the votes of a group of like-minded electors between them, allowing a candidate opposed by that group to win with a plurality. It is the most familiar manifestation of the spoiler effect and a central pathology of plurality (first-past-the-post) voting.

Mechanics

Suppose 55 percent of voters prefer either of two similar candidates, A1 and A2, to a rival B, but the group's support divides 30–25 between A1 and A2. B then wins with 45 percent even though a majority preferred someone else. The problem worsens as more similar candidates enter; it is related to the failure of the independence of clones criterion in social choice theory: a voting system fails this criterion if adding a near-identical copy of a candidate changes the winner.

Examples and responses

Frequently cited examples include the 2000 United States presidential election in Florida, where Ralph Nader's vote total exceeded George W. Bush's margin over Al Gore, and numerous Canadian and British constituency races in which competing parties of the centre-left divided their vote. Political actors respond to splitting with strategic voting, pre-election alliances and stand-down agreements, fusion candidacies, primary elections that consolidate a party behind one nominee, and "clone" candidacies deliberately fielded to split an opponent's vote. Electoral systems such as ranked-choice (instant-runoff) voting, approval voting, and proportional representation are often advocated as structural remedies; under proportional systems vote splitting is of much less consequence because seats are shared in proportion to votes.
