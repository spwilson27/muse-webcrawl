Transmissible spongiform encephalopathy

Spongiform change in brain tissue under the microscope.

Transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), also known as prion diseases, are a group of progressive, incurable and invariably fatal conditions that affect the brain and nervous system of many animals, including humans. The causative agent is believed to be a prion — a misfolded form of a normal cellular protein, the prion protein (PrP), which propagates by inducing other PrP molecules to adopt the abnormal conformation.

Examples

  • Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (CJD) and its variant form vCJD in humans
  • Kuru, spread by funerary cannibalism among the Fore people of Papua New Guinea
  • Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, "mad cow disease") in cattle
  • Scrapie in sheep and goats
  • Chronic wasting disease in deer and elk
  • Fatal familial insomnia and Gerstmann–Sträussler–Scheinker syndrome, inherited human forms

Features

TSEs have very long incubation periods — years to decades — followed by rapid neurological decline with dementia, ataxia and myoclonus. Brain tissue develops characteristic sponge-like vacuolation. There is no immune response to the agent, no effective treatment, and prions resist conventional sterilisation. The prion hypothesis, long controversial, was championed by Stanley Prusiner, who received the Nobel Prize in 1997.
