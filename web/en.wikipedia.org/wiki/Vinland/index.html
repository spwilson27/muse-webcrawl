Vinland, also rendered Vínland, was an area of coastal North America explored and briefly settled by Norse Vikings around the year 1000 CE, some five centuries before Columbus. Described in the Icelandic sagas, it lay west of Greenland beyond Helluland (Baffin Island) and Markland (Labrador), and was named for its wild grapes or berries — vín in Old Norse.

The sagas

Two sagas — the Saga of the Greenlanders and the Saga of Erik the Red — tell, with differing details, of voyages by Leif Erikson, his brother Thorvald, and Thorfinn Karlsefni, who attempted a settlement with livestock and families. The Norse encountered Indigenous peoples they called Skrælings, traded briefly, then fought; within a few years the outposts were abandoned. Freydís Eiríksdóttir and the child Snorri Thorfinnsson — the first known European born in the Americas — figure in the accounts.

Archaeology

Long doubted, the sagas were vindicated in the 1960s when Helge and Anne Stine Ingstad excavated a Norse site at L'Anse aux Meadows on Newfoundland's northern tip: turf halls, a smithy, boat-repair debris and a bronze ring pin dated to about 1000 CE; tree-ring analysis has pinpointed Norse wood-felling there to precisely 1021 CE. Butternuts found at the site, which grow no further north than New Brunswick, show the Norse ranged south into the Gulf of St Lawrence — the probable "Vinland" heartland of grapes and self-sown wheat.
