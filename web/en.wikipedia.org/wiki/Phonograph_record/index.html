Phonograph record

A phonograph record (also called a gramophone record, vinyl record or simply vinyl) is an analogue sound storage medium in the form of a flat disc with an inscribed, modulated spiral groove, usually played on a phonograph (record player) using a stylus.

History

The disc format was developed by Emile Berliner in the late 1880s as an alternative to Edison's phonograph cylinders. Early records were made of shellac and played at about 78 rpm. Columbia introduced the 12-inch Long Play (LP) microgroove record in 1948, and RCA Victor the 7-inch 45 rpm single in 1949; vinyl (polyvinyl chloride) replaced brittle shellac.

Formats

Common sizes are 12-inch LPs and 10-inch records at 33⅓ rpm, and 7-inch singles and EPs at 45 rpm. Sound is encoded in the groove walls, in stereo as independent left and right channels, and equalised using the RIAA curve.

Decline and revival

Compact discs displaced records in the 1980s and 1990s, but vinyl underwent a sustained revival from the 2000s onward, with sales eventually surpassing CDs again in several markets.
