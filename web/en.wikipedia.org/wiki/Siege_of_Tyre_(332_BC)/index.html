The Siege of Tyre (January–August 332 BC) was a siege by Alexander the Great of the Phoenician island city of Tyre during his conquest of the Persian Empire. The Tyrians refused Alexander entry to sacrifice in the temple of Melqart on their island, offering instead submission without occupation. Determined not to leave a hostile naval base in his rear, Alexander besieged the city for seven months, built a great mole (causeway) out to the island, and stormed it in August 332 BC.

Background

Tyre consisted of a mainland town (Old Tyre) and a fortified island city some 800 metres offshore, whose walls were said to rise 150 feet from the sea. Its fleet served Persia, whose squadrons still threatened Alexander's communications. When Tyrian envoys refused his demand to sacrifice within the island temple—effectively refusing him entry—Alexander resolved on a siege that his own officers considered nearly impossible.

The mole and the naval blockade

Using rubble from Old Tyre, Macedonian engineers drove a causeway into the sea; as the water deepened, the work was harassed by Tyrian divers, boats and a fireship loaded with combustibles that destroyed the siege towers at its head. Alexander's fortunes changed when the Phoenician and Cypriot fleets, whose cities had submitted, came over to him, giving him naval superiority. With ships carrying rams and towers he blockaded both harbours and battered the walls from the sea, while the completed mole—whose silted remains still join Tyre to the mainland today—carried his land artillery forward.

The assault

In late July or August, ship-borne rams breached the southern wall. In a coordinated assault Alexander's hypaspists, led by Admetus (who was killed), stormed the breach while other troops seized the walls and harbours. The Tyrians fought to the last around the shrine of Agenor. About 6,000 were killed in the fighting, 2,000 crucified along the shore, and some 30,000 inhabitants sold into slavery; those who had taken refuge in the temple of Melqart, including King Azemilcus and Carthaginian envoys, were spared.

Aftermath

The fall of Tyre, long prophesied in biblical tradition against the proud city, removed the last Phoenician naval base and cleared Alexander's path to Gaza and Egypt. He sacrificed to Melqart in the temple he had been refused—an irony noted by the sources, chiefly Arrian, Diodorus, Curtius and Plutarch.
