Typography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Typography is the art and technique of arranging type to make written language legible, readable and appealing when displayed. The arrangement of type involves selecting typefaces, point sizes, line lengths, line-spacing (leading), and letter-spacing (tracking), as well as adjusting the space within letters pairs (kerning). The term typography is also applied to the style, arrangement, and appearance of the letters, numbers, and symbols created by the process.

Type design is a closely related craft, sometimes considered part of typography; most typographers do not design typefaces, and some type designers do not consider themselves typographers. Typography also may be used as an ornamental and decorative device, unrelated to the communication of information.

History

Typography traces its origins to punches and dies used to make seals and coins in antiquity. Movable type was invented in China by Bi Sheng around 1040 CE using ceramic pieces, and metal movable type was developed in Korea by the 13th century. In Europe, Johannes Gutenberg's printing press (c. 1440) made mass-produced books possible, and the roman and italic typefaces developed by printers such as Nicolas Jenson and Aldus Manutius set conventions still in use.

The Industrial Revolution brought mechanised typesetting (the Linotype and Monotype machines), display typefaces and advertising typography; in the twentieth century, phototypesetting and then digital type — beginning with desktop publishing in the 1980s — transformed the field again. Swiss or International Typographic Style, with its grids, sans-serif typefaces such as Helvetica, and asymmetric layouts, became dominant in postwar graphic design.

Principles

Legibility concerns the clarity of individual characters; readability, the ease with which blocks of text can be read. Typographers manage hierarchy through size, weight and position; alignment (left, right, justified or centred); and the micro-typographic details of hyphenation, ligatures, small capitals and old-style figures. The modernist dictum associated with Beatrice Warde's 1930 essay "The Crystal Goblet" holds that good typography should be invisible — the text, not the container, should hold the reader's attention.

Digital typography

Modern typography uses digital formats such as TrueType, OpenType and variable fonts, and on the web is controlled through CSS. Web typography must additionally handle responsiveness, variable screen resolutions and font loading performance.
