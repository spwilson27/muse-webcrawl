Necrosis

Necrosis (from Ancient Greek νέκρωσις, "death") is a form of cell injury which results in the premature death of cells in living tissue by autolysis. It is caused by factors external to the cell or tissue, such as infection, toxins, or trauma, which result in the unregulated digestion of cell components. In contrast, apoptosis is a naturally occurring, programmed form of cell death. Necrosis is almost always detrimental and can be fatal, because necrotic cells release their contents into surrounding tissue, provoking inflammation and further damage.

Causes

Necrosis can result from a variety of external insults: oxygen deprivation (hypoxia/ischemia), physical trauma, extreme temperatures, chemical agents, infectious pathogens, and immunological reactions. It may also follow reperfusion injury when blood flow returns to ischemic tissue.

Patterns

Pathologists classify necrosis into morphological patterns:

  • Coagulative necrosis — tissue architecture is preserved for a time; typical of infarcts in solid organs such as the heart and kidney.
  • Liquefactive necrosis — tissue is digested into a liquid mass; seen in bacterial abscesses and in the brain after infarction.
  • Caseous necrosis — cheese-like, friable tissue characteristic of tuberculosis granulomas.
  • Fat necrosis — destruction of adipose tissue, e.g. in acute pancreatitis, where lipases produce chalky saponification deposits.
  • Fibrinoid necrosis — seen in immune-mediated vascular injury.
  • Gangrenous necrosis — clinical term for extensive necrosis of a limb, dry (coagulative) or wet (with superimposed infection).

Cellular changes

Necrotic cells swell, their membranes rupture, and nuclei undergo pyknosis (shrinkage and darkening), karyorrhexis (fragmentation), and karyolysis (dissolution). Loss of membrane integrity releases intracellular enzymes — the basis for measuring markers such as troponin after myocardial infarction — and damage-associated molecular patterns that recruit inflammatory cells.

Clinical significance and treatment

Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: restoring perfusion, removing the toxin or infected tissue (debridement), antibiotics, or amputation for unsalvageable gangrene. Dead tissue cannot recover, so management focuses on preventing extension and secondary infection. Research into regulated necrosis pathways, such as necroptosis mediated by RIPK1/RIPK3 and MLKL, has identified potential drug targets in inflammatory and ischemic disease.
