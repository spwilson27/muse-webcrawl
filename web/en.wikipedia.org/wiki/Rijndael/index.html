Rijndael (pronounced "rain-dahl") is a symmetric key block cipher designed by Belgian cryptographers Vincent Rijmen and Joan Daemen. The name is a portmanteau of the designers' surnames. In 2001, Rijndael was selected by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) as the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), following a five-year open competition to replace the aging Data Encryption Standard (DES).

Design

Rijndael is a substitution–permutation network operating on a 4×4 array of bytes called the state. Each round consists of four steps: SubBytes (a nonlinear byte substitution using an S-box based on inversion in the finite field GF(28)), ShiftRows (a transposition of the rows of the state), MixColumns (a mixing operation over the columns), and AddRoundKey (XOR with a round key derived from the key schedule). The number of rounds depends on the key length: 10, 12, or 14 rounds for 128-, 192-, and 256-bit keys.

Rijndael vs. AES

Rijndael as originally specified supports block and key sizes in multiples of 32 bits from 128 to 256 bits; the AES standard fixes the block size at 128 bits while retaining the three key lengths. Rijndael is implemented in hardware and software worldwide, protecting everything from banking transactions to classified government information.
