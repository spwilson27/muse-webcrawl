Borre style

The Borre style is a phase of Viking art, named after a set of gilt-bronze bridle mounts from a ship burial at Borre in Vestfold, Norway. Dated to roughly the mid-9th to the late 10th century, it is the first fully developed Scandinavian animal style of the Viking Age, succeeding the earlier Oseberg style and overlapping with the succeeding Jelling style.

[ Image: Gilt-bronze bridle mount from Borre in the ring-chain pattern characteristic of the style ]

Characteristics

The hallmark of the style is the ring-chain motif: a symmetrical interlace of bands forming circles and lozenges, often filling the whole field of a brooch or mount. Animal ornament typically shows a single ribbon-like beast with a triangular or mask-like head, spiral hips and "gripping" paws that seize the frame or its own body — the origin of the "gripping beast" motif that runs through later Viking art. The style was used on jewellery, weapon fittings, harness mounts and woodwork, and spread across the Viking world from Iceland to Russia.

Development

Art historians place the Borre style between the Oseberg style (c. 775–875) and the Jelling style (c. 870–985), with considerable chronological overlap. Its tight, geometric interlace contrasts with the freer ribbon animals of Oseberg art, and it absorbed influences from Carolingian and Insular ornament seen in traded and looted objects.
