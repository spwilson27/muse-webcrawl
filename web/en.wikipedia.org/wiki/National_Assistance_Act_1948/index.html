National Assistance Act 1948

The National Assistance Act 1948 (11 & 12 Geo. 6. c. 29) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom passed by Clement Attlee's Labour government. Together with the National Insurance Act 1946 and the National Health Service Act 1946, it completed the legislative architecture of the post-war welfare state envisaged by the Beveridge Report. Its first section formally repealed the old Poor Law, whose origins lay in the Elizabethan statute of 1601, and proclaimed that the existing poor law "shall cease to have effect".

Provisions

The Act created the National Assistance Board to provide means-tested financial assistance to people aged 16 or over whose resources were insufficient to meet their needs and who were not in full-time work — a safety net beneath the contributory national insurance scheme for those who fell through it, including people who had not paid enough contributions. It also gave local authorities duties and powers to provide residential accommodation for people who, because of age, illness, disability or "any other circumstances", needed care and attention not otherwise available to them, and to promote the welfare of disabled people. The Act's Part III accommodation duties famously replaced the workhouse system, though many former workhouse buildings continued in use as old people's homes for years afterwards.

Operation and later fate

National Assistance came into force on 5 July 1948, the "Appointed Day" shared with the National Health Service. The National Assistance Board's functions passed in 1966 to the new Ministry of Social Security as "supplementary benefit", and the scheme was recast again as income support in 1988. The benefits provisions of the 1948 Act were thereby superseded, but its local authority welfare provisions — in particular section 21, the duty to provide residential accommodation — remained in force for decades and underpinned modern social care law in England and Wales until replaced by the Care Act 2014 (in force April 2015); related provisions survive longer in Scotland. The Act also underlies the duty used by destitute asylum seekers and people with no recourse to public funds who need care and attention.
