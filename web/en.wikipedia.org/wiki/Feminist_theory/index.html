Feminist theory

Feminist theory is the extension of feminism into theoretical discourse, aiming to understand the nature of gender inequality and women's social roles, experiences, and interests. It emerged from the women's movement and draws on disciplines including anthropology, sociology, economics, literary criticism, psychoanalysis, and philosophy.

Branches

Major strands include liberal feminism, concerned with legal and political equality; radical feminism, which identifies patriarchy as a fundamental system of power; Marxist and socialist feminism, linking gender to capitalism; psychoanalytic feminism; poststructural and postmodern feminism, associated with thinkers such as Judith Butler and her theory of gender performativity; and Black and postcolonial feminisms, which insist on intersectionality — a term coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw.

Key texts

Foundational works include Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792), Simone de Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949), Betty Friedan's The Feminine Mystique (1963), and bell hooks's Ain't I a Woman? (1981).
