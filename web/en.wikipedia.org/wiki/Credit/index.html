Credit

Credit (from Latin creditum, "that which is entrusted") is the trust which allows one party to provide money or resources to another party, wherein the second party does not immediately reimburse the first but promises either to repay or return those resources at a later date. The resources provided may be financial (a loan) or may consist of goods or services (consumer credit).

Types

Major forms include revolving credit (credit cards, lines of credit), instalment credit (mortgages, auto and student loans), trade credit between businesses, and public credit issued as government bonds. Credit may be secured by collateral or unsecured, with price — the interest rate — reflecting the lender's assessment of risk, often informed by a borrower's credit score and credit history maintained by credit bureaus.

Economic role

Credit creation by banks is central to modern monetary systems: lending creates deposits and expands the money supply. Credit allows investment and consumption to be smoothed over time but also enables over-indebtedness and asset bubbles; excessive credit growth is a recurring feature of financial crises, from 1929 to the 2008 financial crisis. The word also survives in accounting, where a credit is an entry on the right-hand side of a ledger, the mirror of a debit.

History

Credit long predates coinage: Mesopotamian tablets record grain loans around 2000 BC, and credit instruments such as bills of exchange underpinned medieval and early modern trade despite religious restrictions on usury.
