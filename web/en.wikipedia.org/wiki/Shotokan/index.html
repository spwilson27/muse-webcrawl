Shotokan (松濤館, Shōtōkan) is a style of karate developed from various martial arts by Gichin Funakoshi and his son Gigo (Yoshitaka) Funakoshi. It is one of the four major traditional styles of Japanese karate, along with Goju-ryu, Shito-ryu and Wado-ryu, and is practised worldwide. The name comes from Funakoshi's pen name Shōtō ("waving pines"), combined with kan ("house" or "hall") — the name of the first permanent dojo built for him in Tokyo in 1936.

Characteristics

Shotokan training is traditionally divided into three parts: kihon (basics), kata (pre-arranged forms) and kumite (sparring). Techniques emphasise long, deep stances that build leg strength and stability, powerful linear strikes delivered with hip rotation, and decisive single techniques — an expression of the principle of ikken hissatsu, "to finish with one blow". The curriculum includes some 26 kata, from the Heian series for beginners to advanced forms such as Kanku-dai, Bassai-dai and Tekki.

History

Funakoshi, a schoolteacher from Okinawa where karate had developed from indigenous fighting methods and Chinese influences, demonstrated karate on mainland Japan in 1922 and remained to teach it, adapting it to Japanese budo culture. His son Gigo modernised the style, introducing longer stances and kicking techniques such as the side thrust kick. After Funakoshi's death, the Japan Karate Association — founded in 1949 with Funakoshi as chief instructor emeritus — spread Shotokan internationally through a celebrated instructor-training programme, though disputes after his death produced several rival organisations.

Philosophy

Funakoshi codified the nijū kun, twenty guiding principles, and the dojo kun — maxims enjoining character, sincerity, effort, etiquette and restraint from violent behaviour. He famously wrote that "karate begins and ends with courtesy" and that there is "no first attack in karate" (karate ni sente nashi).
