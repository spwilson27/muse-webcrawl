Sony BMG copy protection rootkit scandal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sony BMG copy protection rootkit scandal was a controversy in 2005 surrounding copy protection software shipped on music compact discs by Sony BMG Music Entertainment. Around 22 million CDs in the United States were sold with software — Extended Copy Protection (XCP) by First4Internet, and MediaMax by SunnComm — that automatically installed on Windows computers when the discs were played, restricted copying, and, in the case of XCP, used rootkit techniques to hide itself, opening security vulnerabilities that malware soon exploited.

Discovery

On 31 October 2005, computer security researcher Mark Russinovich published an analysis showing that a copy-protected CD had installed a rootkit on his system: device drivers that concealed files, processes, and registry entries whose names began with "$sys$", intercepted system calls, and could not be removed safely by ordinary means. The software also "phoned home" to Sony servers. Russinovich's findings were widely reported, and within days researchers showed that the hiding mechanism was being abused by trojans, while Sony BMG initially downplayed the issue — executive Thomas Hesse remarked that "most people, I think, don't even know what a rootkit is."

Response and consequences

Sony BMG first released an uninstaller that itself created further vulnerabilities, then suspended production of XCP discs in November 2005 and recalled affected titles, offering exchanges and MP3 downloads. Class-action lawsuits in the United States were settled with refunds and free downloads; the US Federal Trade Commission reached a settlement in 2007 barring the company from such practices, and several states pursued their own actions. The affair is a canonical case study in digital rights management backlash: it damaged consumer trust, influenced legislation and platform security policies, and demonstrated that media companies' anti-piracy software could be treated as malware by security vendors.
