A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle

A Drunk Man Looks at the Thistle is a long poem by Scottish poet Hugh MacDiarmid (Christopher Murray Grieve), published in 1926. Written largely in a synthetic form of Scots — a literary language MacDiarmid assembled from dialects and older Scots literature, sometimes called "Synthetic Scots" or Lallans — it is regarded as the masterpiece of the Scottish Renaissance in literature and one of the great long poems of twentieth-century modernism.

Content and style

The poem is framed as the drunken reverie of a man lying on a hillside at night, gazing at the thistle — the national emblem of Scotland — and meditating on Scotland's past, present and spiritual condition. Across some 2,685 lines, the poem moves through philosophy, politics, religion, sexuality, literary satire and cosmic reflection, mixing bawdy humour with passages of high lyricism and learning. It attacks Scottish cultural inferiority (the "Scottish cringe") and argues for a renewed, intellectually ambitious Scottish national culture.

Reception

Though initially controversial for its language and frankness, the poem established MacDiarmid's reputation and became a touchstone of modern Scottish literature, influencing generations of Scots-language writers.
