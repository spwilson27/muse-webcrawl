Overview

Chytridiomycosis is an infectious disease of amphibians caused by the chytrid fungi Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd, identified in the late 1990s) and Batrachochytrium salamandrivorans (Bsal, described 2013). The fungi infect keratinised skin — vital to amphibian water and electrolyte balance — causing thickened skin, lethargy and death, often by disrupting cardiac electrolyte levels.

Global impact

Bd has been detected in over 1,000 amphibian species and is implicated in the decline of at least 500 species and the presumed extinction of around 90 — including the golden toad of Costa Rica and Australia's gastric-brooding frogs — making it the worst disease-driven biodiversity loss known. It spread globally with the trade in African clawed frogs (historically shipped for pregnancy tests) and American bullfrogs. Bsal, from Asia, devastated European fire salamanders and prompted import bans (e.g. the US Lacey Act listing of salamanders, 2016). Conservation responses include captive assurance colonies, probiotic skin-bacteria treatments, antifungal treatment of individuals and habitat manipulation.
