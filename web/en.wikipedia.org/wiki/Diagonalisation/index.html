Diagonalisation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia  ·  Redirected here: for the matrix factorisation, see also Diagonalizable matrix

[Image: Diagonalisation in an eigenbasis]

Diagonalisation is the process of converting a matrix or linear operator into diagonal form, usually by a change of basis. In linear algebra, a square matrix A is diagonalised when there exists an invertible matrix P and a diagonal matrix D such that

A = PDP−1.

The columns of P are then eigenvectors of A, and the diagonal entries of D are the corresponding eigenvalues. Diagonalisation reduces many computations — powers of a matrix, exponentials, and solutions of linear differential equations — to simple operations on the diagonal entries: Ak = PDkP−1.

The diagonalisation theorem

An n × n matrix over a field is diagonalisable if and only if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors, equivalently if the sum of the dimensions of its eigenspaces equals n. A sufficient condition is that the matrix has n distinct eigenvalues. Not every matrix is diagonalisable: a defective matrix, such as a nontrivial Jordan block, lacks a full eigenbasis, and one instead uses the Jordan normal form.

Simultaneous diagonalisation

A family of matrices can be simultaneously diagonalised by a single change of basis if and only if the matrices commute pairwise (given each is diagonalisable). This fact is fundamental in quantum mechanics, where commuting observables can be measured simultaneously.

Diagonal arguments

The term "diagonalisation" is also used in logic and computability for Cantor's diagonal argument and its descendants, in which a list is defeated by constructing an object differing from the n-th listed object in its n-th feature; such arguments prove the uncountability of the real numbers and underlie Gödel's incompleteness theorems and the undecidability of the halting problem.
