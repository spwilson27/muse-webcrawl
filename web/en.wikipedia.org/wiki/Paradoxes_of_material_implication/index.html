Paradoxes of material implication

FieldPhilosophical logic
Concerned withMaterial conditional (→) in classical logic
Key figuresC. I. Lewis, H. P. Grice, Robert Stalnaker, David Lewis
ResponsesStrict implication, relevance logic, conversational implicature, conditional logics

The paradoxes of material implication are a group of formulae in classical logic that are true according to the truth-functional definition of the material conditional, but which appear to contradict intuitive judgements about how "if... then" statements behave. The material conditional P → Q is false only when P is true and Q is false, and true in all other cases — including when P is false, or when Q is true regardless of P. This gives rise to theorems that seem paradoxical when read as conditionals.

The truth table of the material conditional: it is false only for true antecedent and false consequent.

The paradoxes

The best-known paradoxical consequences include:

  • A false proposition implies anything: (¬P) → (P → Q). If the antecedent is false, the conditional is true whatever the consequent — "If 2 + 2 = 5, then I am the Pope."
  • A true proposition is implied by anything: Q → (P → Q). If the consequent is true, the conditional holds for any antecedent — "If grass is green, then if it rains tomorrow the moon is made of cheese" is not covered by this, but "If the moon is made of cheese, then grass is green" is true whenever grass is in fact green.
  • Of two propositions, one implies the other or vice versa: (P → Q) ∨ (Q → P).
  • Excluded middle for conditionals: (P → Q) ∨ (P → ¬Q) — for any P and Q, either P implies Q or P implies not-Q.

History

The material conditional was defined by analogy with the conditional of Philo of Megara in antiquity, and related puzzles were discussed by medieval logicians. In modern form the paradoxes became prominent after Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica (1910–1913) adopted material implication as the formal rendering of "implies." C. I. Lewis, objecting that the paradoxes showed material implication failed to capture genuine entailment, developed systems of modal logic and the notion of strict implication, in which P strictly implies Q when it is impossible for P to be true and Q false.

Responses

Several strategies have been proposed:

  • Strict implication and modal logic (Lewis): replace the truth-functional conditional with a modalised one.
  • Relevance logic: require that antecedent and consequent share content, so irrelevant implications fail.
  • Pragmatic defence (Grice and others): the material conditional's truth conditions are correct, but asserting a conditional when one knows the antecedent false or the consequent true violates conversational maxims, making such assertions misleading rather than false.
  • Conditional logics and counterfactuals (Stalnaker, David Lewis): analyse conditionals in terms of possible worlds, particularly for subjunctive conditionals.

The paradoxes remain a standard motivation for the study of non-classical logics and the philosophy of conditionals.
