Post-2008 Irish banking crisis

The Irish banking crisis of 2008 onwards was a major financial crisis in the Republic of Ireland, connected to the international financial crisis of 2007–2008 and to the collapse of a domestic property bubble. Irish banks had lent heavily to property developers during the Celtic Tiger boom; when property prices fell, the banks faced insolvency, and the state's guarantee of their liabilities transformed a banking crisis into a sovereign debt crisis.

Bank guarantee and bailout

On 30 September 2008 the Irish government issued a blanket guarantee covering the deposits and most liabilities of six Irish banks. Losses, particularly at Anglo Irish Bank, proved far larger than expected. Anglo was nationalised in January 2009, and the National Asset Management Agency (NAMA) was created to acquire toxic property loans. By 2010 the cost of recapitalising the banks was estimated at around €64 billion, roughly 40% of GDP.

EU–IMF programme

In November 2010 Ireland entered an €85 billion EU–IMF rescue programme, accompanied by austerity budgets, tax rises, and a sharp rise in unemployment and emigration. Ireland exited the bailout in December 2013, the first eurozone country to do so. Subsequent inquiries examined regulatory failures by the Central Bank and Financial Regulator, and a later banking inquiry reported in 2016.
