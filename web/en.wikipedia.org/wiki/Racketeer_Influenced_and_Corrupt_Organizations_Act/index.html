Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act

RICO Act
EnactedOctober 15, 1970
Citation18 U.S.C. §§ 1961–1968
Part ofOrganized Crime Control Act of 1970

The Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations (RICO) Act is a United States federal law that provides for extended criminal penalties and a civil cause of action for acts performed as part of an ongoing criminal organisation. Enacted in 1970 to combat the Mafia and organised crime, it allows leaders of an enterprise to be tried for crimes they ordered or assisted, closing a loophole where bosses insulated themselves from the actual acts.

Elements

A RICO violation requires an enterprise, a pattern of racketeering activity (at least two predicate acts within ten years, from a statutory list including murder, kidnapping, gambling, arson, robbery, bribery, extortion, drug dealing, blackmail-related extortion, and certain frauds including securities fraud), and a nexus to interstate commerce. Section 1962 prohibits acquiring, operating, investing in, or conspiring regarding such an enterprise.

Penalties and civil RICO

Criminal penalties include up to 20 years' imprisonment per count (life if the predicate allows), forfeiture, and fines. Section 1964(c) gives private plaintiffs treble damages and attorney's fees, leading to extensive (and controversial) civil RICO use in ordinary business disputes, constrained by requirements of pattern, continuity and proximate causation (Holmes v. SIPC).

Notable uses

RICO was used against Mafia families in the Commission Trial, against Michael Milken-era defendants, FIFA officials, and many state analogues ("Little RICO" statutes) followed.
