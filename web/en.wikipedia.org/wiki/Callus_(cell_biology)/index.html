Callus (cell biology)

Plant callus growing in vitro on a nutrient medium.

In cell biology, a callus is a mass of unorganized, undifferentiated plant parenchyma cells. In plant tissue culture, callus is induced to grow from an explant — a small piece of plant tissue — placed on a nutrient medium containing plant growth regulators, typically an auxin and a cytokinin. The resulting cells are totipotent, meaning they retain the capacity to regenerate a whole plant.

Formation

When a plant is wounded, cells at the wound surface may dedifferentiate and divide to form a protective callus. In culture, this response is exploited deliberately: explants are surface-sterilised, placed on agar or liquid media such as Murashige and Skoog medium, and supplied with hormones whose ratio determines whether the callus remains unorganised or begins organogenesis — forming shoots or roots.

Types

Callus may be friable (loose, easily dispersed, suitable for suspension cultures) or compact and hard. Embryogenic callus contains cells capable of somatic embryogenesis, producing embryos that can develop into plantlets without fertilisation.

Uses

  • Micropropagation — mass production of clonal plants for agriculture and horticulture.
  • Genetic engineering — callus is a common target for transformation by Agrobacterium or particle bombardment, after which transgenic plants are regenerated.
  • Secondary metabolite production — cell cultures can produce valuable compounds in controlled conditions.
  • Plant breeding and research — somaclonal variation arising in callus can yield novel traits; haploid callus from anther culture speeds breeding programmes.
