Shirō Ishii (石井 四郎, 1892–1959) was a Japanese microbiologist and lieutenant general in the Imperial Japanese Army, best known as the commander of Unit 731, the covert biological and chemical warfare research unit that conducted lethal human experimentation on prisoners in Japanese-occupied Manchuria before and during the Second Sino-Japanese War and the Second World War.

Career

Ishii studied medicine at Kyoto Imperial University and joined the army as a surgeon. After a study tour of Western institutions in the late 1920s, he became an energetic advocate of biological warfare, which had been banned by the 1925 Geneva Protocol — a fact he argued proved its effectiveness. With high-level patronage he established a research base first in Harbin and then, from the mid-1930s, at Pingfang, where a large complex was built under the cover name of an epidemic-prevention and water-purification unit.

Unit 731

At Pingfang and through a network of subordinate units, Ishii's organisation conducted experiments on prisoners — mostly Chinese, but also Russians and others — including deliberate infection with plague, cholera, typhoid, and anthrax; vivisection; frostbite studies; and weapons tests. Field attacks disseminated plague-infected fleas and contaminated food and water in Chinese cities and villages. Historians estimate that the experiments killed thousands of prisoners, and the field operations killed many thousands more.

Immunity and later life

After Japan's defeat in 1945, Ishii and his associates negotiated immunity from prosecution by the American occupation authorities in exchange for their experimental data; he was never tried at the Tokyo Tribunal, although some of his subordinates were tried by the Soviet Union at Khabarovsk in 1949. Ishii lived quietly in Japan after the war and died in 1959 of throat cancer.
