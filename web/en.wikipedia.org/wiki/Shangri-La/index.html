Shangri-La

Shangri-La is a fictional place described in the 1933 novel Lost Horizon by the British author James Hilton. It depicts a harmonious, permanently happy valley, enclosed in the western end of the Kunlun Mountains and guided by a lamasery, whose inhabitants age almost imperceptibly. The name has since become a byword for any earthly paradise, utopia or hidden sanctuary.

Origins

Hilton drew on travellers' accounts of the Himalayas and on the Tibetan Buddhist legend of Shambhala, a hidden kingdom of wisdom. In the novel, the British diplomat Hugh Conway and three companions are hijacked to the valley after their plane crashes in the mountains; Conway is gradually drawn into the lamasery's serene philosophy of moderation — "moderately" virtuous, happy and healthy — under the guidance of the ancient High Lama.

Impact

The 1937 film adaptation by Frank Capra made the name world-famous. During the Second World War, US President Franklin D. Roosevelt named the presidential retreat now known as Camp David "Shangri-La", and the Doolittle Raiders of 1942 were jokingly said to have flown from it. The term entered general English usage for idyllic retreats, and numerous hotels (notably the Shangri-La Hotels chain), songs and places have adopted it. In 2001, Zhongdian County in Yunnan, China, was officially renamed Shangri-La (Xianggelila), claiming to be Hilton's inspiration.
