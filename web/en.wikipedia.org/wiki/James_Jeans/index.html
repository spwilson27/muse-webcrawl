Sir James Hopwood Jeans (11 September 1877 – 16 September 1946) was an English physicist, astronomer, and mathematician. He made fundamental contributions to quantum theory, the theory of radiation, and stellar dynamics, and became one of the most successful popularisers of science of his era.

Scientific work

Jeans studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, and was placed Second Wrangler in the Mathematical Tripos. He taught at Princeton (1905–1909) before returning to Cambridge. With Lord Rayleigh he derived the Rayleigh–Jeans law of black-body radiation, whose failure at short wavelengths (the "ultraviolet catastrophe") helped motivate Planck's quantum hypothesis. His analysis of gravitational instability in a gas cloud produced the concept of the Jeans length and Jeans mass — the critical size above which a cloud collapses to form stars — still central to theories of star formation. He also worked on stellar structure, the dynamics of rotating fluid masses (relevant to spiral galaxies), and the kinetic theory of gases, and championed (with Arthur Eddington) cosmogony models that were influential between the wars.

Popular writing

After retiring from research for health reasons, Jeans wrote bestselling popular books, including The Universe Around Us (1929), The Mysterious Universe (1930), The New Background of Science (1933), and Physics and Philosophy (1942). His idealist interpretation of quantum mechanics — famously suggesting that the universe "begins to look more like a great thought than like a great machine" — was widely read and debated. He was knighted in 1928 and received the Order of Merit in 1939.
