Senryū

Senryū (川柳, "river willow") is a Japanese form of short poetry similar to haiku in construction: three phases with 17 on (syllable-like units) in total. Senryū tend to be about human foibles, while haiku tend to be about nature; senryū are often cynical or darkly humorous, while haiku are more serious. Unlike haiku, senryū do not require a seasonal word (kigo) or a cutting word (kireji).

History

Senryū is named after Edo-period haikai poet Karai Hachiemon (1718–1790), whose pen name was Karai Senryū. He served as a judge (tenja) for contests of maekuzuke, in which contestants supplied humorous verses to match given lines. Winning verses were published in anthologies called Yanagidaru ("willow barrel"), issued from 1765 onward. The form thus descends from comic linked verse but developed as an independent short poem.

Characteristics

Though it shares haiku's 5–7–5 structure, senryū is generally more colloquial and playful, treating everyday life, social satire and the weaknesses of human nature, sometimes in an irreverent or vulgar register. In English-language practice the distinction is often drawn by tone: a poem about nature is called haiku, one about people and their follies, senryū — though the boundary is much debated.
