Tractate Sanhedrin

Sanhedrin is a tractate of the Mishnah and Talmud in the order Nezikin ("Damages"). It deals with the structure of Jewish courts, judicial procedure, criminal law, and the penalties for serious offences, and is named after the Sanhedrin, the supreme court of ancient Israel.

Contents

The Mishnah's eleven chapters describe the composition of courts (the Great Sanhedrin of 71 judges, lesser courts of 23 and 3), the qualifications and examination of witnesses, the rules of evidence — including the elaborate warnings given to witnesses in capital cases — and the four modes of execution mentioned in rabbinic law: stoning, burning, strangulation, and beheading. Chapter 10, known as Perek Ḥelek, turns theological, listing those who have "no share in the World to Come" and discussing resurrection and the messiah.

Influence

Sanhedrin's procedural safeguards — the requirement of two witnesses, the disqualification of interested testimony, and the court's active search for grounds of acquittal in capital cases — made capital punishment rare in practice and have been widely studied by legal historians. The tractate exists in both the Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmuds and is a standard part of yeshiva study.
