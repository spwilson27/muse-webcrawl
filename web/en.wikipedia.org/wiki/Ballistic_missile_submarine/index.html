Ballistic missile submarine


USS Alabama, an Ohio-class SSBN.

A ballistic missile submarine is a submarine capable of deploying submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs) armed with nuclear warheads. Such submarines became a key leg of the nuclear triad during the Cold War, providing a survivable second-strike capability because they can remain hidden in the oceans for months. Nuclear-powered ballistic missile submarines are designated SSBN in United States Navy hull classification; the French term is SNLE and Russian boats are often called RPKSN.

History

The first ballistic missile submarine was the Soviet Zulu V-class boat converted in 1955 to carry two R-11FM missiles in its sail. The United States commissioned the USS George Washington in 1959, the first SSBN, armed with sixteen Polaris A-1 missiles; her first deterrent patrol began in November 1960. Successor systems — Poseidon, Trident I, and Trident II — progressively increased range, accuracy, and the number of independently targetable warheads (MIRVs).

The Soviet Union fielded successive generations including the Yankee, Delta, and Typhoon classes; the Typhoon (Project 941 Akula), at over 23,000 tonnes submerged displacement, remains the largest submarine type ever built. Russia's current class is the Borei.

Operators

  • United States — Ohio class (14 SSBNs) armed with Trident II D5; being replaced by the Columbia class.
  • Russia — Borei and Delta IV classes.
  • China — Type 094 (Jin) class, with the Type 096 in development.
  • United Kingdom — Vanguard class (4 boats, Trident II D5), to be replaced by the Dreadnought class; the UK maintains continuous at-sea deterrence.
  • France — Triomphant class (4 boats) with M51 missiles.
  • India — Arihant class, the newest operator, with K-series missiles.

Role and operations

SSBNs conduct long deterrent patrols, remaining submerged and quiet, ready to launch missiles on receipt of authorised orders but otherwise avoiding contact — earning Royal Navy boats the nickname "bombers" and US boats "boomers". Because their location is secret and they are highly survivable, they are considered the most secure leg of the nuclear triad, underpinning the doctrine of mutually assured destruction. Most SSBNs are nuclear-powered, allowing patrols limited mainly by food supplies and crew endurance.
