Sectional curvature

In Riemannian geometry, the sectional curvature is one of the ways to describe the curvature of Riemannian manifolds. It assigns a real number to each two-dimensional tangent subspace at a point of the manifold, and it contains the same information as the full Riemann curvature tensor: a Riemannian manifold's curvature tensor is completely determined by its sectional curvatures.

A manifold has constant sectional curvature if every two-plane at every point has the same Gaussian curvature, as on a sphere (positive), plane (zero), or hyperbolic plane (negative).

Definition

Given a Riemannian manifold M and two linearly independent tangent vectors u and v at the same point p, the sectional curvature of the plane spanned by them is

K(u, v) = ⟨R(v, u)u, v⟩ / (⟨u, u⟩⟨v, v⟩ − ⟨u, v⟩²)

where R is the Riemann curvature tensor. Equivalently, K(u, v) is the Gaussian curvature of the surface obtained by taking the exponential image of the two-plane spanned by u and v — the surface swept out by geodesics starting at p in directions within that plane.

Manifolds of constant sectional curvature

A manifold all of whose sectional curvatures equal a constant K is called a space form. The simply connected complete examples are the sphere (K > 0), Euclidean space (K = 0), and hyperbolic space (K < 0), up to scaling. By Schur's lemma, a connected manifold of dimension at least three whose sectional curvature depends only on the point (not the plane) is necessarily of constant curvature.

Comparison theorems

Bounds on sectional curvature have strong topological and geometric consequences. The Toponogov comparison theorem compares triangles in a manifold with those in a model space form, and the Rauch comparison theorem controls the growth of Jacobi fields. A complete simply connected manifold of nonpositive sectional curvature is diffeomorphic to Euclidean space by the Cartan–Hadamard theorem, while manifolds of positive sectional curvature are subject to results such as the sphere theorem.

Related notions

Averaging sectional curvatures over planes containing a fixed vector gives the Ricci curvature; averaging over all planes at a point gives the scalar curvature. Among all curvature quantities, sectional curvature is the most direct generalization of Gaussian curvature to higher dimensions.
