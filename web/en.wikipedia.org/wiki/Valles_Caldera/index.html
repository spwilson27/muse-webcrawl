The Valles Caldera is a volcanic caldera about 22 kilometres (14 miles) wide in the Jemez Mountains of northern New Mexico. It was formed approximately 1.25 million years ago by a colossal eruption — one of the largest known on Earth — which ejected more than 300 cubic kilometres of material and deposited the upper Bandelier Tuff over a vast area. The caldera is the type locality for resurgent calderas and a classic site in volcanology.

Geology

The eruption that created the caldera emptied a shallow magma chamber, causing the roof rocks to collapse into a roughly circular depression. Following collapse, a resurgent dome rose in the centre of the caldera as magma pushed the floor upward again; Redondo Peak, the highest point on the caldera rim area, is part of this resurgent structure. Ring-fracture dome eruptions after collapse produced rhyolite domes such as Cerro del Medio. The caldera remains volcanically restless, with hot springs and fumaroles, and is studied as a natural laboratory for geothermal energy.

History and the preserve

People have used the caldera's resources for at least 11,000 years; obsidian from the area was traded widely across the Southwest. From 1860 the caldera floor was privately held ranch land, known as the Baca Ranch. In 2000 the United States purchased the ranch under the Valles Caldera Preservation Act, creating the Valles Caldera National Preserve, administered first by a public trust and transferred to the National Park Service in 2015. The preserve covers about 89,000 acres of meadows (valles), forested domes and streams.

Ecology

The caldera supports one of New Mexico's largest elk herds, along with black bears, mountain lions, prairie dogs and golden eagles. Its high-elevation grasslands and the headwaters of the East Fork of the Jemez River and San Antonio Creek make it an important watershed and research site for studies of climate, fire and grazing.
