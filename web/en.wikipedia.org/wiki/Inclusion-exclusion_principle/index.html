Inclusion–exclusion principle

In combinatorics, the inclusion–exclusion principle is a counting technique that computes the number of elements in the union of several sets by adding the sizes of the individual sets, subtracting the sizes of pairwise intersections (whose elements were counted twice), adding back triple intersections, and so on with alternating signs.

Statement

For finite sets A1, …, An: |A1 ∪ … ∪ An| = Σ|Ai| − Σ|Ai ∩ Aj| + Σ|Ai ∩ Aj ∩ Ak| − … + (−1)n+1|A1 ∩ … ∩ An|. For two sets this reduces to |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|.

Applications

The principle underlies the counting of derangements (permutations with no fixed point, giving the subfactorial !n ≈ n!/e), Euler's totient function via prime divisors, the principle of counting surjections, and sieve methods in number theory, including the Legendre sieve and the probability form used for unions of events. In probability, the same formula holds with cardinalities replaced by probabilities.
