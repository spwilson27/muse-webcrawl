The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI, BDI-1A, BDI-II), created by psychiatrist Aaron T. Beck, is a 21-question multiple-choice self-report inventory, one of the most widely used psychometric tests for measuring the severity of depression. Its development marked a shift among mental health professionals, who had until then viewed depression from a psychodynamic perspective, instead of it being rooted in the patient's own thoughts.

In its current version, the BDI-II, it is designed for individuals aged 13 and over, and is composed of items relating to symptoms of depression such as hopelessness and irritability, cognitions such as guilt or feelings of being punished, as well as physical symptoms such as fatigue, weight loss, and lack of interest in sex.

Development and history

According to Beck, when he started studying depression, he had observed a set of cognitions that could be categorized as negative views of the self, the world, and the future. Beck began developing the BDI, and it was first published in 1961. Items were drawn largely from verbatim accounts of patients' attitudes and symptoms recorded during therapy.

The inventory was revised in 1971 and copyrighted in 1978; the BDI-1A was released in 1979, and the second edition, the BDI-II, was published in 1996. The BDI-II was developed in response to the American Psychiatric Association's publication of the DSM-IV, which changed many of the diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder.

Scoring and interpretation

Each of the 21 items is scored on a scale of 0 to 3, giving a total score ranging from 0 to 63. Higher total scores indicate more severe depressive symptoms. Commonly used cut-off scores for the BDI-II are:

  • 0–13: minimal depression
  • 14–19: mild depression
  • 20–28: moderate depression
  • 29–63: severe depression

The BDI is a screening and severity-monitoring instrument; it is not, by itself, a diagnostic tool.

Psychometric properties and use

The BDI has been extensively validated, showing high internal consistency (coefficient alpha typically around 0.9) and good test–retest reliability. It is used worldwide in clinical practice and research, has been translated into many languages, and exists in short forms such as the 13-item BDI-SF and the 7-item BDI-FS. The inventory is a proprietary instrument published by Pearson.

Criticism

Critics note that, like other self-report measures, BDI scores can be easily exaggerated or minimized by the person completing them, and that the instrument may overemphasize somatic symptoms in medically ill populations. Context, like all questionnaires, matters in interpreting results.
