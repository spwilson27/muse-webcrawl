Kenneth MacAlpin (Medieval Gaelic: Cináed mac Ailpín; c. 810 – 13 February 858), modern Gaelic Coinneach mac Ailpein, is traditionally regarded as the founder and first king of Scotland, having united the kingdoms of the Scots (Dál Riata) and the Picts in the 840s — a union from which the kingdom of Alba emerged.

Background

Kenneth was a son of Alpín mac Echdach. According to later king lists, he became king of Dál Riata around 841 and then of the Picts around 843, following the collapse of Pictish royal power after devastating Viking attacks, including the disaster of 839 in which the Pictish king Eóganán mac Óengusa was killed. How Kenneth acquired the Pictish kingship is uncertain: later legend told of conquest and even of a treacherous banquet at which Pictish nobles were murdered (the tale known as "MacAlpin's treason"), but modern historians generally favor a combination of inheritance claims, marriage, and political opportunity, and note that contemporary records call Kenneth simply "king of the Picts."

Reign

Kenneth's reign was spent fighting Viking raiders and expanding southward against the Britons of Strathclyde and the Angles of Lothian; tradition credits him with invading Lothian repeatedly and burning Dunbar and Melrose. He is said to have moved the relics of Saint Columba from Iona to a new church at Dunkeld, shifting the ecclesiastical center of the kingdom eastward. He died at the palace of Forteviot in 858, traditionally of a tumor, and was buried on Iona. He was succeeded by his brother, Donald I (Domnall mac Ailpín), in accordance with the practice of tanistry.

Legacy

The dynasty founded by Kenneth, the House of Alpin, ruled Scotland until the 11th century; nearly all later Scottish kings claimed descent from him. Although modern scholarship questions whether a single "union of the Picts and Scots" occurred in 843 and whether Kenneth was truly "first king of Scotland," he remains the conventional starting point of the Scottish monarchy in regnal lists.
