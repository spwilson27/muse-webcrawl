Indian Country

Indian Country is a legal term in United States law that describes land within which federal and tribal law applies and state law generally does not, in the context of Native American affairs. The modern statutory definition is set out in 18 U.S.C. § 1151, originally enacted as part of the Major Crimes Act framework and the federal Indian Country Crimes Act.

Statutory definition

Section 1151 defines Indian Country as: (a) all land within the limits of any Indian reservation under the jurisdiction of the United States government, notwithstanding the issuance of any patent and including rights-of-way running through the reservation; (b) all dependent Indian communities within the borders of the United States, whether within the original or subsequently acquired territory; and (c) all Indian allotments, the Indian titles to which have not been extinguished, including rights-of-way running through them.

Jurisdiction

The classification determines which sovereign — federal, tribal, or state — may exercise civil and criminal jurisdiction. In general, tribal governments and the federal government share jurisdiction in Indian Country, while state authority is sharply limited, subject to exceptions such as Public Law 280, under which Congress transferred certain jurisdiction to specified states. Landmark Supreme Court decisions, including McGirt v. Oklahoma (2020), which held that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation in Oklahoma had never been disestablished, have reaffirmed the centrality of the concept.

Colloquial use

Beyond its legal meaning, "Indian Country" is used colloquially to refer to Native American communities, lands, and media generally — as in the newspaper Indian Country Today — and was used in US military slang during the Vietnam War and later conflicts to mean hostile territory, a usage widely criticised as offensive.
