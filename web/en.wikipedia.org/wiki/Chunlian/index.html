Chunlian

Chunlian (traditional Chinese: 春聯; simplified Chinese: 春联; pinyin: Chūnlián), or Spring Festival couplets, are pairs of poetic lines written in calligraphy on red paper and pasted on either side of a door or gate during the Chinese New Year (Spring Festival), expressing wishes for prosperity, health, and good fortune in the coming year. A shorter horizontal scroll is often placed above the door frame.

Form and rules

A chunlian consists of two vertical lines of equal length and character count. The lines are antithetical: corresponding characters match in part of speech and relate in meaning, while the tonal patterns are opposed — the final character of the right-hand (upper) line traditionally ends with a falling (oblique) tone, and the left-hand (lower) line with a level tone. The couplet is read right to left when mounted. The four- or five-character horizontal inscription above summarizes the theme.

History

The custom developed from the older practice of hanging "peachwood charms" (taofu) inscribed with the names of door gods to ward off evil. During the Song dynasty the charms moved to paper and became literary; tradition credits Meng Chang, ruler of Later Shu in the tenth century, with writing the earliest recorded couplet. By the Ming dynasty, promoted by the Hongwu Emperor, couplet-writing had become a universal New Year practice, later spreading to Korea (where similar couplets are used), Vietnam, and Chinese communities worldwide.

Modern practice

Today chunlian are produced by hand by calligraphers and street vendors or printed commercially, often with gold characters on red paper, and are put up on New Year's Eve and left until they fade or the next festival. Auspicious themes include abundance ("year after year with surplus"), harmony, longevity, and success in study or business; special forms exist for weddings, birthdays, and mourning (in white, blue, or green paper).
