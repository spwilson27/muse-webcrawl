Wet-bulb temperature

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The wet-bulb temperature is the lowest temperature that can be reached under current ambient conditions by the evaporation of water alone. It is measured with a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in wet muslin and ventilated — a psychrometer — and it is always less than or equal to the ordinary ("dry-bulb") air temperature, the two being equal only at 100% relative humidity.

Significance

Wet-bulb temperature combines heat and humidity into a single measure of how effectively evaporation can cool a surface — including human skin. Because sweating works by evaporation, the wet-bulb temperature sets a physiological limit: a sustained wet-bulb temperature of about 35 °C (95 °F) is considered the theoretical threshold at which even a healthy, resting, well-ventilated person can no longer shed metabolic heat and will die of hyperthermia within hours; later experimental work suggests the practical limit is lower, around 30-31 °C in humid conditions.

Climate change

Under climate change, brief exceedances of very high wet-bulb temperatures have already been recorded at a few coastal stations around the Persian Gulf and in South Asia, and studies project that parts of the tropics and subtropics could face dangerous wet-bulb conditions far more often by late century. Wet-bulb temperature is also used in engineering for evaporative cooling towers and in meteorology to estimate snow levels and thunderstorm potential.
