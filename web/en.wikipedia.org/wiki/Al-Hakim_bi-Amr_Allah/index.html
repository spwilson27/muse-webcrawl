Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah

Al-Hakim bi-Amr Allah (Arabic: الحاكم بأمر الله; 13 August 985 – 13 February 1021), the sixth Fatimid caliph, reigned from 996 until his mysterious disappearance in 1021. One of the most controversial rulers in Islamic history, his erratic decrees, religious persecutions and vanishing gave rise to the Druze faith, whose adherents believe him to be divine.

Reign

Al-Hakim succeeded his father al-Aziz at the age of eleven and came under the control first of the eunuch regent Barjawan, whom he had executed in 1000, thereafter ruling personally. His reign was noted for building projects in Cairo — notably the completion of the great al-Hakim Mosque and the Dar al-Ilm academy — and for sudden, severe edicts: prohibitions on foods and games, restrictions on women leaving their homes, and persecution of Christians and Jews, culminating in the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem in 1009, an act that later helped inflame opinion in the run-up to the Crusades. Many measures were later reversed, and his last years saw a turn to asceticism: he rode out alone on a donkey into the Muqattam hills, where on the night of 13 February 1021 he disappeared; only his donkey and bloodied garments were found.

The Druze faith

In his final years preachers including Hamza ibn Ali proclaimed al-Hakim's divinity — a doctrine rejected by the Fatimid establishment but embraced by a community that became the Druze, for whom al-Hakim is the incarnation of God and who expect his return. For this, and his alleged madness, medieval sources dubbed him "the Mad Caliph"; modern historians offer more nuanced assessments of his fiscal and administrative reforms.
