Liming (soil)

Liming
PurposeRaise soil pH; reduce soil acidity
Common materialsCalcium carbonate, dolomite, slaked lime, quicklime
Practice areasAgriculture, horticulture, forestry, turf management

Liming is the application of calcium- and magnesium-rich materials to soil in order to neutralise soil acidity and raise soil pH. The materials used — collectively called agricultural lime — include limestone (calcium carbonate), dolomite (calcium magnesium carbonate), chalk, quicklime (calcium oxide), and slaked or hydrated lime (calcium hydroxide).

Purpose and effects

Acidic soils limit the growth of most crops: below about pH 5.5, aluminium and manganese become soluble enough to be toxic to plant roots, while the availability of phosphorus, calcium, magnesium and molybdenum declines. Liming neutralises exchangeable acidity (hydrogen and aluminium ions on the soil's cation-exchange complex), precipitates toxic aluminium, supplies calcium and (with dolomitic lime) magnesium, stimulates microbial activity, and improves soil structure in clay soils by promoting flocculation.

Materials and application

The effectiveness of a liming material depends on its neutralising value (usually expressed relative to pure calcium carbonate) and its fineness, since only fine particles react rapidly with soil. Ground limestone is the most common material because it is cheap, safe to handle and slow-acting; burnt and hydrated limes react more quickly but are caustic. Lime requirement is estimated from soil testing that measures both pH and buffer capacity, and lime is normally applied with broadcast spreaders and incorporated into the topsoil. Over-liming can make micronutrients such as iron, manganese, boron and zinc unavailable, so recommended rates are crop- and soil-specific.

History

The beneficial effects of lime and marl on sour soils were known in Roman times and described by authors such as Pliny the Elder. Liming became widespread with the agricultural improvements of the 18th and 19th centuries, and scientific soil testing in the 20th century placed recommended rates on a quantitative footing. Lakes affected by acid rain in Scandinavia and elsewhere have also been limed to restore their chemistry, an application related in principle to agricultural practice.
