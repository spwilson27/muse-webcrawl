Buckminsterfullerene

Buckminsterfullerene (C60) has the shape of a truncated icosahedron.

Buckminsterfullerene is a type of fullerene with the formula C60. It has a cage-like fused-ring structure (a truncated icosahedron) made of twenty hexagons and twelve pentagons, resembling a soccer ball. Each carbon atom is bonded to three others. It is a black solid that dissolves in hydrocarbon solvents to give a violet solution.

Discovery

Buckminsterfullerene was discovered in 1985 by Harold Kroto, James Heath, Sean O'Brien, Robert Curl and Richard Smalley at Rice University, during experiments using laser evaporation of graphite intended to simulate conditions in the atmospheres of red giant stars. Mass spectrometry revealed a remarkably stable cluster of 60 carbon atoms. The team proposed its soccer-ball structure and named it after architect Buckminster Fuller, whose geodesic domes it resembles. Kroto, Curl and Smalley received the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery.

Structure

The molecule has a diameter of about 1.1 nm. Its 60 carbon atoms sit at the vertices of a truncated icosahedron, the geometry of a standard soccer ball, with twelve pentagonal and twenty hexagonal faces. No two pentagons share an edge, following the isolated-pentagon rule that contributes to the molecule's stability.

Occurrence and synthesis

Fullerenes can be produced by striking an arc between graphite electrodes in an inert atmosphere; C60 is the most abundant fullerene in the resulting soot. Trace quantities also occur naturally in soot from combustion and have been detected in geological formations and in interstellar space.

Properties and applications

Pure C60 is an insulator, but doped with alkali metals it can become a superconductor at low temperatures (K3C60 superconducts below about 18 K). Derivatives ("functionalised" fullerenes) are soluble in water and have been studied for drug delivery, photovoltaics, lubricants and materials science, though large-scale commercial applications remain limited.
