Scientism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Scientism is the view that empirical science is the best or only route to genuine knowledge, and that the methods of the natural sciences should — or inevitably will — be extended to all fields of inquiry, including philosophy, the humanities, ethics and social life. The term is most often used pejoratively by critics who regard such extension as an unwarranted overreach; some defenders of strong naturalism have, however, adopted it positively.

Definitions

Philosophers distinguish weaker and stronger senses. In the weak sense, scientism simply expresses confidence in science's authority over empirical questions. In stronger senses, it is the claim that only science yields knowledge (so that questions science cannot answer are meaningless or unanswerable), or that science alone can eventually explain everything worth explaining, including morality and meaning. Related positions include physicalism, reductionism and some forms of positivism.

History of the term

The French word scientisme appeared in the late nineteenth century, and the English term spread in the twentieth. Friedrich Hayek attacked what he called scientism in The Counter-Revolution of Science (1952), blaming the imitation of natural-science methods for errors in economics and social engineering. Karl Popper criticised "scientism" as the uncritical imitation of science's supposed method. In recent decades the word has been used in debates over evolutionary psychology, neuroscience's claims about free will and religion, and the New Atheism: critics such as philosopher Mary Midgley and theologian John Haught accused popular writers of turning science into a total worldview, while others replied that the charge of "scientism" is a rhetorical shield against legitimate scientific findings.

Arguments

For a strong role for science: its track record of predictive success; the steady retreat of non-scientific explanations in domain after domain; and the continuity of humans with the rest of nature, suggesting that human affairs too are amenable to empirical study.

Against scientism: the claim "only science gives knowledge" is not itself a scientific finding, and so appears self-undermining; the humanities use interpretive methods suited to meaning and value, which experiments cannot settle; history, mathematics and ethics seem to yield knowledge by non-empirical means; and science itself rests on philosophical assumptions (about induction, realism and value of truth) that it cannot supply from within.
