Timothy Francis Leary (October 22, 1920 – May 31, 1996) was an American psychologist and writer known for his advocacy of psychedelic drugs. A Harvard lecturer in clinical psychology, he conducted experiments with psilocybin and LSD before becoming the most famous — and controversial — figure of the 1960s counterculture, urging a generation to "turn on, tune in, drop out."

Harvard years

After trying psilocybin mushrooms in Mexico in 1960, Leary founded the Harvard Psilocybin Project with Richard Alpert (later Ram Dass), running studies including the Concord Prison Experiment and the Marsh Chapel "Good Friday" experiment. Growing publicity and lax controls led Harvard to dismiss both men in 1963.

Counterculture icon and fugitive

Leary promoted LSD as a sacrament, co-founded the League for Spiritual Discovery, and published The Psychedelic Experience (1964), based on the Tibetan Book of the Dead. Repeated drug arrests culminated in a 1970 prison sentence; he escaped with help from the Weather Underground and fled abroad, living in Algeria with Eldridge Cleaver's exiled Black Panthers and in Switzerland, before capture in Afghanistan in 1973. President Richard Nixon called him "the most dangerous man in America." Released in 1976, he reinvented himself as a lecturer on space migration, life extension, and computers ("the PC is the LSD of the 1980s"), and remained a fixture of American popular culture until his death in 1996; some of his ashes were flown into space in 1997. Historians such as Theodore Roszak treated him as the era's emblematic guru.
