Slug

Slug, or land slug, is a common name for any apparently shell-less terrestrial gastropod mollusc. The word slug is also often used as part of the common name of any gastropod mollusc that has a very reduced shell, a small internal shell, or no internal shell at all, in contrast to snails, which have a large enough shell that they can completely retract their soft parts into the shell.

Various taxonomic families of land slugs form part of several quite different evolutionary lineages, which also include snails. Thus, the various families of slugs are not closely related, despite a superficial similarity in overall body form. The shell-less condition has arisen many times independently as an example of convergent evolution, and thus the category "slug" is polyphyletic.

Anatomy

The external anatomy of a slug includes tentacles (two pairs: the upper, larger pair bear the eyes at their tips, while the lower pair are used for smelling), a mantle, a tail, a keel (a ridge running down the back in some species), a pneumostome (a breathing hole on the right side of the mantle), and a mucus-secreting foot fringed with cilia. Slugs move by rhythmic waves of muscular contraction travelling along the foot, lubricated by mucus.

Ecology

Slugs play an important role in the ecosystem by eating decaying plant material, fungi and leaf litter. In turn, they are an important food source for many predators, including birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians and insects. However, some species are serious pests of agriculture and horticulture because they feed on crops and garden plants.

Human use

Slugs are generally not used as a food source by humans, though some larger species have occasionally been eaten in survival situations. Slugs are widely used in scientific research, particularly in neurobiology.
