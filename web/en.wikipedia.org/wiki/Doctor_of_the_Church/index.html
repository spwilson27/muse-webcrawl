Doctor of the Church

Doctor of the Church (Latin: doctor, "teacher") is a title given by the Catholic Church to saints recognised as having made a significant contribution to theology or doctrine through their learning, sanctity and proclamation by the Church. As of the mid-2020s there are 38 Doctors of the Church.

History

The title was first conferred in the West on four great Latin fathers — Ambrose, Augustine of Hippo, Jerome, and Pope Gregory I — formally named by Boniface VIII in 1298; the East honours in parallel such teachers as Basil the Great, Gregory of Nazianzus and John Chrysostom. Later additions include Thomas Aquinas and Bonaventure (named together in 1568/1588), Anselm, Bernard of Clairvaux, Alphonsus Liguori, Francis de Sales, and, in the twentieth century, the first women: Teresa of Ávila and Catherine of Siena (both 1970), followed by Thérèse of Lisieux (1997) and Hildegard of Bingen (2012). John of Ávila and Gregory of Narek were added in 2012 and 2015; Irenaeus of Lyons followed in 2022, and John Henry Newman was announced in 2025. The title is formally conferred by the pope, usually after examination of the saint's writings for eminent doctrine.

Criteria and significance

Tradition identifies three requirements: outstanding sanctity (insignis sanctitas), eminent learning (eminens doctrina), and explicit declaration by the Church (ecclesiae declaratio). The title is distinct from that of Church Father, which denotes the early writers of the patristic age. Several Eastern Catholic churches share most of the list, and some saints recognised as doctors are also honoured as teachers in the Eastern Orthodox tradition, though the formal title is Latin.
