Paradox of value

The paradox of value, also known as the diamond–water paradox, is the apparent contradiction that water — essential to life — is cheap, while diamonds — largely decorative — are expensive. Adam Smith described the puzzle in The Wealth of Nations (1776), distinguishing "value in use" from "value in exchange".

Paradox of value

Also called: Diamond–water paradox
Described by: Adam Smith
Resolved by: Marginal utility theory

Resolution

Nineteenth-century economists of the Marginal Revolution — William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras — resolved the paradox with marginal utility: price reflects the value of one additional unit, not total usefulness. Because water is usually abundant, its marginal unit is worth little; because diamonds are scarce, their marginal unit commands a high price. In a desert, the ordering reverses for the marginal litre of water.

Labour theory

Classical economists, and later Marx, connected exchange value to embodied labour, but struggled to explain the paradox without marginal analysis and scarcity.
