Emissivity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The emissivity of the surface of a material is its effectiveness in emitting energy as thermal radiation. It is defined as the ratio of the thermal radiation from a surface to the radiation from an ideal black surface (a blackbody) at the same temperature. The ratio varies from 0 to 1: a perfect blackbody has an emissivity of 1, while a perfect mirror has an emissivity of 0.

Definition and properties

Quantitatively, emissivity ε is the ratio of the radiance of a surface to the radiance of a blackbody at the same temperature, as given by Planck's law. More precisely, emissivity can be specified as a total or spectral quantity, and as a directional or hemispherical quantity. By Kirchhoff's law of thermal radiation, for a surface in thermal equilibrium the emissivity at a given wavelength and direction equals the absorptivity at that wavelength and direction, so good absorbers are good emitters.

Typical values

Polished metals have low emissivities (around 0.02–0.1 for aluminium and silver), which is why they appear bright in thermal images relative to their surroundings. Most non-metallic materials — water, ice, skin, brick, paint and vegetation — have high emissivities of 0.85–0.98 in the infrared. Human skin has an emissivity of about 0.98, nearly a perfect emitter in the thermal infrared.

Measurement and applications

Emissivity is central to infrared thermography and remote temperature measurement: an infrared thermometer or thermal camera infers temperature from received radiation and must be corrected for the target's emissivity, otherwise polished objects read far colder than they are. In climate science, the emissivity of the Earth's surface and atmosphere governs radiative cooling; in building physics it determines radiative heat loss from surfaces, exploited by low-emissivity ("low-e") window coatings.
