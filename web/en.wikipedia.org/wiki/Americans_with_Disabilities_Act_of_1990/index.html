Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990

The Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA) is a civil rights law of the United States that prohibits discrimination based on disability. Signed into law by President George H. W. Bush on 26 July 1990, it affords protections similar to those of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 across employment, public services, public accommodations, and telecommunications, and requires many employers and businesses to provide reasonable accommodations and accessible facilities.

Titles

Title I (Employment) requires employers with 15 or more employees to provide qualified individuals with disabilities an equal opportunity to benefit from employment opportunities, including reasonable accommodations unless they cause undue hardship. Title II applies to state and local government services and public transportation. Title III prohibits discrimination in places of public accommodation — businesses and nonprofit service providers — and sets accessibility standards for new construction and alterations. Title IV requires telecommunications relay services for people with hearing and speech disabilities. Title V contains miscellaneous provisions, including protection against retaliation.

History and amendments

The ADA grew out of the disability rights movement, building on Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973; its passage followed advocacy including the "Capitol Crawl" protest of March 1990. A series of Supreme Court decisions narrowed the definition of disability, leading Congress to pass the ADA Amendments Act of 2008, which broadened the definition and rejected those restrictive readings. The Act's design standards, maintained by the US Access Board and enforced by the Department of Justice, have reshaped the built environment, from curb cuts and accessible transit to elevators and signage.
