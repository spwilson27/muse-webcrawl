Taishō Democracy (大正デモクラシー, Taishō demokurashii) is the term for the liberal and democratic political movement that developed in Japan during the reign of the Taishō Emperor (1912–1926) and into the early Shōwa period. It is associated with the theorist Yoshino Sakuzō's doctrine of minponshugi ("government by and for the people"), mass movements for universal suffrage and labour rights, and the rise of party cabinets between 1918 and 1932.

Development

Political ferment after the Russo-Japanese War produced the Hibiya Incendiary Incident (1905) and growing pressure against rule by the Meiji oligarchy (genrō) and bureaucratic cliques. During the Taishō Political Crisis of 1912–13, mass protests forced the resignation of the Katsura cabinet, demanding constitutional government. The Rice Riots of 1918 toppled the Terauchi cabinet and brought Hara Takashi—the first commoner and party leader to serve as prime minister—to power at the head of a Seiyūkai party cabinet.

Achievements

From 1924 to 1932 all governments were party cabinets, a practice that came to be treated as constitutional convention. The Universal Manhood Suffrage Act of 1925 extended the vote to all men over 25, quadrupling the electorate, though it was paired with the repressive Peace Preservation Law of the same year, used against communists and other dissidents. Academics such as Minobe Tatsukichi promoted the "emperor-organ" theory of constitutional monarchy, and the era saw flourishing journalism, labour unions, tenant-farmer movements and demands for women's suffrage.

Decline

Party government ended with the assassination of Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi in the May 15 Incident of 1932. Economic crisis, military insubordination in Manchuria, and ultranationalist violence eroded the democratic experiment, and by the mid-1930s Japan was moving toward militarist authoritarianism—though historians note that the legacy of Taishō Democracy fed into Japan's post-1945 democratic reconstruction.
