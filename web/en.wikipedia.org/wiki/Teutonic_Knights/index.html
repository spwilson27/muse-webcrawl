The Teutonic Order — formally the Order of Brothers of the German House of Saint Mary in Jerusalem (Latin: Ordo domus Sanctae Mariae Theutonicorum Hierosolymitanorum) — is a Catholic religious institution founded as a military order c. 1190 in Acre, Kingdom of Jerusalem. Its members, the Teutonic Knights (Deutscher Orden), wore white habits bearing a black cross.

Origins

The Order grew from a German field hospital established during the Third Crusade at the siege of Acre. In 1198 it was militarised on the model of the Templars and Hospitallers, combining nursing with the defence of pilgrims and crusader states. Under Grand Master Hermann von Salza (1209–1239), a confidant of Emperor Frederick II, the Order rose to prominence in imperial and papal politics.

Prussia and the Baltic

After an unsuccessful episode defending the Burzenland in Hungary (1211–1225), the Order accepted the invitation of Duke Konrad of Masovia to campaign against the pagan Old Prussians. Armed with the Golden Bull of Rimini (1226) and papal authorisation, the Knights conquered and colonised Prussia over the 13th century, founding towns such as Thorn, Kulm, Elbing and Königsberg, and absorbed the Livonian Brothers of the Sword after 1237. The Order built a monastic state (Ordensstaat) in Prussia and Livonia, ruled from Marienburg Castle from 1309 — the largest brick castle in the world.

Decline

The Christianisation of Lithuania and its union with Poland created a powerful adversary. The Order's expansionist defeat at the Battle of Grunwald (Tannenberg) in 1410, at the hands of a Polish–Lithuanian army, began its decline. The Thirteen Years' War ended with the Second Peace of Thorn (1466), which ceded western Prussia to Poland. In 1525 the last Prussian Grand Master, Albert of Brandenburg, converted to Lutheranism and secularised the Order's Prussian lands as the Duchy of Prussia, held as a Polish fief.

Later history

The Order survived in the Holy Roman Empire under the Deutschmeister at Mergentheim, lost its remaining lands in the Napoleonic era, and was refounded in Austria, surviving suppression under the Nazis. Since 1929 it has been a purely religious order of priests, brothers and sisters, headquartered in Vienna, engaged in pastoral and charitable work.
