The Gàidhealtachd (Scottish Gaelic: [ˈkɛːəl̪ˠt̪əxk]; literally "Gaeldom") usually refers to the Highlands and Islands of Scotland and especially the Gaelic-speaking culture of the area. The corresponding Irish word Gaeltacht refers to Irish-speaking districts in Ireland, while in Scotland the term is also used more loosely for the parts of the country where Scottish Gaelic is or was recently spoken as a community language.

Historically, the Gàidhealtachd covered a much larger area than it does today. In the medieval period Gaelic was spoken across most of Scotland north of the Firth of Forth and in Galloway in the south-west. Over the centuries, through the centralisation of royal power, the Statutes of Iona (1609), the Education (Scotland) Act 1872, and the Highland Clearances, the language retreated to the north-west Highlands and the Outer Hebrides, where it remains strongest today.

Definition and extent

Unlike the Irish Gaeltacht, which is officially defined by government order, the Scottish Gàidhealtachd has never had a single legal definition. The term is used variously to mean:

  • the geographical Highlands of Scotland;
  • the areas where Gaelic is currently spoken by a significant proportion of the population, principally the Outer Hebrides (Na h-Eileanan Siar), the Isle of Skye, parts of Argyll and Bute, and the north-west mainland;
  • the wider cultural region associated with Highland Gaelic culture, language, music and custom.

In the 2011 census, around 57,600 people in Scotland reported being able to speak Gaelic (about 1.1% of the population), with the highest concentrations in the Outer Hebrides, where roughly half of residents are Gaelic speakers. The 2022 census recorded a modest increase in the numbers with some Gaelic skills.

History

Origins

Gaelic arrived in Scotland from Ireland with the settlers of Dál Riata in late antiquity. Under kings such as Kenneth MacAlpin in the 9th century, Gaelic became the dominant language of the emerging Kingdom of Alba. By the 11th and 12th centuries Gaelic was spoken at court and across most of the kingdom.

Decline

From the reign of Malcolm III onwards, the Scottish court increasingly adopted Norman and later Scots and English customs. The Lordship of the Isles preserved a powerful Gaelic polity in the west until its forfeiture in 1493. The Statutes of Iona in 1609 required Highland chiefs to educate their heirs in English-speaking Lowland schools and restricted the bards and the keeping of firearms. After the Jacobite risings of 1715 and 1745, government measures including the Disarming Act and the abolition of heritable jurisdictions weakened the clan system, and the Clearances of the late 18th and 19th centuries displaced much of the remaining Gaelic-speaking population.

Revival

The late 20th and early 21st centuries saw institutional support for the language, including the founding of the Gaelic college Sabhal Mòr Ostaig on Skye (1973), the Gaelic Language (Scotland) Act 2005 which established Bòrd na Gàidhlig, and the launch of the BBC Alba television channel in 2008. Gaelic-medium education is now available in several cities, including Glasgow and Inverness.

Culture

The Gàidhealtachd is associated with a distinctive cultural tradition: Gaelic song and psalm singing, piping (including the classical ceòl mòr), fiddle music, shinty, Highland games, and a strong oral storytelling tradition. The Mòd, an annual festival of Gaelic language and culture modelled on the Welsh Eisteddfod, has been held since 1892.
