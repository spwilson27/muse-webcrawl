Proton–proton chain

The proton–proton chain (PP chain) is a set of nuclear fusion reactions by which stars convert hydrogen to helium. It predominates in stars with masses equal to or less than about 1.3 times the mass of the Sun, whereas the CNO cycle dominates in heavier stars. The proton–proton chain occurs about 9.92×1037 times each second in the core of the Sun, supplying roughly 99% of the Sun's energy output.

The reactions

The first step fuses two protons into deuterium, releasing a positron and an electron neutrino; the positron immediately annihilates with an electron, producing gamma rays. The deuterium then fuses with another proton to give helium-3, and two helium-3 nuclei finally fuse to helium-4, returning two protons to the fuel supply. This main branch (PP I) accounts for about 83–86% of solar helium production. Two rarer branches, PP II and PP III, proceed through lithium-7 and beryllium-7/8 and boron-8, and produce the high-energy neutrinos detected by solar neutrino observatories.

Timescales and conditions

The first fusion step is extraordinarily slow: a proton in the solar core waits on average about 9 billion years before fusing, because the reaction depends on quantum tunnelling and a weak-force beta decay occurring during the brief moment of collision. This slowness is what gives the Sun its ~10-billion-year main-sequence lifetime. The chain requires core temperatures of at least about 3 million kelvin; in the Sun's core (about 15 million kelvin) it releases energy at only about 276 W/m³ at the centre — comparable to a compost heap — but integrated over the Sun's vast volume it yields 3.8×1026 watts.

History

The basic scheme was worked out in the late 1930s by Hans Bethe and Charles Critchfield. Measurements of neutrinos from the chain — particularly the boron-8 neutrinos from the rare PP III branch — were central to the solar neutrino problem and its resolution through neutrino oscillation.
