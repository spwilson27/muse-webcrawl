The Empire State Plaza, officially the Governor Nelson A. Rockefeller Empire State Plaza, is a complex of state government buildings in downtown Albany, New York. Built between 1965 and 1976 at a cost of about $2 billion, it is one of the most ambitious American urban-renewal projects of its era and a landmark of late modernist architecture.

History

Governor Nelson Rockefeller conceived the plaza after a 1959 visit by Princess Beatrix of the Netherlands, when he was embarrassed by the shabby neighbourhoods surrounding the State Capitol — an area of roughly 98 acres housing some 7,000 residents, largely working-class and Italian- and African-American, whose clearance remains controversial. Rockefeller persuaded the city to finance the project through Albany County bonds, circumventing the state constitution's debt limits, and appointed his friend Wallace Harrison as architect.

Design

The plaza is a vast marble platform, a quarter-mile long, raised over parking garages and a concourse. On it stand four identical 23-story Agency Buildings, the slender 44-story Erastus Corning Tower (the tallest building in New York State outside New York City), the egg-shaped performing-arts center known as The Egg, the New York State Museum and Library in the Cultural Education Center, the Swan Street and Justice buildings, and the Legislative Office Building. A long reflecting pool runs down the center, terminating at the New York State Capitol (1879). Rockefeller assembled for the plaza one of the largest public modern-art collections in the United States, with works by Alexander Calder, Louise Nevelson, and others displayed in the concourse and grounds.

Criticism

Architects and critics have long been divided: the complex is praised as a monumental modernist ensemble and condemned as an authoritarian, windswept "city within a city" that destroyed a living neighbourhood. It remains the working seat of New York State government and a symbol of Rockefeller's grand ambitions.
