SOSUS (Sound Surveillance System) was a chain of underwater listening posts established by the United States Navy during the Cold War to track Soviet submarines. Consisting of hydrophone arrays mounted on the ocean floor and connected by cable to shore facilities called Naval Facilities (NAVFACs), SOSUS exploited the deep sound channel (SOFAR channel) to detect low-frequency sound radiated by submarines over very long ranges.

History

Development began around 1949 under the highly classified Project Hartwell and related efforts, applying wartime research on underwater acoustics. The first experimental arrays were laid in the early 1950s off Eleuthera in the Bahamas. Arrays were subsequently placed across strategic choke points, notably the GIUK gap between Greenland, Iceland and the United Kingdom, and along the coasts of North America.

SOSUS provided the first detections of several Soviet submarine classes and generated long-range tracks that cued anti-submarine aircraft and ships. In 1968, SOSUS recordings helped locate the wreck of the lost US submarine USS Scorpion. The system remained secret until the early 1990s; after the Cold War many shore stations were closed and the arrays were partly opened to scientific use, including whale research. SOSUS later became part of the broader Integrated Undersea Surveillance System (IUSS).
