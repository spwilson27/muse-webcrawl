Contour drawing

Contour drawing is an artistic technique in which the artist sketches the outline (contours) of a subject using lines that follow the perceived edges and major ridges of form, with little or no shading. Contour lines define surfaces where planes meet, wrap around forms, or where the silhouette separates the subject from its background. Practising contour drawing trains the eye to observe carefully and to coordinate observation with the hand, and it is a staple exercise in art education.

Techniques

Blind contour

In blind contour drawing, the artist fixes their eyes on the subject and traces its edges slowly with the pencil without looking at the paper at all. The resulting drawing is usually distorted, but the exercise — popularised by Kimon Nicolaïdes in The Natural Way to Draw (1941) and by Betty Edwards in Drawing on the Right Side of the Brain — is valued precisely because it forces sustained, unselfconscious looking and suppresses reliance on memorised symbol shapes.

Modified and cross contour

Modified contour allows occasional glances at the paper to check proportions and relationships while keeping the emphasis on looking at the subject. Cross-contour lines travel across the surface of a form, revealing its volume much as lines of latitude reveal a globe; they may be visible in the final drawing or used as a scaffolding for shading. A continuous line or single-line drawing completes the work without lifting the drawing instrument.

Use in art and design

Beyond the classroom, expressive contour lines are a hallmark of many artists' drawings, from the economical ink figures of Picasso and Matisse to fashion illustration and storyboarding, and contour-like vector outlines structure much digital illustration.
