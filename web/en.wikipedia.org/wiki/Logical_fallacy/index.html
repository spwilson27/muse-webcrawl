Logical fallacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A logical fallacy is an error in reasoning that renders an argument invalid or unsound. Fallacies may be committed intentionally to manipulate or persuade by deception, or unintentionally through carelessness, cognitive bias, or limitations in understanding. The study of fallacies dates to Aristotle, whose Sophistical Refutations identified thirteen types; medieval and modern logicians have expanded the catalogue considerably.

A distinction is commonly drawn between formal fallacies, which are errors in the logical structure of a deductive argument and can be detected by examining its form alone, and informal fallacies, which arise from errors in content, context, or presumption and require examining what the argument actually says.

Formal fallacies

A formal fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that is always wrong regardless of the truth of its premises. Examples include:

  • Affirming the consequent — "If P then Q. Q, therefore P." The conclusion does not follow.
  • Denying the antecedent — "If P then Q. Not P, therefore not Q."
  • Affirming a disjunct — treating an inclusive "or" as exclusive.
  • Fallacy of the undistributed middle — a syllogism in which the middle term is not distributed in either premise.

Informal fallacies

Informal fallacies are grouped in various ways, often by the kind of error involved:

Fallacies of presumption

  • Begging the question (petitio principii) — assuming in a premise what the argument is meant to prove.
  • False dilemma — presenting only two options when more exist.
  • Loaded question — a question built on a contested assumption, such as "Have you stopped beating your wife?"
  • No true Scotsman — redefining a category ad hoc to exclude a counterexample.

Fallacies of relevance

  • Ad hominem — attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself.
  • Appeal to authority — treating a claim as true because an authority (often irrelevant) endorses it.
  • Appeal to emotion — substituting emotional manipulation for evidence.
  • Straw man — misrepresenting an opponent's position to make it easier to attack.
  • Red herring — introducing an irrelevant topic to divert attention.
  • Appeal to ignorance — claiming a proposition is true because it has not been proven false, or vice versa.

Fallacies of ambiguity and induction

  • Equivocation — using a word with two meanings as though it had one.
  • Hasty generalization — drawing a broad conclusion from an unrepresentative sample.
  • Slippery slope — asserting, without evidence, that one step will inevitably lead to a chain of extreme consequences.
  • False cause (post hoc ergo propter hoc) — assuming that because one event follows another, the first caused the second.
  • Appeal to nature — arguing that something is good because it is "natural" or bad because it is "unnatural."

History

Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations (part of the Organon) was the first systematic treatment, dividing fallacies into those dependent on language and those independent of it. The medieval schools developed the study further; many Latin names still in use (ad hominem, ad baculum, ad populum) come from this tradition. In the twentieth century, philosophers such as C. L. Hamblin criticized the standard textbook treatment as superficial, prompting renewed work in informal logic and argumentation theory, where fallacies are increasingly analyzed as context-dependent moves in dialogue rather than as intrinsically defective forms.
