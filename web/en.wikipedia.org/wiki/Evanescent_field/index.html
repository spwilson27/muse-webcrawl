Evanescent field

An evanescent field, or evanescent wave, is an oscillating electric, magnetic or acoustic field that does not propagate as an ordinary wave but instead decays exponentially with distance from the surface at which it is formed. The term evanescent means "tending to vanish". In an evanescent field the time-averaged energy flow in the direction of decay is zero, although energy is stored in the field itself.

Formation

The classic example arises in total internal reflection: when light travelling in a denser medium strikes an interface with a rarer medium above the critical angle, it is completely reflected, yet Maxwell's equations require a field on the far side of the boundary. That field travels along the interface and its amplitude falls off exponentially within a distance of roughly one wavelength. Evanescent fields also occur along waveguides beyond cut-off, near antennas (the reactive near field), and in quantum mechanics, where the wavefunction inside a finite potential barrier decays in an analogous way, enabling quantum tunnelling.

Applications

If a second dense medium is brought within the evanescent tail, light can couple across the gap — frustrated total internal reflection — the basis of many optical couplers. Evanescent-wave sensing underlies fibre-optic chemical sensors and total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy, which illuminates only a ~100 nm layer of a specimen. Surface plasmon resonance instruments, near-field scanning optical microscopes and fingerprint readers all exploit evanescent fields.
