Femicide or feminicide is a term for the hate crime of systematically killing females (women or girls) — in particular by a man and on account of their gender. In 1976, the feminist author Diana E. H. Russell first impliedly used the term femicide in front of the first International Tribunal on Crimes against Women in Brussels; she later defined it as "the killing of females by males because they are female". The term foregrounds the argument that such killings are not random homicides but are rooted in gender inequality, discrimination, and social tolerance of violence against women.

Definition and usage

Definitions vary. The World Health Organization distinguishes intimate femicide (by a current or former partner), honour-related femicide, dowry-related femicide, and non-intimate femicide. United Nations bodies collect data on "gender-related killings of women and girls"; UN Women and UNODC estimated that around 89,000 women and girls were intentionally killed worldwide in 2022, of which roughly 48,800 — more than half — were killed by intimate partners or other family members, making the home the most dangerous place for women statistically.

The Spanish-derived term feminicidio, developed by Latin American feminists such as Marcela Lagarde, adds the connotation of state complicity through impunity, and it entered law in a number of Latin American countries during the 2000s and 2010s, often after campaigns over the murders of women in Ciudad Juárez, Mexico. Many jurisdictions worldwide have since created a specific criminal offence of femicide or introduced gender as an aggravating factor in murder sentencing.

Forms

  • Intimate femicide — killing by a partner or ex-partner, the most common form.
  • Honour-related killing — killing of a woman perceived to have brought dishonour on her family.
  • Dowry-related killing — notably dowry deaths in parts of South Asia.
  • Female infanticide and sex-selective abortion — sometimes treated under related terms such as gendercide.
  • Serial and sexual femicide — killings accompanied by sexual violence, often with high impunity.

Activism and law

Movements such as Argentina's Ni Una Menos (2015) and Mexico's feminist protests of 2019–2020 brought femicide to international attention. The Council of Europe's Istanbul Convention (2011) obliges signatories to criminalise violence against women, and the European Institute for Gender Equality now maintains a femicide terminology and data framework for EU member states.
