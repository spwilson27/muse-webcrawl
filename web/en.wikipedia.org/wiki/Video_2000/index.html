Video 2000 (also marketed as V2000 and the Video Compact Cassette, or VCC) is a home videocassette recorder format developed by Philips and Grundig and launched in Europe in 1979. It was the European contender in the videotape format war against Sony's Betamax and JVC's VHS, and in the judgment of many engineers it was the most technically advanced of the three — yet it was the first to be withdrawn from the market.

The VCC cassette used half-inch tape that, like an audio cassette, could be flipped over and recorded on both sides, doubling playing time: up to four hours per side on later cassettes, for a total of eight hours. The format's Dynamic Track Following (DTF) system eliminated the need for a tracking control and produced noise-free freeze-frame and picture search, capabilities its rivals initially lacked. Video 2000 also offered high-quality stereo sound on later models.

Decline

Despite its technical merits, Video 2000 was marketed only in Europe and a few other PAL/SECAM territories, never gaining a foothold in North America. A smaller rental-movie library, later entry to the market, and VHS's longer recording times in NTSC markets undermined it. Philips conceded the consumer market in 1985 by adopting VHS, and the last Video 2000 machines were produced in 1988.
