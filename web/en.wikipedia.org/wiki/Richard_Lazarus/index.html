Richard Lazarus

Richard Stanley Lazarus (March 3, 1922 – November 24, 2002) was an American psychologist best known for his theory of cognitive appraisal in stress and emotion, and for developing the concept of coping.

Career

Born in New York City, Lazarus earned his doctorate at the University of Pittsburgh and taught for most of his career at the University of California, Berkeley. During the 1960s he conducted influential experiments showing that how a person appraises a situation — as threat, harm, or challenge — determines their emotional and physiological response, rather than the event alone.

Theory

Lazarus distinguished primary appraisal (evaluating significance), secondary appraisal (evaluating coping resources), and reappraisal. With Susan Folkman he published Stress, Appraisal, and Coping (1984), distinguishing problem-focused coping from emotion-focused coping. He also developed, with Folkman, the Ways of Coping questionnaire and earlier the Hassles and Uplifts Scales, arguing daily hassles predict health better than major life events alone.

Legacy

His transactional model shifted stress research away from purely stimulus-based or response-based accounts toward person-environment relationships. He was ranked among the most cited psychologists of the twentieth century.
