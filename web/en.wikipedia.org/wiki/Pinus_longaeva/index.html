Pinus longaeva, the Great Basin bristlecone pine, is a long-living species of bristlecone pine tree found in the higher mountains of California, Nevada and Utah. It is one of three bristlecone species and is famous for its extreme longevity: individual trees are among the oldest known non-clonal organisms on Earth, many exceeding 4,000 years in age.

Description

These trees grow slowly at elevations of roughly 2,900–3,600 m, often on dolomite outcrops, in cold, dry, windy conditions that few other plants tolerate. They are small to medium trees, typically 5–15 m tall, with short dark-green needles in bundles of five that can persist for decades. Much of an ancient tree may be dead, weathered wood — dense, resinous and highly resistant to decay — with life sustained by only a narrow strip of living bark connecting roots to a small crown. This partial dieback reduces the water a tree must supply and contributes to its survival.

Ages are determined by dendrochronology — counting and cross-matching annual rings in increment cores. Edmund Schulman pioneered the research in the 1950s. Bristlecone ring records, extending back more than 9,000 years when living and dead wood is combined, were crucial to calibrating radiocarbon dating.

Threats and protection

Historically protected by their remote, harsh habitat, bristlecone pines now face white pine blister rust, bark beetle outbreaks favoured by warming, and climate change shifting their suitable habitat. Many stands lie within protected areas such as Inyo National Forest's Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest and Great Basin National Park.
