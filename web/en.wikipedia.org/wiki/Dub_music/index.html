Dub music

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A mixing desk in a Kingston studio, hands pushing faders up as echo units glow]
A mixing desk in a Kingston studio, hands pushing faders up as echo units glow

Dub is a musical genre that grew out of Reggae in Jamaica in the late 1960s, consisting predominantly of instrumental remixes of existing recordings — vocals and instruments dropped in and out, drums and bass foregrounded, and the mixing desk itself played as an instrument through echo, reverb and filtering. Pioneered by King Tubby and Lee 'Scratch' Perry, dub invented the modern remix and seeded hip hop, techno, jungle, dubstep and ambient music.

Origins

Dub began with sound-system culture: engineers cut exclusive instrumental 'versions' on acetate for dances. Ruddy Redwood's accidental vocal-less mix (1968) and Tubby's electronics genius — custom-built effects, spring reverb, high-pass filters sweeping the rhythm — turned versioning into creation. Perry's Black Ark studio (1973–79) added tape loops, toys and smoke to produce otherworldly classics before he burned the studio down.

Technique and sound

The dub producer deconstructs: muting everything but the drum-and-bass 'riddim', letting a snare crack trail into space, sending a vocal fragment spiralling through delay. Albums like King Tubbys Meets Rockers Uptown (with Augustus Pablo, 1976) and Scientist's early-80s sets codified the form; Britain's diaspora (Adrian Sherwood's On-U Sound, Mad Professor) carried it into post-punk and beyond.

Legacy

Dub's remix grammar — versioning, drops, bass weight, studio-as-instrument — underlies disco edits, hip-hop breakbeats, house, techno, trip hop, jungle/drum and bass, dubstep and grime. 'Dub' as a verb (to copy, to re-voice) predates and outgrew the genre.
