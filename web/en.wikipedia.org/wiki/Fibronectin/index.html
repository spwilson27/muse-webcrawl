Fibronectin

Fibronectin is a high-molecular-weight (~500 kDa) glycoprotein of the extracellular matrix that binds to membrane-spanning receptor proteins called integrins. It also binds to other extracellular matrix components such as collagen, fibrin, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans (e.g. syndecans). Fibronectin plays a major role in cell adhesion, growth, migration, and differentiation, and it is important for processes such as wound healing and embryonic development. Altered fibronectin expression, degradation, and organization are associated with a number of pathologies, including cancer and fibrosis.

Structure

Fibronectin exists as a dimer of two nearly identical protein chains linked by a pair of C-terminal disulfide bonds. Each monomer is built from repeating modular units — type I, type II and type III domains. It contains binding sites for itself, collagen, heparin, fibrin and cell surfaces; the best-known cell-binding motif is the Arg–Gly–Asp (RGD) sequence in the tenth type III repeat, recognized by integrins such as α5β1.

Types

Two classes are distinguished: soluble plasma fibronectin, produced by hepatocytes and circulating in blood, where it participates in clotting and wound healing, and insoluble cellular fibronectin, assembled by fibroblasts and other cells into fibrils in the extracellular matrix. Alternative splicing of a single pre-mRNA generates some twenty human isoforms.

Function

Fibronectin fibrils guide cell migration during development, help organize the matrix, and are essential for wound repair: plasma fibronectin is incorporated into the clot, and cellular fibronectin forms a provisional scaffold for re-epithelialization. Knockout of the FN1 gene in mice is embryonically lethal, reflecting its central developmental role.
