Nuclear nonproliferation

Nuclear nonproliferation refers to the international efforts to prevent the spread of nuclear weapons and weapons-applicable nuclear technology, to ensure that nuclear materials in civilian programmes are not diverted to military uses, and ultimately to achieve nuclear disarmament. It is distinct from nuclear proliferation, the spread it seeks to prevent.

The Non-Proliferation Treaty

The cornerstone of the regime is the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT), opened for signature in 1968 and in force since 1970. The treaty rests on three pillars: non-proliferation (non-nuclear-weapon states agree never to acquire nuclear weapons), disarmament (the five recognized nuclear-weapon states — the United States, Russia, the United Kingdom, France, and China — commit to pursue negotiations toward disarmament), and the peaceful use of nuclear energy under safeguards.

Safeguards and institutions

The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) verifies compliance through safeguards inspections, material accountancy, and environmental sampling; the post-1991 discovery of Iraq's clandestine programme led to the strengthened "Additional Protocol". Export controls are coordinated through the Nuclear Suppliers Group and the Zangger Committee, while bodies such as the CTBTO monitor nuclear testing.

Challenges

India, Pakistan, and Israel never joined the NPT and developed nuclear weapons outside it; North Korea withdrew from the treaty in 2003 and has since tested nuclear devices. Concerns over Iran's enrichment programme, the spread of uranium enrichment and reprocessing technology, and illicit procurement networks such as that of A. Q. Khan remain central challenges to the regime.
