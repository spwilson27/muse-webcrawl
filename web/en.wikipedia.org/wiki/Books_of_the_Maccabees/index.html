The Books of the Maccabees are a group of Jewish historical and philosophical writings centred on the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BCE), in which Jewish rebels under the Hasmonean family resisted the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes' suppression of Jewish religion. The name derives from the epithet of the revolt's leader, Judas Maccabeus — "Maccabee" possibly meaning "the Hammer." The first two books are important historical sources for the period and are counted as deuterocanonical by the Catholic and Eastern Orthodox churches; Protestants and Jews regard them as valuable but non-canonical Apocrypha.

1 Maccabees

First Maccabees is a sober dynastic history, probably translated from a lost Hebrew original and composed around 100 BCE. It narrates events from the accession of Antiochus IV (175 BCE) to the death of Simon Thassi (134 BCE): the desecration of the Jerusalem Temple, the martyrdoms of the faithful, the guerrilla campaigns of Mattathias's sons Judas, Jonathan, and Simon, the rededication of the Temple (the origin of Hanukkah), and the achievement of Jewish political independence under the Hasmoneans. Its theology is restrained — heaven is invoked but no miracles are narrated, and divine intervention works through human courage.

2 Maccabees

Second Maccabees is an epitome of a five-volume history by Jason of Cyrene, written in Greek and more overtly theological and dramatic than its companion. It covers a shorter span (roughly 180–161 BCE), stresses the martyrdoms of Eleazar and of the mother and her seven sons (chapter 7), and articulates doctrines that became influential: resurrection of the dead, prayer for the dead (12:43–45, a key text in Catholic teaching on purgatory), intercession of the saints, and creation out of nothing (7:28). Vivid episodes include the apparition of the prophet Jeremiah and heavenly horsemen aiding Judas's forces.

3 and 4 Maccabees

Third Maccabees, despite its name, does not concern the Maccabees; it recounts a persecution of Egyptian Jews under Ptolemy IV Philopator (late 3rd century BCE) and their miraculous deliverance. It is canonical in some Eastern Orthodox traditions. Fourth Maccabees is a philosophical discourse in the form of a sermon, using the Maccabean martyrs to argue the Stoic-influenced thesis that devout reason can master the emotions; it is preserved in some Greek manuscripts and printed in Orthodox editions as an appendix.

Historical significance

Together the books are the principal surviving sources for the crisis that produced the feast of Hanukkah and for the emergence of the Hasmonean state. The historian Josephus drew heavily on 1 Maccabees. The Maccabean martyrs were venerated as saints in early Christianity, and their story shaped later Jewish and Christian understandings of martyrdom and religious resistance.
