Leo Szilard (Hungarian: Szilárd Leó; born Leó Spitz; 11 February 1898 – 30 May 1964) was a Hungarian-American physicist and inventor. He conceived the nuclear chain reaction in 1933, patented the idea of a nuclear reactor with Enrico Fermi, and in 1939 wrote the letter signed by Albert Einstein that led to the Manhattan Project that built the atomic bomb. Late in his life, having campaigned against the use of the bomb on Japan, he turned to molecular biology and arms-control advocacy.

Early life and education

Szilard was born in Budapest in the Kingdom of Hungary to a middle-class Jewish family of engineers and physicians. After serving in the Austro-Hungarian army during the First World War, he emigrated to Berlin in 1919 and completed his doctorate in physics at the University of Berlin in 1922, with a dissertation on thermodynamics — "On the extension of phenomenological thermodynamics to fluctuations" — that impressed Einstein and anticipated later work linking information and entropy (the "Szilard engine", a form of Maxwell's demon). In Berlin he worked with Einstein on an electromagnetic refrigerator pump, which they patented; it had no moving parts but was never commercially successful.

The chain reaction and the bomb

Fleeing Nazi Germany in 1933, Szilard arrived in London. Reading of Ernest Rutherford's dismissal of atomic energy as "moonshine", he conceived — famously, while waiting at a traffic light on Southampton Row — that if a neutron-induced reaction released more neutrons than it consumed, a self-sustaining chain reaction, and an explosive of unprecedented power, would be possible. He filed for a patent on the concept in 1934 and, fearing its military potential, assigned it to the British Admiralty for secrecy.

After nuclear fission was discovered in 1938, Szilard, working at Columbia University, confirmed with Fermi that uranium fission emitted secondary neutrons. In August 1939 he drafted the Einstein–Szilárd letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt warning that Nazi Germany might build atomic bombs; Einstein's signature gave it weight, and it set the American bomb programme in motion. Szilard worked on the Manhattan Project at Chicago, where on 2 December 1942 Fermi's team achieved the first self-sustaining chain reaction in Chicago Pile-1.

Opposition and later life

In 1945 Szilard organised the Szilard petition, signed by dozens of Manhattan Project scientists, asking President Truman not to use the bomb on Japan without warning and demonstration; it was suppressed and never reached Truman before the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. After the war he co-founded the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists and campaigned for civilian control of atomic energy and international arms control. He helped found the Pugwash Conferences (1957) and the Council for a Livable World (1962). Shifting to biology, he worked on ageing, memory, and molecular biology, and in 1964 became a resident fellow at the Salk Institute in La Jolla, where he died of a heart attack on 30 May 1964. He also wrote science fiction, including the acclaimed collection The Voice of the Dolphins (1961).
