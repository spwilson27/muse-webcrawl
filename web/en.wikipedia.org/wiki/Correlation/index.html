Correlation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Three scatter plots showing strong positive, near-zero and strong negative correlation]

In statistics, correlation describes the strength and direction of a relationship between two variables: whether higher values of one tend to accompany higher (positive correlation) or lower (negative correlation) values of the other.

The most common measure is Pearson's correlation coefficient, r, ranging from −1 (perfect negative linear relationship) to +1 (perfect positive). It was developed from Francis Galton's work by Karl Pearson in the 1890s.

Interpretation

Correlation measures linear association; two variables can be strongly related in a curved pattern with r near zero. Rank-based alternatives such as Spearman's coefficient capture monotonic relationships.

The dictum 'correlation does not imply causation' warns that an association may arise from a confounding variable, reverse causation, or coincidence — as in the classic spurious examples linking ice-cream sales and drowning deaths through hot weather.
