Shoddy

Shoddy is a recycled woollen yarn and cloth made from shredded rags and recovered fibres. The industry was pioneered in the West Riding of Yorkshire, England — particularly in Batley and Dewsbury, the heart of the "Heavy Woollen District" — in the early nineteenth century; Benjamin Law of Batley is credited with developing the process around 1813.

Production

Soft woollen rags are sorted, cleaned, and torn apart by toothed machines called "devils" or rag-grinders, producing a fibrous material that is blended with new wool ("mungo" being the shorter-fibred product from felted cloth) and respun into yarn, then woven into inexpensive blankets, uniforms and cloth. Because the fibres were shortened by recycling, shoddy goods wore out quickly.

Language and reputation

The association with inferior quality gave English the adjective "shoddy" meaning cheap, careless or poorly made. During the American Civil War, scandalous reports of "shoddy" uniforms disintegrating on Union soldiers — supplied by profiteering contractors dubbed the "Shoddy Aristocracy" — cemented the word's pejorative sense. The textile trade itself was substantial and legitimate, however, and Yorkshire's shoddy and mungo mills supplied global markets for over a century.
