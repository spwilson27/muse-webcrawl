Digestion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Digestion is the process by which organisms break down food into substances small enough to be absorbed and used. It combines mechanical breakdown (chewing, churning) with chemical breakdown by enzymes — a showcase of catalysis in the body — and it is regulated by the nervous and endocrine systems.

The human digestive system

Food travels from mouth (salivary amylase begins starch digestion) through the oesophagus to the stomach, where acid and pepsin begin protein digestion. In the small intestine, bile from the liver and enzymes from the pancreas complete the breakdown of carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and nutrients pass through the intestinal wall into the blood. The large intestine absorbs water and hosts the gut microbiome, whose fermentation produces some vitamins and gases. Waste leaves as faeces. The whole journey typically takes one to three days.

Digestive disorders range from reflux and ulcers (now known to be mostly caused by Helicobacter pylori infection — a triumph of the germ theory) to inflammatory bowel diseases and coeliac disease, an autoimmune reaction to gluten. Digestion's energy harvest feeds directly into metabolism.
