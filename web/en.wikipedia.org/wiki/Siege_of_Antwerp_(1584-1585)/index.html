The siege of Antwerp (1584–1585) was the climactic operation of Alexander Farnese, Duke of Parma, in the Spanish reconquest of the southern Netherlands during the Eighty Years' War. After a thirteen-month siege notable for extraordinary engineering — above all a fortified bridge of boats sealing the River Scheldt — the great commercial city of Antwerp capitulated on 17 August 1585, a turning point that confirmed the division of the Low Countries into a Spanish south and a rebellious Dutch north.

Background

Antwerp, northern Europe's richest port, had suffered the "Spanish Fury" mutiny sack of 1576 and joined the rebel Union of Utrecht in 1579. As governor-general from 1578, Parma methodically recovered the southern provinces; by 1584 only Antwerp, Brussels, Ghent and a few towns held out. Ghent and Brussels fell that winter, isolating Antwerp.

The siege

Parma invested the city from July 1584 and determined to starve it by closing the Scheldt, Antwerp's lifeline to the sea. Through the winter his engineers built a bridge some 730 metres long across the river at Kallo: piles, anchored barges and a roadway, defended by forts (San Fernando and San Felipe) and floating batteries. In April 1585 the defenders sent explosive fireships — "hellburners" designed by the Italian engineer Federigo Giambelli — against the bridge; one explosion killed hundreds of Spanish troops and wrecked part of the structure, but the breach was repaired and no relief fleet broke through. A final Dutch attempt to relieve the city by breaching the Kouwensteinsedijk in May failed. With food exhausted, the burgomaster Philips of Marnix negotiated surrender.

Terms and consequences

The terms of 17 August 1585 were comparatively lenient: no sack, Protestants allowed four years to convert or leave, and civic property respected. Even so, perhaps half of Antwerp's population — disproportionately its merchants and skilled workers — emigrated, many to Amsterdam and other northern cities, fuelling the Dutch Golden Age, while the Dutch blockade of the Scheldt (confirmed by the Peace of Münster in 1648) strangled Antwerp's trade for two centuries. The siege also fixed English intervention: alarmed by Antwerp's fall, Elizabeth I signed the Treaty of Nonsuch (1585) and sent troops under the Earl of Leicester.
