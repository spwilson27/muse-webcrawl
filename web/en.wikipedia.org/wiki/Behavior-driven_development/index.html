Behavior-driven development (BDD) is a software development methodology that evolved from test-driven development (TDD). It encourages collaboration among developers, testers, and non-technical stakeholders by describing a system's behavior in a shared, plain-language format that doubles as executable specification and automated acceptance tests.

History

Dan North introduced BDD around 2003–2006 while teaching TDD, finding that students struggled with the word "test". He reframed tests as specifications of behavior, developed the JBehave framework for Java, and popularized the "Given–When–Then" template for scenarios, adapted from user-story acceptance criteria. Dave Astels and others brought the ideas to Ruby with RSpec. Aslak Hellesøy created Cucumber in 2008, whose plain-text Gherkin language became the most widely used BDD notation.

Practice

Teams first hold structured conversations — sometimes called the "three amigos" meeting of business, development, and testing perspectives — to explore requirements through concrete examples. Examples are written as scenarios in the form:

Scenario: Successful withdrawal
  Given the account balance is $100
  When the customer withdraws $50
  Then the balance should be $50

Each line maps to automated test code, so the scenarios run as acceptance tests and remain as living documentation of the system's intended behavior. BDD is commonly paired with specification by example and with outside-in development, in which acceptance scenarios drive inner unit-level TDD cycles.
