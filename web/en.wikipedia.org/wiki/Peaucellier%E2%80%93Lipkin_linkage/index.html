Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage

The Peaucellier–Lipkin linkage is a mechanical linkage capable of transforming circular motion into perfect straight-line motion. Invented in 1864 by the French officer Charles-Nicolas Peaucellier and independently in 1871 by the Lithuanian mathematician Lipman Lipkin, it was the first exact straight-line mechanism.

Geometry

The linkage consists of a rhombus of four equal bars, with two longer equal bars connecting two opposite vertices of the rhombus to a fixed point. A seventh bar constrains one vertex to move in a circle passing through the fixed point. The mechanism implements circle inversion: the input and output points are inverse points with respect to a fixed circle, so a circular path through the centre of inversion maps to a straight line.

History and use

Exact straight-line motion was a celebrated problem of steam-engine design; James Watt's earlier linkage produced only an approximate straight line. Peaucellier's solution was initially a mathematical curiosity, but the mechanism influenced linkage theory and later found use in engineering applications requiring true linear motion.
