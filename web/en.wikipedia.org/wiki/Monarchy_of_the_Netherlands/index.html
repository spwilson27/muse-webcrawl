The monarchy of the Netherlands is a constitutional monarchy whose head of state is the King (or Queen) of the Netherlands, currently Willem-Alexander. The institution was established in 1815, when William I was proclaimed king after the Napoleonic Wars, although the dynastic house of Orange-Nassau had long served the Dutch Republic as stadtholders.

Constitutional role

Under the Constitution, the King is head of state and, together with the ministers, forms the government. Ministerial responsibility means that ministers, not the King, answer to parliament for acts of government. The King formally appoints and dismisses ministers, signs acts of parliament and royal decrees, delivers the annual Speech from the Throne on Prinsjesdag, and serves as president of the Council of State, though its day-to-day work is chaired by the vice-president.

History

The monarchy dates from the creation of the Kingdom of the Netherlands in 1815. William I ruled over a state that initially included Belgium, which seceded in 1830. Under William II a liberal constitution (1848) sharply curtailed royal power. The twentieth century was dominated by three queens regnant: Wilhelmina (1890–1948), Juliana (1948–1980) and Beatrix (1980–2013). Abdication, rather than death in office, has become the Dutch tradition; all three queens abdicated in favour of their successors, and Beatrix's abdication brought Willem-Alexander to the throne on 30 April 2013.

Succession

Succession is by absolute primogeniture among the legitimate descendants of the monarch, regulated by the Constitution and requiring parliamentary approval of royal marriages. The heir bears the title Prince or Princess of Orange.

Finances and residences

The King receives an annual constitutional allowance covering staff and material costs, and is exempt from income tax on this allowance. Principal palaces include Huis ten Bosch in The Hague (the residential palace), Noordeinde Palace (the working palace) and the Royal Palace on Dam Square in Amsterdam, used for state visits and official receptions.
