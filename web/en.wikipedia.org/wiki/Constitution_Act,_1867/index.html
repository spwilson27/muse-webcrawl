Constitution Act, 1867

The Constitution Act, 1867 (French: Loi constitutionnelle de 1867), originally enacted as the British North America Act, 1867 (BNA Act), is the foundational part of the Constitution of Canada. Passed by the Parliament of the United Kingdom and in force from 1 July 1867, it created the Dominion of Canada by uniting the provinces of Canada (split into Ontario and Quebec), Nova Scotia and New Brunswick in a federal union, and it continues to define the structure of government and the division of powers between the federal Parliament and the provincial legislatures.

History

The Act implemented the resolutions of the Charlottetown and Quebec Conferences (1864) and the London Conference (1866), at which delegates from the British North American colonies — the Fathers of Confederation, led by John A. Macdonald — negotiated a federal union. Confederation was driven by political deadlock in the Province of Canada, fears of American expansion after the Civil War, and the desire for an intercolonial railway. The Act was renamed the Constitution Act, 1867 by the Constitution Act, 1982, which patriated the constitution and added a domestic amending formula and the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

Structure and content

The Act establishes the executive power (vested in the Crown, represented by the Governor General), a bicameral Parliament consisting of the Senate and House of Commons, and provincial legislatures. Its best-known provisions are sections 91 and 92, which enumerate the legislative powers of Parliament (including trade and commerce, defence, criminal law, and "peace, order, and good government") and of the provincial legislatures (including property and civil rights, education under section 93, and local matters). Section 121 provides for free movement of goods among provinces, and section 133 protects the use of English and French in Parliament, the Quebec legislature and the courts.

Amendments and interpretation

Key amendments include the Constitution Act, 1871 (admission of new provinces), the Constitution Act, 1949, and the formula amendments of 1982. Judicial interpretation by the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council — famously describing the constitution as a "living tree" in Edwards v Canada (AG) (the Persons Case, 1929) — and later by the Supreme Court of Canada has shaped Canadian federalism.
