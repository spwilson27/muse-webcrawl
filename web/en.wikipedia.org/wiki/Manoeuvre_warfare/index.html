Manoeuvre warfare

Manoeuvre warfare is a military strategy which emphasizes movement, initiative, and surprise to gain an advantageous position relative to the enemy, rather than defeating the enemy by destroying its forces piece by piece (attrition warfare). Its aim is to attack the enemy's decision-making system and cohesion — to "dislocate" rather than merely destroy — so that the enemy becomes incapable of coherent resistance.

Concepts

Key ideas in modern maneuver theory include the Schwerpunkt (point of main effort), mission command (decentralized orders giving subordinates intent rather than detailed instructions), tempo, and the targeting of surfaces and gaps. Theorists such as John Boyd — with his OODA loop (observe–orient–decide–act) — argued that operating inside an adversary's decision cycle compounds confusion and paralysis. William S. Lind and others promoted manoeuvre warfare within the U.S. military reform movement of the 1970s–80s.

History

Manoeuvre has been practiced since antiquity — the double envelopment at Cannae (216 BC) is the classic archetype. The German Blitzkrieg of 1939–41 and the deep battle concepts of Soviet theorists such as Mikhail Tukhachevsky applied manoeuvre at operational scale with mechanized forces. The U.S. Marine Corps formally adopted manoeuvre warfare doctrine in its 1989 manual Warfighting (FMFM-1), and the U.S. Army's AirLand Battle doctrine embodied similar principles, demonstrated in the coalition ground campaign of the 1991 Gulf War.

Limitations

Critics note that manoeuvre approaches demand high training, logistics, communications, and intelligence; they may fare poorly in urban, insurgent, or protracted conflicts where there is no coherent enemy system to dislocate — situations in which political and attritional factors dominate.
