Artificial life

Artificial life (often abbreviated ALife or A-Life) is a field of study wherein researchers examine systems related to natural life, its processes, and its evolution, through the use of simulations with computer models, robotics, and biochemistry. The discipline was named by Christopher Langton, who organised the first "Workshop on the Synthesis and Simulation of Living Systems" at Los Alamos National Laboratory in 1987.

Overview

Artificial life attempts to understand the fundamental properties of living systems by building artificial systems that exhibit lifelike behaviour — "life as it could be," in Langton's phrase, as opposed to "life as we know it." It is conventionally divided into soft ALife (software simulations), hard ALife (robotics and hardware) and wet ALife (synthetic biochemistry and protocells).

Notable models

Classic models include John von Neumann's self-reproducing cellular automata, John Conway's Game of Life (1970), Thomas Ray's Tierra system of self-replicating computer programs (1991), Karl Sims's evolved virtual creatures (1994), and the Avida platform used for experimental digital evolution. Artificial life techniques overlap with artificial intelligence, evolutionary computation, complex systems and synthetic biology.
