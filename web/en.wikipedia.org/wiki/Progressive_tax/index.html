Progressive tax

A progressive tax is a tax in which the tax rate increases as the taxable amount increases. The term describes a distribution effect on income or expenditure: the effective rate progresses from low to high, so that a taxpayer's average tax rate is less than the marginal tax rate. Progressive taxation is most often applied to personal income taxes, where higher-income earners pay a larger percentage of income than lower-income earners.

Mechanism

Progressivity is usually achieved through graduated tax brackets: income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate, with rates rising across brackets. Deductions, exemptions, and refundable credits can also increase progressivity.

Rationale and debate

Supporters argue from the declining marginal utility of income and ability-to-pay principles that progressive taxes are fairer and reduce inequality. Critics argue they can discourage work and investment and add complexity. The opposite of a progressive tax is a regressive tax, in which the rate decreases as the amount subject to taxation increases; a proportional or flat tax applies a constant rate.

History

The graduated income tax was advocated by Adam Smith in principle and implemented in modern form in the 19th and 20th centuries; top marginal rates exceeded 90% in the United States and United Kingdom during parts of the mid-20th century.
