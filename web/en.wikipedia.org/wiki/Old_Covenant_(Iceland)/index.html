Old Covenant (Iceland)

The Old Covenant (Icelandic: Gamli sáttmáli) was an agreement reached in 1262–1264 between the Icelandic Commonwealth and the Norwegian crown, by which the Icelanders accepted the sovereignty of King Haakon IV of Norway and, later, his son Magnus VI. The covenant ended the Icelandic Commonwealth (founded in 930) and brought Iceland under Norwegian rule, a dependency that later passed to Denmark and lasted until the twentieth century.

Background

By the mid-thirteenth century, the Commonwealth had been destabilised by the Age of the Sturlungs — a period of violent feuding among powerful chieftain families (goðar), whose power had become concentrated in a few hands. The Norwegian king, pursuing longstanding ambitions and backed by the Church, intervened increasingly in Icelandic affairs. Gizur Þorvaldsson, Earl of Iceland, acted as the king's principal agent in negotiating submission.

Terms

Under the covenant, Icelanders swore allegiance and agreed to pay taxes to the Norwegian king. In return, the king undertook to maintain peace, to respect Icelandic law (the covenant was framed so that Icelanders kept their existing laws, subject to royal amendments), and to guarantee shipping — conventionally six ships a year — between Iceland and Norway, securing vital trade. Different districts accepted the agreement at assemblies between 1262 and 1264, and the union was later renewed, notably in 1302 when grievances over trade and law led to a reaffirmation of the terms.

Significance

The Old Covenant is a landmark in Icelandic history: later nationalist historiography treated it as the moment Iceland lost its independence, and it became a reference point in the nineteenth- and twentieth-century independence movement. Historians today debate how contractual or reciprocal the agreement truly was and how contemporaries understood it.
