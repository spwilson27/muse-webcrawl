Cognitive impairment

Cognitive impairment is an inclusive term to describe any characteristic that acts as a barrier to the cognition process or different areas of cognition. Cognition is the process of acquiring knowledge and understanding through thought, experience, and the senses. Cognitive impairment may affect memory, learning, concentration, attention, language, or decision-making, and ranges in severity from mild difficulties to the profound impairment of dementia.

Causes

Cognitive impairment can be congenital (for example, intellectual disability) or acquired later in life. Causes include neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, stroke and other vascular disease, traumatic brain injury, infections, tumours, metabolic and endocrine disorders, vitamin deficiencies, alcohol and drug effects, psychiatric illness such as depression, and the side effects of medications.

Mild cognitive impairment

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is an intermediate stage between the expected cognitive decline of normal ageing and the more serious decline of dementia. People with MCI have measurable difficulties with memory or other cognitive abilities but remain largely independent in daily life. MCI increases the risk of later developing dementia, although some people remain stable or improve.

Assessment and treatment

Assessment commonly uses screening instruments such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), supplemented by laboratory tests and neuroimaging to identify reversible causes. Treatment depends on the underlying cause: correcting metabolic problems or medication effects can reverse some cases, while cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine are used in some dementias. Lifestyle measures—including physical exercise, cognitive activity, and management of cardiovascular risk factors—may reduce risk or slow progression.
