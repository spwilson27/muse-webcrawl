Celibacy

DefinitionVoluntary abstention from marriage and/or sexual relations
FromLatin caelibatus, "unmarried state"
Practised inMany religious traditions; also secular contexts
Related termsChastity, abstinence, continence

Celibacy (from Latin caelibatus) is the state of voluntarily being unmarried, sexually abstinent, or both, usually for religious reasons. It is often associated with the role of a religious official or devotee. In its narrow sense, the term celibacy is applied only to those for whom the unmarried state is a result of a sacred vow, act of renunciation, or religious conviction. In wider senses, it refers to abstinence from sexual activity in general.

In religion

Christianity

In the Roman Catholic Church, celibacy is mandatory for priests of the Latin Church, and bishops are chosen from celibate priests; religious monks and nuns take vows including celibate chastity. Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches ordain married men to the priesthood, but bishops are celibate, and priests may not marry after ordination. Most Protestant traditions reject mandatory clerical celibacy, though it is practised voluntarily by some religious communities.

Buddhism and Jainism

Buddhist monks and nuns observe celibacy as part of the Vinaya monastic code, regarding sexual desire as an obstacle to enlightenment. In Jainism, celibacy (brahmacharya) is one of the five major vows, observed completely by monks and nuns and in modified form by lay followers.

Hinduism

Brahmacharya, the first of the four stages (ashramas) of life, is traditionally a period of student celibacy; celibacy is also lifelong for sannyasis who renounce the world. The concept extends to the disciplined control of the senses generally.

History and discussion

Celibacy has ancient roots, visible in the Vestal Virgins of Rome, some priesthoods of the ancient Near East, and early Christian ascetic movements. The Western Church moved toward obligatory clerical celibacy during the Gregorian reforms of the 11th century, motivated by spiritual ideals and by concerns about church property passing to priests' heirs. In the modern world, celibacy is also adopted outside religion, and involuntary celibacy has become a topic of psychological and sociological study.
