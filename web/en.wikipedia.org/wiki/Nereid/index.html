The Nereids (Ancient Greek: Νηρηΐδες, sg. Νηρηΐς) were sea nymphs in Greek mythology, the fifty daughters of the sea god Nereus and the Oceanid Doris. They often accompanied Poseidon, the god of the sea, and were friendly and helpful to sailors fighting perilous storms. The name Nereid is also used in modern Greek folklore (νεράιδα, neraida) for fairies generally.

Mythology

The Nereids dwelt with their father in a silvery cave at the bottom of the Aegean Sea. Hesiod's Theogony names all fifty. The most famous individual Nereids include Thetis, mother of Achilles; Amphitrite, wife of Poseidon; Galatea, beloved of Acis and pursued by Polyphemus; Psamathe; and Calypso in some lists. The Nereids lamented with Thetis at the death of Achilles, and they were appealed to in the voyage of the Argonauts, whom they guided past the Wandering Rocks.

When Cassiopeia boasted that her daughter Andromeda surpassed the Nereids in beauty, the offended nymphs complained to Poseidon, who sent a flood and the sea monster Cetus against Ethiopia — leading to Andromeda's exposure and rescue by Perseus.

In art

Nereids riding dolphins, hippocamps and other sea creatures are a frequent motif in ancient mosaics, Roman sarcophagi and Renaissance and Baroque painting and sculpture. The Nereid Monument, a 4th-century BC tomb from Xanthos in Lycia, is now in the British Museum.

Legacy

Nereid, the third-largest moon of Neptune, is named after them, as is the family name Nereididae of polychaete worms. In modern Greek tradition the neraides survive as beautiful and dangerous fairy women associated with water and lonely places.
