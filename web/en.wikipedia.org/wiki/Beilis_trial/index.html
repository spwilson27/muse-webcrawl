Beilis trial

The Beilis trial (also called the Beilis affair) was a notorious 1913 trial in Kiev, in the Russian Empire, in which Menahem Mendel Beilis, a Jewish factory worker, was prosecuted on the charge of having ritually murdered a 13-year-old Christian boy, Andriy Yushchinsky, in order to use his blood in religious rites — the ancient blood libel. The case became an international cause célèbre, widely seen as a state-sponsored antisemitic prosecution, and ended in Beilis's acquittal.

Background

Yushchinsky's body was found in a cave near Kiev in March 1911, covered with stab wounds. Although investigators soon suspected a criminal gang connected to a local woman, Vera Cheberiak, right-wing and antisemitic organisations pressed the theory of a Jewish ritual murder. Under pressure from officials in St. Petersburg, Beilis, who worked at a brick factory near the site, was arrested in July 1911 and held for more than two years awaiting trial.

Trial and aftermath

The trial, held from September to October 1913, featured "expert" testimony claiming ritual murder was a Jewish practice, alongside defence evidence that the wounds were consistent with an ordinary crime. The jury — largely composed of Ukrainian peasants — acquitted Beilis, although it accepted that the murder had occurred at the factory, a finding antisemitic campaigners exploited. Beilis emigrated first to Palestine and then to the United States, where he published a memoir, The Story of My Sufferings. The affair is often compared to the Dreyfus affair in France and inspired Bernard Malamud's 1966 novel The Fixer.
