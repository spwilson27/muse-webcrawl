Kwasi Wiredu (1931–2022) was a Ghanaian philosopher, among the most influential African philosophers of the twentieth century, known for the programme of conceptual decolonisation — the critical sifting of African thought-categories inherited through colonial languages — and for influential work on truth, personhood, and consensual democracy in Akan political thought. Educated at the University of Ghana and Oxford, he taught for decades at Legon before moving to the University of South Florida, editing the journal of the field's debates and insisting, against romantics and dismissers alike, that African philosophy be held to the universal standards of clarity and argument.

Conceptual decolonisation

Wiredu argued that African thinkers philosophise in colonial languages (English, French, Portuguese) whose categories silently structure the results: his celebrated analyses work problems twice — in English and in his native Akan — to expose what each language smuggles in. On truth, he argued that the Akan concept maps truth to what is so, not to a correspondence metaphysic; on the person, he mapped Akan communitarian concepts against Western individualism; on mind, he showed the Akan adwene does not divide mind from body as Descartes does. The method — neither rejecting African tradition nor exempting it from critique — defined a generation's middle path between ethnophilosophy and mere imitation (see African philosophy).

Consensus and democracy

His political philosophy recovered the consensual decision-making of traditional Akan councils — where discussion continued until dissenters could accept the outcome without being outvoted into resentment — as a resource for modern African democracy, proposing a non-party consensual model as a thought experiment against imported adversarial systems. Critics questioned scalability; the proposal remains the most discussed African contribution to democratic theory.
