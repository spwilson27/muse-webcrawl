Fulgarite

"Fulgarite" (with an a) is a nonstandard or historical spelling. The accepted term is fulgurite (from Latin fulgur, "lightning"), the glassy tube or crust formed when lightning strikes sand, soil, or rock and fuses it at temperatures of at least 1,800 °C.

Formation and properties

When a cloud-to-ground lightning bolt strikes silica-rich sand, the intense heat vaporises a narrow channel and melts the surrounding grains, which cool almost instantly into a hollow, branching tube of natural glass (lechatelierite). Fulgurites record the path of the discharge, sometimes extending several metres into the ground, and their interiors are often smooth and glassy while the exterior is rough and coated with partially melted grains. They are classified by the material in which they form — sand, clay, caliche, and rock fulgurites.

Scientific significance

Fulgurites preserve information about past lightning activity and can trap gases from the atmosphere at the time of formation, so they are studied in paleoclimatology and in research on the origin of life, because the extreme energy of lightning can reduce phosphorus compounds into more biologically reactive forms. Exceptionally old specimens — for instance a Libyan Desert fulgurite dated to roughly 15,000 years — help reconstruct the environments in which they formed.
