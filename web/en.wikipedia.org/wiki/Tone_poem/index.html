Tone poem

A tone poem or symphonic poem is a piece of orchestral music in a single continuous movement in which an extramusical programme—such as a poem, a story, a painting, or an idea—provides a narrative or illustrative element. The form was pioneered by Franz Liszt in the mid-19th century and became one of the characteristic genres of Romantic programme music.

Development

Liszt composed thirteen symphonic poems between 1848 and 1882, including Les préludes, Prometheus, Mazeppa and Hamlet. He relied on the principle of thematic transformation, in which a single theme changes character to embody successive stages of the programme, loosening the constraints of classical sonata form.

Major works

The genre flourished across Europe. Bedřich Smetana wrote the cycle Má vlast ("My Country"), including Vltava (The Moldau). Richard Strauss carried the form to virtuoso heights with Don Juan (1888), Tod und Verklärung, Till Eulenspiegels lustige Streiche, Also sprach Zarathustra (1896) and Ein Heldenleben. In France, Debussy's Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune and in Finland Sibelius's Finlandia and The Swan of Tuonela extended the idiom; Rachmaninoff's The Isle of the Dead and Respighi's Roman trilogy continued it into the 20th century.

Characteristics

Unlike the multi-movement symphony, a tone poem unfolds in one span, its form dictated by the programme rather than by abstract convention. Orchestration is typically rich and colourful, and composers frequently supplied printed programmes to guide listeners.
