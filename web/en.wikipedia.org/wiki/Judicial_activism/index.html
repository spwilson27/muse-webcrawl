Judicial activism

Debates over judicial activism concern how far courts may go in overriding the political branches.

Judicial activism is a judicial philosophy holding that courts can and should go beyond the applicable law to consider broader societal implications of their decisions. It is sometimes used as an antonym of judicial restraint. The term usually describes judges who are considered to be ruling on the basis of their personal political opinions rather than on precedent, and has been applied to courts of differing political tendencies.

Definition and usage

The phrase was popularized by historian Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr. in a 1947 Fortune article describing the justices of the United States Supreme Court. Because there is no single agreed measure of activism, the label is often contested: decisions that strike down legislation, overturn precedent, or recognize new rights are commonly called activist by their critics, whatever their ideological direction. Legal scholars have proposed more precise criteria, such as the frequency with which a court invalidates statutes or departs from constitutional text and original understanding.

Examples and debate

In United States debates, landmark rulings such as Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Roe v. Wade (1973), and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) have been described as activist by opponents, while supporters defend them as necessary protection of constitutional rights against legislative majorities. Conservative courts have likewise been accused of activism for decisions striking down economic regulation in the Lochner era or campaign-finance laws in Citizens United v. FEC (2010).

Beyond the United States, the concept is prominent in India, where the Supreme Court's public interest litigation and doctrines such as the "basic structure" of the Constitution have been both praised as guardians of rights and criticized as overreach, and in other constitutional courts worldwide.
