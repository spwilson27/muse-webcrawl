Umbra, penumbra and antumbra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The umbra, penumbra and antumbra are three distinct parts of a shadow, created by any light source after impinging on an opaque object. Assuming no diffraction, for a collimated beam (such as a point source) of light, only the umbra is cast.

These names are most often used for the shadows cast by celestial bodies, though they are sometimes used to describe levels of darkness, such as in sunspots.

Umbra

The umbra (Latin for "shadow") is the innermost and darkest part of a shadow, where the light source is completely blocked by the occluding body. An observer within the umbra experiences a total eclipse. The umbra of a round body occluding a round light source forms a right circular cone; viewing from its apex, the two bodies appear the same size.

Penumbra

The penumbra (from Latin paene, "almost", and umbra, "shadow") is the region in which only a portion of the light source is obscured by the occluding body. An observer in the penumbra experiences a partial eclipse. An alternative definition is that the penumbra is the region where some or all of the light source is obscured — distinguishing it from the umbra, where it is wholly obscured.

Antumbra

The antumbra (from Latin ante, "before", and umbra) is the region from which the occluding body appears entirely within the disc of the light source. An observer in this region experiences an annular eclipse, in which a bright ring is visible around the eclipsing body. If the observer moves closer to the light source, the apparent size of the occluding body increases until it causes a full umbra.

Scale in eclipses

During a solar eclipse, the Moon's umbra on Earth is typically only about 100–160 km wide at the equator, which is why total eclipses are rare at any given place, while the penumbra spans thousands of kilometres. Because the Moon's umbral cone only just reaches the Earth, small variations in orbital distance decide whether an eclipse is total, annular or hybrid.
