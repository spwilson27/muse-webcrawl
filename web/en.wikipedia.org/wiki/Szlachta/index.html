The szlachta (Polish: [ˈʂlaxta]) were the noble estate of the realm in the Kingdom of Poland and, later, the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Unusually numerous by European standards—some 6 to 10 per cent of the population—the szlachta enjoyed extensive legal and political privileges, including a decisive role in electing kings and the doctrine of the Golden Liberty (złota wolność), under which all nobles were formally equal regardless of wealth.

Origins and composition

The estate developed from medieval knightly clans sharing coats of arms (herby); membership was hereditary and attached to armorial clans rather than individual titles. Magnates such as the Radziwiłł and Potocki families amassed latifundia, while at the other extreme landless "barefoot" szlachta (gołota) retained noble status and voting rights in local assemblies (sejmiki).

Political power

From 1505 (Nihil novi) no new law could be made without the consent of the Sejm, dominated by the szlachta. Privileges such as Neminem captivabimus (1430s) guaranteed freedom from arrest without court verdict. After 1573 kings were freely elected by the assembled nobility (electio viritim). The unanimity principle and its abuse in the liberum veto increasingly paralysed the state in the 18th century, contributing to the Partitions of Poland. Noble legal status was abolished by the March Constitution of 1921.

Culture

Szlachta culture—Sarmatism—idealised an origin among the ancient Sarmatians, reflected in dress (kontusz, żupan, curved szabla sabre), manor-house (dwór) life and a strong emphasis on honour, hospitality and republican virtue.
