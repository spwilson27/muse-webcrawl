Peripatric speciation

Peripatric speciation is a mode of speciation in which a new species is formed from an isolated peripheral population. It is similar to allopatric speciation in that populations become isolated and prevented from exchanging genes, but differs in that one of the populations is much smaller than the other — typically a small founding group at the edge of the species' range.

Mechanism

Because the peripheral population is small, genetic drift and founder effects are strong, and the population is exposed to different selection pressures in its marginal habitat. These forces can produce rapid genetic divergence and reproductive isolation from the parent population. The concept is closely associated with Ernst Mayr, who proposed that speciation often occurs in small, geographically isolated founder populations, and with the related idea of genetic revolutions following population bottlenecks.

Examples

Peripatric speciation is invoked to explain patterns such as the diversity of species on archipelagos colonised by small numbers of individuals, island endemics derived from mainland ancestors, and ring-like distributions of forms around geographic barriers.
