Brassica oleracea is a plant species in the family Brassicaceae that includes many common cultivars used as vegetables, such as cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts, collard greens, Savoy cabbage, kohlrabi and gai lan. Its uncultivated form, wild cabbage, native to coastal southern and western Europe, is a hardy plant of sea cliffs tolerant of salt and lime but intolerant of competition.

Domestication and cultivar groups

B. oleracea is a classic demonstration of artificial selection: breeders selecting on different organs of the same species produced strikingly different vegetables. Selection on terminal leaf buds gave headed cabbage (Capitata group); on lateral buds, Brussels sprouts (Gemmifera); on the swollen stem, kohlrabi (Gongylodes); on arrested inflorescences, cauliflower (Botrytis) and broccoli (Italica); and on leaves alone, kale and collards (Acephala and Sabellica). Cultivation of leafy forms dates to Greek and Roman antiquity, while headed cabbage, Brussels sprouts and cauliflower were developed in the medieval and early modern periods. The species is one of the three diploid progenitors in the "triangle of U" model of Brassica evolution, proposed by Woo Jang-choon in 1935.

Description

The wild plant is a biennial with fleshy, glaucous blue-grey leaves adapted to store water and nutrients in its cliff habitat. In its second year it produces a tall spike of yellow four-petalled flowers typical of the mustard family, followed by slender seed pods (siliques). Cultivars are generally grown as annuals harvested before flowering.

Nutrition and chemistry

Like other brassicas, B. oleracea vegetables are rich in vitamin C, vitamin K, folate and fibre, and contain glucosinolates — sulphur compounds that break down into biologically active products such as sulforaphane (especially abundant in broccoli sprouts), which have been widely studied for possible health effects. Excessive consumption of raw brassicas can interfere with iodine uptake owing to goitrogenic compounds, though normal culinary amounts are considered safe.
