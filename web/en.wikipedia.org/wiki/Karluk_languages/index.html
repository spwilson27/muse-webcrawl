The Karluk languages (also known as the Qarluq or Southeastern Common Turkic languages) are a branch of the Turkic language family spoken across Central Asia. Their principal modern members are Uzbek, the national language of Uzbekistan, and Uyghur, spoken mainly in the Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region of China. The branch is named after the Karluks, a Turkic tribal confederation prominent in the region from the 7th century onward.

History

The Karluk branch descends from the language of the Karakhanid Khanate, the first Turkic Muslim dynasty, whose literary language ("Khaqani Turkic") produced works such as Yusuf Khass Hajib's Kutadgu Bilig (1070) and Mahmud al-Kashgari's dictionary of Turkic. In the Timurid era its descendant, Chagatai, became the great literary language of Central Asia, used by poets such as Ali-Shir Nava'i; it remained a written standard into the early 20th century.

Modern languages

Uzbek, with over 30 million speakers, is written in a Latin alphabet (having used Arabic and Cyrillic scripts historically) and lacks the vowel harmony typical of other Turkic languages, partly through long contact with Persian. Uyghur, with around 10 million speakers, uses a modified Arabic script in China and preserves vowel harmony. The small Ili Turki language of the Ili valley is endangered. Uzbek and Uyghur are partially mutually intelligible.
