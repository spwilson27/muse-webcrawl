Closeted

Closeted and in the closet are metaphors for people who conceal aspects of their identity — most commonly lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, or otherwise LGBTQ people who have not disclosed their sexual orientation or gender identity. A person may be closeted in some contexts (family, workplace) and out in others.

[Image: A partially open closet door, the visual origin of the metaphor]

Origin of the metaphor

Usage of "closet" in this sense is documented from the late 1960s in American gay culture. The metaphor blends two older images: the closet as a private space where one hides, and the "skeleton in the closet" — a shameful family secret. The complementary process of disclosure is called "coming out of the closet", or simply coming out.

Psychological and social aspects

Research on minority stress links prolonged concealment with anxiety, depression, and difficulties forming relationships, though the safety of disclosure depends heavily on legal and social environments; in many jurisdictions LGBTQ people face criminal penalties, employment discrimination, or family rejection. The deliberate disclosure of another person's concealed identity without consent is called "outing" and is widely regarded as a serious breach of privacy, except where debated in cases of public figures perceived as acting against LGBTQ interests.

Extended uses

The metaphor has been extended to other concealed identities and beliefs — a "closeted" atheist, political supporter, or fan — and to "closet" as an adjective for something kept hidden, as in "closet alcoholic".
