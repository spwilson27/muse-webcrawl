Forensic accounting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forensic accounting, also referred to as forensic accountancy or financial forensics, is the specialty practice area of accounting that investigates whether firms engage in financial reporting misconduct. Forensic accountants apply accounting and investigative skills to matters that may come before a court of law, and the term "forensic" in this context means "suitable for use in a court of law."

Scope of work

Forensic accounting engagements typically fall into two broad categories: investigative accounting, which examines evidence of criminal or fraudulent conduct such as embezzlement, securities fraud, money laundering, and tax evasion; and litigation support, which quantifies economic damages in disputes such as breach of contract, business valuation disagreements, and insurance claims.

Methods

Practitioners reconstruct financial records, trace assets, interview witnesses, and analyse transactions for anomalies. Statistical techniques such as Benford's law analysis may be used to detect irregularities in large datasets. Findings are documented to a standard intended to withstand scrutiny as expert testimony.

Credentials

Forensic accountants are often Certified Public Accountants (CPAs) holding additional credentials such as Certified Fraud Examiner (CFE) or Certified in Financial Forensics (CFF). The field gained prominence following major corporate scandals such as Enron and WorldCom in the early 2000s, which led to reforms including the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002.
