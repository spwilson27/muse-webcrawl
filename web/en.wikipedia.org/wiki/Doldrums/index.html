Doldrums

The doldrums are a colloquial expression derived from historical maritime usage for parts of the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans affected by the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), a low-pressure area around the equator where the prevailing winds are calm. The low pressure is caused by the heat created by the sun in equatorial regions heating the air and causing it to rise vertically rather than blow horizontally.

The doldrums are also noted for calm periods when the winds disappear altogether, trapping sail-powered boats for periods of days or weeks. The term has passed into common usage to refer to a state of inactivity, mild depression, listlessness or stagnation.

Climatology

The ITCZ encircles the Earth near the thermal equator, shifting seasonally with the sun. Air rises through deep convection, producing towering cumulonimbus, heavy rainfall and light, variable surface winds interrupted by violent squalls and thunderstorms. The zone is typically found between about 5° north and 5° south, though its position varies by ocean basin and season.

Maritime significance

In the age of sail, crossing the doldrums was a dreaded part of ocean passages; ships could be becalmed for weeks with dwindling water while surrounded by rain they could not always catch. The region features in Samuel Taylor Coleridge's The Rime of the Ancient Mariner and remains a tactical challenge in modern ocean racing such as the Vendée Globe and The Ocean Race.
