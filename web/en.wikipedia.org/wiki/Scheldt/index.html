The Scheldt (French: Escaut; Dutch: Schelde) is a 350-kilometre (220 mi) long river that originates in northern France, flows through Belgium and the southwestern Netherlands before draining into the North Sea. In the Romanian-language tradition its lower course gives Antwerp its raison d'être: the Port of Antwerp, one of Europe's largest, lies on its banks about 80 km inland.

Course

The Scheldt rises near Gouy in the Aisne department of France, passes Cambrai and Valenciennes, enters Belgium at Tournai, and flows through Ghent — where it receives the Leie (Lys) — and on to Antwerp. Below Antwerp the river becomes the broad tidal Western Scheldt (Westerschelde), flowing through Dutch territory past Terneuzen to the North Sea at Vlissingen (Flushing). A second former mouth, the Eastern Scheldt (Oosterschelde), is now a protected branch of the Delta Works.

History

Control of the Scheldt estuary shaped Low Countries politics for centuries: the Dutch Republic closed the river to Antwerp's trade after 1585, a closure confirmed at Münster in 1648 and ended only in 1795 under French revolutionary occupation. In 1839 Britain guaranteed Belgian independence partly around Scheldt navigation rights. In 1944 the Battle of the Scheldt cleared the estuary so Allied shipping could use Antwerp for the advance into Germany.

Navigation

The river is navigable from Cambrai and forms part of the network linking the Seine basin via the Canal Saint-Quentin; seaward, constant dredging keeps Antwerp accessible to large container ships. The Scheldt–Rhine Canal links Antwerp to Rotterdam.
