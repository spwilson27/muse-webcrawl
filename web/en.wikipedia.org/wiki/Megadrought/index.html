Megadrought

A megadrought is a prolonged drought lasting two decades or longer. Such events are known from paleoclimate records — including tree rings, lake sediments, and cave deposits — which show that past megadroughts reshaped ecosystems and contributed to the decline or migration of human societies.

Historical examples

Tree-ring records from western North America document severe medieval megadroughts during the Medieval Climate Anomaly (roughly 900–1300 CE), coinciding with the abandonment of many Ancestral Puebloan settlements in the Southwest. Other notable megadroughts include those that struck the Sahel in the 20th century, the decades-long drought in Australia known as part of the Federation Drought era pattern, and events implicated in the fall of the Akkadian Empire and stress on Angkor in Southeast Asia.

The modern Southwest North American megadrought

The period from about 2000 onward in southwestern North America has been identified in studies as the driest 22-year span in the region since at least 800 CE, surpassing the severity of the late-1500s megadrought. Research attributes a substantial fraction of its severity to human-caused climate change: higher temperatures increase evaporation and dry soils even where precipitation declines are modest. Effects include record-low levels in Lake Mead and Lake Powell, reduced Colorado River flows, widespread tree mortality, and intensified wildfires.

Drivers

Megadroughts are typically driven by persistent sea-surface temperature patterns — such as cool tropical Pacific (La Niña-like) conditions — that steer storm tracks away from a region for decades, combined in the modern era with warming-driven increases in atmospheric water demand.
