Aerobic exercise (also known as endurance exercise, cardio, or cardio-respiratory exercise) is physical exercise of low to high intensity that depends primarily on the aerobic energy-generating process — the use of oxygen to meet energy demands during sustained activity. Examples include running, cycling, swimming, brisk walking, and dancing. Aerobic exercise is performed by repeating sequences of light-to-moderate intensity activities for extended periods of time.

History

The concept was popularized by US Air Force physician Kenneth H. Cooper, whose 1968 book Aerobics introduced a points system for exercise and helped launch the jogging boom. In the 1980s, group aerobics classes set to music, promoted by figures such as Jane Fonda, became a mass fitness phenomenon.

Physiology

During aerobic exercise, the cardiorespiratory system delivers oxygen to working muscles, which metabolize fats and carbohydrates oxidatively. Regular training increases stroke volume, capillary density, mitochondrial number, and VO2 max (maximal oxygen uptake), the standard measure of cardiorespiratory fitness. By contrast, anaerobic exercise — such as sprinting and heavy weightlifting — relies on energy sources in the muscles themselves and generates lactate.

Health benefits

Regular aerobic exercise is associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke, depression, and several cancers, and with improved mood, sleep, and cognitive function. Public health guidelines such as those of the World Health Organization recommend that adults accumulate at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity, aerobic activity per week.

Aerobics as a sport

Competitive aerobic gymnastics, governed by the FIG, combines choreographed routines with difficult elements and has been contested at world championships since 1995.
