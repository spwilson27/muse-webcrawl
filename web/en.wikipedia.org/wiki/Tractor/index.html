Tractor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A tractor is an engineering vehicle specifically designed to deliver a high tractive effort (or torque) at slow speeds, for the purposes of hauling a trailer or machinery such as that used in agriculture, mining or construction. Most commonly, the term is used to describe a farm vehicle that provides the power and traction to mechanise agricultural tasks, especially (and originally) tillage, and now many more. Agricultural implements may be towed behind or mounted on the tractor, and the tractor may provide hydraulic or electrical power to operate them.

The word tractor is taken from Latin, being the agent noun of trahere, "to pull". The first recorded use of the word in the sense of a machine for pulling wagons and ploughs dates to 1896, following earlier terms such as "traction engine".

History

The first powered farm implements in the nineteenth century were portable steam engines and then traction engines, large steam tractors used to haul ploughing tackle. Internal-combustion tractors followed: the first commercially successful lightweight petrol tractor was built by Dan Albone in England in 1901, and the Froelich tractor of 1892 was an ancestor of the John Deere line. The Fordson Model F (1917) was the first mass-produced, affordable tractor and helped popularise the machine. Harry Ferguson's three-point linkage, introduced in the 1920s–30s and embodied in the Ferguson TE20 ("Little Grey Fergie", 1946), revolutionised implement attachment and became an industry standard. Henry Ford's Fordson and the Farmall Regular (1924) — the first successful row-crop tractor — drove the rapid displacement of horses and mules from farms.

Design and components

A typical modern tractor has large, heavily treaded rear tyres (or tracks) driven by a diesel engine, with smaller front wheels for steering; four-wheel drive is now common. Key features include:

  • Power take-off (PTO) — a rotating shaft, standardised at 540 or 1000 rpm, that transfers engine power to implements such as mowers and balers.
  • Hydraulics — a three-point hitch raises and lowers mounted implements, and remote hydraulic outlets power cylinders on trailed machines.
  • Drawbar — for towing trailers and trailed implements.
  • Cab — modern tractors have enclosed, air-conditioned cabs with suspension seats, electronic displays, GPS guidance and, increasingly, semi-autonomous steering.

Types

Beyond the standard agricultural (utility) tractor there are row-crop tractors with adjustable wheel spacing, orchard tractors with narrow, shielded bodies, compact and sub-compact tractors for smallholdings and landscaping, crawler tractors on steel or rubber tracks, and articulated four-wheel-drive tractors for very heavy work. Other machines called tractors include the truck tractor (the powered front unit of a semi-trailer truck), aircraft tugs, and military prime movers. The tractor also gave its name to the "tractor" propeller configuration, in which the propeller pulls the aircraft from the front.

Manufacturers

Major manufacturers include John Deere (Deere & Company) — the world's largest — CNH Industrial (Case IH and New Holland), AGCO (Massey Ferguson, Fendt, Valtra), Kubota, Mahindra (the largest by volume, in India), Claas, SDF (Same Deutz-Fahr) and Argo Tractors.

Safety

Tractors are among the most dangerous farm machines; overturning is the leading cause of fatal injury, mitigated by rollover protection structures (ROPS) and seat belts. PTO shafts, historically unguarded, are another major hazard. Slow-moving tractors on public roads are required in many countries to display a slow-moving vehicle emblem.
