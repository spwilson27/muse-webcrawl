Hachiman (八幡神, also Yahata no kami) is a syncretic divinity of archery and war in Japanese religion that incorporates elements from both Shinto and Buddhism. Since ancient times Hachiman, widely considered the divine protector of Japan and its people, was venerated by the Imperial Court as the guardian of the state and by the samurai class as the tutelary god of warriors and of the Minamoto clan.

Identity and mythology

Hachiman is conventionally identified with the deified spirit of Emperor Ōjin, the semi-legendary 15th emperor of Japan, enshrined together with his mother Empress Jingū and his wife Himegami. The cult's centre of origin is Usa in present-day Ōita Prefecture, Kyushu, where oracles of the god were recorded from the 6th–8th centuries. In the syncretic shinbutsu-shūgō tradition Hachiman was also revered as a Buddhist deity, receiving the title Hachiman Daibosatsu ("Great Bodhisattva Hachiman") — a rare honour for a kami — and his shrines were often administered together with temples.

Worship and shrines

There are an estimated 25,000–44,000 Hachiman shrines across Japan, the largest network after those of Inari. The three most important are Usa Jingū in Ōita (the head shrine), Iwashimizu Hachimangū in Kyoto, founded in 859 at the god's oracle request, and Tsurugaoka Hachimangū in Kamakura, established by Minamoto no Yoritomo, founder of the Kamakura shogunate. The god's messenger animal is the dove, which appears on shrine crests and ema plaques.

History

During the attempted Mongol invasions of Japan (1274 and 1281), prayers to Hachiman preceded the typhoons — the kamikaze — that destroyed the invasion fleets, cementing his role as divine protector of the country. In the Meiji period the state separated Shinto and Buddhism (shinbutsu bunri), suppressing Hachiman's Buddhist identity, but popular devotion continued; he remains a god of war, of archery, and of the welfare of the nation generally.
