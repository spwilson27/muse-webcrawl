The history of Earth science covers the development of the natural sciences concerned with the planet Earth—above all geology, but also meteorology, oceanography, and geophysics. Questions about Earth's age, structure, and changing surface have been debated since antiquity.

Early ideas

Ancient writers such as Aristotle speculated on earthquakes, fossils, and the slow change of coastlines. Leonardo da Vinci correctly interpreted marine fossils in mountains. In the 17th century Nicolas Steno formulated principles of stratigraphy, and naturalists debated whether rocks formed from water (Neptunism) or heat (Plutonism).

Foundations of geology

James Hutton's Theory of the Earth (1788) argued that geological features were shaped by slow, ongoing processes—uniformitarianism—implying an Earth of vast antiquity. Charles Lyell's Principles of Geology (1830–33) popularized the doctrine and influenced Darwin. William Smith produced the first national geological map of England (1815), using fossils to order strata.

Continental drift and plate tectonics

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, but the mechanism was lacking; only in the 1960s did seafloor spreading and paleomagnetism establish plate tectonics, unifying geology. Radiometric dating fixed Earth's age at about 4.54 billion years.

Modern Earth science

Satellite observation, deep-sea drilling, and Earth-system science now integrate the solid Earth, oceans, atmosphere, and biosphere, informing understanding of climate change and natural hazards.
