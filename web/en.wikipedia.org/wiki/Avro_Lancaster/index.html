The Avro Lancaster is a British Second World War heavy bomber designed and manufactured by Avro as a development of the twin-engined Avro Manchester. Powered by four Rolls-Royce Merlin engines, the Lancaster became the most successful British heavy bomber of the war and the principal aircraft of RAF Bomber Command's night offensive against Nazi Germany.

Design and development

The Manchester, powered by two unreliable Rolls-Royce Vulture engines, was redesigned by Roy Chadwick's team with four Merlins on a new wing; the resulting Type 683 Lancaster first flew on 9 January 1941. Its long, unobstructed bomb bay — 33 feet — allowed it to carry the heaviest bombs used by the RAF, culminating in the 22,000 lb (10,000 kg) Grand Slam "earthquake bomb". A crew of seven was standard: pilot, flight engineer, navigator, bomb aimer, wireless operator, and mid-upper and rear gunners.

Operational history

Lancasters entered service with No. 44 (Rhodesia) Squadron in early 1942 and flew more than 156,000 sorties, dropping over 600,000 tons of bombs. The type is famous for Operation Chastise (May 1943), the "Dambusters" raid by No. 617 Squadron using Barnes Wallis's bouncing bombs against Ruhr dams, and for sinking the German battleship Tirpitz in 1944 with 12,000 lb Tallboy bombs. Lancasters also took part in humanitarian food drops to the Netherlands (Operation Manna) and the repatriation of prisoners of war in 1945.

Canadian-built Lancasters were produced by Victory Aircraft at Malton, Ontario. After the war the type served in maritime reconnaissance, photographic survey, and as the basis for the Lancastrian and Lincoln developments; French naval aviation operated Lancasters into the 1960s. Only two remain airworthy: PA474 of the RAF Battle of Britain Memorial Flight and FM213 of the Canadian Warplane Heritage Museum.
