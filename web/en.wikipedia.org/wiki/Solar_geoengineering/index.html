Solar geoengineering


Solar geoengineering (solar radiation modification) — proposed methods to reflect sunlight and reduce global warming.

Solar geoengineering, or solar radiation modification (SRM), is a type of climate engineering that would aim to limit global warming by reflecting a small fraction of incoming sunlight back to space. Proposed methods include stratospheric aerosol injection, marine cloud brightening, and cirrus cloud thinning.

Proposed methods

Stratospheric aerosol injection would introduce reflective particles, such as sulfate aerosols, into the stratosphere, mimicking the cooling effect of large volcanic eruptions such as the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo. Marine cloud brightening would spray fine sea-salt particles into low marine clouds to increase their reflectivity. Other proposals include space-based reflectors and surface albedo modification.

Risks and governance

Solar geoengineering would not address ocean acidification, and it could alter regional precipitation patterns. Rapid termination after deployment could cause a fast rebound in temperatures, sometimes called termination shock. Because the direct costs of some methods might be relatively low, governance questions — who decides, and with whose consent — are central to the debate. No method has been deployed at scale.
