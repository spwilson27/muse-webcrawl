Nereid Monument

The Nereid Monument is a sculptured tomb from Xanthos in Lycia (modern southwestern Turkey), built around 390–380 BC for Arbinas, a Lycian dynast who ruled western Lycia under the Achaemenid Empire. It took the form of a small Greek Ionic temple standing on a high podium decorated with friezes, and is named for the Nereids — sea nymphs — whose statues stood between its columns. Discovered by Charles Fellows in the 1840s, its sculptures were shipped to London, where the monument is reconstructed in the British Museum.

Architecture and sculpture

The tomb imitated a tetrastyle Ionic temple on a tall base (about 5.5 m), unique among Lycian tombs in fully adopting Greek temple form, though raised tombs were a local Lycian tradition. Its sculptural programme combined Greek style with Lycian and Persian themes: the podium friezes depicted battles, a siege, hunting, and Arbinas receiving embassies and presiding over sacrifice; cella friezes showed feasting and combat. Between the columns stood life-size female figures in wind-blown drapery, standing on sea creatures — interpreted as Nereids, who escorted the souls of heroes across the sea, or as Lycian water nymphs (Eliyaniyami).

Discovery and display

The ruin at Xanthos was recorded by the traveller Charles Fellows; in 1842 and 1844 the sculptures were transported to England. In the British Museum, architect's reconstructions eventually led to a full-scale partial reassembly of the monument's east side in Gallery 17, one of the museum's landmark displays of classical art.

Significance

The monument is the grandest of the Lycian dynastic tombs and a key work for understanding cultural exchange between Greece, Lycia, and Persia in the 4th century BC; it is often compared with the roughly contemporary Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, another Anatolian dynastic tomb in temple form.
