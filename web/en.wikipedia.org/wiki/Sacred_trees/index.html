Sacred trees

Sacred trees are individual trees, or particular species of trees, that are venerated in the religious traditions, mythologies and folklore of cultures around the world. Reverence for trees — sometimes called dendrolatry or tree worship — is among the most widespread forms of nature veneration, and specific sacred trees are often protected as shrines, associated with deities, spirits, enlightenment, or cosmic order.

Famous individual trees

  • The Bodhi Tree at Bodh Gaya, India — the Ficus religiosa under which Siddhartha Gautama attained enlightenment; its descendant, the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi at Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka, planted in 288 BCE, is the oldest living human-planted tree with a known planting date.
  • Yggdrasil — in Norse mythology, the immense world tree (usually imagined as an ash) connecting the nine worlds.
  • The Tree of Life and the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil in the biblical Garden of Eden (Genesis), and the burning bush of Exodus.
  • Anuradhapura and the sacred fig tradition across Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism; the banyan is India's national tree and a symbol of immortality.

Sacred species by tradition

  • Oak — sacred to Zeus, Jupiter, Thor and the Druids; the prophetic oaks of Dodona.
  • Ash and yew — prominent in Norse and Celtic religion; yews still mark ancient British churchyards.
  • Cedar of Lebanon — revered in Mesopotamian, biblical and Phoenician tradition; emblem of Lebanon.
  • Olive — sacred to Athena, symbol of peace; the olive of the Acropolis.
  • Peepal (sacred fig), banyan, neem, tulsi (holy basil), bael and mango — venerated in Hinduism; groves of such trees form sacred groves.
  • Ginkgo and pine — sacred in East Asian temple plantings; the "sakaki" tree in Shinto.
  • Baobab — sacred "upside-down tree" in many African traditions, used as shrine, meeting place and burial site.
  • Ceiba — the world tree of Maya cosmology.

Symbolism and practice

Sacred trees commonly symbolise the axis mundi — the link between heaven, earth and the underworld — as well as immortality, fertility, wisdom and protection. Practices include circumambulation, tying prayer ribbons or threads (as at Celtic clootie wells and Tibetan and Mongolian sacred trees festooned with blue khadag scarves), offerings at shrines at the tree's base, and prohibitions on felling or harming the tree, which have often preserved exceptional ancient specimens into the present.
