Common land

Related: Commons, enclosure, rights of common

Common land is land owned by one person or by a community, over which other people — commoners — have certain traditional rights, known as rights of common, such as grazing livestock (pasture), collecting wood (estovers), digging peat (turbary) or fishing (piscary). The concept is especially developed in English law, where much common land survives, including village greens and large upland commons.

History

Commons formed part of the medieval manorial system, providing waste land for grazing and fuel. From the 16th to 19th centuries, parliamentary enclosure acts privatised much common land in England, a transformation historians link to agricultural improvement and to the dispossession of smallholders. Legislation such as the Commons Act 2006 now regulates registered commons in England and Wales.

Modern commons

Many commons are valued for public access, recreation and nature conservation, with access rights extended under the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000. The idea of the commons has been extended metaphorically to shared resources generally — the subject of Elinor Ostrom's work on governing common-pool resources and of the phrase "tragedy of the commons".
