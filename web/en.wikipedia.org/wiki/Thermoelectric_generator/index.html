Thermoelectric generator

Thermoelectric generator
Principle: Seebeck effect
Function: Converts heat directly into electricity

A thermoelectric generator (TEG), also called a Seebeck generator, is a solid-state device that converts heat flux (temperature differences) directly into electrical energy through a phenomenon called the Seebeck effect. Thermoelectric generators have no moving parts and are completely silent, though they are typically less efficient than conventional heat engines.

Construction

A typical device consists of many pairs of n-type and p-type semiconductor elements — commonly alloys based on bismuth telluride — connected electrically in series and thermally in parallel between two ceramic plates. When one side is heated and the other kept cool, a voltage proportional to the temperature difference is produced.

Applications

TEGs are used in radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) that power deep-space probes such as Voyager and the Curiosity rover, in waste-heat recovery, in camping stoves that charge electronic devices, and in remote sensors where reliability matters more than efficiency.

Efficiency

The efficiency of a thermoelectric material is measured by the dimensionless figure of merit ZT. Commercial devices typically convert about 5–8% of the heat flow into electricity; research materials with ZT above 2 promise higher performance.
