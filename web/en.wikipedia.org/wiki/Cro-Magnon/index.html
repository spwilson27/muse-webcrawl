Cro-Magnon is an informal name for the earliest known populations of early modern humans (Homo sapiens) in Europe, associated with the Upper Paleolithic. The name comes from the Abri de Cro-Magnon rock shelter near Les Eyzies in the Dordogne, France, where skeletons were uncovered in 1868 during railway construction and described by Louis Lartet. In current usage, scientists more often use the term early European modern humans (EEMH).

The type remains

The Cro-Magnon burials included an elderly adult male (Cro-Magnon 1), other adults, and an infant, dated to about 28,000 years ago. They were physically like living humans — tall, with high foreheads and prominent chins — though with somewhat more robust skeletons and slightly larger average brain volumes than present-day populations. Their discovery helped establish the great antiquity of modern humans in Europe.

Culture and genetics

Early European modern humans are associated with the Aurignacian and later Upper Paleolithic cultures: blade tools, carved figurines such as the Venus figurines, personal ornaments, and the cave art of sites such as Chauvet and Lascaux. Ancient DNA shows these populations interbred to a small degree with Neanderthals and contributed ancestry, along with later migrations, to later Europeans; they were largely replaced or absorbed by subsequent hunter-gatherer and farming populations.
