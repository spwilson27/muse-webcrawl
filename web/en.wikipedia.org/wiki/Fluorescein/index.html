Fluorescein

IUPAC name2-(6-hydroxy-3-oxo-3H-xanthen-9-yl)benzoic acid
FormulaC20H12O5
Molar mass332.31 g/mol
AppearanceDark orange/red powder
Absorption max.~494 nm
Emission max.~512 nm (green)

Fluorescein is an organic compound and dye based on a xanthene skeleton. Widely used as a fluorescent tracer, it is a dark orange-red powder slightly soluble in water and alcohol; its solutions have an intense yellow-green fluorescence, visible even at dilutions of parts per billion. Fluorescein was first synthesised by Adolf von Baeyer in 1871 from phthalic anhydride and resorcinol. Its sodium salt, fluorescein sodium (uranine), is much more soluble in water.

Properties

Fluorescein absorbs blue light (maximum near 494 nm) and emits green light (maximum near 512–521 nm), with a high fluorescence quantum yield of about 0.9 in basic solution. Its fluorescence is strongly pH-dependent, with a pKa near 6.4, which makes it useful as a pH indicator in the physiological range. The isothiocyanate derivative, FITC, reacts with amino groups and is a standard reagent for fluorescently labelling antibodies and other proteins.

Uses

Fluorescein is used to trace groundwater flow and detect leaks; to dye water in sewage and plumbing tests; as the traditional dye that turns the Chicago River green for St. Patrick's Day (now a vegetable-based substitute is used) and was used in Second World War sea-marker dye in survival kits. In medicine, intravenous fluorescein angiography images the retinal circulation, and topical fluorescein stains corneal abrasions for examination under cobalt-blue light. Fluorescein-based derivatives such as calcein and carboxyfluorescein are standard viability and leakage probes in cell biology.

Safety

Topical and oral use are generally safe, though intravenous fluorescein can cause nausea and, rarely, allergic reactions. It temporarily colours skin and urine yellow-orange.
