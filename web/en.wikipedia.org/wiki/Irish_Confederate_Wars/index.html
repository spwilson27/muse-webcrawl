The Irish Confederate Wars, also called the Eleven Years' War (Irish: Cogadh na hAon-déag mBliana), took place in Ireland between 1641 and 1653. They were the Irish theatre of the wider Wars of the Three Kingdoms and began with the Irish Rebellion of 1641, a rising by Irish Catholics fearing invasion by anti-Catholic forces of the English Parliament and Scottish Covenanters. The wars ended with the defeat of the Irish Confederates and their Royalist allies by the New Model Army of Oliver Cromwell, and the catastrophic loss of life from fighting, famine and plague.

Course of the war

In 1642 Catholic gentry and clergy formed the Catholic Confederation, seated at Kilkenny, which governed much of Ireland in the name of Charles I while pursuing religious freedom and self-government. Ireland split into a shifting four-way struggle among the Confederates, English Royalists under the Earl of Ormond, English Parliamentarian forces, and a Scottish Covenanter army in Ulster. The papal nuncio Giovanni Battista Rinuccini, arriving in 1645, pushed an uncompromising clerical agenda that deepened Confederate divisions. A Confederate–Royalist alliance in 1649 came too late: Cromwell landed that August, storming Drogheda and Wexford amid massacres, and Parliamentarian commanders completed the conquest by 1653 despite fierce resistance, notably at Clonmel.

Consequences

The wars devastated Ireland: demographic estimates commonly cited suggest the population fell by 15–40 percent through violence, plague and famine, and many Irish soldiers emigrated to continental service. The Act for the Settlement of Ireland (1652) confiscated most Catholic-owned land, transferring it to Parliamentarian soldiers and investors — a dispossession that shaped Irish politics for centuries.
