Wessex culture

The Wessex culture is the predominant prehistoric culture of central southern England in the early and middle Bronze Age, originally defined by the British archaeologist Stuart Piggott. It flourished roughly between 2000 and 1400 BC in the region later comprising the historic counties of Dorset, Wiltshire, Somerset, Hampshire and the Isle of Wight.

Burial practices

The culture is best known from its wealthy burials under round barrows, many of them clustered around Stonehenge and Avebury in Wiltshire — including the famous Bush Barrow and Normanton Down burials. Grave goods included gold ornaments, bronze daggers, amber and jet necklaces, and exotic imports such as Egyptian faience beads, indicating far-reaching trade connections across the Channel and into continental Europe.

Chronology

Piggott divided the culture into Wessex I (c. 2000–1650 BC) and Wessex II (c. 1650–1400 BC). Later scholars, notably Richard Bradley, have questioned whether the rich graves represent a coherent "culture" at all, suggesting instead a prestige-goods network used by competing elites. By the later Bronze Age the distinctive burial tradition faded, replaced by the field systems and settlements of the Deverel-Rimbury culture.
