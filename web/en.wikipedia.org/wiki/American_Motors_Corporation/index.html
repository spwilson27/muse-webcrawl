American Motors Corporation (AMC) was an American automobile manufacturing company formed by the merger of Nash-Kelvinator Corporation and Hudson Motor Car Company on May 1, 1954. At the time, it was the largest corporate merger in United States history. AMC was the last independent American automaker before its acquisition by Chrysler in 1987.

History

Under its first president, George Mason, and then his successor George W. Romney, AMC focused on compact, economical cars under the Rambler marque, a strategy that proved successful during the recession of the late 1950s. Romney resigned in 1962 to become governor of Michigan. In the late 1960s the company, under Roy Abernethy and then Roy D. Chapin Jr., moved toward larger and sportier cars such as the Javelin and AMX pony cars, and introduced the Hornet compact line that spawned the Gremlin, Concord, Spirit, and Eagle derivatives.

AMC purchased Kaiser Jeep Corporation in 1970, acquiring the highly profitable Jeep line, and spun off its military-products division as AM General. Facing rising development costs and the oil crises of the 1970s, AMC formed a partnership with France's Renault in 1979, which took a controlling stake. The four-wheel-drive AMC Eagle of 1980 is regarded as one of the first crossover vehicles. On March 9, 1987, Chrysler Corporation announced it would acquire AMC; the purchase was completed in August 1987, and the AMC and Renault brands were discontinued in the United States, while Jeep became a Chrysler division and the Eagle brand was retained briefly.

Notable models

Notable AMC vehicles include the Rambler American, the Marlin fastback, the Javelin and AMX muscle cars, the Gremlin subcompact, the Pacer, the Hornet, and the Eagle all-wheel-drive line. Jeep models built under AMC ownership included the CJ series, Wagoneer, Cherokee (XJ), and Wrangler (YJ).
