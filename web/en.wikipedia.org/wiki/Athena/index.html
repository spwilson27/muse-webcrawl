Athena is a goddess of ancient Greek religion and mythology, associated with wisdom, warfare in its strategic aspect, handicraft and weaving. She is one of the twelve Olympians and the patron deity of Athens, which bears her name. In Roman mythology she was identified with Minerva.

Birth and character

According to Hesiod, Athena sprang fully grown and armed from the head of Zeus, who had swallowed her mother Metis (cunning intelligence) — a birth that marks her as Zeus's closest child and heir to his wisdom. A virgin goddess, she is the patron of heroes, aiding Odysseus throughout the Odyssey, and of Perseus, Heracles and Jason. Where Ares represents war's violence, Athena represents its discipline and strategy; she is also the mistress of crafts — weaving, pottery, shipbuilding — that turn skill to the city's benefit.

Athens and myth

Athena's contest with Poseidon for Athens — her olive tree against his salt spring (or horse) — was judged in her favour and depicted on the Parthenon's west pediment; the temple housed her colossal gold-and-ivory statue by Phidias and hosted the Panathenaic festival. Her myths include the transformation of the weaver Arachne into a spider for hubristic artistry and the punishment of Medusa. Her epithets include Pallas, Polias (of the city), Parthenos (the maiden) and glaukōpis ("bright-eyed" or "owl-eyed"); the little owl of Athena remains a symbol of wisdom and of the city.

Legacy

Minerva became a standard emblem of learning and statecraft; Athena's owl and olive branch appear on coins ancient and modern (including the Greek one-euro coin), and she presides iconically over universities, libraries and courts.
