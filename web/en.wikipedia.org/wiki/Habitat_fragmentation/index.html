Habitat fragmentation

Habitat fragmentation describes the process by which a large, continuous area of habitat is divided into a number of smaller patches of smaller total area, isolated from each other by a matrix of habitats unlike the original. It is one of the most important causes of biodiversity loss worldwide and arises from land conversion for agriculture and urban development, road and dam construction, and other human land uses, as well as from natural disturbances such as fire and volcanic activity.

Effects

Fragmentation combines habitat loss with increased isolation. Smaller patches support smaller populations, which are more vulnerable to demographic accidents, inbreeding, and local extinction. The increased proportion of edge habitat—the "edge effect"—exposes interior species to altered light, wind, humidity, predators, parasites, and invasive species. Barriers such as roads prevent movement and gene flow between patches, and many species are unwilling or unable to cross gaps in their habitat at all.

Research

Fragmentation research draws on island biogeography and metapopulation theory. Long-running experiments, including the Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments Project in Amazonian Brazil, begun in 1979, have tracked how fragment size and age shape species persistence. Ecologists debate the relative importance of the sheer amount of habitat lost versus the spatial configuration of what remains.

Mitigation

Conservation responses include protecting large habitat blocks, maintaining or restoring connectivity through wildlife corridors and crossing structures over roads, buffering reserves from edge effects, and landscape-level planning that retains permeable habitat in the matrix between protected areas.
