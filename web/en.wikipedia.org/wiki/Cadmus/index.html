Cadmus

In Greek mythology, Cadmus (/ˈkædməs/; Greek: Κάδμος, romanized: Kádmos) was the legendary Phoenician founder of Boeotian Thebes, famed for slaying the dragon of Ares, sowing its teeth to raise the Spartoi ("sown men"), and — according to the Greeks — for bringing the Phoenician alphabet to Greece. He was the son of King Agenor of Tyre and the brother of Europa.

Myth

Sent by his father to search for his abducted sister Europa, and forbidden to return without her, Cadmus consulted the Delphic oracle and was told to follow a cow and found a city where it lay down. In Boeotia he killed the dragon guarding the spring of Ares; at Athena's instruction he sowed the dragon's teeth, from which armed men — the Spartoi — sprang up and fought one another until five remained, who helped him build Thebes. To atone for killing the dragon, Cadmus served Ares for eight years. He then married Harmonia, daughter of Ares and Aphrodite; all the gods attended the wedding, but the gifts — notably a cursed necklace — brought ruin on their descendants, including Oedipus. In old age Cadmus and Harmonia were turned into serpents and translated to Elysium.

Legacy

Cadmus was credited in antiquity (e.g., by Herodotus) with introducing the alphabet — "Cadmean letters" — from Phoenicia to Greece, and he was the ancestor of the Theban royal house. His name has been linked, speculatively, to Semitic roots meaning "east" or "ancient".
