Copper extraction

Copper extraction refers to the methods used to obtain copper from its ores. The conversion of copper ores consists of a series of physical, chemical, and electrochemical processes. Methods have evolved and vary by country depending on the ore source, local environmental regulations, and other factors. Most copper ores contain only a small percentage of copper — commonly well under 1% in mined porphyry deposits — so the metal must first be concentrated before smelting or leaching.

Most copper is mined from large open-pit porphyry deposits containing low-grade sulfide ores.

History

Copper was one of the first metals worked by humans; native copper was hammered into objects thousands of years before smelting was developed. Bronze Age furnaces smelted oxidised and later sulfide ores, and copper production expanded greatly with industrialisation. In the twentieth century, froth flotation made low-grade porphyry ores economic, and from the 1980s solvent extraction and electrowinning (SX-EW) grew into a major route for oxide ores.

Concentration

Mined ore is crushed and ground to liberate the copper minerals, then concentrated, usually by froth flotation: reagents make copper sulfide particles hydrophobic so that they attach to air bubbles and float into a froth, while waste rock (gangue) sinks. The result is a concentrate typically containing 25–35% copper, which is thickened and filtered. The residue, tailings, is stored in impoundments.

Sulfide ores: smelting

Sulfide concentrates are smelted, in modern practice usually by flash smelting, in which dried concentrate is blown into a furnace with oxygen-enriched air; the sulfide sulfur itself provides much of the fuel. Smelting produces matte, a molten mixture of copper and iron sulfides of about 50–70% copper, and a slag that is discarded or cleaned. The matte is then converted in a converter (commonly the Peirce–Smith converter), where air or oxygen blown through it oxidises iron and sulfur, yielding blister copper of about 98.5–99.5% purity, named for the blistered surface caused by escaping sulfur dioxide. Sulfur dioxide from smelting and converting is usually captured and converted to sulfuric acid.

Oxide ores: leaching and SX-EW

Oxidised ores, and some low-grade sulfide material, are treated hydrometallurgically. Crushed ore is stacked in heaps and irrigated with dilute sulfuric acid, which dissolves the copper. The pregnant leach solution is concentrated and purified by solvent extraction, in which an organic extractant selectively loads copper and is then stripped with strong acid, and the copper is recovered by electrowinning: an electric current plates high-purity copper onto cathodes. SX-EW produces cathode copper directly, without smelting, and accounts for a substantial share of world production.

Refining

Blister copper is fire-refined and cast into anodes, then purified by electrorefining: in an acid copper sulfate electrolyte, pure copper deposits on cathodes while impurities fall as anode slime, from which precious metals such as gold and silver are recovered. Electrorefined cathode typically exceeds 99.99% copper purity and is the standard commercial product, melted and cast into wire rod, billets and cakes.
