Temple

A temple (from the Latin templum) is a building reserved for religious or spiritual activities such as prayer, sacrifice, and worship. Temples have been built by most of the world's religions and cultures, from the ziggurats of Mesopotamia and the stone temples of ancient Egypt and Greece to the vast temple complexes of Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism in Asia. The word originally denoted a space ritually marked out by Roman augurs for observing omens, before coming to mean the building erected on sacred ground.

Ancient temples

In Mesopotamia, temples such as the White Temple at Uruk (c. 3500 BCE) stood on staged towers called ziggurats, serving as the household of the city god. Egyptian temples, like Karnak and Luxor, grew over centuries along processional axes, with pylons, hypostyle halls, and an inner sanctuary housing the god's statue. Greek temples — the Parthenon above all — refined the columned rectangular plan, while Roman temples combined Greek orders with Etruscan podium plans and became instruments of imperial cult. In the Americas, Maya and Aztec temples crowned stepped pyramids where ritual offerings were made.

Asian traditions

Hindu temples (mandir) are conceived as the deity's dwelling and as a model of the cosmos, centred on the garbhagriha (womb chamber) beneath a towering shikhara or gopuram; great complexes such as Angkor Wat in Cambodia began as Hindu foundations. Buddhist temples and monasteries house images of the Buddha and relic stupas, and the term "temple" is also applied to Jain, Sikh (gurdwara usage varies), Shinto (usually "shrine"), and Taoist sacred buildings.

Other usages

Judaism's Temple in Jerusalem — destroyed in 70 CE — remains central to Jewish faith and eschatology, and "temple" is used as a synonym for synagogue in some movements. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints builds temples distinct from meetinghouses, reserved for special ordinances. Fraternal orders such as the Freemasons also call their lodge buildings temples.

Common features

Across traditions, temples typically mark a threshold between profane and sacred space, orient themselves to cardinal directions or celestial events, and concentrate communal wealth and art. Many remain active places of worship and pilgrimage after thousands of years.
