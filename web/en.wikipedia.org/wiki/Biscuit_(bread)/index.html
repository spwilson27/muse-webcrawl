Biscuit (bread)

In American English, a biscuit is a small, soft, leavened bread, similar to a savoury scone, made with flour, baking powder or baking soda, salt, and fat such as butter, shortening, or lard, with milk or buttermilk as the liquid. Biscuits are a staple of the cuisine of the Southern United States, commonly served with gravy, butter, jam, or fried chicken. The word means something different in British English, where a biscuit is what Americans call a cookie or cracker.

History

American biscuits developed in the 19th-century South. Soft winter wheat flour (notably White Lily flour), chemical leaveners that became widely available in the mid-1800s, and abundant pork fat made a tender quick bread distinct from earlier hard beaten biscuits, which were pounded to incorporate air. Buttermilk biscuits became a hallmark of Southern home cooking and later of restaurant and fast-food breakfasts.

Preparation

Cold fat is cut into seasoned flour, liquid is stirred in just until a shaggy dough forms, and the dough is briefly kneaded or folded to create layers, cut into rounds, and baked hot until risen and golden. Drop biscuits skip rolling and are spooned onto the pan. Biscuits and gravy — biscuits covered in sausage cream gravy — is a classic breakfast dish.

Terminology

The naming split dates to early modern English, in which biscuit (from Latin bis coctus, "twice cooked") named hard baked goods. American usage shifted to the soft bread as British usage retained the crisp sense, a difference that regularly causes transatlantic confusion.
