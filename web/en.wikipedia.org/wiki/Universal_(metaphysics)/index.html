Universal (metaphysics)

In metaphysics, a universal is what particular things have in common — the qualities, properties, or relations that many individual objects can share or instantiate. For example, many particular red things share the universal "redness"; many circular objects share "circularity."

The problem of universals

The problem of universals asks how to explain the resemblance between distinct particulars and the apparent truth of general statements. It is one of the oldest problems in philosophy, raised in Plato's dialogues and debated intensely by medieval scholastics.

Positions

Realism, associated with Plato and Aristotle, holds that universals really exist — either as transcendent Forms (Platonic realism) or as entities existing in rebus, in the things themselves. Nominalism denies that universals exist as anything beyond words or concepts; on this view, only particulars exist, and general terms are names (nomina). Conceptualism occupies a middle ground, treating universals as concepts in the mind. Trope theory explains resemblance through particularized properties (tropes) rather than shared universals.

History

Boethius's commentary on Porphyry framed the medieval debate. Peter Abelard advanced a sophisticated conceptualism, while William of Ockham championed nominalism. The question persists in modern analytic metaphysics, notably in the work of David Armstrong, who defended a scientific realism about universals.
