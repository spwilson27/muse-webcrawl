Copy protection

Copy protection, also known as content protection, copy prevention and copy restriction, describes measures to enforce copyright by preventing the reproduction of software, films, music, and other media. Because perfect prevention is generally impossible, copy protection typically aims to deter casual copying rather than stop determined adversaries.

History

Copy protection emerged with the home-computer software market of the late 1970s and 1980s, when publishers used non-standard floppy-disk formats, deliberately introduced errors, code wheels and manual look-up schemes to frustrate duplication. Audio and video media saw systems such as Macrovision for VHS tapes, the Content Scramble System (CSS) for DVDs, and HDCP for digital video connections. Product activation and online authentication became common with broadband internet.

Digital rights management

Digital rights management (DRM) extends copy protection into usage control, limiting how purchased media may be played, copied or transferred. Laws such as the U.S. Digital Millennium Copyright Act prohibit circumventing such measures even for otherwise lawful purposes, a provision that remains controversial.

Criticism

Critics argue that copy protection penalises legitimate purchasers, breaks interoperability and preservation, and fails to deter organised piracy; the 2005 Sony BMG rootkit scandal, in which copy-protection software created security vulnerabilities on customers' computers, is a frequently cited example.
