Pudendal nerve

From: Sacral plexus, S2–S4
Branches: Inferior rectal nerve, perineal nerve, dorsal nerve of penis/clitoris
Innervates: Perineum, external anal sphincter, external genitalia

The pudendal nerve is the main nerve of the perineum. It arises from the sacral plexus, carrying fibres from the ventral rami of the second, third and fourth sacral spinal nerves (S2–S4), and supplies sensation to the external genitalia and the skin around the anus and perineum, as well as motor fibres to the muscles of the pelvic floor and the external urethral and anal sphincters. It is therefore essential to sexual function, continence and childbirth.

Course

The nerve leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, hooks around the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament, and re-enters the pelvis through the lesser sciatic foramen. It then runs forward in the pudendal canal (Alcock's canal), a fascial tunnel in the lateral wall of the ischiorectal fossa, accompanied by the internal pudendal vessels.

Branches

  • The inferior rectal nerve supplies the external anal sphincter and sensation to the lower anal canal and perianal skin.
  • The perineal nerve divides into posterior scrotal or labial branches and a deep branch to the muscles of the perineum and the external urethral sphincter.
  • The dorsal nerve of the penis (or clitoris) is the terminal branch, carrying sensation from the glans and skin of the penis or clitoris.

Clinical significance

A pudendal nerve block — local anaesthetic injected near the ischial spine — was historically widely used to relieve pain in the second stage of labour and for perineal surgery. Damage or entrapment of the nerve, known as pudendal neuralgia or Alcock canal syndrome, can cause chronic perineal pain aggravated by sitting, and is a recognised complaint among long-distance cyclists. Stretch injury during childbirth or pelvic surgery can cause faecal or urinary incontinence; the pudendal nerve terminal motor latency test was used to assess such damage.
