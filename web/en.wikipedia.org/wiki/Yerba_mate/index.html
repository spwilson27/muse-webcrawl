Yerba mate

Scientific nameIlex paraguariensis
Native rangeParaná, Paraguay and Uruguay river basins, South America
Key compoundsCaffeine, theobromine, polyphenols
Main producersArgentina, Brazil, Paraguay

Yerba mate (Spanish: yerba mate; Portuguese: erva-mate) is a traditional South American caffeine-rich infused drink made from the dried leaves of the holly species Ilex paraguariensis. It is the national beverage of Argentina, Paraguay, and Uruguay, and is widely consumed in southern Brazil, Chile, Bolivia, and Syria and Lebanon, where it was introduced by returning emigrants.

Preparation

Traditionally, mate is brewed in a hollowed calabash gourd (also called a mate or cuia) filled with loose leaves and hot — but not boiling — water, and drunk through a metal straw called a bombilla (Spanish) or bomba (Portuguese) that filters out the leaf fragments. The gourd is refilled repeatedly. In Paraguay the drink is commonly consumed cold as tereré. Sharing the same gourd and bombilla in a circle is a strong social ritual; the server (cebador) prepares and passes the mate in turn.

History

Mate was consumed by the Guaraní and Tupí peoples long before European contact. Jesuit missionaries in the 17th and 18th centuries domesticated the plant in their reductions in Paraguay — hence its nickname "Jesuit tea" — and commercialized its trade. After the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767, production declined until industrial harvesting and plantation cultivation revived in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, notably in Argentina's Misiones Province.

Composition and health

Yerba mate contains caffeine (roughly comparable to coffee per serving, though variable), theobromine, chlorogenic acids, and saponins. It is marketed as providing alertness without the jitteriness of coffee. Scientific studies have noted antioxidant and metabolic effects, while health authorities have also observed that drinking any beverage at very high temperatures is associated with increased oesophageal cancer risk.
