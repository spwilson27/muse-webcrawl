Born–Haber cycle

The Born–Haber cycle is an approach to analysing the energies of ionic compound formation, developed independently by Max Born and Fritz Haber in 1919. It applies Hess's law to break the standard enthalpy of formation of an ionic solid into a sequence of steps — atomisation, ionisation, electron attachment and condensation into the lattice — so that the lattice enthalpy can be calculated indirectly from measurable quantities.

[ Image: Energy-level diagram of the Born–Haber cycle for sodium chloride ]

The cycle

For a compound such as sodium chloride, the enthalpy of formation ΔHf is equated to the sum of: (1) the enthalpy of atomisation (sublimation) of the metal; (2) the dissociation energy of the non-metal molecules; (3) the ionisation energy of the metal atoms; (4) the electron affinity of the non-metal atoms; and (5) the lattice formation enthalpy, released when the gaseous ions condense into the crystal. If any one term is unknown — usually the lattice enthalpy — it follows from the others.

Uses

Beyond lattice enthalpies, the cycle is used to obtain electron affinities that are hard to measure directly, to rationalise why compounds form in particular stoichiometries (for example why NaCl2 and MgCl are not stable), and to compare experimental lattice enthalpies with theoretical values from the Born–Landé equation, the difference giving a measure of covalent character.
