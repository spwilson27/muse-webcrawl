Group velocity

The group velocity of a wave is the velocity with which the overall envelope shape of the wave's amplitudes—known as the modulation or envelope—propagates through space. It contrasts with the phase velocity, the speed at which individual crests move.

Definition

For a wave with angular frequency ω and wavenumber k, the group velocity is vg = ∂ω/∂k, the derivative of frequency with respect to wavenumber, determined by the medium's dispersion relation. In a non-dispersive medium, group and phase velocities are equal. The concept was introduced by W. R. Hamilton in 1839 and developed by Lord Rayleigh.

Significance

Information and energy in a wave generally travel at the group velocity; for light in vacuum it equals c. In regions of anomalous dispersion, the group velocity can exceed c or become negative, but this does not violate relativity because no information travels faster than light—the front velocity remains bounded by c.
