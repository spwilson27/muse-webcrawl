Silver–Russell syndrome
Other namesRussell–Silver syndrome; Silver–Russell dwarfism
SpecialtyMedical genetics
SymptomsPrenatal and postnatal growth restriction, relative macrocephaly, prominent forehead, body asymmetry, feeding difficulties
CausesGenetic imprinting defects, mainly chromosome 11p15; maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7
FrequencyEstimated 1 in 30,000 to 1 in 100,000 births

Silver–Russell syndrome (SRS), also called Russell–Silver syndrome, is a rare congenital growth disorder characterised by intrauterine and postnatal growth restriction, a distinctive triangular face with a prominent forehead, relative macrocephaly at birth, and asymmetry of the body. It was described independently by Henry Silver in 1953 and Alexander Russell in 1954.

Genetics

SRS is genetically heterogeneous. In a large proportion of cases it results from loss of methylation on chromosome 11p15, affecting imprinted growth-regulating genes such as H19 and IGF2. About 7–10% of cases involve maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 7. Diagnosis is increasingly guided by the Netchine–Harbison clinical scoring system, followed by molecular testing.

Management

Care focuses on early nutritional support (many infants have severe feeding difficulties and risk of hypoglycaemia), management of asymmetry and scoliosis, and growth hormone therapy, which improves final height. Most individuals have normal intelligence, though learning difficulties may occur.
