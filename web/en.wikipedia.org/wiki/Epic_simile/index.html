Epic simile
Also calledHomeric simile
TypeLiterary device
OriginHomer's Iliad and Odyssey
Later usersVirgil, Dante, Milton

An epic simile, also called a Homeric simile, is a detailed comparison in the form of a simile that runs for many lines. It differs from an ordinary simile in its length and in the way the comparison, once begun, is developed into a miniature scene in its own right, often drawn from everyday life, nature, or craftsmanship, before the narrative returns to the main action.

Characteristics

Homer's epics contain well over a hundred extended similes. A warrior falling in battle may be compared to a poplar felled by a chariot-maker; armies clash like waves driven onto a shore by the wind. The vehicle of the simile frequently acquires independent narrative life — describing, for example, the labour of reapers or the behaviour of bees — so that the device provides both vividness and a pause or counterpoint to the heroic action, connecting the battlefield to the ordinary world of peace.

Later use

The epic simile became a defining convention of the epic genre. Virgil adapted it in the Aeneid, and it remained a marker of epic ambition in Dante's Divine Comedy and Milton's Paradise Lost, where similes comparing Satan's legions to autumn leaves or locusts extend over many lines and sometimes deliberately strain or complicate the comparison.
