Chronic traumatic encephalopathy

Tau protein deposits characteristic of CTE in brain tissue

Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is an Alzheimer's disease-like neurodegenerative disease that is most often seen in people that frequently receive direct and repeated blows to the head. It has been found in athletes who take part in contact sports, and in military personnel exposed to blast injuries. The symptoms onset can be delayed for years or decades after the trauma.

The condition was initially described in boxers as dementia pugilistica, or "punch-drunk syndrome". Neuropathologically, CTE is characterised by the accumulation of hyperphosphorylated tau protein in neurons and astrocytes around blood vessels at the depths of cortical sulci.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms of CTE generally begin 8-10 years after repeated mild traumatic brain injuries and progress slowly. They include memory loss, confusion, impaired judgment, aggression, depression, anxiety, and progressive dementia. In advanced stages, individuals may develop motor symptoms resembling Parkinson's disease.

Causes

CTE is believed to be caused by repetitive head impacts, including both concussions and subconcussive blows that do not produce obvious symptoms. It has been documented in American football players, ice hockey players, rugby players, soccer players, wrestlers, and boxers. A definitive diagnosis can currently only be made post-mortem by examining brain tissue.

Research

Research centres such as the Boston University CTE Center have studied the brains of deceased athletes, reporting high rates of CTE pathology among professional American football players who donated their brains. The findings have prompted rule changes and concussion protocols across contact sports, though the prevalence and causal mechanisms remain subjects of scientific debate.
