Đại Việt (Vietnamese: [ɗâjˀ vìət]; literally "Great Việt") was the name of the Vietnamese state from 1054, under the Lý dynasty, until 1804, when Emperor Gia Long renamed the country Việt Nam. The name had a brief interruption between 1400 and 1407, when Hồ Quý Ly renamed the state Đại Ngu. Đại Việt's territory grew from the Red River Delta core of northern Vietnam southward over the centuries in the process known as Nam tiến ("southward advance").

History

The name was adopted in 1054 by Emperor Lý Thánh Tông, replacing Đại Cồ Việt, the name used since Đinh Bộ Lĩnh founded the kingdom in 968 after a millennium of Chinese domination. Under the Lý (1009–1225) and Trần (1225–1400) dynasties, Đại Việt built a Confucian-style bureaucratic monarchy, founded the Temple of Literature (1070) and the first national university, and famously repelled three Mongol invasions (1258, 1285, 1287–88) under generals such as Trần Hưng Đạo, culminating in the Battle of Bạch Đằng (1288).

After the Hồ dynasty's fall and a period of Ming occupation (1407–1427), Lê Lợi's Lam Sơn uprising restored independence, and the Lê dynasty (1428–1789) presided over territorial expansion, legal codification (the Hồng Đức code), and administration reforms, before effective power passed to rival Trịnh and Nguyễn lords. The Tây Sơn rebellion reunified the country in the 1780s–90s, and in 1802 Nguyễn Ánh (Gia Long) founded the Nguyễn dynasty; in 1804 the country was renamed Việt Nam.

Culture

Đại Việt culture blended Mahayana Buddhism (dominant under the Lý and Trần), Confucian statecraft, and indigenous traditions; chữ Nôm, a demotic script for Vietnamese, developed alongside classical Chinese chữ Hán for literature and administration.
