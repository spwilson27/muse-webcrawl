Michel Ney

Michel Ney, 1st Duke of Elchingen, 1st Prince of the Moskva (10 January 1769 – 7 December 1815), was a French military commander and Marshal of the Empire who fought in the French Revolutionary Wars and the Napoleonic Wars. Napoleon called him le Brave des braves — "the bravest of the brave". The son of a cooper from Saarbrücken, Ney rose from private hussar to marshal by sheer courage and ability, distinguished himself in the defence and retreats of the Empire as much as in its victories, and became its most tragic figure: after commanding the rearguard in the retreat from Moscow, he pressed Napoleon to abdicate in 1814, served the restored Bourbons, then rejoined Napoleon in the Hundred Days — promising Louis XVIII to bring him back "in an iron cage" — and was executed for treason by firing squad in Paris after the second Restoration.

Early career

Ney enlisted as a hussar in 1787, was commissioned in the Revolutionary armies, and rose rapidly in the Army of the North and in Germany, distinguishing himself by personal bravery — capturing Mannheim almost single-handed in legend, and finishing the 1790s as a general of division under Masséna in Switzerland. Created a marshal in 1804, he won his dukedom at Elchingen in the Ulm campaign of 1805, storming the abbey heights above the Danube, and fought at Jena (1806), Eylau and Friedland (1807). In Spain he quarrelled famously with Masséna during the retreat from Portugal.

Russia and the retreat

In 1812 Ney commanded III Corps in the invasion of Russia. At Borodino his attacks on the flèches earned him the title Prince of the Moskva. In the retreat from Moscow he commanded the rearguard — the most terrible duty of the campaign — holding off the Russians day by day while the Grande Armée disintegrated around him. At the Berezina crossing he defended the bridges to the last, and crossed the river with the final detachment, arriving half-frozen with a handful of survivors: Napoleon greeted "the last of the French". Ney fought on through Germany in 1813 — wounded at Lützen and Leipzig — and in France in 1814, where he became the spokesman of the marshals who at Fontainebleau demanded Napoleon's abdication.

Hundred Days and death

The restored Louis XVIII made Ney a peer of France and gave him commands, though the returned émigré court slighted the plebeian marshal and his wife. When Napoleon escaped from Elba in March 1815, Ney assured the king he would bring the usurper back in an iron cage — then, confronting his old soldiers at Auxerre, found he could not fire on them, read out Napoleon's proclamation, and joined him. Given the left wing of the Armée du Nord in June, he fought the Battle of Quatre Bras on 16 June — his handling of d'Erlon's corps, recalled from the march on Ligny, drew lasting criticism — and at Waterloo two days later led the great cavalry charges against the Allied squares and the final attacks of the Guard, on foot, sword in hand, the last of his five horses killed under him.

Arrested after the Restoration, Ney was tried by the Chamber of Peers and sentenced to death for high treason, despite a defence arguing that his birthplace of Saarlouis, ceded to Prussia by the new treaty, made him no longer French. Refusing a blindfold, he gave the order to fire himself — "Soldiers, when I give the command to fire, fire straight at my heart" — in the Luxembourg Gardens on 7 December 1815. His execution shocked Europe and made him a martyr of the Napoleonic legend.
