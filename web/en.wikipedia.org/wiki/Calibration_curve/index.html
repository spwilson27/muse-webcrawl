Calibration curve

In analytical chemistry, a calibration curve, also known as a standard curve, is a general method for determining the concentration of a substance in an unknown sample by comparing the unknown to a set of standard samples of known concentration. A calibration curve is one approach to the problem of instrument calibration; other standard approaches may mix the standard into the unknown, giving an internal standard.

General use

The calibration curve is a plot of how the instrumental response, the so-called analytical signal, changes with the concentration of the analyte (the substance to be measured). The operator prepares a series of standards across a range of concentrations near the expected concentration of analyte in the unknown. The concentrations of the standards must lie within the working range of the technique (instrumentation) they are using. The resulting responses are fitted, typically by polynomial or linear functions, and the fitted function can then be used to compute the concentration of an unknown sample from its measured response.

Error sources

Deviations from linearity, matrix effects in the sample, and instrumental drift can all introduce error. The limit of detection and limit of quantification are commonly derived from the calibration curve's slope and the variability of the blank response.

Applications

Calibration curves are used in techniques such as spectrophotometry (via the Beer–Lambert law), atomic absorption spectroscopy, chromatography, and immunoassays such as ELISA.
