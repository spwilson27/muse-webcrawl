Atta (ant)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atta is a genus of New World ants of the subfamily Myrmicinae, comprising the major species of leafcutter ants together with the related genus Acromyrmex. The genus contains about 15–17 known species, distributed from the southern United States to Argentina. Mature colonies are among the largest insect societies known, with up to eight million workers in species such as Atta cephalotes and Atta colombica.

Fungus farming

Atta ants do not eat the leaves they cut directly. Instead, workers harvest vegetation fragments and use them as substrate to cultivate a fungus (family Lepiotaceae), on which the colony feeds. Worker castes are strongly polymorphic and divide the labour of cutting, transporting, shredding and gardening; tiny minima workers ride on leaf fragments to guard against parasitic phorid flies. The ants also host antibiotic-producing bacteria that protect the fungus garden from parasitic moulds.

Colony structure

Colonies are founded by a single queen after a nuptial flight, carrying a pellet of fungus in her mouth. Nests are vast underground systems of chambers connected by tunnels, with prominent mounds of excavated soil; a large colony can move tonnes of soil and defoliate a substantial fraction of local vegetation, making Atta significant agricultural pests in parts of Latin America — but also important ecosystem engineers and nutrient cyclers in tropical forests.

Species

Well-known species include Atta cephalotes, A. colombica, A. sexdens, A. texana (the Texas leafcutter ant, the northernmost species) and A. laevigata.
