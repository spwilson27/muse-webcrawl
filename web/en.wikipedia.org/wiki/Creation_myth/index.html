Creation myth

A creation myth or cosmogonic myth is a symbolic narrative of how the world began and how people first came to inhabit it. Such myths are found in nearly every known culture and are usually shared and preserved within a religious tradition. Creation myths attempt to explain the origin of the universe, the earth, life, and humanity, and they typically feature a creator deity, primordial chaos, or the actions of supernatural beings.

Classification

Scholars classify creation myths into recurring types, including: ex nihilo creation, in which a god creates the world from nothing (as in the opening of Genesis); creation from chaos, where order is imposed on a formless primordial state; world parent myths, in which the world arises from the union or separation of primordial parents (such as Heaven and Earth, e.g. Rangi and Papa in Māori tradition, or Geb and Nut in Egyptian myth); emergence myths, in which humanity climbs up through successive worlds (common among the peoples of the American Southwest); and earth-diver myths, in which an animal dives into primordial waters to bring up the mud from which the earth is formed.

Examples

Well-known creation accounts include the Babylonian Enūma Eliš, in which Marduk fashions the world from the body of the slain goddess Tiamat; the ancient Egyptian myths of Heliopolis, where Atum emerges on the primordial mound; the Chinese myth of Pangu separating heaven and earth; the Norse account in which the world is made from the body of the giant Ymir; and the Popol Vuh of the K'iche' Maya.

Function

Creation myths serve to ground a society's worldview, legitimating social order, kingship, and ritual by projecting them back to the beginning of time. Comparative mythologists such as Mircea Eliade stressed their role as sacred history — models for human behaviour re-enacted in ritual.
