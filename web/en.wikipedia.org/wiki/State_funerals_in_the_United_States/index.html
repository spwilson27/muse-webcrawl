State funerals in the United States

State funerals in the United States are official memorial ceremonies conducted by the federal government to honour individuals who have rendered distinguished service to the nation, most often presidents. They are marked by extensive military ceremonial, a period of lying in state in the Rotunda of the United States Capitol, a funeral procession in Washington, D.C., and a national day of mourning declared by the sitting president. Planning is overseen by the Military District of Washington, and much of the ritual descends from the funeral of Abraham Lincoln in 1865.

Elements of the ceremony

A typical presidential state funeral unfolds over several days: the announcement of death and proclamation of mourning; a service at the president's home or house of worship; transport of the flag-draped casket to Washington; a procession to the Capitol, often by horse-drawn caisson accompanied by a riderless horse with reversed boots in the stirrups, symbolising a fallen leader; lying in state in the Rotunda, where the public files past; a funeral service at Washington National Cathedral; and burial, frequently with a 21-gun salute, military flyover in missing-man formation, and the playing of "Taps".

History

The first American state funeral was held for Benjamin Franklin in 1790, and Congress adopted formal lying-in-state honours for Henry Clay in 1852. Lincoln's funeral — with its 1,700-mile funeral train to Springfield — set the template, and John F. Kennedy's 1963 funeral, televised worldwide, remains the most watched. Presidents who have received state funerals include William Howard Taft, Warren G. Harding, Franklin D. Roosevelt, Dwight D. Eisenhower, Lyndon B. Johnson, Ronald Reagan, Gerald Ford, George H. W. Bush and Jimmy Carter. Non-presidents occasionally honoured include Generals John J. Pershing and Douglas MacArthur, Senator John McCain (who lay in state) and the Unknown Soldiers of the world wars and Korea. The Unknown Soldier of World War I received the first full state funeral of the modern era in 1921.

Regulation

State funerals are authorised by the president in office; former presidents plan their own ceremonies in advance with military planners. Federal regulations govern flag half-staffing periods (30 days for a president), and federal offices close on the national day of mourning.
