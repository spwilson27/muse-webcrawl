Derivative (finance)

In finance, a derivative is a contract that derives its value from the performance of an underlying entity. This underlying entity can be an asset, index, or interest rate, and is often simply called the underlying. Derivatives can be used for a number of purposes, including insuring against price movements (hedging), increasing exposure to price movements for speculation, or getting access to otherwise hard-to-trade assets or markets.

Common typesFutures, options, swaps, forwards
MarketsExchange-traded, over-the-counter

Basic types

Some of the more common derivatives include forwards, futures, options, swaps, and variations of these such as synthetic collateralized debt obligations and credit default swaps. Most derivatives are traded over-the-counter (off-exchange) or on an exchange such as the Chicago Mercantile Exchange.

Uses

Derivatives are used to transfer risk, to speculate on the future value of an underlying asset, and for arbitrage. Because the value of a derivative depends on the value of something else, derivatives are considered highly leveraged instruments.

Criticism

Derivatives, especially credit default swaps, played a significant role in the 2007–2008 financial crisis, drawing criticism from figures such as Warren Buffett, who famously called them "financial weapons of mass destruction".
