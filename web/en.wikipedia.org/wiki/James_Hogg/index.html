James Hogg

James Hogg (1770 – 21 November 1835) was a Scottish poet, novelist, and essayist who wrote in Scots and English. A shepherd by occupation, he was known as the Ettrick Shepherd, and is best remembered for his novel The Private Memoirs and Confessions of a Justified Sinner (1824), now regarded as a classic of Scottish literature.

Life

Hogg was born at Ettrick in the Scottish Borders and worked as a shepherd and farm labourer, teaching himself to read and write. His mother, Margaret Laidlaw, was a noted source of traditional ballads and tales. Encouraged by Walter Scott, for whom he collected ballad material, Hogg moved to Edinburgh in 1810 to pursue a literary career. He contributed to Blackwood's Magazine, where he became a central figure in its fictionalised Noctes Ambrosianae dialogues, though relations with the magazine's proprietors were often fraught.

Works

Hogg's poetry includes the collection The Mountain Bard (1807) and the long poem The Queen's Wake (1813), which brought him fame. His prose works include The Brownie of Bodsbeck (1818), The Three Perils of Man (1822), and the Confessions of a Justified Sinner, a psychologically unsettling tale of religious fanaticism, doubling, and possible diabolic influence, published anonymously in 1824. Underappreciated in his lifetime, the novel was rediscovered in the twentieth century and is now his most celebrated work.
