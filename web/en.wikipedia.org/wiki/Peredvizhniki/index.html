The Peredvizhniki (Russian: Передви́жники), often called The Wanderers or The Itinerants, were a group of Russian realist artists who formed an artists' cooperative, the Society for Travelling Art Exhibitions, in protest at academic restrictions in 1870. The society mounted exhibitions that travelled from Saint Petersburg and Moscow to provincial cities across the Russian Empire, bringing art with social and national themes to a broad public outside the capitals.

Origins

In 1863 a group of fourteen students at the Imperial Academy of Arts, led by Ivan Kramskoi, refused to paint the prescribed competition subject — the "Feast in Valhalla" — in protest against the Academy's insistence on classical subjects and its detachment from contemporary Russian life; their walkout became known as the "Revolt of the Fourteen". The seceders first formed the Artel of Artists, and in 1870 joined with Moscow painters to found the Society for Travelling Art Exhibitions, whose charter was approved in November of that year. Its first exhibition opened in Saint Petersburg in 1871.

Art and aims

The Peredvizhniki worked chiefly in a realist manner, depicting the life of peasants, workers, and the provincial middle class; Russian history and landscape; and portraits of the leading intellectuals of the day. Their art often carried an implicit or explicit social criticism of the conditions of the poor and of the institutions of the empire, in line with the critical realism promoted by writers such as Nikolai Chernyshevsky and the critic Vladimir Stasov, the movement's most energetic public champion. Patronage from the merchant-collector Pavel Tretyakov, who bought works directly for his gallery in Moscow, provided crucial financial support.

Major figures included Kramskoi and Vasily Perov; the landscape painters Ivan Shishkin, Alexei Savrasov, and Arkhip Kuindzhi; the historical painter Vasily Surikov; Nikolai Ge; and, from the 1870s and 1880s, Ilya Repin, whose Barge Haulers on the Volga (1873) became an emblem of the movement. Later members and exhibitors included Valentin Serov and Isaac Levitan.

Later years

The society held 48 travelling exhibitions between 1871 and 1923. By the turn of the 20th century its realism had lost its oppositional edge and the society became increasingly conservative relative to newer movements, though many former members joined the reformed Imperial Academy. The society dissolved in 1923, and its legacy — the equation of Russian art with truthful depiction of national life — profoundly influenced Soviet-era socialist realism.
