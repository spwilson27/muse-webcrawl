Erotic literature

An early edition of a classic erotic novel (image placeholder)

Erotic literature comprises fictional and factual stories and accounts intended to arouse sexual feelings in the reader. It is one of the oldest forms of literature, with examples in ancient Greek and Roman poetry, the Indian Kama Sutra tradition, medieval fabliaux, and the tales of One Thousand and One Nights.

History

Famous works include the poems of Sappho and Ovid's Ars Amatoria; the Italian Renaissance writings of Pietro Aretino; John Cleland's Fanny Hill (1748), one of the most prosecuted and banned books in history; the works of the Marquis de Sade; and Leopold von Sacher-Masoch's Venus in Furs (1870). In the twentieth century, works such as D. H. Lawrence's Lady Chatterley's Lover, Henry Miller's Tropic of Cancer and Anaïs Nin's Delta of Venus were at the centre of obscenity trials that reshaped publishing freedom in Britain and the United States.

Genres and reception

Erotic literature ranges from romantic fiction with sexual content to explicit erotica written largely by and for women, and contemporary commercial successes such as Fifty Shades of Grey. Scholars distinguish erotica, intended to be read with one hand "in praise" of sexual desire as a creative force, from pornography, though the boundary is contested. Erotic literature has frequently been the target of censorship, and its legal status has been a recurring test of freedom of expression.
