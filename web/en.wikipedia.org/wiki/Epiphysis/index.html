Epiphysis

An epiphysis (from Ancient Greek epi, "on top of", and physis, "growth") is one of the rounded ends of a long bone, the portion that articulates with adjacent bones at a joint. Between the epiphysis and the shaft (diaphysis) lies the metaphysis, including the epiphyseal plate (growth plate) in growing bones.

Structure

The epiphysis is filled with red bone marrow, which produces blood cells. It consists of spongy (cancellous) bone covered by a thin shell of compact bone, and its joint surface is coated with articular cartilage. In adults, once growth is complete, the growth plate is replaced by the epiphyseal line and the epiphysis and diaphysis fuse.

Types

There are four types of epiphysis:

  • Pressure epiphysis — forms part of a joint and transmits pressure, e.g. the head of the femur.
  • Traction epiphysis — a site of muscle attachment that does not contribute to a joint, e.g. the greater trochanter.
  • Atavistic epiphysis — a phylogenetically independent bone fused to another, e.g. the coracoid process of the scapula.
  • Aberrant epiphysis — an anomalous epiphysis, such as at the head of the first metacarpal.

Clinical significance

Pathologies of the epiphysis include slipped capital femoral epiphysis in adolescents, Legg–Calvé–Perthes disease (avascular necrosis of the femoral head), and epiphyseal fractures classified by the Salter–Harris system. The pineal gland is also known as the epiphysis cerebri.
