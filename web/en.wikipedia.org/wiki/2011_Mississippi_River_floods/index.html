2011 Mississippi River floods

Event details
PeriodApril – June 2011
LocationCentral United States; Mississippi River basin
DeathsAt least 20 (direct, in associated storms)
DamageEstimated US$2–4 billion
Key actionsOpening of the Morganza Spillway for the first time since 1973; intentional breaching of levees at Birds Point, Missouri

The 2011 Mississippi River floods were among the largest and most damaging recorded along the Mississippi River in the United States, comparable in scale to the Great Flood of 1927 and the Great Flood of 1993. Months of heavy rain across the Ohio and Mississippi valleys, combined with snowmelt, pushed river levels to record heights at numerous gauges during April and May 2011, inundating farmland and communities in states including Illinois, Missouri, Kentucky, Tennessee, Arkansas, Mississippi and Louisiana.

Causes

The flood resulted from an unusually wet spring: a series of major storm systems — including the tornado outbreaks of April 2011 — dropped record rainfall over the Ohio River valley, while a deep snowpack in the upper Midwest melted into already saturated ground. Flows down the Ohio and the upper Mississippi converged at Cairo, Illinois, where the river reached an all-time record crest.

Flood control measures

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers activated emergency components of the Mississippi River and Tributaries Project. On 2 May 2011, explosives breached the Birds Point levee in Missouri, activating the Birds Point–New Madrid Floodway to relieve pressure on Cairo. On 14 May, the Morganza Spillway in Louisiana was opened for the first time since 1973, diverting water into the Atchafalaya Basin to protect Baton Rouge and New Orleans. The Bonnet Carré Spillway near New Orleans was also opened, discharging into Lake Pontchartrain.

Impact

Tens of thousands of residents were evacuated or displaced; the river port and casino economies of towns such as Tunica, Mississippi, were shut down for weeks, and millions of acres of farmland were flooded, delaying planting. Memphis, Tennessee, experienced its second-highest crest on record. Despite the enormous flows, the levee and spillway system largely performed as designed, and major cities were spared catastrophic flooding.
