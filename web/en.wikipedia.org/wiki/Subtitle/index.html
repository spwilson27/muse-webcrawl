Subtitles

Subtitles translate or transcribe dialogue and are usually shown at the bottom of the screen.

Subtitles are texts representing the contents of the audio components of a broadcast or screen product. They usually appear at the bottom of the screen and are most commonly translations of dialogue into the viewer's language, but they may also be same-language transcriptions for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing.

Subtitles differ from dubbing, in which the original voices are replaced by recorded dialogue in another language. In many countries, subtitling is the standard method of presenting foreign-language film and television, while others predominantly use dubbing.

History

The ancestors of subtitles were the intertitles of silent films, printed cards inserted between scenes to convey dialogue and narration. With the coming of sound in the late 1920s, distributors sought ways to present films abroad; early methods included dubbing, translated intertitles, and eventually burning text into the film print itself.

Types

Open subtitles are permanently embedded in the image, while closed subtitles (closed captions in North America) are encoded separately and can be switched on or off by the viewer. Subtitles for the deaf and hard of hearing (SDH) additionally describe relevant sounds, speaker identification, and music. "Forced" subtitles translate only foreign-language passages within an otherwise accessible programme.

Formats

Digital subtitle formats include SubRip (.srt), WebVTT, SubStation Alpha, and EBU-STL for broadcast. Streaming platforms typically deliver subtitles as timed text sidecar files, allowing viewers to select languages and adjust appearance.
