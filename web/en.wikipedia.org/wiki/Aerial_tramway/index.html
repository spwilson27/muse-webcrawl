An aerial tramway, also called a cable car, ropeway, or (in French usage) téléphérique, is a type of cable transport in which one or two large cabins are suspended from stationary support cables and pulled by a haulage cable driven by a motor at one terminal. In the common jig-back configuration, two cabins are attached to opposite ends of the same loop of cable, so that as one ascends the other descends, counterbalancing each other.

Design

The cabins hang from a carriage running on two stationary track ropes (in a bicable system) and can pass over long unsupported spans between widely spaced towers, making tramways suitable for crossing gorges, rivers, and steep mountain faces where other transport cannot go. Capacities range from small industrial installations to cabins carrying 200 people; the Palm Springs Aerial Tramway in California, opened in 1963, uses the world's largest rotating tramcars, whose floors revolve slowly during the ascent of Chino Canyon.

Uses

Tramways serve ski resorts and scenic summits — the Aiguille du Midi cable car in France rises to 3,842 metres — and increasingly urban public transport: New York's Roosevelt Island Tramway (1976) was a pioneering urban installation, and cities such as Medellín and La Paz have built extensive gondola and tramway networks integrated with transit. Material ropeways carry ore and bulk goods in mining districts, historically a major application in the Alps and the Andes.

History

Ropeways for freight were built from the nineteenth century, and passenger tramways proliferated in the Alps and the Americas in the early twentieth century; the Sugarloaf cable car in Rio de Janeiro opened in 1912 and remains one of the most famous.
