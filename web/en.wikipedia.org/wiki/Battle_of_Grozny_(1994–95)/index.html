The Battle of Grozny of 1994–95 was the Russian Army's offensive to capture the Chechen capital, Grozny, at the opening of the First Chechen War. Beginning with a disastrous armoured assault on New Year's Eve 1994, the battle became one of the most destructive urban engagements since the Second World War, leaving much of the city in ruins and killing thousands of civilians.

Background

Chechnya had declared independence in 1991 under Dzhokhar Dudayev, but Russia refused to recognise it. After a failed covert attempt to overthrow Dudayev in November 1994, President Boris Yeltsin ordered a full invasion on 11 December 1994. Defence Minister Pavel Grachev boasted the city could be taken in a couple of hours by a single airborne regiment.

The New Year's Eve assault

On 31 December 1994 Russian columns of tanks and armoured personnel carriers drove into the city centre with poor maps, communications and infantry support. Chechen defenders — many of them veterans of the Soviet Army, coordinated by chief of staff Aslan Maskhadov from the Presidential Palace — let the columns penetrate, then destroyed them from basements and rooftops with rocket-propelled grenades. The 131st Maikop Motor Rifle Brigade was virtually annihilated at the railway station; Russian armoured losses in the first days ran into the hundreds.

The fight for the city

The Russians regrouped, brought up artillery and airpower, and reduced Grozny block by block. The Presidential Palace fell on 19 January 1995, and organised resistance in the centre ended by early March, though fighting across Chechnya continued until the 1996 Khasavyurt Accord. Russian military dead were estimated at 1,500–2,000 or more; Chechen fighter losses were lower but civilian deaths in the battle and war reached into the tens of thousands.

Significance

The battle became a byword for the perils of armoured operations in cities and for the post-Soviet decline of the Russian armed forces, prompting later reforms and influencing urban-warfare doctrine worldwide.
