Pomo

The Pomo are an Indigenous people of California, historically concentrated in the region around Clear Lake and along the coast and Russian River valley of what are now Sonoma, Mendocino, and Lake counties. At the time of European contact, the Pomo spoke seven distinct but related languages of the Pomoan language family and lived in hundreds of independent villages. The name "Pomo" derives from a village name and was applied by outsiders to the whole group.

Culture

The Pomo are renowned for their basketry, considered among the finest in the world: feather baskets decorated with iridescent feathers, clamshell disk beads, and intricate geometric designs, as well as utilitarian burden baskets and fish traps. Acorns — especially from tanbark oaks — were a staple food, processed into meal and mush; fishing, hunting, and gathering supplemented the diet. Pomo society had no overarching tribal government; villages were led by chiefs, and customs included the Kuksu religious ceremonies shared with neighbouring peoples.

History

Spanish mission expansion largely bypassed the Pomo homeland, but Russian settlement at Fort Ross (1812) and, later, Mexican and American settlement brought disease, violence, and dispossession. The Bloody Island massacre of 1850, in which US cavalry killed scores of Pomo people at Clear Lake, is a notorious episode of the California genocide. In the twentieth century the Pomo fought legal battles for recognition and land; today there are more than twenty federally recognised Pomo rancherías and tribes, and efforts to revitalise the Pomoan languages continue.
