From 1885 to 1908, the Congo Free State, a private colony personally owned by King Leopold II of Belgium, was the site of widespread atrocities committed against the indigenous Congolese population. Under Leopold's regime, the territory's rubber and ivory were extracted through a system of forced labour enforced by the Force Publique, the colony's military force, using hostage-taking, mutilation, mass killings, and punitive expeditions.

The rubber system

As world demand for rubber soared in the 1890s, concession companies and state agents imposed quotas of wild rubber on Congolese villages. Failure to meet quotas was punished by imprisonment, flogging with the chicotte (a hippopotamus-hide whip), the taking of women as hostages, and killings. Soldiers were required to account for cartridges used, and severed hands of victims became a notorious form of proof — sometimes taken from living people. Disease, famine, and social disruption caused by the regime compounded the death toll.

Exposure and reform

Reports by missionaries, the British consul Roger Casement (whose 1904 report documented the abuses), and the Congo Reform Association founded by E. D. Morel and Casement mobilised international opinion. Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness (1899) drew on his 1890 journey up the Congo. Under mounting pressure, Leopold was forced in 1908 to cede the territory to the Belgian state, after which it became the Belgian Congo and the worst features of the rubber terror were dismantled.

Death toll and legacy

Estimates of excess deaths vary widely because no reliable population baseline exists. The demographer commonly cited by historians is Adam Hochschild's popularised figure of around 10 million, while other scholars argue for lower or higher figures; the Belgian historian's consensus acknowledges millions of deaths from murder, disease, and starvation combined. In 2020, King Philippe of Belgium expressed "deepest regrets" for the colonial wounds, and debates over restitution, statues of Leopold II, and Belgium's colonial museum in Tervuren continue.
