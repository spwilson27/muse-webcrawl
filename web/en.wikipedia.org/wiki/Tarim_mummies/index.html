Tarim mummies

The Tarim mummies are a series of naturally mummified human remains, dating from roughly 2100 BC to the first centuries AD, discovered in the Tarim Basin in present-day Xinjiang, China. Preserved by the region's extreme aridity and salty soils, the more than 200 mummies — among them the "Beauty of Loulan" (c. 1800 BC) and the burials at Xiaohe Cemetery — retain skin, hair, clothing and grave goods in remarkable condition.

Discovery and study

Early finds were made in the early 20th century by explorers such as Sven Hedin and Aurel Stein, who moved through the region along the ancient Silk Road, and by the Swedish archaeologist Folke Bergman in the 1930s. Major excavations resumed in the later 20th century, notably at the Xiaohe Cemetery, where boat-shaped coffins were found in a forest of wooden poles.

Origins

The mummies' apparently western physical features, woollen plaid textiles resembling European weaves, and wheat-and-dairy grave goods long fuelled speculation that they were migrants from the Eurasian steppe, possibly early Tocharian speakers. Genome-wide studies published in 2021, however, showed that the earliest (Bronze Age) population, represented at Xiaohe, was genetically an isolated local population descended largely from Ancient North Eurasians, with little outside admixture — suggesting they adopted pastoralism and agriculture from neighbours without large-scale migration. Their diet included milk proteins preserved in dental calculus, among the earliest evidence of dairying in East Asia.
