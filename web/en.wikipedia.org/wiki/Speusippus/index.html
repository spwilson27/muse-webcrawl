Speusippus

Speusippus (Greek: Σπεύσιππος; c. 408 – 339/8 BC) was an ancient Greek philosopher. He was the nephew of Plato, being the son of Plato's sister Potone, and succeeded Plato as head (scholarch) of the Academy in Athens upon Plato's death in 347 BC. He led the Academy for eight years, until his own death, when he was succeeded by Xenocrates.

Life

Speusippus accompanied Plato on his third journey to Sicily. According to Diogenes Laërtius, he suffered from a wasting disease (often described as dropsy or paralysis) and, unable to continue as scholarch, summoned Xenocrates to take over the school; some accounts report that he took his own life. Though none of his works survive intact, he was reported to have written numerous treatises, including works on pleasure, justice, the soul, and a work entitled On Similar Things, concerned with the classification of natural kinds.

Philosophy

Speusippus departed from Plato in important respects. He abandoned the Theory of Forms, replacing the Forms with mathematical numbers as the primary realities, and he held that reality is structured in distinct levels — numbers, magnitudes, soul, sensible things — each with its own principles, rather than deriving everything from a single pair of principles. He denied that the good could be a first principle: following an evolutionary picture of reality, he held that value, like beauty in living things, emerges at later stages of development rather than being present at the origin. In ethics, he maintained that the wise person aims at a state of freedom from disturbance (aochlêsia), and treated pleasure and pain as two evils opposed to the good.

Legacy

Speusippus's classification of animals and plants in On Similar Things anticipated later biological taxonomy, and his definition of the soul as "the form of the everywhere extended" was discussed by later authors. Aristotle, who had studied alongside him in the Academy, criticized several of his doctrines.
