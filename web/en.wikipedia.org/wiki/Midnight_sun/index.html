Midnight sun

The midnight sun is a natural phenomenon that occurs in the summer months in places north of the Arctic Circle or south of the Antarctic Circle, when the Sun remains visible at the local midnight. It results from the tilt of the Earth's axis relative to its orbit: near the summer solstice the pole is inclined toward the Sun, so the Sun never fully sets.

Duration and geography

At the poles themselves, the sun rises and sets only once a year, giving six months of daylight. At the Arctic Circle, the sun stays above the horizon for roughly a single day around the solstice, while at higher latitudes the period lengthens: at Svalbard (about 78°N) the midnight sun lasts from mid-April to late August. Territories experiencing the phenomenon include Alaska, northern Canada, Greenland, Iceland (briefly), Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia; the largest cities are Murmansk, Reykjavík sits just below the circle but has near-24-hour daylight in June, and Tromsø.

Effects

Continuous daylight affects sleep, circadian rhythms and daily life; residents use blackout curtains, and summer festivals celebrate the light. The opposite phenomenon in winter is the polar night, when the sun never rises. Locations such as Hammerfest and Tromsø in Norway, and Barrow (Utqiagvik) in Alaska, are popular destinations for viewing the midnight sun.
