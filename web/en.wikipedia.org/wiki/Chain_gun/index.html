Chain gun

A chain gun is a type of autocannon or machine gun that uses an external source of power — typically an electric motor driving a continuous chain — to cycle the weapon's action, rather than diverting excess energy from firing the cartridge as in gas-operated or recoil-operated weapons. The operating cycle (loading, locking, firing, unlocking and extracting) is mechanically driven, so the rate of fire is determined by the motor speed, and a misfired round is simply ejected as the cycle continues, giving chain guns a reputation for high reliability.

Operating principle

In a chain gun, an endless roller chain loops around four sprockets arranged in a rectangle. One link of the chain carries a pin that engages the bolt, driving it back and forth as the chain circulates. As the chain travels along the front side of its rectangle, the bolt dwells locked in battery while the round fires; the geometry of the loop therefore controls the timing of the cycle precisely and independently of ammunition power. Because the action is powered externally, the gun does not depend on the energy of the cartridge, so it can safely handle dud rounds and its rate of fire can be varied by changing motor speed.

Examples

The best-known chain guns are the products of Hughes Helicopters (later McDonnell Douglas and now Northrop Grumman). The EX-34 7.62 mm chain gun was adopted by the British Army as the L94A1, used as a coaxial weapon on armoured vehicles. The 25 mm M242 Bushmaster arms the Bradley Fighting Vehicle and the LAV-25; the 30 mm M230 is mounted beneath the AH-64 Apache helicopter; and larger members of the family include the 35 mm and 50 mm Bushmaster III and Bushmaster IV designs. "Chain Gun" is a registered trademark of Northrop Grumman for these weapons.

Comparison with other actions

Unlike rotary cannons such as the M61 Vulcan, a chain gun has a single barrel and breech, giving a lower maximum rate of fire but a much lighter and simpler weapon. Unlike gas-operated guns, it is unaffected by variations in propellant charge and does not vent propellant gases into the vehicle, an advantage for turret installations.
