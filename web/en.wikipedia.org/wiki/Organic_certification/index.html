Organic certification

Organic certification

Applies to: Food, textiles, cosmetics, other products

Administered by: Accredited certifying bodies

Organic certification is a certification process for producers and handlers of organic agricultural products, verifying that they comply with an organic standard and entitling them to market their goods with an organic label or seal. Certification is carried out by independent bodies accredited by governments or by standard-setting organisations, and typically involves an application, a farm or facility inspection, review of records and inputs, and annual renewal.

Standards

Organic standards generally require that crops be grown without most synthetic pesticides and fertilisers, that genetically modified organisms and irradiation be excluded, that soil be managed for health and fertility, and that livestock have access to pasture or outdoor space and be raised without routine antibiotics or growth hormones. Land must usually undergo a transition period — commonly three years — free of prohibited substances before its produce can be certified. Processed products must meet thresholds for organic ingredient content; under the United States rules, for example, a product labelled "organic" must contain at least 95% organic ingredients.

Major systems

  • United States: the USDA National Organic Program (established by the Organic Foods Production Act of 1990, with uniform rules from 2002) accredits certifiers and owns the USDA Organic seal.
  • European Union: EU organic regulations, first adopted in 1991 and recast in 2018, govern production and labelling across member states under the green "Euro-leaf" logo.
  • Other systems: Canada, Japan (JAS), India (NPOP), Australia and many other countries operate national schemes, with equivalency arrangements facilitating trade; private standards such as those of the Soil Association in Britain and Demeter for biodynamic produce may exceed public requirements.

Issues

Debates around certification include its cost burden on small farmers, fraud in international supply chains, disputes over whether hydroponic production or large-scale livestock operations qualify, and the growth of participatory guarantee systems as an alternative for local markets in the developing world.
