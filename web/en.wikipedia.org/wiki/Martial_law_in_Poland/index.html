Martial law in Poland (Polish: Stan wojenny w Polsce) refers to the period between 13 December 1981 and 22 July 1983, when the authoritarian communist government of the Polish People's Republic drastically restricted everyday life by introducing martial law and a military junta in an attempt to counter political opposition, in particular the Solidarity movement.

T-55 tanks in Warsaw during the first days of martial law.

Background

The August 1980 Gdańsk strikes had forced the government to legalise Solidarity, the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, which grew to some ten million members. Amid economic collapse, mass strikes and growing pressure from Moscow, the authorities prepared plans for a crackdown. On 18 October 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski became First Secretary of the Polish United Workers' Party, concentrating party, government and military power in his hands.

Declaration and crackdown

In the night of 12–13 December 1981, the Military Council of National Salvation (WRON) took power. Thousands of Solidarity activists — eventually more than 10,000 people — were interned; the union was suspended and later outlawed. A curfew was imposed, borders were sealed, telephone lines were cut and recorded warnings announced every call was controlled, travel between cities required permits, and independent media were shut down. Nine striking miners were shot dead by ZOMO riot police at the Wujek coal mine on 16 December 1981. Around 91 people in total are documented to have been killed during the martial-law period.

Lifting and aftermath

Martial law was suspended on 31 December 1982 and formally lifted on 22 July 1983, followed by an amnesty, though many repressive laws remained. Jaruzelski defended the decision for the rest of his life as the "lesser evil" that averted a Soviet invasion; historians continue to debate whether Moscow truly intended to intervene. In 1992 the Sejm declared the imposition of martial law illegal, and some of its organisers were later tried for their roles.
