The Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA), enacted in 1990, is a United States federal law that requires federal agencies and institutions receiving federal funds (such as museums and universities) to return certain Native American cultural items — human remains, funerary objects, sacred objects, and objects of cultural patrimony — to lineal descendants, Indian tribes, and Native Hawaiian organisations.

Provisions

The Act requires institutions to complete inventories of human remains and associated funerary objects, and summaries of other cultural items, in consultation with tribes, and to repatriate them upon a valid claim. On federal and tribal lands, the Act governs the excavation and disposition of Native American remains discovered after 1990, requiring consultation before removal. It also prohibits trafficking in Native American human remains and cultural items obtained in violation of the Act.

Background

For more than a century, museums and scientific institutions collected the remains of hundreds of thousands of Native Americans, often excavated from graves without consent. Native activists campaigned for decades for their return, arguing that the dead were ancestors entitled to reburial according to tribal tradition. NAGPRA was a landmark in the recognition of Indigenous rights in the United States, though its implementation has been slow and contested.

Notable cases and revisions

The remains known as Kennewick Man (the Ancient One) were the subject of lengthy litigation between scientists and tribes before being repatriated to claimant tribes in 2017 and reburied. Revised regulations that took effect in January 2024 strengthened the duty to consult, required deference to Indigenous knowledge, and set deadlines intended to speed up repatriation.
