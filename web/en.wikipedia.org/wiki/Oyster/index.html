Oyster

Oyster is the common name for a number of different families of salt-water bivalve molluscs that live in marine or brackish habitats. In some species, the valves are highly calcified, and many are somewhat irregular in shape. Many, but not all, oysters are in the superfamily Ostreoidea. Some types of oysters are commonly consumed as food, cooked or raw, and in some locales are regarded as a delicacy. Some types of pearl oysters are harvested for the pearl produced within the mantle.

Anatomy and ecology

Oysters are filter feeders, drawing water over their gills to extract plankton and detritus; a single oyster can filter tens of litres of water per day, improving water clarity. True oysters cement one valve to a hard surface and often grow in dense aggregations called beds or reefs, which provide habitat for many other species. They are sequential hermaphrodites or change sex during life in many species, and reproduce by broadcast spawning of eggs and sperm.

As food

Oysters have been eaten since prehistory. Important food species include the Pacific oyster (Magallana gigas), the eastern oyster (Crassostrea virginica), and the European flat oyster (Ostrea edulis). They are farmed extensively in aquaculture. Raw oysters carry a risk of infection with Vibrio bacteria and norovirus, so health authorities advise care for vulnerable people.

Pearls

Pearl oysters (family Pteriidae) are not closely related to true oysters but produce most cultured pearls when an irritant becomes coated in nacre within the mantle.

Environmental role

Oyster reefs have declined drastically worldwide through overharvesting, disease, and pollution; restoration projects re-establish reefs for their water-filtration and shoreline-protection benefits.
