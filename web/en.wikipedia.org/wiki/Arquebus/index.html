An arquebus (also spelled harquebus, from Dutch haakbus, "hook gun") is a form of long gun that appeared in Europe and the Ottoman Empire during the 15th century. An early type of muzzle-loaded firearm, it was fired from the shoulder or braced against the chest and was the first gun to be equipped with a trigger mechanism, the matchlock. Arquebusiers became a standard component of early modern infantry, complementing pikemen in pike-and-shot formations.

History

Early hand cannons required the shooter to apply a heated wire or coal to a touch hole. The matchlock, developed around the 1470s, held a smouldering slow match in a serpentine arm released by a trigger, letting the soldier aim with both hands. A hooked projection on early examples could be braced against a wall to absorb recoil. Resting forks were used to support the heavy barrel.

Use in warfare

Arquebusiers proved decisive at battles such as Cerignola (1503), often called the first battle won by gunpowder small arms, and Pavia (1525), where Spanish arquebusiers helped defeat the French. The weapon spread worldwide: Portuguese traders introduced the arquebus (tanegashima) to Japan in 1543, transforming Japanese warfare. In the later 16th century the heavier, more powerful musket gradually supplanted the arquebus in most European armies, though the term persisted into the 17th century.

Characteristics

Typical arquebuses had smoothbore barrels of roughly 0.5–0.75 inch calibre, effective aimed ranges of perhaps 50–100 metres, and a slow rate of fire of about one shot per minute or two — offset by cheap production, minimal training requirements, and the ability to penetrate armour at close range.
