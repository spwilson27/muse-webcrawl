Uranium mining in Canada has a long history and the country has been one of the world's largest producers of uranium since the mid-20th century. Canada's uranium production is concentrated in the Athabasca Basin of northern Saskatchewan, which hosts some of the highest-grade uranium deposits in the world, including Cigar Lake and McArthur River.

History

Uranium was first discovered in Canada at LaBine Point on Great Bear Lake in the Northwest Territories in 1930, where pitchblende was mined initially for radium. During the Second World War, the Eldorado mine at Port Radium supplied uranium for the Manhattan Project. Post-war demand drove exploration in Ontario (Elliot Lake), Saskatchewan (Beaverlodge), and the Northwest Territories. Elliot Lake became a major mining centre in the 1950s, but production declined as Saskatchewan's high-grade Athabasca deposits came on stream.

Athabasca Basin

The Athabasca Basin deposits are unconformity-related and exceptionally rich: ore grades at Cigar Lake and McArthur River can exceed 10–20% U3O8, hundreds of times the world average. Because of the extreme grades, mining is conducted largely by remote-controlled methods such as raise boring and jet boring, with the ore frozen in place at Cigar Lake to control groundwater inflow. Cameco and Orano Canada are the principal operators.

Economics and policy

Uranium mining is a significant employer in northern Saskatchewan, with a large share of the workforce drawn from local Indigenous communities. Provincial royalties and federal regulation under the Canadian Nuclear Safety Commission govern the industry. Saskatchewan maintains a policy supportive of uranium mining, while other provinces — notably British Columbia and Nova Scotia — have had moratoria or bans. Canadian uranium is exported for peaceful uses under bilateral safeguards agreements.
