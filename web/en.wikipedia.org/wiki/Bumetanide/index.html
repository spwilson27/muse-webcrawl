Bumetanide, sold under the brand name Bumex among others, is a loop diuretic medication used to treat fluid retention (oedema) caused by heart failure, liver disease, or kidney disease, and sometimes to treat high blood pressure. It is considerably more potent milligram-for-milligram than furosemide — roughly 1 mg of bumetanide is equivalent to about 40 mg of furosemide — and it is available in oral, intravenous, and intramuscular forms. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Medical uses

Bumetanide is used for oedema associated with congestive heart failure, hepatic cirrhosis, and renal disease including nephrotic syndrome. In acute decompensated heart failure it is given intravenously for rapid diuresis. Because its oral bioavailability is high and predictable (around 80-90 percent), it may be preferred over furosemide in patients with gut oedema or poor absorption.

Mechanism of action

Like other loop diuretics, bumetanide inhibits the sodium-potassium-chloride cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle in the kidney. Blocking reabsorption of sodium, potassium, and chloride at this site — where a large fraction of filtered sodium is normally reclaimed — produces a powerful increase in urine output, along with increased excretion of calcium and magnesium.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects relate to excessive fluid and electrolyte loss: dehydration, low potassium (hypokalaemia), low sodium, low magnesium, and rises in uric acid that can provoke gout. High doses, particularly in renal failure, can cause ototoxicity (hearing damage), a risk shared with other loop diuretics. Bumetanide is a sulfonamide derivative, and caution is advised in patients with sulfonamide allergy, although cross-reactivity is debated.

History and other uses

Bumetanide was developed by the Danish company Leo Pharma and patented in the late 1960s, entering medical use in the 1970s. It has been misused as a masking agent and for rapid weight loss in sport and is prohibited by the World Anti-Doping Agency. Research has also explored bumetanide's effects on the neuronal chloride transporter NKCC1 in conditions such as neonatal seizures and autism, though evidence for benefit in autism has not been confirmed in large trials.
