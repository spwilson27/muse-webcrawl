Brisket is a cut of meat from the breast or lower chest of beef or veal. The beef brisket is one of the nine primal cuts of beef and consists of the superficial and deep pectoral muscles. Because the brisket supports much of the animal's weight, it contains a large amount of connective tissue, making it tough but flavourful — and well suited to slow cooking methods such as smoking, braising, and curing.

Anatomy and grades

A whole packer brisket comprises two muscles: the leaner, rectangular flat and the fattier, thicker point, separated by a layer of fat. The point is also called the deckle end. Butchers may sell the flat alone ("first cut") or the point ("second cut"). Whole briskets commonly weigh between 3 and 8 kilograms.

Culinary uses

Barbecue

Brisket is the signature cut of Central Texas barbecue, where it is seasoned with salt and coarse black pepper and smoked over oak for 12 or more hours at low temperature until the collagen converts to gelatin and the meat becomes tender, developing a dark crust ("bark") and a pink smoke ring. Sliced brisket and chopped "burnt ends" (from the point, a Kansas City speciality) are served with sauce on the side or none at all.

Jewish cuisine

Because brisket is kosher (from the forequarter) and economical, braised brisket became a staple of Ashkenazi Jewish holiday cooking, slow-cooked with onions, carrots, and sometimes sweet sauces for Passover and Rosh Hashanah. Brisket is also the cut corned for corned beef and cured and smoked for pastrami and Montreal-style smoked meat.

Other traditions

In Korean cuisine, brisket (chadolbaegi) is sliced paper-thin and grilled or used in hot pot. Vietnamese phở commonly includes brisket (nạm). In the United Kingdom, brisket is typically sold rolled and braised as a pot roast.
