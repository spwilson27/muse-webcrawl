Magnetic field

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Photograph of iron filings arranged along the field lines of a bar magnet]
Iron filings reveal a bar magnet's field.

A magnetic field is a physical field that describes the magnetic influence on moving electric charges, electric currents and magnetic materials. A moving charge in a magnetic field experiences a force perpendicular to its own velocity and to the field (the Lorentz force, F = qv × B), which is how electric motors work and how the aurora's particles are guided. The SI unit is the tesla.

Magnetic fields are produced by electric currents and by the intrinsic spin magnetic moments of elementary particles — the source of permanent magnets (aligned spins in ferromagnetic domains). Together with the Electric field, the magnetic field forms the electromagnetic field of Maxwell's equations; a changing magnetic field induces an electric field (the principle of generators and transformers). Field strengths span the cosmos: Earth's ~50 μT, a fridge magnet's ~5 mT, MRI scanners' 1.5–7 T, the strongest laboratory fields above 40 T, neutron stars' 10⁸ T and the 10¹¹ T of magnetars. No isolated magnetic pole (Magnetic monopole) has ever been observed; field lines form closed loops.
