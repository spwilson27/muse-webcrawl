In radiometry, irradiance is the radiant flux (power) received by a surface per unit area. The SI unit of irradiance is the watt per square metre (W/m²). The corresponding photometric quantity — weighted by the sensitivity of the human eye — is illuminance, measured in lux. Irradiance is the standard measure of how much electromagnetic energy falls on a surface and underpins fields from solar energy to climatology to medical phototherapy.

Solar irradiance

Solar irradiance is the power per unit area received from the Sun in the form of electromagnetic radiation. The solar constant — the irradiance at the top of Earth's atmosphere at the mean Earth–Sun distance — is approximately 1,361 W/m² (the "total solar irradiance", TSI). After reflection by clouds and the atmosphere (Earth's albedo of about 0.3) and absorption, the average irradiance at the surface is roughly 170 W/m² globally, varying enormously with latitude, time of day, season and cloud cover.

Variations in solar irradiance, driven by the 11-year solar cycle and by longer-term changes in solar output and Earth's orbit, are a key natural forcing of climate. Accurate satellite measurement of TSI began in 1978; the observed ~0.1% variation over the solar cycle is too small to explain recent warming, which is dominated by greenhouse-gas forcing.

Other types

Spectral irradiance measures irradiance per unit wavelength (or frequency), typically in W·m−2·nm−1, and is used in solar-cell characterisation. Peak sun hours, the equivalent number of hours per day at 1 kW/m², is the standard sizing metric for photovoltaic installations. In medicine, irradiance doses are specified for phototherapy treatments such as neonatal jaundice (blue light) and psoriasis (UV).

Mathematical definition

Irradiance Ee is defined as the radiant flux Φe incident on a surface divided by the area A of the surface: Ee = ∂Φe/∂A. For a surface tilted at an angle θ to the incoming beam, the irradiance is reduced by the cosine of the incidence angle (Lambert's cosine law).
