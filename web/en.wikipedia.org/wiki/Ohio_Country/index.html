Ohio Country

Region: Lands west of the Appalachian Mountains, in the Ohio River valley

Era: c. 17th–18th centuries

Contested by: Iroquois Confederacy, France, Great Britain, United States

The Ohio Country (sometimes called the Ohio Territory in the colonial sense) was a historical name for the trans-Appalachian lands in North America west of the Allegheny Mountains and north of the upper Ohio River, encompassing roughly the present-day states of Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, and parts of Pennsylvania, West Virginia, and Minnesota. The term was used by Native Americans, the British, and later the Americans during the eighteenth century for a region prized for its fur trade and fertile land and contested by empires and confederacies.

History

The Iroquois Confederacy claimed the region by right of conquest from the Beaver Wars of the seventeenth century, though it was actually inhabited and hunted over by Shawnee, Delaware (Lenape), Miami, Wyandot, and other peoples, many of them refugees from colonial expansion. French traders and soldiers moved into the valley in the early 1700s, building posts such as Fort Miami and claiming the watershed for New France. British colonial traders followed, and the Ohio Company of Virginia obtained a large royal grant in 1749, sharpening Anglo-French rivalry.

Competition for the Ohio Country triggered the French and Indian War (1754–1763): the young George Washington's skirmish at Jumonville Glen and defeat at Fort Necessity, and General Braddock's disastrous 1755 expedition, were early episodes of the struggle. France ceded the region to Britain in the Treaty of Paris (1763), but Pontiac's War and the Royal Proclamation of 1763, which reserved the west for Native peoples, checked British settlement. In the American Revolutionary era the region again saw warfare; by the Treaty of Paris (1783) it passed to the United States, and Congress organized it as the Northwest Territory in 1787, over the continuing resistance of the Western Confederacy, which ended only after the Battle of Fallen Timbers (1794) and the Treaty of Greenville (1795).
