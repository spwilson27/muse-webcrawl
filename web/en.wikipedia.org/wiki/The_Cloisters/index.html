The Cloisters

The Cloisters, officially the Met Cloisters, is a museum in Fort Tryon Park in Upper Manhattan, New York City, specialising in European medieval art and architecture. A branch of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, it opened in 1938. The building incorporates elements from five medieval French cloisters — Saint-Michel-de-Cuxa, Saint-Guilhem-le-Désert, Bonnefont-en-Comminges, Trie-en-Bigorre, and Froville — disassembled in Europe, shipped to New York, and rebuilt, and its gardens and chapels evoke a medieval monastery overlooking the Hudson River.

History

The core of the collection was assembled by the sculptor and dealer George Grey Barnard, who bought medieval French architectural fragments in the early 1900s and exhibited them in his own "Cloisters" in Washington Heights. John D. Rockefeller Jr. purchased Barnard's collection in 1925 and donated it to the Metropolitan Museum, adding major pieces of his own — including the celebrated Unicorn Tapestries — and funding the new building and the park land around it, plus land across the Hudson to preserve the view. The museum was designed by architect Charles Collens in a manner evoking Romanesque and Gothic monastic buildings.

Collection

The Cloisters houses some 5,000 works (with a core display drawn from the Met's medieval holdings), including the Unicorn Tapestries (South Netherlandish, c. 1495–1505), the Belles Heures of Jean de France, duc de Berry, the Cloisters Cross, Robert Campin's Mérode Altarpiece, stained glass, ivory carvings, enamels, and monumental sculpture. The Bonnefont Cloister garden is planted with species grown in medieval herb gardens, and the museum's three cloister gardens are central to its atmosphere. Programming includes concerts of medieval music in the Fuentidueña Chapel.
