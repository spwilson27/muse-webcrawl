Brackish water, sometimes termed brack water, is water that has more salinity than fresh water, but not as much as seawater. Technically, brackish water contains between 0.5 and 30 grams of salt per litre — more often expressed as 0.5 to 30 parts per thousand. It results from the mixing of seawater with fresh water, as in estuaries, or may occur in brackish fossil aquifers underground.

Occurrence

The most extensive brackish habitats are estuaries, where rivers meet the sea and salinity fluctuates with the tides, and mangrove swamps. Entire seas can be brackish: the Baltic Sea, fed by many rivers and poorly connected to the ocean, has surface salinities of only about 5–8 ppt in its northern parts, while the Caspian Sea is roughly one third as salty as the ocean. The Black Sea's surface layer is also brackish (around 17–18 ppt). Coastal lagoons, deltas such as the Sundarbans, and some desert lakes and aquifers — including the brackish groundwater of the Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System — hold brackish water too.

Ecology

Brackish environments are physiologically demanding: organisms must cope with salinity that changes over hours or seasons. Species able to tolerate wide salinity ranges are called euryhaline; many estuarine fish, crabs, oysters and cordgrasses fall in this group, and estuaries serve as nurseries for numerous marine species. Truly brackish-water specialists are comparatively few, and brackish faunas are usually mixtures of marine and freshwater species.

Human use

Brackish water is generally unfit for drinking, livestock or most irrigation, but it can be desalinated — brackish water reverse osmosis is cheaper than seawater desalination because of the lower salt content — and brackish sources are used for cooling in industry and for aquaculture of euryhaline species such as shrimp and tilapia. In the home aquarium hobby, "brackish" tanks keep species such as monos, archerfish and certain puffers at intermediate salinities.
