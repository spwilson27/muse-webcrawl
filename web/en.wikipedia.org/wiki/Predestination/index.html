Predestination

Predestination, in theology, is the doctrine that all events have been willed by God, usually with reference to the eventual fate of the individual soul. In its narrower sense, particularly in Christianity, it concerns the teaching that God has foreordained who will be saved (the elect) and, in stronger formulations, who will be damned.

In Christianity

The doctrine was developed by Augustine of Hippo in his disputes with Pelagius, and was later a central point of contention in the Reformation. John Calvin taught a strong form sometimes called "double predestination," in which God actively elects some to eternal life and passes over or ordains others to condemnation; this teaching was codified at the Synod of Dort (1618–1619) in response to the Arminian Remonstrants, who held that election is conditional on foreseen faith. The Canons of Dort summarize the Reformed position under the acronym TULIP, the "U" standing for unconditional election.

In other traditions

In Islam, the question of qadar (divine decree) versus human free will was debated by schools such as the Qadariyah and Jabriyah, with Ash'ari theology adopting a middle position of "acquisition" (kasb). Related concepts elsewhere include karma in Indian religions and notions of fate and determinism in philosophy.
