Ketamine is a dissociative anaesthetic drug used medically for inducing and maintaining anaesthesia and, at lower doses, for sedation, pain management and — in the form of its S-enantiomer, esketamine — treatment-resistant depression. It produces a trance-like state characterised by sedation, analgesia and amnesia while preserving breathing and airway reflexes better than most other anaesthetics, and it is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines. Ketamine is also used illicitly as a recreational drug for its dissociative and hallucinogenic effects.

History

Ketamine was synthesised in 1962 by Calvin Stevens at Parke-Davis as a safer alternative to phencyclidine (PCP). It was first tested in humans in 1964 and approved for medical use in the United States in 1970. It saw extensive battlefield use in the Vietnam War and remains important in emergency, paediatric, veterinary and low-resource medicine.

Pharmacology

Ketamine acts chiefly as a non-competitive antagonist of the NMDA receptor, disrupting communication between the thalamus and cortex and producing "dissociative" anaesthesia. It also interacts with opioid, monoamine and cholinergic systems. Characteristic side effects include emergence phenomena (vivid dreams, hallucinations, confusion on waking), raised intracranial and intraocular pressure historically of concern, nausea, and — with chronic heavy recreational use — ulcerative cystitis ("ketamine bladder") and cognitive impairment.

Psychiatric use

From the 2000s, clinical studies showed that sub-anaesthetic intravenous ketamine could relieve depressive symptoms within hours, including in treatment-resistant depression and acute suicidality. In 2019 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved esketamine nasal spray (Spravato) for treatment-resistant depression under restricted distribution, and ketamine clinics offering infusions have proliferated, though long-term efficacy and safety remain under study.
