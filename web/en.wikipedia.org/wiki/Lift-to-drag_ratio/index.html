Lift-to-drag ratio

In aerodynamics, the lift-to-drag ratio (or L/D ratio) is the lift generated by an aerodynamic body such as an aerofoil or aircraft, divided by the aerodynamic drag created by its movement through air. A higher L/D ratio indicates more efficient aerodynamics: it is one of the main measures of an aircraft's efficiency, determining its glide performance, range, and fuel economy.

Significance

For an aircraft in unpowered glide, the L/D ratio equals the ratio of horizontal distance travelled to height lost: an airliner with an L/D of about 17 descends one unit of altitude for every 17 units of forward travel in still air. Maximum L/D occurs at a specific angle of attack and airspeed; flying slower or faster increases total drag, either through induced drag (dominant at low speed) or parasite drag (dominant at high speed).

Typical values

Modern airliners achieve maximum L/D ratios of roughly 15–18; high-performance sailplanes reach 40–60 or more; birds range widely, with soaring species such as albatrosses around 20; early aircraft were far less efficient, with L/D values below 10. The ratio is influenced by wing aspect ratio, airfoil shape, surface smoothness, and configuration changes such as flaps and landing gear.

Related concepts

The aerodynamic efficiency of a design is often summarised by its polar curve, plotting drag against lift over a range of angles of attack. Engineers seek to maximise L/D at cruise while balancing structural weight, cost, and handling qualities.
