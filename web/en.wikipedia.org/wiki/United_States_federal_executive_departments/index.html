The United States federal executive departments are the principal administrative arms of the executive branch of the U.S. federal government. There are fifteen departments, each headed by a secretary who is appointed by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate and serves as a member of the President's Cabinet.

The oldest departments — State, Treasury, and the now-defunct War Department — were created in 1789. The departments in order of creation are State, Treasury, Defense, Justice, Interior, Agriculture, Commerce, Labor, Health and Human Services, Housing and Urban Development, Transportation, Energy, Education, Veterans Affairs, and Homeland Security, the last established in 2002.

Role and size

Departments range enormously in size, from the Department of Defense, the nation's largest employer, to the Department of Education, the smallest Cabinet department. Executive departments are created and funded by Congress, and together they employ the great majority of the federal civilian workforce.
