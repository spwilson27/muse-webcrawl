Coulometer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A coulometer is a device for measuring an electric charge or a quantity of substance produced or consumed in an electrochemical reaction. The name derives from the coulomb, the unit of charge. The instruments were historically called voltameters (not to be confused with voltmeters), and Michael Faraday performed foundational experiments with them in the 1830s that led to his laws of electrolysis.

Types

In a volumetric coulometer, the gas evolved during electrolysis is measured; in a gravimetric (weight) coulometer, the mass of metal — typically silver or copper — deposited on an electrode is weighed. Mercury coulometers once served as integrating current meters. Because the amount of chemical change is directly proportional to the charge passed (Faraday's laws), the measurement is absolute and requires no calibration.

Modern use

Modern electronic coulometers integrate current over time digitally and are used in coulometric titration, battery testing, and precision electrochemistry.
