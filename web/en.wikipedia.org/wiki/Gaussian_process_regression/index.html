Gaussian process regression

Gaussian process regression (GPR), also known as kriging in geostatistics, is a non-parametric Bayesian approach to regression. Rather than fitting a fixed parametric form, it places a prior distribution directly over functions: a Gaussian process is a collection of random variables, any finite subset of which has a joint Gaussian distribution, specified by a mean function and a covariance (kernel) function. Conditioning the process on observed data yields a posterior distribution over functions, giving predictions together with principled uncertainty estimates.

Formulation

Given training inputs X with noisy outputs y, and a kernel k(x, x′) such as the squared exponential (RBF) kernel, the predictive distribution at new inputs is Gaussian with mean K*T(K + σ²I)−1y and a covariance obtained by subtracting the explained variance from the prior variance. Kernel hyperparameters — length scales, signal variance and noise variance — are usually learned by maximising the log marginal likelihood, an automatic Occam's razor that trades off data fit against model complexity.

Computation

Exact GPR requires inverting an n × n covariance matrix, costing O(n³) time and O(n²) memory for n training points, which limits the basic method to tens of thousands of observations. Sparse approximations using inducing points, structured kernel approximations and iterative solvers extend the method to much larger datasets.

Applications

Gaussian process regression is widely used where uncertainty quantification matters: Bayesian optimisation of expensive experiments and machine-learning hyperparameters, robotics and control, spatial interpolation of ore grades and environmental data (kriging), time-series forecasting, and surrogate modelling in engineering design. Its geostatistical form was developed from the work of Danie Krige and formalised by Georges Matheron in the 1960s; the modern machine-learning treatment was popularised by Rasmussen and Williams's textbook Gaussian Processes for Machine Learning (2006).
