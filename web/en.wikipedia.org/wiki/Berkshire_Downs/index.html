Berkshire Downs

Open chalk grassland on the Berkshire Downs, part of the North Wessex Downs.

The Berkshire Downs are a range of chalk hills in southern England, lying mainly in Berkshire and Oxfordshire, with the greater part now included in the North Wessex Downs Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty (National Landscape). The downs form the western continuation of the chalk spine that runs across southern England, bounded by the Vale of White Horse to the north and the Kennet valley to the south.

Geography

The scarp slope of the downs rises steeply from the Vale of White Horse, reaching 297 metres at Walbury Hill, the highest point in Berkshire and in South East England. Dry valleys — formed when surface water disappeared as the chalk became permeable after the last Ice Age — cut into the plateau, and the rolling downland is a mixture of arable fields, sheep-grazed chalk grassland rich in wildflowers and orchids, and beech hangers. The ancient Ridgeway track traverses the length of the downs.

History

The downs are dense with prehistoric monuments. The White Horse of Uffington, a stylised chalk figure cut into the hillside, overlooks the vale near the Iron Age hillfort of Uffington Castle; Wayland's Smithy, a Neolithic long barrow, and the hillforts of Segsbury and Barbury lie along the Ridgeway. In the Anglo-Saxon period the downs saw the Battle of Ashdown (871), traditionally placed here, where the young Alfred the Great helped defeat a Danish army. From the medieval period the open downs were classic sheep country — the "sheep–corn" husbandry of chalkland England.

Horse racing

The springy turf of the downs has made Lambourn, in the valley of the River Lambourn, one of Britain's principal centres of racehorse training since the 19th century — the "Valley of the Racehorse" — with gallops spread across the surrounding downland.
