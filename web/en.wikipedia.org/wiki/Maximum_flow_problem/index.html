Maximum flow problem

In optimization theory, maximum flow problems concern finding a feasible flow through a network that achieves the greatest possible rate. A flow network is a directed graph whose edges have capacities; a flow assigns each edge a value between zero and its capacity such that, at every vertex other than the source s and the sink t, inflow equals outflow. The value of the flow is the total leaving the source, and the task is to maximise it.

The max-flow min-cut theorem

The central result of the theory, proved by L. R. Ford Jr. and D. R. Fulkerson in 1956, states that the value of a maximum flow equals the capacity of a minimum s–t cut — a partition of the vertices separating source from sink, whose capacity is the sum of capacities of edges crossing from the source side to the sink side. The problem originated in the study of Soviet rail networks and American studies of transport interdiction during the Cold War.

Algorithms

The Ford–Fulkerson method repeatedly augments flow along paths in the residual graph; with integral capacities it terminates at an optimum, and with breadth-first search path selection it becomes the Edmonds–Karp algorithm, running in polynomial time. Dinic's algorithm improves this using layered networks and blocking flows. Push–relabel algorithms (Goldberg–Tarjan) achieve still better bounds, and since 2022 a series of results has produced algorithms running in almost-linear time, resolving a decades-old open problem.

Applications

Maximum flow models transportation and communication networks, bipartite matching and assignment problems, image segmentation in computer vision, sports-elimination questions, and circulation and scheduling problems. The related minimum-cost flow problem adds a cost per unit of flow on each edge.
