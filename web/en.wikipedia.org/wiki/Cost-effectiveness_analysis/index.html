Cost-effectiveness analysis

Cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) is a form of economic analysis that compares the relative costs and outcomes (effects) of different courses of action. It differs from cost-benefit analysis in that outcomes are expressed in natural units — such as life-years gained, cases prevented, or millimetres of mercury of blood pressure reduction — rather than being converted into money.

Method

The core measure is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER): the difference in cost between two interventions divided by the difference in their effects. Results are often plotted on the cost-effectiveness plane, whose quadrants distinguish interventions that are more effective and less costly (“dominant”) from those that are more costly and less effective (“dominated”), with most real decisions falling in the trade-off quadrant where the ICER must be judged against a willingness-to-pay threshold.

Use in health care

CEA is used most prominently in health economics and pharmacoeconomics, where outcomes are frequently measured in quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) — the variant known as cost-utility analysis — or disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). The British National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) uses thresholds of roughly £20,000–£30,000 per QALY when appraising new technologies for the National Health Service. Similar bodies exist in many countries, and registries of published analyses, such as the Tufts CEVR registry, maintain league tables of cost-effectiveness.

Other applications

Beyond health, CEA is used in environmental policy (for example, cost per tonne of carbon abated), education, transport safety, and military planning, wherever a single measurable objective can be defined but monetizing benefits is impractical or controversial.

Criticisms

Debate surrounds the ethics and equity of QALY-based thresholds, discounting of future benefits, and the perspective adopted (health-system versus societal). Methods guidance stresses sensitivity analysis to address uncertainty in costs, effects, and model assumptions.
