Con artist

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The confidence trick relies on gaining the victim's trust

A con artist (also called a confidence trickster, scam artist, or con man/con woman) is a person who cheats or tricks others by persuading them to believe something that is not true, typically to gain the victim's trust and exploit it for money, property, or advantage. The term comes from the confidence trick (or confidence game), in which the perpetrator first wins the victim's confidence.

History

The term "confidence man" was coined in 1849 in reporting on the New York swindler William Thompson, who would approach strangers, chat with them, and ask, "Have you confidence in me to trust me with your watch until tomorrow?" Many victims handed over their watches, which Thompson kept. Herman Melville's 1857 novel The Confidence-Man drew on the figure.

Types of schemes

Confidence tricks range from short cons performed on the street — such as the shell game, three-card Monte, and the pigeon drop — to long cons involving elaborate staged settings, fake businesses, and accomplices ("shills"). Modern variants include advance-fee fraud, romance scams, Ponzi and pyramid schemes, and online phishing. Notorious historical con artists include Charles Ponzi, Victor Lustig (who "sold" the Eiffel Tower twice), and Frank Abagnale.

Psychology

Successful con artists exploit traits such as greed, vanity, dishonesty, compassion, and credulity in their marks. A classic maxim holds that a good con succeeds because the victim wants something for nothing; many schemes also make the victim complicit in a dishonest act, discouraging them from reporting the crime.
