Chinese: 胡適; pinyin: Hú Shì
Born: 17 December 1891, Shanghai, Qing China
Died: 24 February 1962 (aged 70), Taipei, Taiwan
Occupation: Philosopher, essayist, diplomat
Known for: Literary revolution, baihua vernacular movement, liberalism in China

Hu Shih (Chinese: 胡適; 17 December 1891 – 24 February 1962) was a Chinese philosopher, essayist, and diplomat. He was a leading figure in China's literary revolution and liberal movements, advocating the replacement of Classical Chinese with written vernacular Chinese (baihua) and the re-evaluation of traditional culture using modern, scientific methods influenced by pragmatism.

Education and New Culture Movement

Hu studied at Cornell University and Columbia University, where he was a student of John Dewey. Returning to China in 1917 to teach at Peking University, his article "A Preliminary Discussion of Literary Reform" helped ignite the New Culture and May Fourth movements. He promoted vernacular literature, critical scholarship — notably on the novel Dream of the Red Chamber — and intellectual pluralism.

Later career

Hu served as ambassador of the Republic of China to the United States (1938–1942) and as president of Peking University. After 1949 he lived in the United States and then Taiwan, where he headed Academia Sinica from 1957 until his death.
