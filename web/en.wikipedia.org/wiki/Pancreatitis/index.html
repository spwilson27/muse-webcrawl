Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, occurring when digestive enzymes — normally activated in the small intestine — become activated inside the pancreas itself and begin to digest the organ, a process called autodigestion. It may arise suddenly (acute pancreatitis) or develop progressively over many years (chronic pancreatitis).

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark of acute pancreatitis is severe upper abdominal pain that often radiates to the back, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and sometimes fever and rapid pulse. Severe cases can progress to systemic inflammation, shock, and multi-organ failure. Chronic pancreatitis causes recurring or persistent pain, malabsorption with fatty stools (steatorrhea), weight loss, and diabetes mellitus as insulin-producing tissue is lost.

Causes

Worldwide, the two commonest causes of acute pancreatitis are gallstones obstructing the pancreatic duct outlet and heavy alcohol use. Other causes include very high blood triglycerides, certain medications, abdominal trauma, infections such as mumps, genetic mutations (e.g., in PRSS1, causing hereditary pancreatitis), and complications of the procedure ERCP. Chronic pancreatitis is most often due to long-term alcohol use, but also to genetic disorders, autoimmune disease, and duct obstruction.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on characteristic pain plus elevated blood amylase or lipase (lipase at least three times the upper limit of normal is the standard criterion) and imaging by CT, MRI, or ultrasound. Treatment is mainly supportive: intravenous fluids, pain control, nutritional support (increasingly early oral or enteral feeding), and treatment of the underlying cause — for example, gallbladder removal after gallstone pancreatitis or alcohol abstinence. Severe acute pancreatitis may require intensive care, antibiotics for infected necrosis, and endoscopic or surgical drainage. Chronic disease is managed with enzyme replacement, pain relief, and diabetes care.

Prognosis

Most acute attacks are mild and resolve within days; roughly one in five is severe, with mortality in necrotizing pancreatitis reaching 15–30 percent. Severity scores such as APACHE II, BISAP, and the revised Atlanta classification guide prognosis.
