Wildlife trade

Scope: Legal and illegal commerce in wild animals and plants

Key treaty: CITES (1973)

Wildlife trade refers to the commerce of products derived from non-domesticated animals or plants, usually extracted from their natural environment or raised under controlled conditions. It encompasses trade in live animals for the pet trade, animal products such as ivory, skins, and traditional medicine ingredients, timber and other plants, and food including bushmeat.

Scale and impact

The legal wildlife trade is worth tens of billions of dollars annually, while the illegal trade is frequently estimated as one of the largest illicit markets in the world. Unsustainable trade is a major driver of biodiversity loss: species such as elephants (for ivory), rhinoceroses (for horn), pangolins (for scales and meat), and many orchids and cacti have been pushed toward extinction. Wildlife markets also pose risks of zoonotic disease transmission.

Regulation

International trade is regulated chiefly by the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES), signed in 1973, which lists species in three appendices according to the degree of protection required and requires permits for trade. Many countries supplement CITES with domestic laws such as the U.S. Endangered Species Act and Lacey Act.

Illegal trade and enforcement

Poaching and trafficking are countered by customs seizures, ranger patrols, demand-reduction campaigns, and organisations such as TRAFFIC, which monitors trade networks. High-profile measures include China's 2017 ban on domestic ivory trade.
