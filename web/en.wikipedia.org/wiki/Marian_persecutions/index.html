Marian persecutions

The Marian persecutions were the persecution of Protestants during the reign of Queen Mary I of England (1553–1558). Mary, a devout Roman Catholic, sought to restore England to Catholicism and revive the medieval heresy laws. Between 1555 and 1558, nearly 300 Protestants were burned at the stake for heresy, earning Mary the posthumous epithet "Bloody Mary" in Protestant historiography. The victims are known collectively as the Marian Martyrs.

Background

On her accession in 1553, Mary repealed the religious legislation of Edward VI and restored the Mass and clerical celibacy. In November 1554 England was formally reconciled with Rome under Cardinal Reginald Pole, and Parliament revived the old heresy statutes, which prescribed burning for obstinate heretics. Mary's marriage to Philip of Spain in 1554 was deeply unpopular and associated her restoration of Catholicism with foreign domination.

The burnings

The first victim, the preacher John Rogers, was burned at Smithfield on 4 February 1555. Among the most famous of the martyrs were the Oxford Martyrs — bishops Hugh Latimer and Nicholas Ridley, burned in Oxford on 16 October 1555, and Thomas Cranmer, the former Archbishop of Canterbury, burned on 21 March 1556 after renouncing his earlier recantations. Most victims, however, were artisans, labourers, and women, and the burnings were concentrated in London and the south-east. Around 800 wealthier Protestants fled into exile in Frankfurt, Strasbourg, Zurich, and Geneva — the Marian exiles.

Impact and memory

The persecutions were recorded in John Foxe's Actes and Monuments (1563), popularly known as Foxe's Book of Martyrs, which fixed the image of the burnings in English Protestant memory for centuries and helped shape English national identity and anti-Catholic sentiment. Modern historians debate the effectiveness of the persecution: it failed to eradicate Protestantism and, by creating martyrs, may have strengthened commitment to the Reformation settlement established under Elizabeth I.
