Copyright trap

Trap streets are fictitious entries planted in maps to detect copying.

A copyright trap is a deliberate, fictitious entry inserted into a work — such as a map, dictionary, encyclopedia, directory or database — intended to provide evidence of copyright infringement if the work is copied. Because facts themselves are generally not copyrightable, publishers use invented material as a marker: if the invented entry appears in a competitor's product, it strongly suggests the whole work was copied rather than independently compiled.

Examples

  • Trap streets: nonexistent streets (or subtly altered features) drawn on maps. One famous case is the "paper town" of Agloe, New York, invented by mapmakers Otto G. Lindberg and Ernest Alpers, which later appeared on other publishers' maps — and even, confusingly, became real when a store named itself after the map entry.
  • Fictitious dictionary entries: the New Oxford American Dictionary included the invented word "esquivalience", defined as "the willful avoidance of one's official responsibilities", planted to catch online dictionaries copying its entries.
  • Easter eggs and fictitious entries in encyclopedias, phone directories ("bugging" subscribers with invented names), and genealogical or scientific datasets.

Legal status

The legal effectiveness of copyright traps varies. In the United States, the Supreme Court's decision in Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service (1991) held that facts and compilations lacking originality are not protected by copyright, which limits claims based purely on copied facts. Nonetheless, a trap entry can still serve as powerful evidence that a defendant had access to and copied the plaintiff's work, and traps are also relevant in jurisdictions that grant database rights, such as the European Union.
