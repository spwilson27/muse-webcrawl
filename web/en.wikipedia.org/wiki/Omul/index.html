The omul (Coregonus migratorius; Russian: омуль) is a whitefish species of the salmon family Salmonidae endemic to Lake Baikal in Siberia, Russia. Long regarded as a subspecies of the Arctic cisco (Coregonus autumnalis), it is now generally treated as a distinct species that evolved in the lake's isolation.

Description

The omul is a slender, silvery pelagic fish, typically 30 to 40 centimetres long and weighing under a kilogram, though larger individuals occur. It has the small adipose fin characteristic of salmonids and feeds chiefly on zooplankton — especially the endemic amphipod-rich plankton of Baikal — and on small fish. Several ecological forms are recognised, differing in spawning grounds and depth preferences.

Ecology

Omul undertake seasonal migrations within the lake, moving into tributary rivers such as the Selenga in autumn to spawn, then returning to the open lake. Together with the Baikal oilfish (golomyanka) and the Baikal seal, it is one of the lake's emblematic endemic animals.

Fishery

For centuries omul was the staple commercial fish of Baikal, eaten fresh, salted, and especially smoked; omul sold at lakeside markets such as Listvyanka is a famous local delicacy for visitors. Industrial fishing in the 20th century landed tens of thousands of tonnes annually at its peak.

Conservation

Stocks declined sharply through overfishing and poaching, with spawning biomass falling to critically low levels in the 2010s. In October 2017 the Russian government imposed a ban on commercial omul fishing in Lake Baikal, with limited exceptions for scientific catches and the traditional fishing of indigenous communities, in an effort to let the population recover.
