Herodotus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Bust of Herodotus, Roman copy of a Greek portrait]

Herodotus (c. 484 – c. 425 BC) was an ancient Greek historian from Halicarnassus in Asia Minor, author of the Histories — a vast inquiry (historia) into the origins of the Greco-Persian Wars, interwoven with geography, ethnography and legend from Egypt, Scythia, Babylon and beyond. Cicero called him the 'Father of History'; critics since antiquity have also dubbed him the 'Father of Lies' for his credulous tales. Both reputations endure: he essentially invented Western historical writing while cheerfully reporting what he 'heard' alongside what he saw.

The Histories

Composed in the mid-5th century BC and later divided into nine books named for the Muses, the Histories open with mythic abductions and the fall of Croesus, then build through the growth of Persia — Cyrus, Cambyses in Egypt, Darius among the Scythians — to the great invasions of 490 and 480–479 BC. Herodotus travelled (or claimed to) from Egypt to Babylon and the Black Sea, interviewed priests and survivors, weighed rival accounts, and repeatedly distinguishes autopsy from hearsay — the beginnings of source criticism, even where his numbers (millions of Persian troops) collapse under scrutiny.

Reputation

Thucydides implicitly rebuked his predecessor's storytelling; Plutarch wrote a whole essay On the Malice of Herodotus. Yet modern archaeology has vindicated many Herodotean reports (Scythian burial customs, Babylon's walls, Egyptian practices), and his ethnographic curiosity — his famous dictum that custom is king — makes the Histories the richest surviving portrait of the 5th-century world. He died, it seems, at Thurii in Italy, the panhellenic colony he had joined.
