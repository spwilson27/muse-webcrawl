Isomorphism

Isomorphism
From Greekisos "equal" + morphe "form, shape"
Notation≅
RelatedHomomorphism, automorphism, isomorphism theorems

In mathematics, an isomorphism is a structure-preserving mapping between two structures of the same type that can be reversed by an inverse mapping. Two mathematical structures are isomorphic (often written ≅) if an isomorphism exists between them: they are then, for the purposes of that type of structure, indistinguishable — every property expressible in the language of the structure holds for one if and only if it holds for the other.

Examples

In algebra, a group isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism: the cyclic group of order 6, for example, is isomorphic to the product of the cyclic groups of orders 2 and 3. The exponential function gives an isomorphism between the real numbers under addition and the positive real numbers under multiplication, which is the basis of logarithms. In graph theory, an isomorphism between graphs is a bijection between their vertex sets preserving adjacency — deciding whether one exists is the famous graph isomorphism problem. In linear algebra, isomorphic vector spaces are exactly those of equal dimension. Category theory generalises the notion: an isomorphism is a morphism with a two-sided inverse, and much of modern mathematics is organised around classifying objects up to isomorphism.

Related uses

The term is also used outside mathematics: in chemistry and crystallography for substances with analogous formulae or crystal forms; in biology (in older usage) for resemblance between unrelated organisms; and in sociology (institutional isomorphism) for the tendency of organisations in a field to become alike.
