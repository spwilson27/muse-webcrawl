Strategic planning

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Strategic planning is an organisation's process of defining its strategy, or direction, and making decisions on allocating its resources to pursue this strategy. It may also extend to control mechanisms for guiding the implementation of the strategy. Strategic planning became prominent in corporations during the 1960s and remains an important aspect of strategic management.

Process

A typical strategic planning process includes:

  1. Situational analysis — assessing the internal and external environment, often using tools such as SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) or PEST analysis of political, economic, social, and technological factors.
  2. Direction setting — articulating vision, mission, and values, and defining strategic goals and objectives.
  3. Strategy formulation — selecting competitive strategies, initiatives, and priorities.
  4. Implementation planning — assigning resources, responsibilities, budgets, and timelines.
  5. Monitoring and evaluation — measuring performance against targets, using tools such as the balanced scorecard and key performance indicators.

Tools and frameworks

Well-known strategic planning tools include the SWOT analysis, Porter's five forces analysis of industry competition, the BCG growth–share matrix, scenario planning, and the balanced scorecard. Planning horizons vary from one to ten or more years depending on the organisation and industry.

Criticism

Henry Mintzberg and others have criticised rigid formal planning for failing in unpredictable environments, arguing that strategy often emerges from patterns of action rather than being fully planned in advance. Modern practice often blends deliberate planning with adaptive, iterative approaches.
