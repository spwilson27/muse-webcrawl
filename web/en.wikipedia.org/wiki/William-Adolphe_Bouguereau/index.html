William-Adolphe Bouguereau

William-Adolphe Bouguereau (30 November 1825 – 19 August 1905) was a French academic painter. In his own time he was considered one of the greatest painters in the world, celebrated for realistic genre paintings and mythological scenes executed with a polished, idealised technique, and he enjoyed significant commercial success in France and the United States.

Career

Born in La Rochelle, Bouguereau studied at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris and won the Prix de Rome in 1850. He became a dominant figure of the French academic establishment, exhibiting regularly at the Paris Salon, teaching at the Académie Julian, and wielding considerable influence over official taste. Famous works include The Birth of Venus (1879), Nymphs and Satyr (1873), Dante and Virgil (1850), and innumerable tender depictions of peasant girls, children, and Madonnas.

Reputation

A staunch opponent of Impressionism and the avant-garde, Bouguereau saw his reputation collapse with the triumph of modernism; his name became a byword for sentimental, lifeless academicism. From the 1980s onward, however, his technical mastery was reappraised, and his paintings again command high prices and major exhibitions.
