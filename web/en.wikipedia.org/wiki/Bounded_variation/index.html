Bounded variation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A function of bounded variation can be written as the difference of two monotone functions (image placeholder)

In mathematical analysis, a real-valued function is said to have bounded variation on an interval if the total amount by which it oscillates there is finite. Formally, for f on [a, b], the total variation is the supremum over all partitions of the sum of |f(xi+1) − f(xi)|; f is of bounded variation if this supremum is finite.

Properties

The concept was introduced by Camille Jordan in 1881. Jordan's theorem states that a function is of bounded variation if and only if it is the difference of two monotone increasing functions. Consequences include that such functions have left and right limits everywhere, have at most countably many discontinuities, and are differentiable almost everywhere (Lebesgue's theorem). Monotone functions and Lipschitz functions are of bounded variation; the continuous function x sin(1/x) on (0, 1] is a standard example of a continuous function that is not.

Applications

Functions of bounded variation are exactly those against which Riemann–Stieltjes integrals of continuous functions are well behaved, and they arise in the theory of Fourier series, rectifiable curves — a curve is rectifiable precisely when its coordinate functions have bounded variation — probability (cumulative distribution functions), and modern variational image-processing models such as total-variation denoising.
