Quantum field theory

In theoretical physics, quantum field theory (QFT) is a theoretical framework that combines classical field theory, special relativity and quantum mechanics. QFT is used in particle physics to construct physical models of subatomic particles and in condensed matter physics to construct models of quasiparticles.

In QFT, particles are treated as excited states (quanta) of underlying quantum fields, which are more fundamental than the particles themselves. The theory's most successful applications are Quantum electrodynamics (QED), Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) and the electroweak theory, unified in the Standard Model of particle physics, whose predictions have been confirmed to extraordinary precision.

History

QFT was born in 1927 with Paul Dirac's quantisation of the electromagnetic field, which explained the spontaneous emission of light by atoms. Through the 1930s the theory struggled with divergent integrals; the resolution came after the Second World War with renormalisation, developed by Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, Julian Schwinger, Richard Feynman and Freeman Dyson, which turned QED into the most precisely tested theory in physics.

The 1954 Yang–Mills theory introduced non-Abelian gauge fields, at first apparently irrelevant to nature; two decades later, with asymptotic freedom (1973) and the Higgs mechanism, gauge theory became the architecture of the Standard Model. Kenneth G. Wilson's renormalisation group (1970s) recast QFT as a tower of effective field theories, each valid at its own scale — arguably the deepest conceptual revision of the framework.

Principles

A quantum field theory specifies fields (scalar, spinor or vector) and a Lagrangian invariant under required symmetries — Poincaré symmetry, and for gauge theories a local symmetry group such as U(1), SU(2) or SU(3). Quantisation promotes the fields to operators; interactions are typically computed with perturbation theory and organised graphically by Feynman diagrams, while non-perturbative methods include lattice gauge theory.

Mandatory consequences of combining quantum mechanics with relativity include antiparticles, the spin–statistics theorem, and the necessity of fields (rather than fixed numbers of particles). The vacuum of QFT is a structured medium with zero-point fluctuations, measurable in effects such as the Casimir effect and the Lamb shift.

Condensed matter and beyond

QFT methods — field operators, Green's functions, broken symmetry, renormalisation — are equally central to condensed matter physics, describing Superconductivity, superfluidity, critical phenomena and topological phases. The still-missing piece of the framework is a complete quantum theory of gravity; candidates such as String theory and Loop quantum gravity build on, or react against, QFT's principles.
