Ratatouille

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A Provençal ratatouille of glossy eggplant, zucchini, peppers and tomato]

Ratatouille is the summer vegetable stew of Nice and Provence: eggplant, zucchini, bell peppers, tomato, onion and garlic, simmered in olive oil with herbs until the vegetables surrender into one another. Born as peasant food — a way to use a glut of August vegetables — it was refined in two schools: all-in-one-pot rustic, and the classic method in which each vegetable is cooked separately so it keeps its character, then married in the pot at the end. Its modern fame owes much to a film: Pixar's Ratatouille (2007), whose climax plates the dish as confit byaldi — the sliced, layered, restaurant version devised by chef Thomas Keller.

Name and family

The name mixes rata — soldiers' slang for a stew — with touiller, to stir. Ratatouille belongs to a Mediterranean family of vegetable medleys: Catalan samfaina, Majorcan tumbet, Spanish pisto, Sicilian caponata (sweet-sour, with olives and capers), Basque piperade (with egg). All answer the same question asked by the same hot sun over the same vegetables: what do we do with August?

Cooking it

Purists salt and drain the eggplant, sweat each vegetable in its own pan, and combine only for a final gentle braise; pragmatists note that a one-pot ratatouille made slowly can be nearly as good, and better the next day, when it is often served at room temperature as a starter, a side for grilled lamb or fish, or spooned over eggs. Like most peasant dishes it improves in the fridge and forgives improvisation — the one unbreakable rule is ripe vegetables and good oil.
