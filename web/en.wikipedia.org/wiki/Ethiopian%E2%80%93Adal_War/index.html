Ethiopian–Adal War

The Ethiopian–Adal War (1529–1543) was a military conflict between the Ethiopian Empire and the Adal Sultanate in the Horn of Africa. Led by Imam Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, Adal forces, supported by the Ottoman Empire, conquered much of Ethiopia before a joint Ethiopian–Portuguese army defeated them at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543.

Background

Relations between the Christian Ethiopian Empire and the Muslim Adal Sultanate to its east had been tense for generations, with periodic raids across the frontier. Under Imam Ahmad, known as "the Conqueror", Adal unified its forces and, equipped with Ottoman-supplied firearms, launched a full invasion of the Ethiopian highlands in 1529.

Course of the war

Adal forces won major victories at Shimbra Kure (1529) and Amba Sel, and over the following years overran most of the empire, forcing Emperor Dawit II to flee and destroying churches and monasteries. Ethiopian appeals to Portugal brought a contingent of about 400 musketeers under Cristóvão da Gama in 1541. Da Gama was captured and executed in 1542, but his surviving men joined Emperor Gelawdewos and defeated Ahmad at Wayna Daga on 21 February 1543, where Ahmad was killed; the Adal army disintegrated soon after.

Consequences

The war devastated both states and weakened them in the face of the great Oromo migrations that followed. It also opened Ethiopia to Jesuit missionaries, whose activities contributed to later internal conflicts.
