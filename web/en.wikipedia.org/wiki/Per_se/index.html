Per se

Per se is a Latin phrase meaning "by itself" or "in itself". It is used in English to mean "intrinsically", "as such", or "essentially": something that is true per se is true by virtue of its own nature, independently of external circumstances or of any wider context. For example, a statement may not be offensive per se, but may become so in a particular context.

Usage

The phrase is a borrowing from Latin, where per means "through" or "by" and sē is the reflexive pronoun "itself". In writing it is often, though not always, set in italics as a foreign term, and pronunciation guides in English dictionaries give forms such as "pur SAY". Common constructions include "not X per se, but..." and "the thing in itself".

In philosophy

In scholastic philosophy the distinction between what belongs to a thing per se (in virtue of what it is) and what belongs to it per accidens (accidentally) is fundamental. Thomas Aquinas, for example, distinguishes propositions that are per se nota — self-evident, known through themselves — and arguments that demonstrate propter quid from effects known per se. The phrase continues to be used in analytic philosophy in discussions of intrinsic properties and essentialism.

In law

In law, a "per se rule" or an act that is illegal per se is one that is inherently wrongful, so that no further proof of harm or intent is required beyond the act itself. In United States antitrust law, certain practices — such as horizontal price fixing — are treated as per se violations of the Sherman Act, in contrast to practices judged under the "rule of reason". Similarly, driving above the legal blood-alcohol limit is a per se offence in many jurisdictions: the measured concentration alone establishes the violation regardless of whether the driver appeared impaired. In tort law, violation of a safety statute may constitute negligence per se.
