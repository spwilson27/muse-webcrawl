Hiram I

Hiram I (Phoenician: 𐤇𐤓𐤌 Ḥirōm, "my brother is exalted"; Hebrew: חִירָם Ḥīrām) was the Phoenician king of Tyre according to the Hebrew Bible and other accounts. He is generally dated to have reigned from about 980 to 947 BCE. Hiram succeeded his father Abibaal as king of what was then the most powerful of the Phoenician city-states.

Reign

Hiram is best known for his alliance with the Israelite kings David and Solomon. According to the biblical narrative in 2 Samuel and 1 Kings, Hiram sent messengers and materials — cedar wood, carpenters and masons — to David, who built his palace in Jerusalem with Tyrian help. After Solomon succeeded David, Hiram supplied cedar and cypress timber from Lebanon, skilled workmen, and large quantities of gold for the construction of the First Temple in Jerusalem. In return, Solomon provided wheat and olive oil for Hiram's household and later ceded twenty towns in Galilee, which Hiram, reportedly unimpressed, called the "land of Cabul".

Hiram and Solomon also jointly sponsored fleets of ships sailing from Ezion-Geber on the Red Sea to Ophir, bringing back gold, precious stones and exotic goods. Josephus, citing the Tyrian annals of Menander of Ephesus, credits Hiram with major public works in Tyre itself: joining the two islands of the city by landfill, enlarging the harbour, and building temples to Heracles (Melqart) and Astarte.

Historical sources

Besides the Hebrew Bible, Hiram is mentioned by the Jewish historian Josephus, who drew on Phoenician records, and the reign lengths given by Josephus are a key anchor for the chronology of the Phoenician kings and, by extension, for dating Solomon's temple to around 967 BCE. Later tradition, especially in Freemasonry, associates Hiram with the legendary master craftsman Hiram Abiff, a distinct figure in the biblical text (a skilled artisan sent by Hiram).

Legacy

Hiram's reign marks the height of Tyre's early expansion as a maritime and trading power across the Mediterranean. The cedar trade and the Tyrian-Jerusalem alliance exemplify the close commercial and political ties between Phoenicia and ancient Israel in the 10th century BCE.
