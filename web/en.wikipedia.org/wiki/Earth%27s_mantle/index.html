Earth's mantle

The Earth's mantle is a layer of silicate rock between the crust and the outer core. It has a mass of 4.01 × 1024 kg and thus makes up 67% of the mass of Earth. It has a thickness of approximately 2,900 kilometres (1,800 mi), making up about 84% of Earth's volume. It is predominantly solid but, on geological time scales, behaves as a viscous fluid, sometimes described as having the consistency of caramel.

The mantle is divided into the upper mantle, which includes the rigid lithospheric mantle and the weaker asthenosphere, and the lower mantle, which extends down to the core–mantle boundary. Partial melting of the mantle at mid-ocean ridges produces oceanic crust, and partial melting at subduction zones produces continental crust.

Structure

The boundary between the crust and mantle, the Mohorovičić discontinuity (Moho), occurs at an average depth of about 7 km beneath oceanic crust and about 35–70 km beneath continents. Within the upper mantle, seismic discontinuities at depths of 410 km and 660 km mark phase transitions in the mineral olivine. Below the 660 km discontinuity lies the lower mantle, composed largely of the mineral bridgmanite together with ferropericlase.

Convection

Heat from the core and from radioactive decay drives slow convection currents in the mantle. This mantle convection is the driving force behind plate tectonics: the lithospheric plates ride on the convecting asthenosphere, producing continental drift, earthquakes, and volcanism. Rising plumes of hot mantle material from deep in the Earth may feed volcanic hotspots such as Hawaii and Iceland.

Composition

Mantle rock is rich in magnesium and iron silicates. The upper mantle is dominated by peridotite, a rock composed chiefly of olivine and pyroxenes. Samples of the mantle reach the surface in ophiolites, in xenoliths carried up by kimberlite eruptions, and in volcanic rocks erupted at oceanic islands.
