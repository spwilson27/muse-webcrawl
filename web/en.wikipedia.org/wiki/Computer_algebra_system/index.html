A computer algebra system (CAS) or symbolic algebra system (SAS) is any mathematical software with the ability to manipulate mathematical expressions in a way similar to the traditional manual computations of mathematicians and scientists. The development of the computer algebra systems in the twentieth century is part of the discipline of "computer algebra" or "symbolic computation", which has spurred work in algorithms over mathematical objects such as polynomials.

Symbolic manipulations

Computer algebra systems may be divided into two classes: general-purpose systems, which handle a wide range of mathematics, and special-purpose systems, which are devoted to a specific part of mathematics, such as grading of ideals, Gröbner bases, or group theory. Symbolic manipulations supported by general-purpose systems include:

  • Simplification to a canonical form, including automatic simplification of expressions
  • Substitution of symbols or numeric values for certain expressions
  • Change of form of expressions: expansion, factoring, collecting terms, and partial fractions
  • Symbolic differentiation, including partial derivatives, and symbolic integration
  • Exact arithmetic, such as computation with integers of arbitrary size, fractions and arbitrary-precision floating-point numbers
  • Solution of linear and some non-linear equations over various domains, including differential and recurrence equations
  • Factorization of polynomials and integers, and computation of Gröbner bases

History

The beginnings of computer algebra systems date to the 1960s, driven by the needs of theoretical physics and the limitations of computation by hand. The first widely used systems were Macsyma (developed at MIT under Joel Moses) and Reduce (developed by Anthony C. Hearn). Originally, programs for manipulating formulas ran on batch-processing mainframes, and gave results in printout; researchers working with them had to reconstruct the results by hand. A breakthrough came with the development of polynomial manipulation algorithms, including the computation of greatest common divisors.

In the 1980s, systems such as SMP (a precursor of Mathematica) and Maple brought symbolic computation to workstations, and by the 1990s integrated environments combining symbolic computation with numerical computation, graphics, and typesetting had become standard tools in mathematics, physics, and engineering. The twenty-first century saw the rise of free and open-source systems such as SageMath and SymPy, and of web-based systems.
