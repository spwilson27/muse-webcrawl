Bathythermograph

A bathythermograph, or BT, is an instrument for measuring the temperature of ocean water as a function of depth. It consists of a torpedo-shaped housing containing a temperature-sensitive element and a pressure-sensitive bellows that moves a stylus across a smoked or gold-coated glass slide, tracing a graph of temperature against depth as the device sinks.

History

The bathythermograph was developed in the late 1930s by Athelstan Spilhaus at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, building on work by Carl-Gustaf Rossby. It came into wide use during the Second World War, when navies needed rapid measurements of the thermocline: the depth and sharpness of temperature gradients strongly affect sonar performance, and submarines could hide beneath a strong thermocline layer. Ships could deploy a BT while under way, a major advantage over earlier stop-and-lower methods.

Expendable bathythermograph

The expendable bathythermograph (XBT), introduced in the 1960s, uses a small falling probe connected by a fine wire that spools out as it sinks; temperature data are transmitted up the wire and the probe is abandoned. XBTs launched from research vessels and volunteer merchant ships have produced millions of profiles and remain a backbone of upper-ocean temperature monitoring and climate research, alongside CTD instruments and Argo floats.
