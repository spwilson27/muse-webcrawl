Religion in ancient Rome

Religion in ancient Rome consisted of varying imperial and provincial religious practices, which were followed both by the people of Rome and those brought under its rule. The Romans thought of themselves as highly religious, and attributed their success as a world power to their collective piety (pietas) in maintaining good relations with the gods. Their polytheistic religion is known for having honoured many deities.

Gods and rites

The presence of Greeks on the Italian peninsula introduced some religious practices such as the cult of Apollo. The Romans looked for common ground between their major gods and those of the Greeks (interpretatio graeca), adapting Greek myths and iconography for Latin literature and Roman art. Etruscan religion was also a major influence, particularly on the practice of augury.

According to Roman myth, most of Rome's religious institutions could be traced to its founders, particularly Numa Pompilius, the Sabine second king of Rome, who negotiated directly with the gods. This archaic religion was the foundation of the mos maiorum, "the way of the ancestors" or simply "tradition", viewed as central to Roman identity.

Priesthoods and the state

Roman religion was practical and contractual, based on the principle of do ut des, "I give that you might give". Religion depended on knowledge and the correct practice of prayer, ritual, and sacrifice, not on faith or dogma. Public religious rites were overseen by colleges of priests such as the pontifices and augurs; the chief priest was the pontifex maximus, an office later held by the emperor.

Religious festivals

The Roman calendar was organised around religious observances. Festivals included the Saturnalia, the Lupercalia, and the Parilia. Even at the latest date, religious festivals accounted for more than a third of the year in the calendar of Philocalus.

Imperial cult and Christianity

From Augustus onward, the divinity of deceased emperors was officially recognised, and the imperial cult became a unifying element of the empire. In the 4th century CE, Christianity spread and was eventually adopted by the emperors; in 380 the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the state religion, and traditional public cults were suppressed by the end of the century.
