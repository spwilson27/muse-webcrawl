Finite group

In abstract algebra, a finite group is a group whose underlying set is finite — that is, a set equipped with an associative binary operation, an identity element, and inverses, having finitely many elements. The number of elements is called the order of the group. Finite groups are among the most studied objects in algebra, with applications in number theory, geometry, combinatorics, physics, and cryptography.

Basic theory

Fundamental results include Lagrange's theorem — the order of any subgroup divides the order of the group — and its corollaries: every element raised to the power of the group order equals the identity, and every group of prime order is cyclic. Cauchy's theorem and the Sylow theorems guarantee subgroups of prime-power order and describe their structure and conjugacy. Every finite group can be represented as a group of permutations (Cayley's theorem).

Examples

  • Cyclic groups Z/nZ, generated by a single element, such as the integers modulo n under addition.
  • Symmetric groups Sn of all permutations of n objects, and their alternating subgroups An.
  • Dihedral groups, the symmetry groups of regular polygons.
  • Matrix groups over finite fields, such as GL(n, q) and the simple groups of Lie type.
  • The simple abelian groups, exactly the cyclic groups of prime order.

Classification

Every finite group is built from finite simple groups — groups with no nontrivial normal subgroups — via extensions, formalised by the Jordan–Hölder theorem. The classification of finite simple groups, completed around 2004 and comprising tens of thousands of pages across hundreds of authors, states that every finite simple group is one of: a cyclic group of prime order; an alternating group An (n ≥ 5); a simple group of Lie type; or one of 26 sporadic groups, the largest of which, the Monster group, has order about 8 × 1053. William Burnside's paqb theorem and the Feit–Thompson odd-order theorem (every finite group of odd order is solvable) are landmarks of the theory.

Applications

Finite groups encode symmetry in chemistry and crystallography (point groups and space groups), underpin Galois theory's account of polynomial solvability, appear in particle physics, and support cryptographic constructions such as Diffie–Hellman key exchange over finite cyclic groups and elliptic curves.
