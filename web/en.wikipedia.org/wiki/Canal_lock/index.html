Canal lock

A lock is a device used for raising and lowering boats, ships and other watercraft between stretches of water of different levels on river and canal waterways. The distinguishing feature of a lock is a fixed chamber whose water level can be varied; the chamber either lifts or lowers the vessels passing through it.

Construction

A typical lock consists of a chamber with watertight gates at each end — commonly pairs of mitre gates that point upstream and are sealed by water pressure. Valves or sluices (paddles) control the flow of water into and out of the chamber. To go upstream, a boat enters the chamber, the gates and paddles are closed, and the chamber is filled from the upper level until the water equalises; the upper gates then open and the boat leaves. The process is reversed for downstream passage.

History

Early "flash locks" on rivers were simple sluices through weirs, dangerous and wasteful of water. The pound lock, with a chamber between two sets of gates, developed in China by the 10th century (attributed to the engineer Qiao Weiyue in 984) and in Europe in the 14th and 15th centuries; Leonardo da Vinci is credited with perfecting the mitre gate in Milan in the 1490s. Locks made the canal age possible, allowing canals to climb over hills by flights of locks or staircase arrangements.

Variants

Where water is scarce or the rise is great, alternatives include boat lifts, inclined planes and caisson locks. Very large locks serve seagoing ships, as in the Panama Canal locks, which raise vessels 26 metres above sea level. Modern locks may be operated by keepers or by boat crews themselves.
