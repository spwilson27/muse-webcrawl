Phonograph record

A 12-inch vinyl LP

A phonograph record (also known as a gramophone record, especially in British English), a vinyl record (for later varieties), or simply a record, is an analog sound storage medium in the form of a flat disc with an inscribed, modulated spiral groove. The groove usually starts near the periphery and ends near the center of the disc. Emile Berliner developed the disc record as an improvement on Thomas Edison's phonograph cylinder in the late 1880s.

Formats

Early records were made of shellac and played at about 78 revolutions per minute (the "78"). In 1948 Columbia Records introduced the 12-inch Long Play (LP) disc spinning at 33⅓ rpm, made of polyvinyl chloride—"vinyl"—with much finer "microgrooves". RCA Victor responded with the 7-inch 45 rpm single. The LP became the dominant album format for decades.

Decline and revival

Sales declined sharply after the introduction of the compact disc in the 1980s and digital downloads and streaming in the 2000s. However, from the late 2000s vinyl experienced a sustained revival, and in the early 2020s vinyl records again outsold compact discs in several major markets, valued by collectors and listeners for their sound character, artwork and physicality.

Playback

Records are played on a record player or turntable, where a stylus traces the groove and its vibrations are converted into sound by a cartridge, moving either a magnet or a coil to generate an electrical signal.
