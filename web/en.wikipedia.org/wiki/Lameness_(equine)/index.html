Lameness (equine)

Lameness

A horse presented for lameness evaluation

Lameness is an abnormal gait or stance of an animal that arises from dysfunction of the locomotor system. In the horse, lameness is the most common cause of lost use and veterinary evaluation, and may result from pain in a limb, a mechanical restriction of movement, or a neurological disorder.

Evaluating the lame horse involves observing the animal at rest and in motion, palpation and manipulation of the limbs, hoof testing, flexion tests, diagnostic nerve and joint blocks, and imaging such as radiography, ultrasonography, scintigraphy and MRI.

Signs

Forelimb lameness typically shows a characteristic "head nod": the head rises as the lame limb strikes the ground and drops as the sound limb bears weight. Hindlimb lameness may show asymmetric hip movement (a "hip hike"), reduced fetlock drop, shortened stride, or toe dragging. Other signs include reluctance to bear weight, abnormal hoof wear, and behavioural changes under saddle.

Grading

The American Association of Equine Practitioners (AAEP) scale grades lameness from 0 (not perceptible) to 5 (most pain borne on the opposite limb or inability to move).

Causes

Common causes include foot abscesses, navicular syndrome, laminitis, osteoarthritis, tendon and ligament injuries such as suspensory desmitis, fractures, and joint infections. Many conditions are managed with rest, corrective shoeing, anti-inflammatory medication, intra-articular therapy or surgery.

Diagnosis

Because horses cannot localise pain verbally, systematic diagnostic analgesia ("nerve blocks") that sequentially desensitises regions of the limb is central to pinpointing the source, followed by targeted imaging of the identified region.
