A SQUID (Superconducting Quantum Interference Device) is an extremely sensitive magnetometer used to measure very weak magnetic fields. It operates by exploiting superconductivity and the Josephson effect: a superconducting loop interrupted by one or two Josephson junctions has electrical properties that vary periodically with the magnetic flux threading the loop, in units of the magnetic flux quantum. SQUIDs can detect fields thousands of times weaker than those measured by any other magnetometer.

History and design

The device was invented in 1964 by Robert Jaklevic, John Lambe, James Mercereau and Arnold Silver at the Ford Research Laboratory, shortly after Brian Josephson predicted the tunnelling of Cooper pairs. Two main configurations exist. The DC SQUID contains two Josephson junctions in parallel in a superconducting loop; a bias current produces a voltage that oscillates with applied flux. The RF SQUID contains a single junction and is read out through a resonant radio-frequency circuit. Because superconductivity requires cryogenic temperatures, conventional SQUIDs are cooled in liquid helium to about 4 K, although high-temperature superconducting versions operating in liquid nitrogen (77 K) have been developed.

Applications

  • Medicine: magnetoencephalography (MEG) and magnetocardiography, recording the minute magnetic fields of the brain and heart.
  • Geophysics and prospecting: measuring subsurface magnetic properties for mineral exploration and archaeology.
  • Materials testing: non-destructive evaluation and scanning SQUID microscopy of tiny samples and electronic circuits.
  • Fundamental research: particle detection, gravitational-wave instrumentation, dark-matter searches (as amplifiers) and low-temperature physics.

Limitations

SQUIDs require elaborate magnetic shielding and cryogenic cooling, and their sensitivity to vibration and ambient magnetic noise constrains deployments. Despite these demands, for measurements at the femtotesla level they remain unmatched, and SQUID-based amplifiers also serve in radio astronomy and quantum computing read-out.
