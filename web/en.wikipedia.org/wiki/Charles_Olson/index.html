Charles Olson (1910–1970) was an American poet and essayist, a central figure of postwar American poetry. His 1950 essay "Projective Verse" became a manifesto for a generation of poets associated with Black Mountain College, and his epic The Maximus Poems, centered on the fishing port of Gloucester, Massachusetts, is considered a landmark of the American long poem.

Charles Olson, poet of Gloucester

Early life

Olson was born in Worcester, Massachusetts, to Swedish immigrant parents. He studied at Wesleyan University and Harvard, where he worked on a doctorate on Herman Melville before abandoning it. His scholarly study Call Me Ishmael (1947), a reading of Moby-Dick, remains influential. During the Second World War he worked for the Office of War Information and the Democratic National Committee, experiences that disillusioned him with politics.

Black Mountain College

Olson first taught at Black Mountain College in North Carolina in 1948 and served as its rector from 1951 until its closure in 1957. There he gathered around him poets including Robert Creeley and Robert Duncan, and articulated the theory of "projective verse" — poetry composed by the breath and the ear, treating the poem as an open field of energy rather than a closed form. His correspondence with Creeley helped define the poetics of the "Black Mountain school."

The Maximus Poems

Beginning in the 1950s, Olson wrote The Maximus Poems, a vast, unfinished sequence addressed from the figure of Maximus to the city of Gloucester, where he lived from 1957. Drawing on history, geography, mythology, and local archives, the poems explore the relation of the individual to place and polis. Volumes appeared in 1960 and 1968, with a third published posthumously.

Later years and legacy

Olson taught at the State University of New York at Buffalo in the 1960s, influencing a younger generation. He died of liver cancer in New York in 1970. His ideas about composition — "one perception must immediately and directly lead to a further perception" — shaped poets from the Beat generation to the Language poets.
