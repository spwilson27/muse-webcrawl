Otzi, also called Ötzi the Iceman, is the natural mummy of a man who lived around 3300 BC and whose body was preserved in the ice of the Ötztal Alps on the border between Austria and Italy. Discovered by hikers Helmut and Erika Simon on 19 September 1991, he is Europe's oldest known natural human mummy and has given scientists an unprecedented view of Copper Age life: his clothes, tools, tattoos, diet and health have all been studied in detail.

Discovery and preservation

The body was recovered from a gully at about 3,210 m above sea level, where it had been encased in ice for more than 5,000 years. Because the find was first handled as a modern fatality, the body and artefacts suffered some damage before their antiquity was recognised. Ötzi is now kept in a refrigerated cell at the South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology in Bolzano, Italy, at −6 °C and high humidity to prevent decay.

Body, possessions and death

Ötzi was about 46 years old, roughly 1.60 m tall, with brown eyes, a beard, and 61 tattoos made by rubbing charcoal into small cuts, many located near joints affected by arthritis. His equipment included a high-quality copper axe, a flint dagger, an unfinished yew longbow with a quiver of arrows, birch-bark containers (one holding embers for fire), a backpack frame, and clothing of goat and calf leather, bearskin cap and grass cape. Analysis of his stomach and intestines revealed last meals of ibex, red deer, einkorn wheat and herbs. CAT scans revealed an arrowhead lodged in his left shoulder: Ötzi bled to death from the wound, making his one of the earliest known cases of homicide, and he also had a head injury. His genome, sequenced in 2012, showed ancestry related to early European farmers and a predisposition to cardiovascular disease; he was lactose intolerant and carried Lyme-disease bacteria.
