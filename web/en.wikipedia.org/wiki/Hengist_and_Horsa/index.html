Hengist and Horsa

Hengist and Horsa are legendary Germanic brothers said to have led the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes in their invasion of Britain in the fifth century AD, founding the Kingdom of Kent. Their story is told by Bede in his Ecclesiastical History, in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, and by Geoffrey of Monmouth.

The legend

According to the tradition, the British king Vortigern invited the brothers and their followers, who landed at Ebbsfleet in Kent around 449, to serve as mercenaries against the Picts. Their names mean "stallion" and "horse" in Old English, suggesting they may originally have been horse deities or heroic figures of folklore, comparable to the divine twins of Indo-European myth. Horsa was later killed in battle against the Britons; Hengist, reinforced by his son, quarrelled with Vortigern, treacherously slaughtered British nobles at a peace meeting (the "Treason of the Long Knives" in later tradition), and established himself as king of Kent. The Chronicle gives his death as 488.

Historicity

Most modern historians doubt that Hengist and Horsa were real individuals; no contemporary source mentions them, and the neat pairing of alliterating brothers is characteristic of foundation legend. The underlying events — Germanic settlement of eastern Britain in the fifth century and the emergence of a Kentish kingdom — are historical, though gradual migration and acculturation are now emphasised over a single organised invasion.
