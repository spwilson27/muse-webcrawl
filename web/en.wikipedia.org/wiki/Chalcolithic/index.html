Chalcolithic

The Chalcolithic (from Greek khalkós, copper, and líthos, stone), also known as the Copper Age or Eneolithic, is an archaeological period characterised by the first significant use of copper alongside stone tools. It stands between the Neolithic (New Stone Age) and the Bronze Age, when copper came to be alloyed with tin. In the Near East the Chalcolithic lasted roughly from 4500 to 3300 BC, though its timing varied greatly by region.

Characteristics

Early copper working exploited native copper, which could be hammered cold; smelting of copper ores followed, with evidence from sites such as Belovode in Serbia (c. 5000 BC) among the earliest in the world. Copper was too soft and scarce to displace flint and stone, which remained dominant for everyday tools. The period nevertheless saw intensifying social complexity: craft specialisation, long-distance trade in metal and prestige goods, fortified settlements, and rich burials such as those of the Varna necropolis in Bulgaria (c. 4600–4200 BC), whose gold grave goods are the oldest processed gold known.

Regional developments

In Mesopotamia the Chalcolithic encompasses the Ubaid and Uruk periods, culminating in the first cities and the invention of writing at the threshold of the Bronze Age. In the southern Levant, the Ghassulian culture produced remarkable copper casting by the lost-wax method, exemplified by the Nahal Mishmar hoard. In Europe the period saw megalithic monument building and the spread of metallurgy westward. Ötzi the Iceman (died c. 3300 BC in the Alps) carried a nearly pure copper axe, a classic Chalcolithic artefact.

Debate

Because the "age" is defined by a technology that coexisted with stone for millennia and appeared at very different dates in different places, some archaeologists treat the Chalcolithic as a transitional phase of the Neolithic rather than a universal stage in the three-age system. In regions such as Egypt and Mesopotamia the term is still widely used in periodisation; in the Americas, where copper was worked early in the Great Lakes region but smelting remained rare, it is seldom applied.
