Meuse–Argonne offensive

The Meuse–Argonne offensive was the principal American operation of the First World War, fought in north-eastern France between 26 September and 11 November 1918. The American Expeditionary Forces, commanded by General John J. Pershing, attacked German positions between the Meuse river and the Argonne Forest as part of the Allied Hundred Days Offensive that ended the war.

Plan and opening attacks

Pershing's First Army, over a million men strong, was tasked with driving north to sever the Sedan railway, a key German supply artery. The battle area was formidable: the heights of the Meuse, the fortified Kriemhilde Stellung, and the thick woods of the Argonne, all held by experienced German divisions. The assault began on 26 September behind one of the largest artillery bombardments of the war.

Stalemate and breakthrough

Progress was slower than planned. Inexperienced staff work caused supply columns to jam the few roads, while German reserves stiffened resistance. In the forest, a battalion-sized force of the 77th Division — the celebrated "Lost Battalion" — held out surrounded for five days. Renewed attacks in October and early November, with Hunter Liggett now commanding First Army, cracked the German line; the road to Sedan lay open when the Armistice took effect at 11 a.m. on 11 November 1918.

Aftermath

The offensive cost the United States around 26,000 dead and nearly 96,000 wounded. It established the young American army as a decisive force on the Western Front and remains the largest land battle ever fought by the United States in terms of troops engaged.
