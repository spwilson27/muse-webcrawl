A land snail is any of the numerous species of snail that live on land, as opposed to the sea snails and freshwater snails. They are terrestrial gastropod mollusks, most of them belonging to the group Stylommatophora, which breathe air through a lung-like cavity rather than gills. Tens of thousands of species exist, from minute litter-dwellers to the giant African land snails (genus Achatina), whose shells can exceed 20 cm.

Land snails are a major component of terrestrial ecosystems, breaking down leaf litter and fungi, dispersing seeds and spores, and serving as prey for birds, mammals, reptiles and invertebrates. Some species are pests of gardens and agriculture, and some are invasive when introduced beyond their native ranges.

Anatomy

Like other gastropods, land snails have a muscular foot for crawling on a film of mucus, a head with two pairs of retractable tentacles — the upper pair bearing eyes at their tips — and, in most species, a coiled external shell of calcium carbonate into which the animal can withdraw. The shell grows by accretion at the aperture, and a calcareous epiphragm may seal the opening during dry or cold dormancy (aestivation or hibernation).

Diet

Most land snails are herbivorous or detritivorous, grazing algae, fungi, decaying leaves and live plant material with a rasping radula; a minority are predators of other snails and worms.

Reproduction

Stylommatophoran snails are simultaneous hermaphrodites, and many species court with a calcareous "love dart" before exchanging sperm; eggs are typically laid in moist soil or litter.

Human relations

Some species, notably the escargots Helix pomatia and Cornu aspersum, are farmed for food. Conversely, introduced giant African snails and predatory rosy wolfsnails have caused severe ecological damage, including extinctions of island snail faunas in the Pacific.
