Thai calendar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thailand uses two main calendar systems: the Thai solar calendar, the Gregorian calendar adapted for official and civil use, and the Thai lunar calendar, used for traditional and Buddhist religious observances. Dates in Thailand are commonly expressed in the Buddhist Era (B.E.), which is 543 years ahead of the Christian/Common Era (A.D./C.E.).

Thai solar calendar

The solar calendar was introduced by King Chulalongkorn (Rama V) in 1888, replacing the lunar calendar as the official calendar. Originally the new year began on 1 April; in 1941 Prime Minister Plaek Phibunsongkhram moved New Year's Day to 1 January to align with international practice, making 1940 only 273 days long. Months and days are identical to the Gregorian calendar, with Thai names for the months, but the year is counted from the Buddhist Era. Thailand's legal and official documents are dated in B.E.; the A.D. year is obtained by subtracting 543.

Thai lunar calendar

The lunar calendar determines Buddhist holy days (wan phra), festivals such as Visakha Bucha, Magha Bucha, and Asalha Bucha, and traditional events like Songkran's astrological calculations. It is a lunisolar system related to the Burmese and other Southeast Asian calendars, with intercalary months and days added to keep it in step with the solar year. Chinese calendar influences also appear in the twelve-year animal cycle.
