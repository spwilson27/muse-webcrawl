Guaranteed minimum income (GMI) is a system of social welfare provision that guarantees all citizens or families in a state an income sufficient to meet a defined minimum standard of living, provided they meet certain conditions. Unlike a universal basic income, which is paid to everyone regardless of means, a guaranteed minimum income is typically means-tested: the state tops up the income of households whose earnings and other benefits fall below the threshold, and entitlement usually requires the recipient to be available for, or actively seeking, work or training.

Design

Schemes specify a guarantee level (often linked to a poverty line), a withdrawal ("taper") rate at which the payment is reduced as earnings rise, and conditions such as residency, asset tests and job-search requirements. A closely related mechanism is the negative income tax, proposed by Milton Friedman, in which the top-up is administered through the tax system. Advocates argue GMI provides a safety net that reduces poverty with lower fiscal cost than universal payments; critics point to poverty traps created by high effective marginal tax rates as benefits are withdrawn, administrative complexity, and stigma attached to means-testing.

Examples

Most European welfare states operate some form of last-resort minimum income scheme: France's revenu de solidarité active (RSA, replacing the RMI of 1988), Germany's unemployment benefit II (Hartz IV, replaced by the Bürgergeld in 2023), and Spain's ingreso mínimo vital, introduced in 2020 as the country's first nationwide scheme. In the United States, programmes such as Supplemental Security Income and TANF perform partial versions of the function, and local guaranteed-income pilots (for example in Stockton, California, from 2019) have tested unconditional variants for low-income residents.
