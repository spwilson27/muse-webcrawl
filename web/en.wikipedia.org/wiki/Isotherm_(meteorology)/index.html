Isotherm (meteorology)

An isotherm (from Greek isos, "equal", and therme, "heat") is a line on a map or chart connecting points of equal temperature at a given time or over a given period. Isotherms are the most familiar of the isolines used in meteorology, alongside isobars (equal pressure) and isotachs (equal wind speed).

Use in weather analysis

Plotting isotherms on surface and upper-air charts lets meteorologists visualise temperature gradients, locate fronts (where isotherms are tightly packed), and track the advection of warm and cold air masses. World isothermal maps were pioneered by Alexander von Humboldt in 1817, one of the first thematic maps in science; they revealed, for example, that western Europe is warmer than eastern North America at the same latitude because of ocean currents and prevailing winds.

Climatologists use mean isotherms to define climatic regions: the 10 °C July isotherm in the Northern Hemisphere, for instance, is a common definition of the Arctic treeline and the boundary of polar climates in the Köppen classification, since trees cannot grow where the warmest month averages below about 10 °C. The position of the 0 °C isotherm is important in forecasting snow levels and freezing rain.

Related terms

In physical chemistry and thermodynamics, an isotherm is a curve of pressure against volume at constant temperature, as in Boyle's law and in adsorption isotherms such as the Langmuir and BET models. In meteorology the term sometimes refers specifically to vertical temperature structure — an "isothermal layer" is one in which temperature does not change with height, a situation that can trap pollutants near the ground.
