Phosphorylation is the addition of a phosphate group (PO43−) to a molecule. In biochemistry it most often refers to the covalent attachment of phosphate to proteins, but phosphorylation of sugars and lipids is also fundamental to metabolism, and the production of ATP itself occurs by phosphorylation of ADP.

Protein phosphorylation

In eukaryotes, proteins are phosphorylated mainly on the hydroxyl groups of serine, threonine and tyrosine residues by enzymes called kinases, which transfer the terminal phosphate of ATP; the reaction is reversed by phosphatases. First demonstrated by Edmond Fischer and Edwin Krebs in 1954–55 for glycogen phosphorylase (work that won them the 1992 Nobel Prize), reversible protein phosphorylation is now recognised as the cell's dominant regulatory switch: it controls enzyme activity, protein–protein interactions, localisation and degradation, and underlies signal transduction cascades such as the MAPK pathway and the insulin response. Roughly 500 kinases are encoded in the human genome, and abnormal phosphorylation is implicated in many cancers, making kinase inhibitors major drug targets.

Other forms

  • Oxidative phosphorylation — synthesis of ATP driven by the electron transport chain in mitochondria and bacteria, the main source of ATP in aerobic organisms.
  • Substrate-level phosphorylation — direct transfer of a phosphate from a high-energy substrate to ADP, as in glycolysis.
  • Photophosphorylation — ATP synthesis powered by light in photosynthesis.
  • Sugar phosphorylation — e.g. glucose to glucose-6-phosphate, trapping sugar inside the cell at the first step of glycolysis.
