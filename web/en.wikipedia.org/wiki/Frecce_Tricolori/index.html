Frecce Tricolori

The Frecce Tricolori (Italian: [ˈfrettʃe trikoˈloːri], literally "Tricolour Arrows"), officially the 313° Gruppo Addestramento Acrobatico, is the aerobatic demonstration team of the Italian Air Force (Aeronautica Militare). Based at Rivolto Air Base in Friuli, it is the world's largest aerobatic team, flying ten aircraft, and is famous for its tricolour smoke trails in the green, white and red of the Italian flag.

Active: 1961–present
Country: Italy
Branch: Italian Air Force
Base: Rivolto Air Base, Udine
Aircraft: Aermacchi MB-339-A/PAN
Team size: 10 aircraft (9 + solo)

History

Italy had a strong pre-war tradition of collective aerobatics dating to the 1930s, and several squadrons fielded display teams after the Second World War, flying aircraft such as the F-84 and F-86 Sabre. In 1961 the Air Force decided to form a single permanent national team at Rivolto, the Frecce Tricolori. The team flew the Canadair Sabre until 1963, then the Fiat G.91, and since 1982 the specially modified Aermacchi MB-339, painted in a deep blue livery with tricolour trim.

The display

The formation consists of nine aircraft plus a soloist ("Pony 10") who performs independent manoeuvres between formation figures. Signature manoeuvres include the Bomb, the Cardioid (a heart pierced by the solo) and the Lomcovák. The smoke system releases coloured trails that form the Italian tricolour — at 5,000 metres of smoke, one of the longest national flags flown in the sky. Pilots are selected from experienced fighter squadrons and usually serve several seasons; the commander flies as "Pony 0" from the ground.

Ramstein disaster

On 28 August 1988, during an airshow at Ramstein Air Base in Germany, three of the team's aircraft collided during the Cardioid manoeuvre. The wreckage and fire killed all three pilots and 67 spectators, with hundreds injured — the deadliest airshow accident in history. The disaster led to sweeping changes in airshow safety rules worldwide, including greater separation between display lines and crowds.
