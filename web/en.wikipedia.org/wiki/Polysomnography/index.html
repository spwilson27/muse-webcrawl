Polysomnography

Polysomnography (PSG), a type of sleep study, is a multi-parametric test used in the study of sleep and as a diagnostic tool in sleep medicine. The test result is called a polysomnogram. The name derives from Greek and Latin roots: poly ("many"), somnus ("sleep"), and graphein ("to write"). Polysomnography is the gold standard for diagnosing sleep-disordered breathing and many other sleep disorders.

Recorded parameters

A typical in-laboratory polysomnogram simultaneously records:

  • Electroencephalography (EEG) — brain waves used to stage sleep;
  • Electrooculography (EOG) — eye movements, for identifying rapid eye movement sleep;
  • Electromyography (EMG) — chin and leg muscle activity;
  • Electrocardiography (ECG) — heart rate and rhythm;
  • Airflow at the nose and mouth, respiratory effort from chest and abdominal belts, and blood oxygen saturation by pulse oximetry;
  • Snoring sounds, body position, and synchronised video.

Indications

Polysomnography is used to diagnose or evaluate obstructive and central sleep apnoea, narcolepsy (in combination with the multiple sleep latency test the following day), periodic limb movement disorder, REM sleep behaviour disorder, parasomnias, nocturnal seizures, and insomnia in selected cases. Recorded events are scored in 30-second epochs according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) manual, producing measures such as the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI), sleep efficiency, and the percentages of sleep stages N1, N2, N3, and REM.

Types

Diagnostic studies are commonly divided into full-night studies and split-night studies, in which the second half of the night is used to titrate continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy. Home sleep apnoea tests record a reduced set of channels and are used for uncomplicated patients with a high pre-test probability of obstructive sleep apnoea, but attended in-lab polysomnography remains the reference standard.

History

The physiological recording of sleep developed from Hans Berger's EEG in the 1920s and the discovery of REM sleep by Eugene Aserinsky and Nathaniel Kleitman in 1953. Standardised scoring criteria were published by Rechtschaffen and Kales in 1968 and remained in use until the AASM manual of 2007.
