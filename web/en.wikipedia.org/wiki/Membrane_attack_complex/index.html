Membrane attack complex

The membrane attack complex forms a transmembrane pore

The membrane attack complex (MAC) or terminal complement complex (TCC) is a complex of proteins typically formed on the surface of pathogen cell membranes as a result of the activation of the host's complement system, and as such is an effector of the immune system. Antibody-mediated complement activation leads to MAC deposition on the surface of infected cells and the subsequent destruction of the cell.

Structure and function

The membrane attack complex is made up of complement proteins C5b, C6, C7, C8, and multiple molecules of C9. The activation of the complement system, whether by the classical, lectin, or alternative pathway, produces the C5 convertase, which cleaves C5 into C5a and C5b. C5b binds C6 and C7, and the complex inserts into the lipid membrane. C8 joins, followed by polymerization of 10 to 16 molecules of C9, forming a ring-shaped pore roughly 10 nanometres in internal diameter.

Mechanism of killing

The pore spans the membrane and allows free diffusion of ions and small molecules. Water enters the target cell by osmosis, and the cell swells and lyses. The MAC is especially effective against Gram-negative bacteria, whose outer membrane is directly exposed; Gram-positive bacteria, with their thick peptidoglycan layer, are relatively resistant. Nucleated host cells can resist attack by shedding MAC-bearing membrane vesicles and by regulatory proteins such as CD59, which blocks C9 polymerization.

Clinical relevance

Deficiencies in terminal complement components predispose individuals to recurrent infections with Neisseria species, including meningococcal disease. Conversely, uncontrolled MAC deposition contributes to tissue damage in disorders such as paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, in which red blood cells lack CD59 and related regulators. The drug eculizumab, an antibody against C5, prevents MAC formation and is used to treat this condition.
