Atomic number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Atomic number
Symbol: Z
Definition: Number of protons in an atomic nucleus
Established by: Henry Moseley (1913)

The atomic number of a chemical element is the number of protons in the nucleus of each of its atoms. It is the property that defines the element: every carbon atom has 6 protons, every uranium atom 92. Atoms of the same element with different neutron counts are isotopes (such as carbon-14).

Mendeleev's table ordered elements by atomic weight, which mostly works but produced anomalies (tellurium before iodine, against their chemistry). In 1913 Henry Moseley, measuring X-ray spectra of the elements, found they follow a simple law in the nuclear charge — establishing atomic number as the physical basis of the table and predicting missing elements by their numbers. Moseley was killed at Gallipoli in 1915, aged 27, one of science's great losses to war.

In a neutral atom the atomic number also equals the electron count, which governs chemistry through the configuration of electron shells. Nuclear reactions change Z (that is transmutation — the alchemists' dream, achieved by radioactive decay and by accelerators), while chemical reactions never do.
