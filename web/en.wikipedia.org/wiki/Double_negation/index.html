Double negation

A double negation is the use of two negative markers in a single clause. In some languages and dialects the two negatives cancel each other out and yield an affirmative meaning, as in standard literary usage ("I do not disagree"), whereas in others they reinforce the negation — a phenomenon known as negative concord — as in "I don't know nothing" in many varieties of English, or standard usage in languages such as Spanish, Italian and Russian.

In logic

In classical logic, double negation elimination is the rule that from ¬¬P one may infer P: the negation of a negation is equivalent to the original proposition. In intuitionistic logic this rule is not generally valid, and accepting or rejecting it is one of the key differences between classical and constructive reasoning.

In language

Negative concord is the historical norm in English: Chaucer could stack four negatives for emphasis. The idea that two negatives must make a positive in language derives from an analogy with mathematics, promoted by 18th-century grammarians such as Robert Lowth, and became a rule of Standard English, while many dialects — including African American Vernacular English and Cockney — retain negative concord as a systematic grammatical feature.

Double negative in literature

Deliberate double negation can also serve as litotes, an understated affirmation ("not uncommon", "not bad"), in both writing and speech.
