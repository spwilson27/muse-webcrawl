Expulsion of Germans from Czechoslovakia

The expulsion of Germans from Czechoslovakia after World War II was part of the wider flight and expulsion of Germans from Central and Eastern Europe. Between 1945 and 1947, roughly three million ethnic Germans — most of them Sudeten Germans from Bohemia, Moravia and Czech Silesia — were expelled or fled from the re-established Czechoslovak state.

Background

The Sudeten Germans had formed a large minority in interwar Czechoslovakia. After the 1938 Munich Agreement annexed the Sudetenland to Nazi Germany, and the subsequent occupation of the Czech lands, the exiled Czechoslovak government of President Edvard Beneš resolved that the German minority should be removed after the war to prevent a recurrence. The Allied powers endorsed population transfers at the Potsdam Conference in 1945, stipulating they should be "orderly and humane".

The expulsions

So-called "wild" expulsions in the spring and summer of 1945, carried out locally before Potsdam, involved violence and massacres, including the Brno death march and the Ústí massacre. Organised transfers followed in 1946–47, mainly to the American and Soviet occupation zones of Germany. The Beneš decrees stripped most ethnic Germans of citizenship and property. Death toll estimates vary widely, from around 15,000–30,000 confirmed deaths in Czech research to much higher figures in older German expellee literature.

Legacy

The expulsions remain a sensitive subject in Czech–German relations. The 1997 Czech–German Declaration expressed regret for excesses and injustices on both sides. Property and legal claims arising from the Beneš decrees continue to surface in European politics.
