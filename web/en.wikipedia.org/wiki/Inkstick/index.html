Inkstick

An inkstick (Chinese: 墨; pinyin: mò; Japanese: 墨, sumi; Korean: 먹, meok) is a type of solid ink used traditionally in several East Asian art and calligraphy forms. Inksticks are made mainly of soot and animal glue, sometimes with incense or medicinal scents added. They are ground against an inkstone with a small quantity of water to produce liquid ink, which is then applied with an ink brush.

History

Solid ink in China can be traced to the late Warring States period, with early forms made from charred materials. The craft matured during the Han dynasty, and by the Tang and Song dynasties ink-making had become a refined art. Huizhou in Anhui province became especially famous for its inks, and celebrated makers such as the Shexian workshops produced sticks decorated with carved designs, gilding and calligraphy, making fine inksticks valued as collectible art objects in their own right.

Manufacture

The main ingredients of an inkstick are soot and glue. Soot is gathered from burning oil (oil soot, valued for a glossy, bluish-black ink) or pine wood (pine soot, giving a matte, deep black). The soot is mixed with animal glue, kneaded, pressed into wooden moulds that imprint designs and the maker's mark, then dried slowly — sometimes for months — to prevent cracking. Additives such as musk, camphor, pearl dust or gold leaf may be included in luxury sticks.

Use

To prepare ink, water is placed on the inkstone and the stick is ground in a circular motion on its flat surface until ink of the desired consistency forms. Freshly ground ink is generally preferred by calligraphers and painters because its particle size and glue content can be controlled, producing subtle gradations of black. Inkstick ink is one of the Four Treasures of the Study, together with the brush, paper and inkstone.
