Jewish self-determination

Jewish self-determination is the principle that the Jewish people, as a nation, have the right to govern themselves in their own sovereign state. It is the core political claim of Zionism, the national movement founded in the late 19th century in response to European antisemitism, which achieved its goal with the establishment of the State of Israel in 1948.

Origins

Ideas of national self-determination spread through 19th-century Europe, and proto-Zionist writers such as Moses Hess (Rome and Jerusalem, 1862) and Leon Pinsker (Auto-Emancipation, 1882) argued that Jews could escape persecution only through national sovereignty. Theodor Herzl's Der Judenstaat (1896) and the First Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897 organised the movement politically, aiming "to establish a home for the Jewish people in Palestine secured under public law".

Realisation

The Balfour Declaration of 1917 and the League of Nations Mandate gave international recognition to the goal of a Jewish national home. After the Holocaust and the struggle against British rule — including the Jewish insurgency in Mandatory Palestine — the UN voted in 1947 to partition Palestine, and Israel declared independence on 14 May 1948. Israel's Basic Law: Israel as the Nation-State of the Jewish People (2018) declares that "the exercise of the right to national self-determination in the State of Israel is unique to the Jewish people."

Debate

The concept is contested in the context of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict: Palestinian advocates argue that the Palestinian people hold an equivalent right to self-determination, and that the two claims must be reconciled, commonly through a two-state solution or a binational arrangement. Supporters frame Jewish self-determination as one instance of the general right of peoples recognised in the UN Charter; critics argue over how it is implemented in practice.
