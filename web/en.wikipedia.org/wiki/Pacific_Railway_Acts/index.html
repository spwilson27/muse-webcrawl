Pacific Railway Acts

The Pacific Railway Acts are a series of United States federal laws that promoted the construction of a transcontinental railroad across the country, authorising the issuance of government bonds and extensive land grants to railroad companies.

The Act of 1862

The Pacific Railroad Act of 1862, signed by President Abraham Lincoln during the Civil War, chartered the Union Pacific Railroad and authorised the Central Pacific Railroad of California to build eastward from Sacramento to meet it. The companies received rights of way, alternating sections of public land along the route, and loans of government bonds per mile of track laid, with higher rates in mountain terrain.

Later acts

An amending act of 1864 doubled the land grants, eased the terms of the loans and allowed the companies to issue their own first-mortgage bonds. Further acts authorised other transcontinental lines, including the Northern Pacific. The two original lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on 10 May 1869.

Legacy

The acts transferred vast areas of public land to the railroads, accelerated settlement of the West, and were later entangled in scandals such as Crédit Mobilier. The land-grant system shaped the development of the American interior for generations.
