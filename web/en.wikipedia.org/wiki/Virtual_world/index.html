Virtual world

A virtual world is a computer-simulated environment which may be populated by many simultaneous users who can create a personal avatar and independently explore the virtual world, participate in its activities, and communicate with others. These avatars can be textual, graphical representations, or live video avatars with auditory and touch sensations.

Users inhabit persistent virtual worlds through customisable avatars.

Characteristics

Virtual worlds are generally characterised by persistence (the world continues to exist and change when individual users log off), shared spatiality (many users occupy the same space simultaneously), real-time interaction via avatars, and some degree of user agency and customisation — building objects, owning virtual property, and creating content. Some virtual worlds are games with goals and rules (massively multiplayer online games), while others, such as Second Life, are open-ended social platforms without objectives.

History

Text-based predecessors include MUDs (multi-user dungeons) of the late 1970s and the object-oriented MOO environments of the 1990s. Lucasfilm's Habitat (1986) pioneered graphical avatars; the World Wide Web era brought Active Worlds (1995) and 3D chat spaces. Second Life (2003) brought virtual worlds to mainstream attention, with its user-created content and virtual currency economy; large game worlds such as World of Warcraft (2004) reached tens of millions of users. More recent platforms include Minecraft, Roblox, VRChat, and various "metaverse" initiatives.

Applications

Beyond entertainment, virtual worlds have been used for education and virtual classrooms, remote collaboration, military and medical training simulations, architectural visualisation, virtual tourism, concerts and events, and research in psychology and economics. Embassies, universities, and corporations have at various times maintained a presence in them.

Social and economic issues

Virtual worlds raise questions about virtual property rights, taxation of virtual income, governance and moderation, addiction, identity and anonymity, and the legal status of acts committed through avatars. Economies in some worlds have produced real-world incomes for creators and traders, blurring the boundary between play and work.
