Encapsulation (computer programming)

In object-oriented programming (OOP), encapsulation refers to the bundling of data with the methods that operate on that data, or the restricting of direct access to some of an object's components. Encapsulation is used to hide the values or state of a structured data object inside a class, preventing direct access to them by clients in a way that could expose hidden implementation details or violate state invariants maintained by the methods.

Information hiding

Publicly accessible methods are generally provided to access or modify the state more abstractly in a well-defined way. This motivates the use of accessor and mutator methods ("getters" and "setters") and access specifiers such as public, private, and protected in languages like Java, C++, and C#. The principle descends from David Parnas's work on information hiding in the 1970s: modules should be designed so that their internal decisions are hidden from the rest of the system, allowing them to be changed without affecting clients.

Benefits and criticism

Encapsulation supports abstraction, modularity, and the enforcement of invariants, and it localises the impact of design changes. Critics note that indiscriminate use of getters and setters can recreate the exposure encapsulation was meant to avoid, and that rigid encapsulation can complicate testing and flexible composition; alternative designs therefore expose behaviour-rich interfaces rather than data accessors.
