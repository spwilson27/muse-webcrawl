Moon type

Moon type (also called the Moon System of Embossed Reading) is a system of raised, embossed characters — a tactile alphabet — designed for blind and partially sighted people to read by touch. It was invented in 1845 by Dr William Moon of Brighton, England. Unlike braille, which uses abstract dot patterns, Moon type is based on simplified, raised outlines derived from ordinary printed letters, which makes it easier for people who lost their sight later in life and already know the shapes of letters, and for people whose fingertip sensitivity is reduced.

Design

The Moon alphabet consists of embossed lines and curves on thick paper, with characters roughly the size of ordinary print. Only parts of each letter's shape are used, producing a simplified but still alphabetic system. Text is read in a boustrophedon-like arrangement in some editions, though modern Moon printing generally uses consistent left-to-right lines with alternating direction markers. Numbers and contractions are represented with additional signs.

History and use

William Moon, who lost his sight at 21, devised the system to teach blind people, including a young girl he tutored, and founded a printing society in Brighton that produced books, maps, and sheet music in Moon type. The system spread through the British Empire in the 19th century. Although braille became the dominant tactile reading system worldwide because it is faster to read and write and more compact, Moon type survives for readers who learn touch reading late or who cannot master braille; in the United Kingdom, the RNIB continues to lend Moon books, and societies in several countries maintain small libraries.

Comparison with braille

Moon characters are much larger than braille cells, so Moon books are bulky and expensive to produce, and reading speeds are lower. Its advantages are ease of learning for the newly blind and legibility for those with numb fingers, for example from diabetes. Moon type is consequently used mainly by older readers.
