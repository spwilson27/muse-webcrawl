Fire ecology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Many ecosystems depend on periodic fire for regeneration.

Fire ecology is a scientific discipline concerned with natural processes involving fire in an ecosystem and the ecological effects, the interactions between fire and the abiotic and biotic components of an ecosystem, and the role of fire as an ecosystem process. Many ecosystems, particularly prairie, savanna, chaparral, and coniferous forests, have evolved with fire as an essential contributor to habitat vitality and renewal.

Fire adaptations

Many plants have evolved adaptations to fire: thick bark protects trunks; serotinous cones (as in some pines) open only after exposure to heat, releasing seeds onto ash-enriched soil; some species resprout from root crowns or lignotubers; and smoke chemicals such as karrikins stimulate germination of dormant seeds. Fire regimes are described by their frequency, intensity, severity, and seasonality.

Fire suppression and management

Decades of aggressive fire suppression in some regions allowed fuel to accumulate, contributing to unusually severe wildfires. Modern land management therefore uses prescribed burning and managed wildfire to restore natural fire regimes and reduce hazard.
