The House of Saud (Arabic: آل سعود, ʾĀl Suʿūd) is the ruling royal family of Saudi Arabia. It is composed of the descendants of Muhammad bin Saud, founder of the Emirate of Diriyah, known as the first Saudi state, in 1727, and his brothers. The family is estimated to number many thousands of members, of whom a few hundred hold governing authority.

Three Saudi states

In 1744 Muhammad bin Saud allied with the religious reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, a pact that still shapes the Saudi state. The first Saudi state (Emirate of Diriyah, 1727–1818) was destroyed by Ottoman-Egyptian forces; a second, the Emirate of Nejd (1824–1891), fell to the rival Al Rashid. Abdulaziz (Ibn Saud) retook Riyadh in 1902 and by conquest and diplomacy unified the Nejd and the Hejaz, proclaiming the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932.

Succession and power

Since Abdulaziz's death in 1953, the throne has passed among his sons — Saud, Faisal, Khalid, Fahd, Abdullah, and Salman — a horizontal system of succession governed in part by seniority and family consensus; the Allegiance Council was created in 2006 to formalise the process. In 2017 King Salman appointed his son Mohammed bin Salman as crown prince, signalling the move to a new generation. Members of the family hold the senior ministries, governorships, and command positions, and the discovery of oil from 1938 onward gave the state — and the family — enormous wealth.

Criticism

The family's absolute rule, its promotion of the Wahhabi interpretation of Islam, its human-rights record, and the opulence of senior princes have drawn sustained international criticism, while its supporters credit it with unifying Arabia and underwriting stability in the Gulf.
