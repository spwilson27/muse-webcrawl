Bubble car

Bubble car is a term used for small, economical microcars, usually produced in Europe in the years following the Second World War, characterised by their rounded, egg-like body shape and often a single door at the front of the vehicle. The term is most associated with the Italian-designed Iso Isetta and its licence-built versions, notably the BMW Isetta, but is also applied to cars such as the Messerschmitt KR200, the Heinkel Kabine and the Fuldamobil.

History

Bubble cars emerged from the austerity of post-war Europe, when fuel was expensive, raw materials were scarce, and many people could afford neither a full-sized car nor the running costs of one. Aircraft manufacturers such as Messerschmitt and Heinkel, barred from building aircraft, turned their expertise in lightweight construction and canopy-style enclosures to tiny road vehicles. In Britain, three-wheeled bubble cars were attractive because they could be driven on a motorcycle licence and were taxed at motorcycle rates.

The Isetta, originally designed by the Italian firm Iso and launched in 1953, was built under licence in several countries. BMW's version, produced from 1955, became one of the best-selling single-cylinder cars in the world and is credited with helping the company survive a financial crisis. The entire front of the car, including the steering column and wheel, swung open as the door.

Decline and legacy

The arrival of the BMC Mini in 1959 — a full four-seater at a price not much higher than a bubble car — together with rising prosperity, caused the bubble car market to collapse in the early 1960s. Surviving examples are now prized collectors' items, and the format has been echoed in modern city cars and electric microcars such as the Microlino, a modern electric homage to the Isetta.
