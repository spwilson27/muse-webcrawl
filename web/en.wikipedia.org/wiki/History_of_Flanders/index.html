History of Flanders

The history of Flanders concerns the region in the low countries of northwestern Europe that corresponds roughly to today's Flemish Region of Belgium, French Flanders in France, and part of Dutch Zeelandic Flanders. For much of the Middle Ages the County of Flanders was one of the wealthiest regions of Europe, built on the cloth trade of cities such as Bruges, Ghent and Ypres.

Early history

The area was inhabited by the Celtic Belgae and conquered by Rome. After the Frankish invasions, the Treaty of Verdun (843) split the territory between West and East Francia along the Scheldt. The County of Flanders emerged in the 9th century under Baldwin I, expanding from its base around Bruges as a march against Viking raids.

Medieval prosperity

From the 11th to the 13th centuries, Flemish towns grew rich importing English wool and exporting finished cloth across Europe. The cities gained extensive self-government, often clashing with their counts and with France: at the Battle of the Golden Spurs (1302), Flemish urban militias famously defeated the knights of France near Kortrijk. Internal strife — including the revolts of Jacob van Artevelde's Ghent — marked the 14th century, before Flanders passed to the Dukes of Burgundy in 1384.

Habsburg rule and revolt

Under the Burgundian and then Habsburg Netherlands, the economic centre shifted towards Antwerp and later Amsterdam. Flanders became a battleground of the Dutch Revolt and the Eighty Years' War; the southern provinces remained Spanish (later Austrian) while the north became the independent Dutch Republic. Southern Flanders was also repeatedly invaded by France, which annexed its western part (French Flanders) in the 17th century.

Modern era

After 1815 Flanders was part of the United Kingdom of the Netherlands, then of independent Belgium (1830). Although Dutch-speaking Flemings were the majority, the Belgian state operated in French, giving rise to the Flemish Movement, which gradually won language equality (1898), Dutch-language education and administration, and ultimately the federalisation of Belgium. In the First World War the Flanders front around Ypres became synonymous with trench warfare. Today the Flemish Region and Flemish Community form one of Belgium's federal entities, with Brussels as capital.
