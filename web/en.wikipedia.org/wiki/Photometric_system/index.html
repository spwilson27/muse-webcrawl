Photometric system

In astronomy, a photometric system is a set of well-defined passbands (or optical filters), with a known sensitivity to incident radiation. Each passband is fully characterized by its mean wavelength, width and shape. Photometric systems provide a calibrated method of comparing the brightness of objects measured by different telescopes or instruments at different times.

Transmission curves of a typical set of photometric filters.

Common systems

  • The Johnson–Morgan (UBV) system, introduced in the 1950s, measures ultraviolet, blue and visual bands and was later extended to red and infrared (UBVRI).
  • The Strömgren system (uvbyβ) uses four narrow- to intermediate-band filters plus an Hβ filter, designed for classifying stars and measuring interstellar reddening.
  • The Sloan Digital Sky Survey system (u′g′r′i′z′) uses five broad bands in the near-ultraviolet to near-infrared and underlies much modern survey photometry.
  • The Geneva, Vilnius, Walraven and other systems were designed for specific classification or temperature/reddening diagnostics.

Calibration

A photometric system is defined by a set of standard stars whose magnitudes in each band are known precisely. Vega long served as the primary standard, defining magnitude zero across bands, although modern systems increasingly use absolute flux calibration based on white dwarf model atmospheres or AB magnitudes, in which a source with constant flux density per unit frequency has the same magnitude in every band.

Colour indices

Differences between magnitudes in two bands, such as B−V, are called colour indices. Because hotter stars radiate relatively more blue light, colour indices measure temperature largely independently of distance, and they are also affected by interstellar reddening, which systems such as Strömgren were designed to disentangle.
