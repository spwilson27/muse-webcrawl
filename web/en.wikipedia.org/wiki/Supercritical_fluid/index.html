Supercritical fluid

Above its critical point, a substance becomes a supercritical fluid with properties between those of a gas and a liquid.

A supercritical fluid (SCF) is any substance at a temperature and pressure above its critical point, where distinct liquid and gas phases do not exist, but below the pressure required to compress it into a solid. It can effuse through porous solids like a gas and dissolve materials like a liquid.

Properties

In the supercritical region, small changes in pressure or temperature produce large changes in density, allowing the solvating power of the fluid to be finely tuned. Supercritical fluids have low viscosities and high diffusion coefficients compared with liquids, which makes them effective solvents and reaction media.

Applications

The most widely used supercritical fluid is carbon dioxide, whose critical point (31.1 °C and 73.8 bar) is easily accessible. Supercritical CO2 is used to decaffeinate coffee, extract hops and flavours, and as a dry-cleaning and industrial solvent. Supercritical water is used in waste oxidation and in supercritical steam cycles for power generation. Supercritical fluids also find uses in chromatography, nanoparticle production, and enhanced oil recovery.

Natural occurrence

Supercritical fluids occur naturally in hydrothermal vents at the ocean floor and in the atmospheres of gas giant planets, where hydrogen and helium exist under supercritical conditions.
