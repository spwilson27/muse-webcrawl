Lactate dehydrogenase

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH or LD) is an enzyme found in nearly all living cells. It catalyzes the reversible interconversion of pyruvate and lactate, with the simultaneous conversion of NADH and NAD+: pyruvate + NADH + H+ ⇌ lactate + NAD+. In animals it is a tetramer composed of subunits encoded by the LDHA (M, muscle) and LDHB (H, heart) genes, giving five isozymes (LDH-1 through LDH-5) with tissue-specific distributions.

Function

LDH is essential for cellular respiration. When oxygen is scarce — for example in vigorously exercising muscle — it regenerates NAD+ so glycolysis can continue, producing lactate. In the Cori cycle, lactate travels to the liver, where LDH converts it back to pyruvate for gluconeogenesis. Cancer cells frequently overexpress LDH-A, supporting the aerobic glycolysis known as the Warburg effect.

Clinical significance

Because LDH is abundant inside cells, its release into the blood signals tissue damage. Elevated serum LDH is a nonspecific marker used in assessing hemolysis, myocardial and liver injury, some cancers (especially lymphomas and germ cell tumors), and as a prognostic marker; patterns of the five isozymes historically helped localize the source of injury. Inherited deficiency of LDH-A causes exercise intolerance and muscle breakdown.
