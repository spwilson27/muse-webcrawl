Union bound

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In probability theory, the union bound (also called Boole's inequality, after George Boole) states that the probability that at least one of a collection of events occurs is no greater than the sum of the individual probabilities:

P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ … ∪ Aₙ) ≤ P(A₁) + P(A₂) + … + P(Aₙ)

The inequality holds for any events, dependent or independent, and for countable collections as well as finite ones. It is an equality when the events are pairwise disjoint; otherwise the sum over-counts the overlaps, so the bound can be loose — even useless if the sum exceeds 1 — yet its simplicity and lack of assumptions make it one of the most frequently used tools in the probabilistic method, randomised algorithms, coding theory, and statistical physics.

Uses

The union bound is the engine of countless existence proofs: if the sum of the probabilities of all "bad events" is less than 1, then with positive probability none of them occur, so a good object must exist. Erdős-style probabilistic arguments, analyses of random graphs, and error bounds for randomised algorithms typically proceed this way. In multiple hypothesis testing, the Bonferroni correction is essentially the union bound applied to family-wise error control. Tighter estimates are available from the inclusion–exclusion principle, of which the union bound is the first-order truncation; alternating truncations yield the Bonferroni inequalities.
