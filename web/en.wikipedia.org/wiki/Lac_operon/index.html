The lactose operon (lac operon) is an operon required for the transport and metabolism of lactose in Escherichia coli and many other enteric bacteria. It is the classic model of gene regulation in prokaryotes, worked out by François Jacob and Jacques Monod (with Arthur Pardee, in the "PaJaMo" experiment) around 1961, earning Jacob and Monod the Nobel Prize in 1965.

Structure

The operon contains three structural genes transcribed as a single polycistronic mRNA: lacZ, encoding β-galactosidase, which cleaves lactose into glucose and galactose (and converts some lactose to the inducer allolactose); lacY, encoding lactose permease, a membrane transporter that imports lactose; and lacA, encoding a transacetylase of less clear necessity. Upstream lie a promoter and an operator site, and nearby the separate lacI gene encoding the repressor.

Regulation

The system integrates two signals. First, negative control: in the absence of lactose, LacI repressor binds the operator and blocks transcription. When lactose is present, its isomer allolactose binds the repressor, which detaches from the DNA, permitting expression. Second, positive control (catabolite repression): full expression requires the activator CAP bound to cyclic AMP; cAMP levels are high only when glucose is absent. Thus the operon is strongly expressed only when lactose is available AND glucose is not — accounting for diauxic growth, in which E. coli consumes glucose before switching to lactose. Mutants in lacI or the operator that abolish repression (constitutive mutants, e.g. lacI⁻ and lacOᶜ) were key evidence for the model. LacZ's β-galactosidase became a ubiquitous reporter gene (producing blue colonies with X-gal on indicator plates), and the inducible lac promoter system (with the gratuitous inducer IPTG) underpins much of recombinant protein expression technology.
