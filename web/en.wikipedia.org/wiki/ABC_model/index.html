ABC model

The ABC model of flower development is a framework in plant developmental biology that explains how combinations of three classes of homeotic genes specify the four types of organs found in a typical flower: sepals, petals, stamens and carpels. The model was formulated by Enrico Coen and Elliot Meyerowitz in 1991, drawing on genetic studies of floral mutants in the model organisms Arabidopsis thaliana and Antirrhinum majus (snapdragon).

The model

A flower develops in concentric rings, or whorls, from the outside in: sepals (whorl 1), petals (whorl 2), stamens (whorl 3) and carpels (whorl 4). In the ABC model, organ identity in each whorl is determined by which class of gene activity is present:

WhorlGene classes activeOrgan formed
1ASepals
2A + BPetals
3B + CStamens
4CCarpels

Class A and class C activities are mutually repressive: loss of A function lets C expand into the outer whorls, converting sepals to carpels and petals to stamens; loss of C function produces the reverse pattern and indeterminate flowers with repeated whorls of sepals and petals, as in classic "double flower" ornamentals. In Arabidopsis, the A-class genes include APETALA1 and APETALA2, the B-class genes APETALA3 and PISTILLATA, and the C-class gene AGAMOUS. Most of these encode MADS-box transcription factors.

Extensions

Later work added D-class genes, required for ovule identity, and E-class genes (the SEPALLATA genes) needed in all whorls for proper floral organ formation, leading to the extended ABCE model and the "floral quartet" model, in which tetramers of MADS-box proteins specify organ identity. The ABC model remains a foundational example of combinatorial gene regulation in development and has guided research into the evolution of flowers across angiosperms.
