Shakers

The Shakers, formally the United Society of Believers in Christ's Second Appearing, are a millenarian Christian sect founded in England around 1747 and organized in the United States in the 1780s under the leadership of Ann Lee, known to her followers as Mother Ann. They were popularly called "Shaking Quakers" or "Shakers" because of the ecstatic dancing and trembling that formed part of their worship.

The Shakers are known for their commitment to celibacy, communal living, gender and racial equality, pacifism, and simple, functional craftsmanship. Their villages were models of orderly, prosperous communal life in the nineteenth century, and Shaker furniture, architecture, boxes, and inventions — including the circular saw and the clothespin, among designs attributed to them — remain influential in American design.

History

Ann Lee and eight followers emigrated from Manchester, England, to New York in 1774. After persecution and Lee's death in 1784, her successors established the first communal village at New Lebanon, New York, and the movement spread across New England, the Midwest, and the South. Membership peaked at roughly 6,000 around the 1840s. Because Shakers practiced celibacy, the movement depended on converts and adopted orphans, and it declined steadily after the Civil War as industrialization and changing religious tastes drew potential members away.

Beliefs and practices

Shakers taught that God is both male and female, that Christ's second appearing came in the person of Mother Ann, and that salvation required confession of sin, celibacy, separation from the world, and communal ownership of goods. Worship featured marching, dancing, and singing; the best-known Shaker hymn is "Simple Gifts" (1848), later adapted by Aaron Copland in Appalachian Spring. Their guiding maxim was "Hands to work, and hearts to God."

Legacy

By the twenty-first century only one active Shaker village remained, at Sabbathday Lake in Maine, with a handful of members. Shaker museums and preserved villages, including Hancock Shaker Village in Massachusetts and Shaker Village at Pleasant Hill in Kentucky, keep the movement's material culture and history alive.
