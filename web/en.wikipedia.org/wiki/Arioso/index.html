Arioso

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An arioso lies between recitative and aria in lyrical intensity.

In classical music, arioso (Italian, "airy") is a category of solo vocal piece, usually occurring in an opera or oratorio, falling between recitative and aria in style. Literally, arioso is a melodic form of speech, a "songlike" recitative—more melodic than recitative proper, but less formally structured and less expansive than a full aria.

Characteristics

An arioso typically preserves the free rhythm and text-setting of recitative while introducing a more lyrical, expressive melodic line and a richer orchestral accompaniment. Ariosos are usually shorter than arias and do not follow closed forms such as the da capo design. The term was used loosely by composers from the Baroque period onward; in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries "arioso" often simply indicated a passage of recitative sung in a more measured, expressive manner.

Examples

Famous examples include the arioso passages in Handel's and Bach's sacred works, the arioso "Comfort ye" expansions in Messiah, and numerous short lyrical solos in nineteenth-century opera. In the twentieth century, Benjamin Britten used "arioso" explicitly for some movements, and the style influenced through-composed vocal writing generally. The related term arietta denotes a short, simple aria.
