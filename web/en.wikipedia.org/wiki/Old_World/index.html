Old World

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The Old World: Afro-Eurasia, the parts of the world known to Europeans before 1492.

The Old World is a term for the parts of the world known to Europeans before the voyages of Christopher Columbus — principally Europe, Asia and Africa, collectively also called Afro-Eurasia — as opposed to the New World, the Americas (and later Oceania). The distinction originated in the early sixteenth century, after European contact with the Americas overturned the classical tripartite division of the world among the continents known since antiquity.

Usage

The terms survive mainly in historical, biological and commercial contexts rather than as precise geography:

  • Biogeography and agriculture: "Old World" species are those native to Europe, Asia and Africa — e.g. Old World monkeys, Old World vultures — and "Old World crops" and livestock (wheat, barley, cattle, sheep) are contrasted with New World ones (maize, potatoes, tomatoes, turkeys) in accounts of the Columbian exchange.
  • Wine: "Old World wine" denotes wines from the traditional growing regions of Europe and the Mediterranean, typically associated with terroir-driven styles and appellation rules, in contrast to "New World wine" from the Americas, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa.
  • History: the term is used loosely for the Eastern Hemisphere civilisations before, or apart from, contact with the Americas.

Depending on context, the Old World may be taken to include Australasia or not: biogeographically, Australia's fauna was unknown to pre-Columbian Europe but belongs with the "eastern" landmasses in some classifications. Because the terms reflect a European vantage point, modern scholarship increasingly prefers neutral alternatives such as Afro-Eurasia and the Americas.
