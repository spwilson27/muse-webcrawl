Drug design

Drug design, often referred to as rational drug design or simply rational design, is the inventive process of finding new medications based on the knowledge of a biological target. The drug is most commonly an organic small molecule that activates or inhibits the function of a biomolecule such as a protein, which in turn results in a therapeutic benefit to the patient.

Background

Historically, drugs were discovered by identifying the active ingredient from traditional remedies or by serendipitous discovery. Later chemical libraries of synthetic small molecules, natural products, or extracts were screened in intact cells or whole organisms to identify substances that have a desirable therapeutic effect in a process known as classical pharmacology. Human proteins whose genes are revealed by genomics now provide many new targets, and drug design seeks to exploit structural and mechanistic knowledge of those targets.

Approaches

Ligand-based drug design

Ligand-based (or indirect) design relies on knowledge of other molecules that bind to the target of interest. From these, a pharmacophore model — the ensemble of steric and electronic features necessary for interaction — or a quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) can be derived and used to design new ligands.

Structure-based drug design

Structure-based (or direct) design relies on knowledge of the three-dimensional structure of the biological target, obtained by methods such as X-ray crystallography, NMR spectroscopy, cryo-electron microscopy, or homology modelling. Candidate molecules are docked computationally into the binding site and scored for predicted affinity. Landmark successes include the HIV protease inhibitors and imatinib.

Stages

The design process is iterative: a hit compound identified by screening is optimised into a lead with improved potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetic properties (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion — ADME), while minimising toxicity. Rules of thumb such as Lipinski's rule of five guide the search for "drug-like" molecules. Optimised candidates then pass through preclinical development and clinical trials before approval.

Computer-aided drug design

Computational methods — molecular docking, virtual screening of libraries containing millions of compounds, molecular dynamics simulation, and free-energy calculations — are now standard. Machine-learning models, including deep generative models that propose novel structures, have become a major growth area since the late 2010s.
