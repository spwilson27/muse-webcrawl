Organolithium reagent

Examples: n-butyllithium, tert-butyllithium, methyllithium, phenyllithium

Uses: Strong bases; nucleophiles; polymerisation initiators

Hazards: Pyrophoric; reacts violently with water

An organolithium reagent is an organometallic compound containing a carbon–lithium bond. Because carbon and lithium differ greatly in electronegativity, the bond is strongly polarised toward carbon, making organolithium compounds powerful nucleophiles and among the strongest bases used in synthesis. They are indispensable reagents in organic chemistry and in the manufacture of elastomers.

Common reagents

The commercially dominant reagent is n-butyllithium, supplied in hexanes; sec-butyllithium and the pyrophoric tert-butyllithium are stronger bases, and methyllithium and phenyllithium are common nucleophiles. Lithium diisopropylamide (LDA) is a bulky, non-nucleophilic lithium amide base prepared from diisopropylamine and butyllithium.

Structure and preparation

Organolithiums aggregate in solution and the solid state into tetramers, hexamers and other clusters bridged by carbon, which moderates their reactivity; donor solvents and additives such as TMEDA break up the aggregates. They are prepared industrially by reacting organic chlorides with lithium metal, and in the laboratory by halogen–lithium exchange or directed ortho metalation.

Reactions

Organolithiums deprotonate weakly acidic C–H bonds, add to carbonyl compounds much like Grignard reagents (often more aggressively), and undergo exchange reactions used to generate functionalised intermediates. Anionic polymerisation initiated by butyllithium produces styrene-butadiene rubbers and thermoplastic elastomers on a large industrial scale.

Handling

Concentrated solutions ignite spontaneously in air and react violently with water and carbon dioxide, so they are manipulated under inert gas with syringe and cannula techniques and titrated before use.
