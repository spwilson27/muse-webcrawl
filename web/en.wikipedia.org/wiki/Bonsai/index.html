Bonsai

Bonsai (Japanese: 盆栽, "tray planting") is the Japanese art of growing and shaping miniature trees in containers. Using cultivation techniques such as pruning, root reduction, wiring and grafting, practitioners produce small trees that mimic the shape and proportions of mature, full-sized trees in nature. Bonsai are not genetically dwarfed plants: any tree species can be used, and the illusion of age and scale comes from technique and artistic styling.

History

The art derives from the Chinese practice of penjing, which reached Japan by the Kamakura period (1185–1333), where it was refined under the influence of Zen Buddhism. During the Edo period bonsai became a widespread hobby and art form, and after Japan opened to the world in the 19th century, exhibitions and exports introduced bonsai to the West. It is now practised worldwide, with major public collections in Japan, the United States, China and Europe. Some famous specimens are centuries old; a ficus at the Crespi museum in Italy is claimed to be over 1,000 years old.

Techniques and aesthetics

Growers shape trees by pruning branches and roots, wiring trunks and limbs into position, defoliating to reduce leaf size, and creating deadwood features (jin and shari) that suggest great age. Trees are kept small by confinement in shallow pots and regular root pruning; they require attentive watering, feeding and repotting, and most species must be kept outdoors. Classical styles include the formal upright (chokkan), informal upright (moyogi), slanting (shakan), cascade (kengai), windswept (fukinagashi) and forest plantings (yose-ue). Sizes range from a few centimetres (shito) to over a metre.

Cultural significance

In Japan, bonsai embodies ideals of harmony, balance and the beauty of imperfection associated with wabi-sabi. A well-kept bonsai is a living artwork that changes with the seasons, and prized trees pass between generations of caretakers.
