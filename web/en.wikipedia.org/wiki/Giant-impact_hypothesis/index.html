Giant-impact hypothesis

The giant-impact hypothesis — sometimes called the Theia impact — is the prevailing scientific hypothesis for the formation of the Moon. It proposes that, roughly 4.5 billion years ago, in the Hadean eon, the young Earth collided with a Mars-sized planetesimal named Theia. Debris from the collision, consisting largely of material from the two bodies' mantles, was thrown into orbit around Earth and accreted to form the Moon.

Evidence

The hypothesis explains several otherwise puzzling facts: the Moon's relatively small iron core and low density (because it formed mainly from mantle material rather than iron-rich core material); the near-identity of oxygen isotope ratios in Earth and Moon rocks (suggesting thorough mixing of the two bodies' material); the high angular momentum of the Earth–Moon system; and the Moon's depletion in volatile elements, consistent with formation from hot, vaporised rock. Samples returned by the Apollo missions were decisive in establishing the Moon's composition and hence in favouring the impact model over older ideas such as fission, capture, or co-accretion.

Details and open questions

In the canonical model, Theia struck Earth at an oblique angle at relatively low velocity, was largely destroyed, and its core merged with Earth's. Computer simulations have explored variants — high-energy, high-angular-momentum impacts producing a doughnut-shaped vapour structure called a synestia; multiple smaller impacts; and a nearly head-on collision between two similar-sized bodies — each attempting to reproduce the isotopic similarity and the system's angular momentum simultaneously. The exact timing, impactor mass, and impact geometry remain active research questions.
