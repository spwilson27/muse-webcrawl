Topos

In mathematics, a topos (UK: /ˈtɒpɒs/, US: /ˈtoʊpoʊs, ˈtoʊpɒs/; plural topoi or toposes) is a category that behaves like the category of sheaves of sets on a topological space (or more generally: on a site). Topoi behave much like the category of sets and possess a notion of localization; they are a direct generalization of point-set topology. The Grothendieck topoi find applications in algebraic geometry; the more general elementary topoi are used in logic.

History

The concept originated in the algebraic geometry of the 1960s, where Alexander Grothendieck and his collaborators, seeking a general notion of "space" for étale cohomology, introduced categories of sheaves on sites as generalized spaces. F. William Lawvere and Myles Tierney later isolated the elementary (first-order) axiomatization of such categories.

Grothendieck topoi

A Grothendieck topos is a category equivalent to the category of sheaves on some site. The category of sheaves on a topological space is the motivating example. Grothendieck topoi can be characterized intrinsically by Giraud's axioms: roughly, a category with all small limits and colimits, a small generating set, and well-behaved disjoint coproducts and equivalence relations.

Elementary topoi

An elementary topos is a category with finite limits, cartesian closed structure, and a subobject classifier — an object Ω equipped with a morphism true : 1 → Ω such that every monomorphism arises uniquely as a pullback of true. The subobject classifier plays the role of the two-element set {0, 1} in the category of sets, but in a general topos Ω is a Heyting algebra rather than a Boolean algebra, giving topos logic its intuitionistic character.

Topos theory and logic

Every elementary topos has an internal language (the Mitchell–Bénabou language) in which one can reason "as if" the objects were sets, except that the logic is intuitionistic. This makes topoi a bridge between geometry and logic: geometric morphisms between topoi correspond to interpretations of theories, and classifying topoi represent mathematical theories much as classifying spaces represent groups.

Examples

  • The category Set of sets and functions.
  • Sheaf categories Sh(X) for a topological space X, and more generally for a site.
  • Presheaf categories on small categories, including the effective topos of recursive realizability.
  • Categories of G-sets for a group G.
