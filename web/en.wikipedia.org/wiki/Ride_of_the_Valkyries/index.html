Ride of the Valkyries

Native nameWalkürenritt
ComposerRichard Wagner
WorkDie Walküre, WWV 86B (Act 3 opening)

The "Ride of the Valkyries" (German: Walkürenritt) is the popular name for the beginning of Act 3 of Die Walküre, the second of the four operas of Richard Wagner's Der Ring des Nibelungen. The music depicts the Valkyries — warrior maidens who carry fallen heroes to Valhalla — gathering on a mountaintop amid storm and lightning, greeting one another with battle cries. Built on a galloping ostinato in B minor and the Valkyries' brass leitmotif, it is among the most famous excerpts in all orchestral music, and is frequently performed and recorded separately from the opera.

Music

Wagner wrote the first version of the leitmotif on 23 July 1851 and drafted the full Ride in 1854–56. In the opera, a brief orchestral introduction of swirling figures and storm music precedes the arrival of eight Valkyries (Gerhilde, Ortlinde, Waltraute, Schwertleite, Helmwige, Siegrune, Grimgerde and Rossweisse), whose overlapping cries of "Hojotoho!" ride over the galloping accompaniment. Brünnhilde then enters carrying the heroine Sieglinde, setting the act's drama in motion. The purely orchestral version, arranged by Wagner for concert use, lasts about five minutes.

Performance history

Die Walküre was first performed at the National Theatre, Munich, on 26 June 1870, against Wagner's wishes; the complete Ring followed at Bayreuth in 1876. The Ride was an early audience favourite and featured in early phonograph recordings and in the young medium of film; Wagner himself disliked the piece being treated as a detached showpiece.

In popular culture

The Ride's association with aerial warfare was fixed by Francis Ford Coppola's Apocalypse Now (1979), in which helicopters attack a Vietnamese village while the music blares from loudspeakers — one of cinema's most famous sequences. The music had earlier accompanied aerial scenes in D. W. Griffith's The Birth of a Nation (1915) and numerous wartime newsreels and cartoons; Elmer Fudd sings his own version ("Kill the wabbit") in the Chuck Jones cartoon What's Opera, Doc? (1957). The piece remains a staple shorthand for dramatic assault, heroism and parody alike.
