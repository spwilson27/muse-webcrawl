Common assault

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Common assault is an offence under the common law of England and Wales, charged under section 39 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988, consisting of an assault or a battery that causes no injury, or only very minor injury. It is the least serious offence against the person in the charging hierarchy below assault occasioning actual bodily harm (ABH) and grievous bodily harm (GBH).

Elements

Following Fagan v Metropolitan Police Commissioner and later authorities, an assault is an act that causes the victim to apprehend immediate unlawful violence, done intentionally or recklessly; a battery is the actual application of unlawful force, however slight, such as a push or slap. Words alone can amount to an assault, and an assault can be committed through silence, as in R v Ireland, which also confirmed that psychiatric injury may amount to bodily harm for more serious charges.

Procedure and sentence

Common assault is a summary-only offence, triable in the magistrates' court, with a maximum sentence of six months' imprisonment (extended in specified circumstances, for example assaults on emergency workers under the Assaults on Emergency Workers (Offences) Act 2018). Section 40 of the Criminal Justice Act 1988 allows a count of common assault to be added to an indictment as an alternative verdict. Since 2019, common assault of an emergency worker is triable either way with a higher maximum.

Racially aggravated form

Section 29 of the Crime and Disorder Act 1998 creates a racially or religiously aggravated form of common assault, triable either way and carrying a maximum of two years' imprisonment.
