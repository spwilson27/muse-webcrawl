KingdomAnimalia
PhylumPlatyhelminthes
ClassTrematoda
SubclassesAspidogastrea; Digenea

The liver fluke Fasciola hepatica, a well-known trematode.

Trematoda is a class of flatworms known as trematodes, commonly called flukes. They are obligate internal parasites with complex life cycles involving a molluscan intermediate host. Some 18,000 to 24,000 species have been described. Trematodes are flattened oval or worm-like animals, usually a few centimetres long, equipped with one or two suckers for attachment within their hosts.

Life cycle

The great majority of trematodes belong to the subclass Digenea. Their life cycles typically pass through a snail first intermediate host, in which asexual reproduction produces free-swimming cercariae, and a vertebrate definitive host, in which the adults reproduce sexually. Many species require a second intermediate host, such as a fish, crab or plant surface, on which the infective metacercariae encyst.

Human disease

Trematodes infect an estimated 200 million people worldwide. Important human parasites include the blood flukes Schistosoma species, which cause schistosomiasis; the liver flukes Clonorchis sinensis, Opisthorchis and Fasciola hepatica; the intestinal fluke Fasciolopsis buski; and the lung fluke Paragonimus westermani. Infections are typically acquired by drinking contaminated water or eating raw or undercooked fish, crustaceans or aquatic plants. Most fluke infections can be treated with praziquantel; fascioliasis responds best to triclabendazole.

Economics

Liver flukes of sheep and cattle cause major livestock losses, damaging livers and reducing milk and meat production, while fish-borne flukes affect aquaculture across Asia.
