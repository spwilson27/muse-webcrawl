Edwin Sutherland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Edwin Hardin Sutherland (13 August 1883 – 11 October 1950) was an American sociologist and criminologist, author of the field's defining textbook (Criminology, 1924, later editions with Donald Cressey) and of the theory of differential association: criminal behaviour is learned, in interaction with others, through acquiring an excess of definitions favourable to violation of law over definitions unfavourable to it — the most influential learning theory in criminology, set out in nine propositions in the 1939 edition of his Principles of Criminology.

Sutherland also coined the term white-collar crime in his 1939 presidential address to the American Sociological Society (published as a monograph in 1949), arguing that crime by persons of respectability and high status in the course of their occupation was both pervasive and systematically excluded from crime statistics and theory — a challenge to poverty-based explanations of crime that founded the study of corporate and occupational offending. As president of the American Sociological Society (1939) and the dominant figure of the Chicago-influenced sociological school at Indiana University, he trained the generation that built modern criminology.
