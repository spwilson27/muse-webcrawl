Media multitasking
Using several media streams at once
FieldCognitive psychology, media studies
Key measureMedia Multitasking Index (Ophir, Nass & Wagner, 2009)

Media multitasking is the practice of engaging with multiple media streams simultaneously — for example, watching television while browsing social media on a phone, or listening to a podcast while answering email. The term gained prominence with research in the 2000s and 2010s on the cognitive consequences of dividing attention among media, particularly among adolescents and young adults.

Research

The 2009 Stanford study

A widely cited 2009 study by Eyal Ophir, Clifford Nass and Anthony Wagner at Stanford University compared "heavy" and "light" media multitaskers, identified using a Media Multitasking Index (MMI) based on self-reported simultaneous media use. Counterintuitively, heavy media multitaskers performed worse on tests of task-switching and resistance to distraction, suggesting that chronic multitaskers may be more, not less, susceptible to interference from irrelevant stimuli. Clifford Nass summarised the finding by saying that heavy multitaskers "are lousy at everything that's related to multitasking".

Subsequent findings

Later research has associated heavy media multitasking with poorer performance on some working-memory and long-term-memory tasks, greater impulsivity and sensation-seeking, and — in some studies of adolescents — differences in brain structure and in social and emotional functioning. The direction of causation remains debated: multitasking may impair attention, or people with weaker attentional control may be drawn to multitasking. Meta-analyses find small but consistent negative associations with cognitive performance, and experiments show that the mere presence of a smartphone can reduce available cognitive capacity.

In education and work

Studies in classrooms find that multitasking on laptops during lectures lowers comprehension — including for nearby students who can see the screens. Organisations and educators commonly recommend "single-tasking" strategies, notification management and scheduled media use as countermeasures.
