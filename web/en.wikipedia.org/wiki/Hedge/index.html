A hedge or hedgerow is a line of closely spaced shrubs and sometimes trees, planted and trained to form a barrier or to mark the boundary of an area, such as between neighbouring properties or along fields and roads. Hedges used to divide farmland into fields are properly called hedgerows, and often include a ditch and bank.

History

Some hedgerows in Britain date from the Bronze and Iron Ages; many were planted during the Parliamentary Inclosure Acts of the 18th and 19th centuries. Hooper's rule offers a rough dating method: the number of woody species in a 30-yard stretch approximates the hedge's age in centuries. Enormous lengths of hedgerow were removed after the Second World War to enlarge fields for mechanised agriculture, a major driver of farmland biodiversity loss; protective regulations followed in 1997.

Ecology and uses

Hedgerows are vital wildlife corridors, providing food, shelter and nesting sites for birds, small mammals, and insects, and reducing soil erosion and wind speed. Traditional hedges are maintained by hedgelaying — cutting part-way through the stems and bending them over — rather than mechanical flailing. In gardens, formal hedges of yew, box, privet or beech are shaped by clipping, and hedges form the walls of mazes and topiary. The word is also used figuratively in finance, where "hedging" means offsetting risk.
