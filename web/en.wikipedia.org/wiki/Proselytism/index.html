Proselytism

Proselytism is the policy of attempting to convert people's religious or political beliefs. Carried out historically by religions, it can also refer to other types of persuasion, including secular forms. The term proselyte derives from the Greek prosēlytos (προσήλυτος), meaning "one who has come to a place", and was used in the Septuagint for a convert to Judaism.

By religion

Christianity

Many Christians consider it their obligation to follow the Great Commission of Jesus, recorded in the Gospel of Matthew, to "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19). Missionary work is the most visible form of Christian proselytism, ranging from evangelism campaigns to long-term missions providing education and medical care alongside preaching.

Islam

In Islam, inviting people to the faith is known as da'wah. The Qur'an instructs believers to invite others "with wisdom and good instruction" (16:125), and classical Islamic teaching holds that there is "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).

Judaism

Historically, Judaism actively sought converts in the Hellenistic period, but rabbinic Judaism generally does not seek converts, though it accepts those who undertake a formal conversion process.

Legal status

The right to attempt to persuade others is protected in international human rights law as a manifestation of freedom of religion or belief; however, many states restrict proselytism, particularly toward minors or by foreign missionaries. The European Court of Human Rights distinguished "bearing witness" from "improper proselytism" in Kokkinakis v. Greece (1993).

Criticism

Proselytism is controversial when directed at members of other faiths, vulnerable populations, or when tied to material aid. Some religious groups, particularly Eastern Orthodox churches in post-Soviet states, have objected to Western missionary activity in their traditional territories.
