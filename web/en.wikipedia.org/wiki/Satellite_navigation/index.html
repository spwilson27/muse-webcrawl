Satellite navigation or satnav is the use of artificial satellites to provide autonomous geographic positioning anywhere on Earth. A system with global coverage is called a global navigation satellite system (GNSS). Receivers determine their position by measuring the travel time of radio signals broadcast by several satellites and trilaterating their location from the known satellite positions.

A receiver needs signals from at least four satellites to fix its position and correct its clock.

How it works

Each satellite continuously broadcasts its identity, precise orbital data and the time from an onboard atomic clock. A receiver measures the signal delays from at least four satellites and computes a three-dimensional position together with a correction for its own clock error. Errors arise from atmospheric delay, satellite clock drift, multipath reflections and, for military systems, deliberate jamming or spoofing; augmentation systems such as WAAS and EGNOS broadcast corrections that improve accuracy to around a metre.

Systems

The first operational system was the U.S. Navy's Transit, followed by the Global Positioning System (GPS), which reached full operational capability in 1995 and became a ubiquitous civil utility. Russia's GLONASS, the European Union's Galileo and China's BeiDou provide independent global coverage, and most modern receivers — including virtually all smartphones — combine signals from several constellations. Regional systems include India's NavIC and Japan's QZSS.

Applications

Beyond turn-by-turn road navigation, satellite navigation underpins aviation and maritime navigation, precision agriculture, surveying, timing for telecommunications and power grids, financial timestamping, and location-based services of every kind.
