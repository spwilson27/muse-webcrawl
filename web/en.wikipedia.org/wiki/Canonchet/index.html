Canonchet

Canonchet (c. 1610s – April 3, 1676) was a sachem of the Narragansett people of Rhode Island and the son of Miantonomo, the Narragansett leader executed in 1643. He became the principal Narragansett leader during King Philip's War (1675–1676) and was captured and executed by colonial forces in 1676 after refusing terms that required him to betray his Wampanoag allies.

Life

Canonchet inherited leadership in a period of mounting pressure on the Narragansett from English colonists and their Mohegan rivals under Uncas, who had engineered Miantonomo's death. For decades the Narragansett attempted to preserve neutrality and their land base through diplomacy and treaty, even as settlers encroached and colonial courts imposed fines and humiliations.

King Philip's War

When war broke out between the Wampanoag under Metacomet ("King Philip") and the New England colonies in 1675, the Narragansett sheltered Wampanoag noncombatants. Colonial forces treated this as a breach of treaty and in December 1675 launched a pre-emptive attack — the Great Swamp Fight — destroying the Narragansett fortified village in the Great Swamp, in present-day South Kingstown, Rhode Island, and killing hundreds, including many women, children, and elders. The attack drove the surviving Narragansett fully into the war.

Canonchet led Narragansett forces in attacks on colonial settlements in the spring of 1676, including the raid that destroyed Providence in March. On April 2, 1676, he was captured at the Blackstone River near present-day Cumberland while returning from a mission to obtain seed corn. Brought before colonial officials and allied Native leaders at Stonington, he refused peace terms that required surrendering Wampanoag refugees. According to colonial accounts, he accepted his sentence, saying he liked it well and would die before his heart was soft or he had spoken anything unworthy of himself. He was shot on April 3, 1676.

Legacy

Canonchet is remembered in Rhode Island place names, including streets and a farm in Hopkinton, and as a figure of dignity and resolve in Narragansett oral tradition and later histories of the war.
