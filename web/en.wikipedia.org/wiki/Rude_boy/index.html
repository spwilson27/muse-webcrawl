Rude boy

Rude boy (also rudeboy, rudie or rudy) is a term denoting a subculture that emerged in Jamaica in the early 1960s, associated with disadvantaged, disaffected youth, the ska and rocksteady music scenes, and a sharp, defiant style of dress. The term later travelled with Jamaican emigrants to Britain, where it became intertwined with the skinhead and mod scenes, and was revived during the late-1970s 2 Tone ska movement.

Jamaican origins

In post-independence Jamaica, many young men in Kingston's ghettos found little work and few prospects. Some adopted the "rude boy" persona—wearing sharp suits, thin ties and pork pie or trilby hats, often imitating the gangsters of American films and the "ratchet knife" toughness of street life. Sound system operators and record producers courted this audience, and singers addressed the rudies directly—sometimes celebrating them, sometimes urging them to reform. Songs such as the Wailers' "Simmer Down" (1963) and Prince Buster's "Judge Dread" (1967), in which an Ethiopian judge sentences rude boys to harsh punishments, reflected the public debate around youth violence.

Music and style

Rude boy culture was the core audience for ska and then the slower rocksteady style. Its look—cropped trousers, tonic suits, button-down shirts and loafers—was adapted by British working-class youth. In the UK, "rude boy" merged with skinhead style and, in the late 1970s, the 2 Tone label (with bands such as The Specials, Madness and The Selecter) self-consciously revived the rude boy image, checkered black-and-white graphics and all, framing it as a symbol of multiracial unity. The term survives in everyday Jamaican slang, where it can still mean a tough or delinquent young man.
