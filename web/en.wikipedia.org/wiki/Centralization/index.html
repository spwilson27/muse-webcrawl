Centralization

[Image: Diagram contrasting centralised and decentralised organisational structures]

Centralization (or centralisation) is the process by which the activities of an organization, particularly planning and decision-making, become concentrated within a particular location or group. In politics, centralization refers to the concentration of a government's power and authority in a central authority, as opposed to distribution among regional or local bodies — the opposite tendency being decentralization and, in state structure, federalism.

In government

Unitary states such as France are historically highly centralised, with policy directed from the capital, whereas federal states such as the United States and Germany constitutionally divide power between central and regional governments. Advocates of centralization argue it promotes uniformity of policy, economies of scale, redistribution between rich and poor regions, and coordinated responses to crises. Critics argue it weakens local knowledge and accountability, creates bottlenecks, and risks authoritarianism when power is unchecked.

In organizations and economics

In management, centralization describes how much decision authority is retained at the top of a hierarchy. Companies centralise functions such as purchasing, IT and human resources to cut costs and enforce standards, but may decentralise to respond faster to local markets. In economics and computing the term is also used for market concentration and for network architectures in which control or data is held by a single operator — a central point of failure — contrasted with distributed and peer-to-peer systems.
