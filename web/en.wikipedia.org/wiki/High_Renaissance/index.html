High Renaissance

In art history, the High Renaissance was a short period of the most exceptional artistic production in the Italian states, particularly Rome, capital of the Papal States, and in Florence, during the Italian Renaissance. Most art historians state that the High Renaissance began around 1495 and ended in 1527 with the Sack of Rome by troops of Charles V.

The term "High Renaissance" denotes a style characterised by harmony, balance and the confident mastery of the techniques developed in the preceding century: linear perspective, chiaroscuro, sfumato, and anatomical accuracy. By general agreement, the High Renaissance in painting began with Leonardo da Vinci's Last Supper (1495–1498) in Milan.

Artists

The period is dominated by three figures: Leonardo, whose Mona Lisa and Last Supper defined the era; Michelangelo, sculptor of the David (1501–1504) and painter of the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–1512); and Raphael, whose Vatican Stanze frescoes, including The School of Athens, exemplify classical harmony. In architecture, Donato Bramante's Tempietto and his 1506 design for the new St Peter's Basilica set the classical standard, while in Venice Giorgione and the young Titian developed a painterly, colour-based alternative.

Historical context

The High Renaissance coincided with the Italian Wars, as France and Spain fought over the peninsula, and with the ambitious patronage of popes Julius II and Leo X, who sought to restore Rome as the centre of Christendom and classical culture. The period ended abruptly with the Sack of Rome in 1527, which dispersed artists across Italy and into the courts of Europe, seeding the style known as Mannerism.
