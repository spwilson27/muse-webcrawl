The Hallmarks of Cancer

"The Hallmarks of Cancer" is a landmark scientific paper by Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg published in the journal Cell in January 2000. It proposes that the bewildering diversity of cancers can be organised around a small number of shared functional capabilities — "hallmarks" — that normal cells must acquire as they evolve into malignancy. It is among the most cited papers in cancer biology.

The original six hallmarks (2000)

The 2000 paper identified six acquired capabilities:

  1. Self-sufficiency in growth signals — tumour cells produce or respond abnormally to their own growth signals.
  2. Insensitivity to anti-growth signals — loss of response to growth inhibitors such as those acting through the retinoblastoma pathway.
  3. Evading apoptosis — resistance to programmed cell death.
  4. Limitless replicative potential — maintenance of telomeres, often via telomerase, avoiding senescence and crisis.
  5. Sustained angiogenesis — induction of new blood vessels to supply the growing tumour.
  6. Tissue invasion and metastasis — the ability to invade surrounding tissue and seed distant sites, the cause of most cancer deaths.

Update (2011) and beyond

In a 2011 follow-up, "Hallmarks of Cancer: The Next Generation," Hanahan and Weinberg added two further hallmarks — deregulating cellular energetics (such as the Warburg effect) and avoiding immune destruction — and two "enabling characteristics": genome instability and mutation, and tumour-promoting inflammation. A 2022 update by Hanahan added further dimensions including phenotypic plasticity, non-mutational epigenetic reprogramming, polymorphic microbiomes and senescent cells.

Significance

The framework has shaped research programmes, drug development (many targeted therapies map onto individual hallmarks) and the teaching of oncology, while also attracting criticism for oversimplifying tumour heterogeneity and evolution.
