Neuroendocrinology

Neuroendocrinology is the branch of biology and medicine that studies the interaction between the nervous system and the endocrine system — how the brain controls hormone release, and how hormones in turn affect the brain and behaviour. Its central structure is the hypothalamus, which integrates neural signals and regulates the pituitary gland, the "master gland" of the endocrine system.

History

The field emerged in the mid-20th century from work by Geoffrey Harris and others demonstrating that hypothalamic neurons secrete releasing factors into the portal blood vessels supplying the anterior pituitary. Ernst and Berta Scharrer had earlier described neurosecretion in the hypothalamus. In 1977 Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally received the Nobel Prize for isolating hypothalamic releasing hormones such as thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH).

Major systems

Hypothalamic–pituitary axes

Neuroendocrine neurons in the hypothalamus release hormones that control the pituitary's secretion of growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), and the gonadotropins LH and FSH. These form feedback-regulated axes — the HPA axis for stress and cortisol, the HPT axis for thyroid function, and the HPG axis for reproduction.

Neurohypophysial hormones

Magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei synthesise oxytocin and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone), transporting them to the posterior pituitary, from which they are released into the general circulation — the classic example of neurosecretion.

Clinical relevance

Disorders studied within neuroendocrinology include pituitary tumours, hypopituitarism, disorders of growth and puberty, and neuroendocrine tumours. Hormonal feedback onto the brain also links the field to appetite and energy balance (leptin, ghrelin), stress physiology, circadian rhythms, and aspects of biological psychiatry.
