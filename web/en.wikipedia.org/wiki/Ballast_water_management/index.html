Ballast water management

A vessel discharging ballast water

Ballast water management is the control of water carried in ships' ballast tanks, used to maintain stability and trim. Ships take on ballast water in one port — along with the organisms living in it — and discharge it in another, a process estimated to move billions of tonnes of water annually. This is recognised as one of the principal pathways by which invasive aquatic species spread worldwide; notorious examples include zebra mussels in the North American Great Lakes, the comb jelly Mnemiopsis leidyi in the Black and Caspian Seas, and toxic dinoflagellates transported to new coasts.

Regulation

The International Maritime Organization adopted the Ballast Water Management Convention in 2004; it entered into force on 8 September 2017. The Convention requires ships to manage their ballast water to a performance standard (the D-2 standard), which sets maximum concentrations of viable organisms by size class, generally requiring type-approved treatment systems using filtration, ultraviolet light, chlorination or deoxygenation. An earlier interim standard (D-1) relied on ballast water exchange in the open ocean, at least 200 nautical miles from land, so that coastal organisms would not survive in oceanic water and vice versa. Ships must carry a ballast water management plan and record book, and are subject to port State control sampling and inspection.
