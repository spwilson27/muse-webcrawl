Liverpool Waters

The central docks area covered by the Liverpool Waters scheme

Liverpool Waters is a large-scale, long-term regeneration scheme for the northern docklands of Liverpool, England, promoted by the Peel Group (Peel Waters). Covering about 60 hectares (150 acres) of former dockland along the River Mersey — including Central Docks, Clarence Docks, and areas around Princes Dock and the former Bramley-Moore Dock — the £5.5 billion masterplan envisages mixed-use development over roughly 30–50 years, with offices, housing, leisure uses, and tall buildings organised into neighbourhoods.

History

The project was announced in 2007 and granted outline planning permission by Liverpool City Council in 2012, despite objections from heritage bodies. Because the site lay largely within the Liverpool Maritime Mercantile City World Heritage Site, UNESCO warned that the scheme's tall buildings and infill of historic docks would damage the site's outstanding universal value; after years on the List of World Heritage in Danger, Liverpool was removed from the World Heritage List in July 2021, with Liverpool Waters among the developments cited.

Peel has delivered the scheme in phases, beginning around Princes Dock and the "Liverpool 2" port development nearby. In 2018 the scheme gained prominence as the proposed location of Everton Football Club's new stadium at Bramley-Moore Dock, approved in 2021 and built on the dock site, opening in 2025 after Everton's move from Goodison Park.

Components

  • Princes Dock area — offices, hotels, and residential buildings, including the Liverpool Cruise Terminal.
  • Central Docks — planned as the largest neighbourhood, with thousands of homes, a park, and cultural uses.
  • Clarence Docks and King Edward Triangle — mixed-use quarters north of the city centre.
  • Bramley-Moore Dock — Everton Stadium (Hill Dickinson Stadium), the centrepiece of the northern section.

Criticism

Beyond the heritage dispute, critics have questioned the pace of delivery, the loss of historic dock water spaces through infill, and the balance between private development and public benefit. Supporters argue that the docks had been derelict for decades and that regeneration, jobs, and the new stadium justify the change.
