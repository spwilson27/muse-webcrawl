Shōen

Shōen (荘園 or 庄園) were private landed estates in Japan that flourished from the 8th century through the 16th century. Largely exempt from taxation and government interference, they played a central role in the weakening of the imperial state and the rise of the warrior class.

History

The shōen system grew out of the breakdown of the ritsuryō land-allotment system. From the early Heian period, aristocrats, temples and shrines accumulated estates by reclaiming new land or by receiving "commended" land from local cultivators seeking protection and tax immunity. Proprietors in the capital drew income from the estates while local managers administered them.

Administration

Rights over a shōen were divided among several layers of rights-holders (shiki), including the absentee proprietor, estate managers and cultivators. During the Kamakura period the shogunate appointed military stewards (jitō) to many estates, giving warriors a foothold in local administration. The system declined during the Sengoku period as regional daimyo consolidated control, and Hideyoshi's land surveys in the late 16th century effectively ended it.
