PIC (usually pronounced as "pick") is a family of microcontrollers made by Microchip Technology, derived from the PIC1640 originally developed by General Instrument's Microelectronics Division. The name PIC initially referred to Peripheral Interface Controller, and is currently expanded as Programmable Intelligent Computer, though Microchip now generally does not use the expansion. The first parts of the family were available in 1976; by 2013 the company had shipped more than 12 billion individual parts, used widely in embedded systems.

Architecture

PIC devices use a modified Harvard architecture in which program memory and data memory are separate, with instruction words wider than the 8-bit data path (12, 14 or 16 bits in the classic 8-bit families). The instruction set is small and orthogonal-ish, built around a single working register (W) and a banked file of special-function and general-purpose registers. The 8-bit families are grouped into baseline (PIC10/12/16 with 12-bit instructions), mid-range (14-bit), enhanced mid-range and PIC18 (16-bit). Higher-end lines include the 16-bit PIC24 and dsPIC digital signal controllers, and the 32-bit PIC32 series based on the MIPS architecture (and, in later parts, ARM cores).

Features and ecosystem

PICs integrate flash or ROM program memory, SRAM, EEPROM, timers, analog-to-digital converters, comparators, PWM outputs and serial interfaces (USART, SPI, I²C, CAN, USB, Ethernet) in pin counts from 6 to over 100, making single-chip solutions for appliances, automotive systems, industrial controls, medical devices and consumer electronics. Development is centered on Microchip's MPLAB X IDE, the XC compilers, and low-cost programmers/debuggers such as the PICkit. In-circuit serial programming and wide availability made PICs a staple of hobbyist electronics education in the 1990s and 2000s, before Arduino-style AVR and ARM boards became dominant.

History

The original PIC1650 was designed around 1975 at General Instrument as a programmable I/O controller to accompany its CP1600 microprocessor; spun off with the rest of the division, the product line became the core of the newly formed Microchip Technology in 1989. Successive generations added electrically erasable/Flash memory (making PICs among the first widely used reprogrammable microcontrollers), lower power consumption and richer peripherals, keeping the family one of the highest-volume microcontroller lines in the industry.
