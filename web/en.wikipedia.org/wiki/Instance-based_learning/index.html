Instance-based learning

In machine learning, instance-based learning (sometimes called memory-based learning or lazy learning) is a family of learning algorithms that, instead of performing explicit generalisation, compares new problem instances with instances seen in training, which have been stored in memory. It is a type of lazy learning because it does not attempt to construct a general internal model of the data, but simply stores the training instances; generalisation is postponed until a query is made.

Overview

Hypothesis complexity in instance-based learning grows with the data: a prediction for a new instance is derived from the stored examples most similar to it under some distance metric, such as Euclidean distance. The best-known instance-based algorithm is the k-nearest neighbours algorithm (k-NN), which classifies a point by majority vote of its k nearest neighbours, or averages their values for regression.

Other instance-based methods include kernel-weighted local learning, case-based reasoning systems, which reuse solutions to similar past problems, and locally weighted regression. Support vector machines can also be viewed as related, since they retain a subset of training points (the support vectors).

Advantages and disadvantages

Because training consists merely of storing examples, instance-based learners can adapt immediately to new data and can model complex, irregular decision boundaries. Their disadvantages are the cost of storing and searching all instances at prediction time, sensitivity to irrelevant features and to the choice of distance metric (the "curse of dimensionality"), and susceptibility to noise in the training set. Techniques such as prototype selection, feature weighting, and spatial indexing structures like kd-trees mitigate some of these costs.
