Microservices

Microservices — also known as the microservice architecture — is an architectural style that structures an application as a collection of small, loosely coupled, independently deployable services, each running its own process and communicating through lightweight mechanisms, often HTTP-based APIs or messaging. It stands in contrast to the traditional monolithic architecture, in which all components are interwoven into a single unit.

Principles

Microservices are organised around business capabilities, owned by small autonomous teams, independently scalable and replaceable, and decentralised in both governance and data management — each service typically manages its own database. The approach grew out of service-oriented architecture (SOA) and was popularised in the early 2010s by companies such as Netflix and Amazon, and by writers including Martin Fowler and James Lewis, whose 2014 article helped define the style.

Benefits and trade-offs

Advantages include independent deployment and scaling, technology diversity, and fault isolation: the failure of one service need not bring down the whole system. The costs are the complexity of a distributed system — network latency, eventual consistency, distributed transactions, more difficult testing and debugging, and the need for mature DevOps practices such as containerisation (e.g. Docker), orchestration (Kubernetes), continuous delivery, monitoring and automated deployment.
