Greco-Bactrian Kingdom

The Greco-Bactrian Kingdom was a Hellenistic state in Central Asia, centred on Bactria (in modern Afghanistan, Tajikistan and Uzbekistan), which existed from about 256 BC, when the satrap Diodotus I broke away from the Seleucid Empire, until about 125 BC, when it was overrun by nomadic peoples including the Yuezhi. It was the easternmost extent of the Greek world, famous for its wealth, its splendid coinage and the fusion of Greek, Iranian and Indian cultures.

History

Diodotus I and his son Diodotus II established the kingdom's independence around the middle of the third century BC. Euthydemus I, who seized power about 230 BC, repelled an attempted Seleucid reconquest by Antiochus III, who recognised the kingdom after a long siege of Bactra (208–206 BC). Under Demetrius I (c. 200–180 BC) Greco-Bactrian arms crossed the Hindu Kush into north-western India, beginning the line of Indo-Greek kings.

The usurper Eucratides I (c. 171–145 BC) built an empire that briefly stretched from Margiana to India; his assassination by his own son triggered civil wars that fatally weakened the kingdom. The last Greco-Bactrian king, Heliocles I, abandoned Bactria around 130–125 BC under pressure from the Saka and Yuezhi migrations. Greek successor kingdoms survived in India for another century or more.

Culture and economy

The excavated city of Ai-Khanoum on the Oxus revealed a thoroughly Greek city — gymnasium, theatre, temples and inscriptions of Delphic maxims — on the edge of Central Asia. Bactria's position astride trade routes to India, China and the west made it, in Strabo's words, a land of "a thousand cities". Its kings issued some of the finest Hellenistic coinage, including the largest gold coin of antiquity, a 20-stater piece of Eucratides. Greco-Bactrian art and its Gandharan successors transmitted Greek forms deep into Asia, shaping the earliest anthropomorphic images of the Buddha.
