Covent Garden is a district in London, on the eastern fringes of the West End, between St Martin's Lane and Drury Lane. It is associated with the former fruit-and-vegetable market in the central square, now a popular shopping and tourist site, and with the Royal Opera House, which itself is sometimes referred to as "Covent Garden". The district is split by the main thoroughfare of Long Acre, north of which is mainly given over to independent shops centred on Neal's Yard and Seven Dials, while the south contains the central square with its street performers and most of the historical buildings, theatres and entertainment facilities.

History

The area was settled by the Romans as part of Londinium. The name "Covent Garden" derives from the "garden of the convent" — the market gardens of the Abbey and Convent of St Peter at Westminster that occupied the site in the Middle Ages. In the 1630s the 4th Earl of Bedford commissioned Inigo Jones to build a fashionable residential square, modelled on Italian piazzas, with the church of St Paul's at its western end. By the 18th century the square had become home to a thriving fruit, vegetable and flower market.

The market grew steadily and by the late 19th century was the largest fruit and vegetable market in Britain. Congestion in the narrow streets around the piazza eventually forced its relocation; in 1974 the market moved to the New Covent Garden Market site at Nine Elms, Battersea. The central market buildings were subsequently redeveloped as a shopping centre, opening in 1980, and the area became a major tourist attraction.

The market buildings

The Central Market building, designed by Charles Fowler and built in 1828–30, is a Grade II* listed structure of three parallel ranges linked by glass roofs. The adjacent Flower Market building (now home to the London Transport Museum) and the Jubilee Market were added later in the 19th century. Apple Market and Jubilee Market now sell antiques, crafts and general goods.

Culture

Covent Garden is home to the Royal Opera House, the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, and numerous other theatres. St Paul's Church, known as the "actors' church", stands on the western side of the piazza; street performers have entertained crowds in front of it since the 17th century — Samuel Pepys recorded seeing a Punch and Judy show there in 1662, an event commemorated each May by the annual Punch and Judy festival.

Transport

Covent Garden station on the Piccadilly line, opened in 1907, is famous for its 193-step spiral staircase and for being one of the few central London stations where passengers are advised to walk from nearby Leicester Square, which is only about 260 metres away — the shortest distance between two stations on the London Underground network.
