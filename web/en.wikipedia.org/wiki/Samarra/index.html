Samarra

Country: Iraq
Governorate: Saladin
Population: approx. 350,000
UNESCO: Samarra Archaeological City, World Heritage Site (2007)
Known for: Abbasid capital; Al-Askari Shrine

Samarra (Arabic: سامراء, Sāmarrāʾ) is a city in Iraq, standing on the east bank of the Tigris about 125 kilometres north of Baghdad. In 836 CE the Abbasid caliph al-Mu'tasim founded a new imperial capital there, and for roughly half a century Samarra was one of the largest and most splendid cities in the world before the court returned to Baghdad in 892.

History

Al-Mu'tasim moved his capital from Baghdad partly to house his new Turkish slave-soldier regiments away from the Baghdad populace. His successors built palaces, racecourses, and the Great Mosque of Samarra, whose spiral Malwiya Minaret (52 m high) remains the city's emblem. Eight caliphs reigned from Samarra, a period that included the "Anarchy at Samarra" after 861, when Turkish officers made and murdered caliphs. The city's name is popularly derived from the phrase surra man ra'ā—"a joy for whoever sees it".

Religious significance

Samarra is holy to Shia Muslims: the al-Askari Shrine houses the tombs of the tenth and eleventh Imams, Ali al-Hadi and Hasan al-Askari, and stands over the cellar from which the twelfth Imam, the Mahdi, is believed to have entered occultation. A bombing of the shrine in February 2006 destroyed its golden dome and helped ignite Iraq's sectarian civil war; it was attacked again in 2007 and later rebuilt.

Archaeology

The ruins of Abbasid Samarra stretch for kilometres along the Tigris and constitute one of the largest archaeological sites in the world. Samarra also lends its name to a prehistoric Neolithic "Samarra culture" identified at nearby sites. The archaeological city was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2007 and listed as endangered.
