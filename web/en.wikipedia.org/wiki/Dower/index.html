Dower secured a widow's support from her husband's lands.

Dower is a provision accorded by law to a wife for support in the event that she should survive her husband. It was settled on the bride (by agreement at the time of the wedding) by agreement of the groom's family, or in English common law, it was one-third (the widow's third) of the husband's landed property that a widow could claim for the duration of her natural life (in support of herself and her children), although the husband may have supported her during his life through other means or settlements.

Dower is the gift given by the groom to his bride, and its common-law protection arose from the difficulty widows faced when male kin treated a marriage as a league between families. A wealthy widow (a dowager) could provide for her own household; the terms dowager and dower house derive from this institution.

At common law

Under English common law, a widow was entitled to a life interest in one-third of all lands of which her husband had been seised during the marriage in an estate of inheritance, provided a child of the marriage could have inherited. Dower attached as an inchoate right during the marriage and could not be defeated by the husband's conveyance of the land without her consent — a rule that made dower a persistent obstacle in land transactions and required wives to join in deeds, often by fine or common recovery. It could be barred by a jointure settlement made before marriage.

History and abolition

Magna Carta (1215) protected a widow's dower and her right not to be forced to remarry. In England, the Dower Act 1833 allowed husbands to defeat dower by deed or will, and the Administration of Estates Act 1925 abolished dower (and curtesy) for deaths after 1925. In the United States, where dower had been received into the law of most states, it was progressively replaced during the twentieth century by the elective share — a statutory fraction of the decedent's estate regardless of land ownership — although dower survives in a few states, notably in modified form in Ohio, Kentucky, and Michigan (which abolished it in 2016, effective 2017).

Distinctions

Dower is not to be confused with dowry, property brought by the bride's family; nor with mahr, the dower gift required in Islamic marriage law, which belongs to the wife herself; nor with curtesy, the widower's corresponding life estate, traditionally extending to all of his wife's inheritable lands if a child was born alive.
