Standard gravity

The standard acceleration of gravity or standard acceleration of free fall, sometimes abbreviated g0 or gn, is the nominal gravitational acceleration of an object in a vacuum near the surface of the Earth. It is a defined constant: exactly 9.80665 m/s² (32.1740 ft/s²), adopted by the 3rd General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1901.

Purpose

The value is a mid-latitude, sea-level approximation of Earth's actual surface gravity, which in reality varies from about 9.78 m/s² at the equator to about 9.83 m/s² at the poles because of the Earth's rotation and oblateness. The constant defines the relationship between mass and force units: the kilogram-force is defined as the force exerted by standard gravity on a one-kilogram mass, and the standard atmosphere is defined in terms of it. It is also the unit of the g-force used in aviation, rocketry and physiology.

History

The 1901 definition was based on measurements at the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures in Sèvres, corrected to 45° latitude. Although the SI has since redefined its base units, gn retains its exact conventional value for use in metrology, pressure measurement and engineering standards (ISO 80000-3).
