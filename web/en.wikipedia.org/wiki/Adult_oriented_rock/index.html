Adult oriented rock

Other namesAlbum-oriented rock (AOR)
Stylistic originsRock, progressive rock, hard rock, soft rock
Cultural originsLate 1960s–1970s, United States (FM radio)

AOR emphasised polished production and album tracks over singles

Adult oriented rock, widely known by the initials AOR and originally an abbreviation of album-oriented rock, is both a radio format and a style of rock music characterised by polished production, tight arrangements, strong melodies, and virtuoso musicianship. As a format, AOR stations of the 1970s broke with Top 40 radio by playing album tracks in depth rather than only hit singles, building the commercial foundation of FM rock radio.

History

The format grew out of freeform FM stations of the late 1960s, such as those influenced by DJ Tom Donahue in San Francisco, and was systematised in the 1970s by consultants including Lee Abrams, whose "SuperStars" format targeted listeners aged roughly 18 to 34. AOR playlists favoured established album acts — Led Zeppelin, Pink Floyd, the Eagles, Fleetwood Mac, and later arena-rock bands such as Boston, Foreigner, Journey, Kansas, Styx, and REO Speedwagon.

Musical style

As a genre label, AOR denotes melodic rock with layered keyboards and guitars, prominent choruses, and high production values, often shading into soft rock balladry on one side and hard rock on the other. In the United Kingdom the term was sometimes used interchangeably with melodic rock, and bands such as Toto and Foreigner became emblematic of the sound.

Decline

By the mid-1980s the AOR format fragmented into classic rock, mainstream rock, and adult album alternative (AAA) formats, and the term fell out of everyday industry use, though "AOR" survives as a genre tag for melodic rock releases.
