Closer settlement (Australia)

Closer settlement was a policy pursued by Australian colonial, state, and Commonwealth governments from the mid-nineteenth century into the twentieth century, aimed at breaking up large pastoral estates and settling the land more densely with small-scale farmers. The policy reflected both democratic pressure for access to land — "unlock the lands" — and the ideal of a yeomanry of independent family farmers.

History

Early selection laws, such as the Robertson Land Acts of 1861 in New South Wales, allowed "free selection before survey" but were widely evaded by squatters and pastoralists through practices such as "dummying" (using nominal buyers). From the 1890s and especially after Federation, governments introduced closer settlement legislation — for example, New South Wales' Closer Settlement Acts from 1901 — under which the Crown could compulsorily repurchase subdivided estates and resell them in small farms on long terms. Repurchased land included irrigation areas along the Murray and Murrumbidgee rivers.

Soldier settlement

After both world wars, closer settlement principles were applied in soldier settlement schemes, which settled returned servicemen on subdivided estates. Many such blocks proved too small or marginal to be viable, and large numbers of soldier settlers walked off their land in hardship, though the schemes permanently altered settlement patterns in many districts.

Assessment

Closer settlement did place more families on the land and underpinned closer-settlement landscapes of small farms, towns, and railways, but historians note mixed economic results: many holdings were uneconomic, and the policy often displaced Aboriginal people from land without recognition or compensation.
