Mail (armour)

Mail (sometimes spelled maille, and commonly but incorrectly called chainmail) is a type of armour consisting of small metal rings linked together in a pattern to form a mesh. It was in use from at least the 4th century BCE until the 16th century in Europe, and far longer in parts of Asia and the Middle East. A mail shirt, the hauberk, could contain tens of thousands of rings, each individually riveted or welded shut, representing months of skilled labour.

History

Mail is generally credited to the Celts, and the earliest examples come from Celtic burials of the 4th century BCE. The Romans adopted it as the lorica hamata, their standard armour for centuries, and it spread across Europe, Persia, India, China, and Japan (where it was called kusari). Through the early and high Middle Ages, mail was the premier body defence of knights and men-at-arms; garments included the hauberk (a long shirt), the shorter haubergeon, coifs for the head, chausses for the legs, and mittens (mufflers). From the 14th century, plate armour increasingly supplemented and then replaced mail for the wealthy, but mail remained in use under plate, in standards and voiders protecting the joints, and by ordinary soldiers well into the gunpowder era.

Construction and effectiveness

Mail is usually woven in a "four-in-one" pattern, each ring passing through four others. Riveted rings offer far greater strength than butted rings, whose ends merely meet. Mail gives excellent protection against cutting blows and, worn over a padded garment (a gambeson or aketon), also resists thrusts and blunts impact; it is flexible and relatively easy to repair, though heavy — a hauberk might weigh 10–14 kg, borne largely on the shoulders and belted waist. Its principal weaknesses are concentrated thrusts and, later, firearms.

Modern uses

Mail survives today in butchers' and oyster-shuckers' protective gloves, shark-resistant diving suits, and in historical re-enactment and decorative craft.
