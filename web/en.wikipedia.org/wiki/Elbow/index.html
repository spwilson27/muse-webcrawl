Elbow

The elbow is the region surrounding the elbow joint — the joint connecting the upper arm to the forearm. It is composed primarily of the articulation between the humerus in the upper arm and the radius and ulna in the forearm, which allows the forearm and hand to be moved towards and away from the body. The elbow joint is a hinge joint with a rotatory component, permitting flexion and extension as well as pronation and supination of the forearm.

Structure

The elbow joint is formed by three articulations sharing one joint cavity: the trochlea of the humerus with the trochlear notch of the ulna, the capitulum of the humerus with the head of the radius, and the proximal radioulnar joint. The joint capsule is strengthened by the ulnar and radial collateral ligaments, and the annular ligament holds the radial head against the ulna. The bony point at the back of the elbow is the olecranon process of the ulna. Important structures crossing the elbow include the brachial artery and the median, radial and ulnar nerves — the ulnar nerve passes behind the medial epicondyle, where it is vulnerable and produces the tingling sensation known as hitting one's "funny bone".

Function

Flexion is produced mainly by the biceps brachii, brachialis and brachioradialis; extension by the triceps brachii and anconeus. The elbow's normal range of flexion is roughly 0° to 145°. The carrying angle — the slight outward angle of the forearm relative to the upper arm — is typically greater in women than in men.

Clinical significance

Common conditions include lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow") and medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow"), olecranon bursitis, fractures of the radial head and olecranon, and dislocation, which is particularly common in children in the form of radial head subluxation ("nursemaid's elbow"). Arthritis, gout and infections can also affect the joint.
