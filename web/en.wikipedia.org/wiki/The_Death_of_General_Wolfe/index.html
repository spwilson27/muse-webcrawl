The Death of General Wolfe (1770) by Benjamin West.

The Death of General Wolfe is a 1770 painting by Anglo-American artist Benjamin West, commemorating the death of British General James Wolfe at the 1759 Battle of Quebec during the French and Indian War (Seven Years' War). Wolfe fell at the moment of victory over the French forces of the Marquis de Montcalm on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec City.

Composition

West shows the dying Wolfe surrounded by a group of officers in a composition modeled on the Lamentation over the Dead Christ, with a tall flag taking the place of the cross. An Indigenous warrior kneels pensively in the foreground. Wolfe is depicted in contemporary uniform rather than classical dress, which was revolutionary at the time: academic authorities such as Sir Joshua Reynolds advised West to use classical drapery, but West insisted on historical accuracy, and the painting's success helped establish modern-dress history painting.

History and reception

The picture was exhibited at the Royal Academy in 1771 and made West famous; King George III nonetheless declined to buy it because of its modern costume, though he later commissioned a copy. Engravings by William Woollett spread its fame widely, making it one of the best-known images of the 18th century. The original hangs in the National Gallery of Canada in Ottawa, a gift of the Duke of Westminster in 1918. Several autograph versions exist, including one in the Royal Ontario Museum and one at Ickworth House.
