Harvey Sacks (1935 – November 14, 1975) was an American sociologist who is credited with founding the field of conversation analysis (CA). Working at the University of California, first at UCLA and later at Irvine, Sacks developed a rigorous method for studying the organization of everyday talk using recordings of naturally occurring interaction.

Work

Sacks initially trained as a lawyer before turning to sociology, studying under Harold Garfinkel and collaborating with Erving Goffman and Harold Garfinkel's ethnomethodology circle. His early research examined calls to a suicide prevention center, from which he developed key ideas about how callers and call-takers organize the opening moments of conversation.

His central contributions include the analysis of turn-taking — formalized with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson in the 1974 paper "A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation" — sequence organization, and membership categorization analysis. Sacks insisted that social order is visible in the smallest details of talk, and that recordings, transcribed in fine detail, should be the data of social science.

Legacy

Sacks died in a car accident in 1975 at the age of forty. His lectures from 1964 to 1972 were published posthumously as Lectures on Conversation (1992), edited by Gail Jefferson. Conversation analysis has since become an influential approach across sociology, linguistics, anthropology, and communication studies.
