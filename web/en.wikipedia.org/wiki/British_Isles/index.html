The British Isles is a geographical term for the archipelago off the north-western coast of continental Europe, comprising the islands of Great Britain, Ireland and over six thousand smaller islands, including the Isle of Man, Anglesey, the Hebrides, Orkney, Shetland, the Isle of Wight, the Isles of Scilly and the Channel Islands. It is a purely geographical grouping: politically it contains two sovereign states, the United Kingdom and Ireland, as well as the Crown Dependencies.

Terminology

The term is disputed in Ireland: because "British" is sometimes read as implying British political identity, the Irish government does not use it and many Irish writers prefer "Britain and Ireland" or "the islands of the North Atlantic". In academic and official usage in the UK, the term remains common as a geographical label, distinct from "Great Britain" (the largest island) and "British Islands" (a legal term for the UK, the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands).

Geography

Great Britain is the largest island (about 209,331 km²), followed by Ireland (about 70,273 km²). The archipelago extends from the Shetland Islands in the north to the Isles of Scilly in the south-west. The English Channel and the North Sea separate it from continental Europe; the Irish Sea lies between Great Britain and Ireland. The climate is temperate oceanic, with mild winters and cool summers moderated by the North Atlantic Drift.

History

The islands have been inhabited since the end of the last ice age. They saw successive Celtic, Roman, Anglo-Saxon, Viking and Norman influences. The political unification of the larger island began with the Kingdoms of England and Scotland, united as Great Britain in 1707, and the United Kingdom was formed with Ireland in 1801. Most of Ireland became independent in 1922, leaving the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland.
