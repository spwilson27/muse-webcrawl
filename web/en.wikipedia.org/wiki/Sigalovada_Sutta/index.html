Sigalovada Sutta

The Sigālovāda Sutta is a discourse of the Buddha in the Pali Canon, preserved as the 31st sutta of the Dīgha Nikāya. In it, the Buddha gives a young householder named Sigāla a complete code of lay ethics, reinterpreting his ritual worship of the six directions as duties toward six classes of people: parents, teachers, spouse, friends, servants and workers, and religious teachers. The discourse is often described as the "layperson's Vinaya" (disciplinary code) and is one of the most popular texts of practical Buddhist ethics.

Summary

Early one morning the Buddha encounters Sigāla, a young man who, following his dying father's instruction, worships the six directions (east, south, west, north, nadir and zenith) after bathing. The Buddha does not disparage the rite but reinterprets it: in the discipline of the Noble One, honouring the directions means honouring the people to whom one has reciprocal obligations.

Before this, the Buddha warns Sigāla against four defilements of action (killing, stealing, lying, sexual misconduct), four impure motives (desire, hatred, delusion, fear), six channels for dissipating wealth (addiction to intoxicants, roaming the streets at unseemly hours, frequenting shows, gambling, bad companionship and laziness), and he distinguishes false friends from true "good-hearted" friends, who protect, rejoice in one's prosperity, restrain one from evil and support one in trouble.

The six directions

  • East — parents and children: children support and honour their parents and keep the family traditions; parents restrain children from evil, train them in skills, arrange suitable marriages and hand over their inheritance.
  • South — teachers and students: students rise to greet, attend, listen eagerly, serve and learn diligently; teachers train thoroughly, ensure grasp of the teaching, introduce them to colleagues and provide for their security.
  • West — husband and wife: the husband honours his wife, entrusts her with household authority and provides adornments; the wife manages the household well, is hospitable to relatives and staff, faithful and thrifty.
  • North — friends: mutual generosity, kind speech, helpfulness, impartiality and honesty.
  • Nadir — employers and workers: employers assign work according to ability, give fair wages and food, tend workers in sickness, share special treats and grant leave; workers rise before, retire after, take only what is given, work well and speak well of their employers.
  • Zenith — laypeople and ascetics: laypeople serve religious teachers with loving acts, words and thoughts and supply their needs; ascetics teach the Dhamma, restrain them from evil and encourage them in good.

Significance

The sutta gives Buddhism's classic charter of social and domestic ethics, grounding harmonious households and workplaces in reciprocal duty rather than hierarchy alone. It remains widely taught in Theravāda countries and is frequently reprinted for lay distribution.
