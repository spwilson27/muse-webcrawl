Women in ancient Greece

Women in ancient Greece were generally subject to a patriarchal social order in which their legal and political rights were severely restricted compared with those of men. Their status varied considerably between city-states and periods: the women of classical Athens lived largely secluded lives centred on the household, while Spartan women enjoyed comparatively greater freedom, education and property rights.

Classical Athens

Athenian citizen women married young, often around the age of fourteen, to men roughly twice their age. Their primary duties were managing the household (oikos), weaving textiles, and raising children—particularly legitimate male heirs. Women could not vote, own substantial property in their own right, or represent themselves in court; they acted through a male guardian (kyrios). Respectable women were expected to remain in the women's quarters (gynaikeion) and appear in public only for religious festivals and funerals.

Sparta

Spartan women were educated in reading, writing, music and athletics, and were encouraged to exercise in public to bear healthy children. They could own and inherit land—by the fourth century BC women reportedly controlled a large share of Spartan territory. Spartan women married later than Athenians, around eighteen to twenty, and husbands and wives often lived apart while men served in the military.

Religion and public life

Religion offered women their most visible public roles. Priestesses, such as the Pythia at Delphi, held positions of great prestige, and festivals like the Thesmophoria were exclusively female. Hetairai—courtesans and companions—formed an exception to the seclusion of citizen wives, participating in symposia and intellectual life.

Notable figures

Well-known women of the Greek world include the poet Sappho of Lesbos, Aspasia of Miletus, the philosopher Hypatia (of late antique Alexandria), and figures of myth and drama such as Antigone, Medea and Lysistrata, through whom Greek attitudes toward women are often read.
