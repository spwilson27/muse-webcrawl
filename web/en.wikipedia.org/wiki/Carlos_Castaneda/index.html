Carlos Castaneda (December 25, 1925 – April 27, 1998) was a Peruvian-born American author and anthropologist. His books, beginning with The Teachings of Don Juan: A Yaqui Way of Knowledge (1968), recount his apprenticeship to a Yaqui "man of knowledge" named don Juan Matus, who taught him a shamanic path involving the use of psychoactive plants such as peyote, datura, and psilocybin mushrooms. The books became bestsellers and influential texts of the New Age movement.

Books and claims

Castaneda wrote twelve books, including A Separate Reality (1971), Journey to Ixtlan (1972), and Tales of Power (1974). UCLA awarded him a doctorate in anthropology in 1973 on the basis of the work presented in his first three books. The later books moved away from plant use toward practices of dreaming, "stalking," and the concept of the assemblage point.

Controversy

Castaneda's works were initially received favourably by some anthropologists, but scholars such as Richard de Mille published detailed critiques (Castaneda's Journey, 1976) arguing that the books were works of fiction: chronologies were inconsistent, the described practices did not match Yaqui ethnography, and library records suggested Castaneda had been in Los Angeles during periods he claimed to be in Mexico. Academic consensus today generally treats the books as fiction rather than ethnography, though they remain popular as spiritual literature.

Later life

In the 1990s Castaneda, with associates including three female companions known as the "witches," promoted Tensegrity, a system of movements derived from his teachings, through the company Cleargreen. He died of liver cancer in Los Angeles in 1998; his death was not publicly announced for two months, and several of his close associates disappeared shortly afterwards.
