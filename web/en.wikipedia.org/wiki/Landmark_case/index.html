Landmark case

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A landmark case (also called a landmark decision) is a court case that is studied because it has historical and legal significance. The most significant cases are those that have had a substantial change in the way the law is interpreted, often altering a previously established precedent or establishing a new one. Landmark cases feature prominently in law school curricula, where they anchor the case method of legal education.

Characteristics

A decision is likely to become a landmark if it does one or more of the following: establishes a new legal principle or concept; overrules or substantially modifies earlier precedent; creates a test or standard that lower courts must apply; resolves a conflict between courts; or produces a major social or political effect beyond the immediate parties.

United States examples

  • Marbury v. Madison (1803) — established judicial review.
  • Brown v. Board of Education (1954) — declared state-sponsored segregation in public schools unconstitutional.
  • Miranda v. Arizona (1966) — required police warnings to detained suspects.
  • Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) — guaranteed counsel to indigent criminal defendants.
  • New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964) — set the "actual malice" standard for libel of public officials.
  • Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) — recognised a constitutional right to same-sex marriage.

Other jurisdictions

Comparable decisions exist worldwide: Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) founded the modern law of negligence in the United Kingdom; Mabo v Queensland (1992) recognised native title in Australia; the Lüth judgment (1958) shaped German constitutional doctrine on fundamental rights; and in India, Kesavananda Bharati (1973) established the basic-structure doctrine limiting constitutional amendment.
