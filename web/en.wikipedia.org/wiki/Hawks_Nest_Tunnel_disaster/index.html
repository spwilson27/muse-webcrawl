Hawks Nest Tunnel disaster

The Hawks Nest Tunnel disaster was an industrial disaster in the early 1930s near Gauley Bridge, West Virginia, United States, considered one of the worst in American history. Workers excavating a tunnel through silica-rich sandstone for a hydroelectric project were exposed to massive concentrations of silica dust and developed acute silicosis, killing hundreds — estimates range from several hundred to over a thousand deaths.

The project

The tunnel, roughly three miles long, was built from 1930 by a contractor for a subsidiary of Union Carbide to divert water from the New River through Hawks Nest Mountain to a power plant supplying an alloy plant at Alloy, West Virginia. The workforce, largely migrant labourers, many of them Black workers recruited from the South, drilled through sandstone that proved to be nearly pure silica. Dry drilling generated dense dust; workers were given few or no respirators, though company engineers and supervisors visiting the tunnel reportedly wore masks.

Deaths and aftermath

Many men sickened within months and died of acute silicosis, a rapidly progressive form of the disease that usually takes years to develop. The companies settled lawsuits quietly and cheaply; bodies of some workers were buried in unmarked graves. Congressional hearings in 1936 brought national attention, and the scandal — publicised by writers including Muriel Rukeyser in her poetry sequence The Book of the Dead (1938) — helped spur reforms in occupational health and workers' compensation law, though major federal mine-safety legislation came only decades later.
