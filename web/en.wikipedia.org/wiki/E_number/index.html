E number

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

E numbers are codes for substances permitted as food additives for use within the European Union and the European Free Trade Association. The "E" stands for "Europe". E numbers appear on food labels across Europe, and the scheme, administered following safety evaluation by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), is also referenced in Australia and New Zealand, where the same codes are used without the E prefix in some contexts.

Classification

Numbers are grouped by function:

E100–E199: colours (e.g. E100 curcumin, E120 cochineal, E150 caramel). E200–E299: preservatives (e.g. E220 sulphur dioxide, E250 sodium nitrite). E300–E399: antioxidants and acidity regulators (e.g. E300 ascorbic acid, E330 citric acid). E400–E499: thickeners, stabilisers and emulsifiers (e.g. E412 guar gum, E471 mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids). E500–E599: acidity regulators and anti-caking agents. E600–E699: flavour enhancers (e.g. E621 monosodium glutamate). E900s: glazing agents and gases; E950 onwards: sweeteners (e.g. E951 aspartame, E960 steviol glycosides).

Authorisation and safety

An additive receives an E number only after authorisation, which requires toxicological assessment and usually specifies maximum permitted levels and the foods in which it may be used; many approvals specify an acceptable daily intake. EFSA periodically re-evaluates older additives, and some have been withdrawn or restricted — titanium dioxide (E171) was banned as a food additive in the EU in 2022 after genotoxicity concerns could not be excluded.

Public perception

Despite the authorisation process, E numbers acquired a negative popular image, associated in the public mind with "artificial" ingredients; some manufacturers market "E-number-free" products while using the same substances labelled by their chemical names. Many E-numbered substances are naturally occurring compounds such as citric acid, lecithin and beeswax.
