Wylam Dilly is one of the two earliest surviving steam locomotives in the world; the other is its sister engine Puffing Billy. The two were built about 1813–1815 by William Hedley, Jonathan Forster and Timothy Hackworth for Christopher Blackett, owner of Wylam Colliery near Newcastle upon Tyne in Northumberland, England, to haul coal wagons on the Wylam wagonway to staithes on the River Tyne at Lemington.

Design

The locomotives were built to test Hedley's experiments demonstrating that smooth iron wheels on smooth rails generated sufficient adhesion to haul trains, refuting the assumption that rack-and-pinion drive was necessary. Wylam Dilly began as an experimental vehicle on a test carriage before being mounted on its own wheels. Its vertical cylinders drove the wheels through beams and gearing. The engines famously worked past the house where George Stephenson, born at Wylam, had grown up.

Working life and preservation

The pair initially proved too heavy for the cast-iron plate rails, breaking them, and were rebuilt with eight wheels to spread the load, later simplified to four as rails improved. Wylam Dilly worked at the colliery until 1862. Both locomotives survive: Puffing Billy is in the Science Museum, London, and Wylam Dilly is displayed at the National Museum of Scotland in Edinburgh, making them the oldest and second-oldest surviving locomotives, predating Stephenson's famous Rocket by more than a decade.
