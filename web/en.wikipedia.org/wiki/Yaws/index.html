Yaws

Yaws
Cause: Treponema pallidum subsp. pertenue
Transmission: Skin-to-skin contact
Areas: Tropics of Africa, Asia, the Pacific
Treatment: Single-dose azithromycin; benzathine penicillin

Yaws is a chronic tropical infection of the skin, bones, and joints caused by the spirochete bacterium Treponema pallidum subspecies pertenue. It is transmitted by direct skin contact with an infected lesion, most often among children in warm, humid rural areas. Yaws belongs to the group of endemic (non-venereal) treponematoses, together with bejel and pinta, and is related to syphilis, which is caused by a nearly identical subspecies.

Clinical course

After an incubation of roughly three weeks, a primary lesion — the "mother yaw" — appears at the inoculation site: a painless papilloma that can grow into a yellow-crusted, raspberry-like sore (the name derives from a Carib word, yaya, for a sore; the raspberry resemblance also earned it the French name frambesia). Secondary lesions spread over the skin, and painful softening of the bones of the fingers (dactylitis) and long bones may follow. Without treatment, about ten percent of cases progress to destructive late disease with gummas, bone deformity such as "sabre tibia", and the mutilating facial condition called gangosa.

Eradication efforts

Mass treatment campaigns run by the WHO and UNICEF in the 1950s, using injectable penicillin, cut global prevalence by some 95 percent, but the disease resurged as surveillance lapsed. India was certified free of yaws in 2016. In 2012 the WHO adopted the Morges strategy, based on a study in Papua New Guinea showing that a single oral dose of the antibiotic azithromycin is as effective as penicillin injection; the strategy aims at total community treatment followed by targeted treatment of residual cases, with a global eradication target that has since been reset to 2030. Diagnosis is by clinical appearance and treponemal serology; molecular testing can distinguish the subspecies.
