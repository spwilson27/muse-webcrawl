Malay (Bahasa Melayu) is an Austronesian language spoken principally in the Malay Peninsula, eastern Sumatra, coastal Borneo and neighbouring areas. Standardised varieties of Malay are the official languages of Malaysia, Brunei and Singapore, and — under the name Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia) — of Indonesia. Together the standard forms are spoken by some 200 million people, making the language one of the most widely spoken in the world.

History

Old Malay inscriptions in an Indic-derived script date from the 7th century CE, in the era of the Srivijaya empire. Classical Malay flourished as a language of trade, diplomacy and Islamic scholarship from the 15th century under the Malacca Sultanate, written in the Arabic-derived Jawi script. Malay's role as the archipelago's lingua franca led Indonesian nationalists in 1928 to pledge it, as Indonesian, as the unifying national language.

Classification and features

Malay belongs to the Malayic branch of Malayo-Polynesian. It is an agglutinative language with extensive affixation (prefixes such as meN-, ber-, di- and the suffixes -kan and -i) used to derive verbs and nouns, no grammatical gender or verb conjugation for tense, and a system of classifiers for counting nouns. Word order is generally subject–verb–object. Dutch, Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian, Portuguese, Chinese languages and English have all contributed loanwords.

Standard varieties

Malaysian (Bahasa Malaysia) and Indonesian were deliberately kept harmonised through joint language councils and a common spelling system adopted in 1972, though vocabulary differences have grown. The two standard forms remain highly mutually intelligible.
