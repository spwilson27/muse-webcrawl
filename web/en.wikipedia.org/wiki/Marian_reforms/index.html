Marian reforms

A legionary of the reformed professional army

The Marian reforms were a series of reforms to the military structure of the Roman Republic in the late 2nd century BC, traditionally attributed to Gaius Marius, who was elected consul a record seven times and consul in 107 BC after victory in the Jugurthine War. Historians now see many changes as part of a longer development, but Marius's reforms are credited with transforming the Roman army into a professional standing force.

Background

Under the earlier system, the army was a militia of property-owning citizens, recruited by census class, who equipped themselves and served only for the campaigning season. By the late 2nd century BC, Roman territory and wars had grown, the middle classes were shrinking, and armies were harder to raise — a crisis highlighted by the disasters of the Cimbrian War.

The reforms

Marius opened recruitment to the capite censi, the landless poor, and had the state pay for their equipment, standardized across the army: the gladius sword, pilum javelin, scutum shield, and segmented armour. Soldiers enlisted for 16 (later 20 or 25) years and were promised land on retirement. The army was reorganized around the cohort (480 men) as the tactical unit, grouped into legions of ten cohorts, replacing the earlier maniple system. Generals' baggage trains were reduced by having soldiers carry their own kit, earning them the nickname "Marius's mules". He also granted citizenship to Italian allies who served and standardized the eagle (aquila) as the legion's symbol.

Consequences

The reforms created a formidable professional army that enabled Rome's conquests, but they shifted soldiers' loyalty from the state to their generals, who became responsible for their retirement benefits. This personal allegiance contributed directly to the rise of warlords such as Sulla, Pompey, and Julius Caesar, and to the fall of the Republic.
