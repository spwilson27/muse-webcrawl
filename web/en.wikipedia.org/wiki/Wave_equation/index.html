Wave equation

Wave equation
TypeLinear second-order hyperbolic partial differential equation
Key figuresJean le Rond d'Alembert, Leonhard Euler, Daniel Bernoulli
ApplicationsAcoustics, electromagnetism, seismology, fluid dynamics

The wave equation is a second-order linear partial differential equation for the description of waves — including mechanical waves in strings, water and seismic media, and electromagnetic waves such as light. In its simplest form, for a scalar quantity u depending on position x and time t:

∂²u/∂t² = c² ∇²u

where c is a constant, the speed of propagation of the wave. The one-dimensional equation was derived and solved by Jean le Rond d'Alembert in 1746, and extended by Euler and Daniel Bernoulli in the study of vibrating strings.

Solutions

In one space dimension, d'Alembert's formula shows that the general solution is the sum of an arbitrary right-travelling and an arbitrary left-travelling wave of fixed shape: u(x,t) = f(x − ct) + g(x + ct). Solutions in higher dimensions exhibit refraction, diffraction, reflection and dispersion phenomena; plane waves and spherical waves are standard building blocks, and finite-energy solutions propagate with finite speed, respecting causality. Maxwell's equations imply that each component of the electric and magnetic fields satisfies a wave equation with c equal to the speed of light, one of the great unifying results of nineteenth-century physics.

Variants and applications

Related equations include the inhomogeneous wave equation (with a source term), the damped wave equation, the Klein–Gordon equation of relativistic quantum theory, and nonlinear generalisations used in optics and fluid mechanics. Numerical methods such as finite differences routinely solve the equation for seismic imaging ("reverse time migration"), architectural acoustics and antenna design.
