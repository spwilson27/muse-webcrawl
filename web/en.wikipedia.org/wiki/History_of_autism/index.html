History of autism

The history of autism concerns the development of the concept of autism and autism spectrum disorder, from the coining of the word autismus by Eugen Bleuler in 1911, through the clinical descriptions of Leo Kanner and Hans Asperger in the 1940s, to the modern spectrum concept, changing diagnostic criteria, debates over causes, and the rise of autistic self-advocacy and the neurodiversity movement.

Early uses of the term

The Swiss psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler coined the term "autism" (from Greek autos, "self") around 1911 to describe a symptom of schizophrenia — withdrawal into an inner world of fantasy. In 1926, the Soviet child psychiatrist Grunya Sukhareva published a description of six boys with what she called "schizoid psychopathy", a profile strikingly similar to later descriptions of autism, though her work was long overlooked in the West.

Kanner and Asperger

In 1943, Leo Kanner at Johns Hopkins University published "Autistic Disturbances of Affective Contact", describing 11 children with a distinctive syndrome he called early infantile autism, characterised by social aloofness, insistence on sameness, and language peculiarities. Independently, in Vienna, Hans Asperger published his 1944 thesis on "autistic psychopathy", describing verbally fluent children with intense interests and social difficulties. Asperger's work, written in German during wartime, only became widely known after Lorna Wing introduced the term "Asperger syndrome" in 1981 and Uta Frith translated his thesis in 1991. Later research raised questions about Asperger's conduct under the Nazi regime, and the eponym has since fallen out of official diagnostic use.

Mid-20th century: psychogenic theories

For decades, autism was classified as a form of childhood schizophrenia, and psychogenic theories blamed cold parenting — Bruno Bettelheim popularised the damaging "refrigerator mother" idea. Bernard Rimland's 1964 book Infantile Autism helped shift thinking toward a neurobiological basis. Behavioural treatment, notably Ivar Lovaas's applied behaviour analysis, emerged in the 1970s, though some of its early methods and goals later drew ethical criticism.

Diagnostic evolution

Autism was first listed as a distinct diagnosis in the DSM-III (1980) as "infantile autism" under pervasive developmental disorders. The DSM-IV (1994) added Asperger disorder and PDD-NOS. Lorna Wing's concept of an "autistic spectrum" and the "triad of impairments" broadened understanding beyond Kanner's narrow picture. In 2013, the DSM-5 merged the subcategories into a single diagnosis, autism spectrum disorder, graded by support needs; the ICD-11 followed a similar approach.

Prevalence and causes debate

Reported prevalence rose from about 4–5 per 10,000 in early studies to around 1 in 100 children in recent estimates, attributed mainly to broader criteria, greater awareness, and diagnostic substitution. Research established a strong genetic contribution. In 1998, Andrew Wakefield published a fraudulent study linking the MMR vaccine to autism; it was retracted in 2010, Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register, and large studies consistently found no link, though the claim had lasting effects on vaccination rates.

Advocacy and neurodiversity

From the 1990s, autistic adults built a self-advocacy movement, founding organisations such as the Autism Network International (1992). Sociologist Judy Singer coined "neurodiversity" in the late 1990s, framing autism as a form of human variation rather than purely a disorder. Debates continue over identity-first language ("autistic person"), functioning labels, and the goals of intervention, alongside research into co-occurring conditions, adult diagnosis, and autistic women and girls, who were historically under-recognised.
