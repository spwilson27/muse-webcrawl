Penal reform

DefinitionEfforts to reform penal systems, prisons and sentencing
Early reformersCesare Beccaria, John Howard, Elizabeth Fry
Key themesProportionality, rehabilitation, humane conditions, alternatives to custody

John Howard's inspections of 18th-century gaols launched the modern penal reform movement.

Penal reform refers to efforts to improve the penal system—the institutions and laws governing punishment, especially prisons—and to make punishment more just, humane and effective. Reform campaigns have addressed sentencing law, prison conditions, the treatment of particular groups (women, juveniles, people with mental illness), and alternatives to imprisonment.

History

The Italian jurist Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments (1764) argued for proportionate, certain and publicly legislated penalties and against torture and capital punishment. In England, John Howard's The State of the Prisons (1777), based on inspections across Europe, exposed disease, filth and corruption; the Quaker Elizabeth Fry campaigned in the 19th century for women prisoners and their children. The penitentiary itself—promoted through the separate and silent systems in the United States—was an early reform experiment later criticised as psychologically cruel.

Modern themes

Contemporary penal reform debates centre on mass incarceration (especially in the United States), racial and socioeconomic disparities, overcrowding, restorative justice, rehabilitation and re-entry, decriminalisation of some offences, and international standards such as the UN's Mandela Rules (2015) for the treatment of prisoners. Organisations such as Penal Reform International and the Howard League for Penal Reform carry the earlier reform tradition into the present.
