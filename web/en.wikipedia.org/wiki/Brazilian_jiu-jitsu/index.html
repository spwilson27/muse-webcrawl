Brazilian jiu-jitsu (BJJ; Portuguese: jiu-jitsu brasileiro) is a martial art and combat sport based on ground fighting (ne-waza) and submission holds. It focuses on taking an opponent to the ground, gaining a dominant position, and using joint locks and chokeholds to force a submission ("tap out"). BJJ was developed in Brazil in the early 20th century from the Kodokan judo taught by Mitsuyo Maeda, a Japanese judoka who emigrated to Brazil, and was refined above all by the Gracie family, who promoted it through no-holds-barred challenge matches (vale tudo).

The art's central premise is that a smaller, weaker person can defeat a larger opponent by using leverage, position, and technique rather than strength. Its effectiveness in the early Ultimate Fighting Championship events — where Royce Gracie, of slight build, submitted larger opponents in 1993–94 — made BJJ a foundational discipline of modern mixed martial arts and transformed martial arts training worldwide.

History

Mitsuyo Maeda, a prizefighting judoka of the Kodokan, settled in Brazil in 1914–17 and taught Carlos Gracie, son of his friend Gastão Gracie, in Belém. Carlos taught his brothers, including the frail youngest, Hélio Gracie, who — legend holds — adapted techniques to rely on leverage so they worked for a small practitioner, developing the family's guard-centred ground game. The Gracies issued open challenges to fighters of other styles for decades; Hélio's famous matches included a 1951 loss to judoka Masahiko Kimura (whose shoulder lock is still called the "Kimura" in BJJ) and a marathon bout with his former student Waldemar Santana in 1955. A parallel lineage developed under Luiz França and Oswaldo Fadda, whose students came from Rio's poorer districts.

In the 1970s–90s, members of the family (notably Rickson Gracie in vale tudo and Rorion Gracie's co-founding of the UFC in the United States in 1993) carried the art abroad. Royce Gracie's victories at UFC 1, 2, and 4 demonstrated grappling's importance to a global audience and ignited worldwide demand for BJJ instruction.

Technique and training

Training centres on live sparring ("rolling") and drilling. The positional hierarchy progresses from inferior positions (bottom of mount or side control) through neutral positions such as the guard — in which the bottom player controls the opponent with the legs — to dominant positions (mount, back control) from which submissions are applied: joint locks (armbar, kimura, americana, leg locks including heel hooks) and strangles/chokes (rear naked choke, triangle choke, cross-collar choke, bow-and-arrow choke). Practice is divided between gi training, using the heavy jacket's grips and lapels, and no-gi training in shorts and a rash guard, with somewhat different techniques (and greater emphasis on leg locks and wrestling-style takedowns).

Belt system

Adult ranks progress through white, blue, purple, brown, and black belts, typically with degrees (stripes) at each level; black belts advance by degrees over years of teaching and practice, leading to coral (red-and-black, then red-and-white) belts and ultimately the red belt, reserved for the art's grand masters. Promotion standards are informal and vary between academies, traditionally requiring years of mat time at each belt — a black belt commonly takes a decade — and demonstrated skill in sparring.

Competition

Sport BJJ is governed in large part by the International Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Federation (IBJJF), whose World Championship (the "Mundials," held in California since 2007) and events such as the Pan Championship and European Championship award points for positional control and advantages, with immediate victory by submission. Important variations include submission-only and no-gi formats, the Abu Dhabi Combat Club (ADCC) Submission Fighting World Championship, and professional superfight promotions. The rules generally prohibit strikes and certain dangerous techniques (such as heel hooks in gi competition and reaping the knee at lower levels).

Relation to MMA

BJJ is one of the core disciplines of mixed martial arts, alongside striking arts and wrestling; virtually all elite MMA fighters train in it for ground defence and submissions. Within MMA, BJJ techniques are adapted for the presence of strikes — the guard is used more cautiously, and top control is prized — while occasional specialists, from Royce Gracie to Demian Maia and Charles Oliveira, have built championship careers primarily on jiu-jitsu.
