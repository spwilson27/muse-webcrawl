Foederati

Latinfoederati (sing. foederatus)
Meaning"Federated" peoples bound by treaty (foedus)
PeriodRoman Republic and Empire, esp. 4th–5th centuries AD

Foederati (Latin for "federated") were peoples and cities bound by a treaty, called a foedus, with Rome. During the Roman Republic, the term described the states in Italy that were ultimately consolidated into Roman Italy. During the Roman Empire, it came to refer to barbarian peoples who were permitted to settle within the imperial borders in exchange for providing military contingents to the Roman army.

History

In the Republic, socii and foederati supplied troops under treaty obligations rather than serving as Roman legionaries. In late antiquity, the practice expanded dramatically. After the Gothic crossing of the Danube in 376 and the Roman defeat at the Battle of Adrianople in 378, the emperor Theodosius I settled Goths within the Balkans under a treaty of 382 that is often taken as a turning point: the Goths kept their own leaders and a measure of autonomy while owing military service to the empire.

By the fifth century, entire peoples — Franks, Burgundians, Vandals, Ostrogoths and Visigoths — were settled as foederati across the western provinces. Their leaders, such as Alaric I, Stilicho (himself of Vandal descent) and later Theodoric the Great, shaped imperial politics, and foederati armies formed a large part of Rome's military strength in the West. In the East, the empire more often paid subsidies and integrated groups into regular commands, though foederati also served there.

Significance

Historians debate how far the foederati system represented pragmatic integration of newcomers and how far it hollowed out Roman authority, since settlement under native kings and laws created states within the state that became the successor kingdoms of the post-Roman West.
