Antibiotic resistance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Antibiotic resistance (also antimicrobial resistance, AMR) is the ability of bacteria to survive and grow despite antibiotics that would normally kill them or stop their growth. It evolves by natural selection whenever antibiotics are used — in human medicine, farming and the environment — and is considered one of the greatest threats to global health, making infections harder to treat and surgery, Chemotherapy and transplants riskier.

Mechanisms and spread

Bacteria resist by destroying the drug (as beta-lactamases destroy penicillins), pumping it out, altering its target, or shielding themselves in biofilms. Resistance genes spread between bacteria on plasmids. Notorious organisms include MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), drug-resistant tuberculosis, and carbapenem-resistant gut bacteria. A landmark 2019 study estimated bacterial AMR was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths that year.

Response

Countermeasures include antibiotic stewardship (using the right drug, dose and duration, and avoiding antibiotics for viral infections), infection prevention and vaccination, rapid diagnostics, surveillance, and developing new antibiotics and alternatives such as phage therapy — though the commercial pipeline for new antibiotics remains weak.
