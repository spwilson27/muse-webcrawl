Jig

Traditional Irish dancing, in which the jig is a central form.

The jig (Irish: port, Scottish Gaelic: port-cruinn) is a form of lively folk dance in compound meter, as well as the accompanying dance tune. It developed in 16th-century England and was quickly adopted in Ireland and Scotland, where it was extensively adapted. Today it is most associated with Irish dance music, Scottish country dance, and the Québécois and Métis traditions in Canada.

Music

Jigs are written in compound meters such as 6/8, 9/8, or 12/8. In Irish traditional music, varieties include the double jig (6/8), the slip jig (9/8), and the single jig. Jigs are played on instruments such as the fiddle, flute, tin whistle, accordion, and concertina, and a tune is typically built from two eight-bar parts, each repeated. In Scottish tradition, jigs in 6/8 are common in country dancing and pipe music.

Dance

In Irish stepdance, jigs are danced in both soft shoes (light jigs and slip jigs) and hard shoes (the treble or heavy jig). Historically the jig was a popular theatrical and social dance in England — the Elizabethan clown Will Kempe famously danced a jig from London to Norwich in 1600 — before becoming a staple of the folk traditions of Ireland and Scotland.
