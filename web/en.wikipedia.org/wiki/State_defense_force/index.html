State defense force

State defense forces (SDFs) in the United States are military units that operate under the sole authority of a state government. They are authorised by state and federal law — principally 32 U.S.C. § 109 — and are under the command of the governor of the state through the adjutant general. Unlike the National Guard, state defense forces cannot be federalised or called into federal service, and they receive no federal funding; they are distinct from both the organised militia of the National Guard and from private, unrecognised militia groups.

History

State guards trace their origin to the state home guards raised during the First and especially the Second World War, when National Guard units were federalised for overseas service and states required forces to guard infrastructure and respond to emergencies at home. After 1945 many were stood down, but federal law of 1955 authorised permanent peacetime forces, and interest revived after the Cold War and again after the September 11 attacks and major hurricanes, when SDFs supplied medical, communications, search-and-rescue and logistics support.

Organisation and roles

About twenty states (plus Puerto Rico) maintain active state defense forces, including the Texas State Guard, the New York Guard, the California State Guard, the Georgia State Defense Force and the Virginia Defense Force. Members are overwhelmingly unpaid volunteers who serve part-time; they wear uniforms (distinct from those of federal forces), hold state military ranks and train to state standards. Typical missions include disaster response and relief, medical and chaplain support, communications, cyber defence assistance, ceremonial duties and augmenting the National Guard during state emergencies. A few states maintain naval militias or air components within their SDF structures. Because members are state-only personnel, they generally lack federal veterans' benefits and federal legal protections that attach to federal service, though states may extend state benefits.
