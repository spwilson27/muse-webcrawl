Chaparral

Chaparral is a shrubland plant community found primarily in the U.S. state of California, in southern Oregon, and in the northern part of the Baja California Peninsula in Mexico. It is shaped by a Mediterranean climate — mild wet winters and hot dry summers — and by infrequent, high-intensity wildfires, to which its plants are adapted.

Vegetation

Chaparral is dominated by dense, evergreen, sclerophyllous shrubs with hard, leathery leaves that conserve water. Characteristic species include chamise (Adenostoma fasciculatum), many species of manzanita (Arctostaphylos) and Ceanothus, scrub oak, and toyon. Most plants are between one and four metres tall.

Fire ecology

Chaparral plants survive fire by resprouting from protected buds in root crowns and burls, or by seeding: many species have seeds whose germination is triggered by heat, smoke or charred wood. Crown fires burning whole stands at intervals of roughly 30 to 150 years are part of the natural regime, but fires that recur too frequently — usually ignited by people in the Santa Ana wind season — can convert chaparral to invasive grassland, while long-term fire exclusion is far less damaging than was once assumed.

Global analogues

Similar shrublands occur in the other Mediterranean-climate regions of the world: the maquis and garrigue of the Mediterranean Basin, the matorral of central Chile, the fynbos of South Africa, and the kwongan and mallee of southwestern and southern Australia.
