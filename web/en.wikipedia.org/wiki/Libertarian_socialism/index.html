Libertarian socialism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Libertarian socialism is a political philosophy that combines socialism — social ownership of the means of production and opposition to capitalism — with a libertarian commitment to freedom, federalism and the abolition or radical limitation of the state. It encompasses anarchism in its collectivist, communist and syndicalist forms, together with anti-authoritarian Marxist currents such as council communism and parts of the New Left.

Libertarian socialists argue that both capitalist property and state bureaucracy are systems of domination, and that a free society must be built from below through workplaces and communities governed directly by their members. Thinkers associated with the tradition include Proudhon, Bakunin, Kropotkin, Rosa Luxemburg (in part), and in recent decades Noam Chomsky. The tradition's practical reference points include the Paris Commune, the Ukrainian Makhnovshchina, revolutionary Spain in 1936, and contemporary experiments in cooperative and municipal governance — though the interpretation of each is contested.

Relation to other socialisms

Libertarian socialism defines itself against both social democracy, which it criticises for managing capitalism through a centralised state, and Leninism, whose vanguard party and transitional dictatorship it predicts — following Bakunin — will produce a new ruling class. Within the tradition, anarchists and libertarian Marxists dispute the need for any transitional state and the role of markets versus planning in a socialist economy.
