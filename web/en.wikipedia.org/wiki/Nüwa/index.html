Nüwa

Nüwa (Chinese: 女媧), also read Nügua, is a mother goddess figure in Chinese mythology, credited with creating humanity and repairing the pillars of heaven. She is often depicted with a human head and the body of a serpent, and is regarded as the sister and wife of the culture hero Fuxi.


Nüwa, holding the compass, as depicted in a Han-dynasty style portrait.

Creation of humanity

In the myth recorded in the Fengsu Tongyi (2nd century AD), Nüwa moulded human beings from yellow earth, fashioning them carefully one by one; when this proved too slow, she dipped a rope in mud and flicked it, the droplets becoming the common people — an explanation for the difference between nobles and commoners. She is also credited with instituting marriage.

Repairing the sky

In the most famous myth, told in the Huainanzi, the god of water Gonggong smashed his head against Mount Buzhou, one of the pillars holding up the sky, tilting heaven and unleashing fire and flood. Nüwa smelted five coloured stones to patch the sky, cut the legs off a giant tortoise (Ao) to serve as new pillars, killed a black dragon, and dammed the waters with reed ashes, restoring cosmic order. The episode is retold in the Ming-dynasty novel Investiture of the Gods, in which Nüwa also sends the fox spirit Daji to bring about the fall of the corrupt King Zhou of Shang.

Worship

Nüwa was venerated as a high goddess and ancestress, and temples to her — notably the Wahuanggong complex in She County, Hebei — remain sites of pilgrimage. Together with Fuxi she is counted among the legendary Three Sovereigns of China's prehistory.
