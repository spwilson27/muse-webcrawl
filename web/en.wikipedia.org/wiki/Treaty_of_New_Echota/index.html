The Treaty of New Echota was an agreement signed on 29 December 1835 at New Echota, the capital of the Cherokee Nation in present-day Georgia, by which the Cherokee ceded all their remaining lands east of the Mississippi River to the United States in exchange for five million dollars and new land in Indian Territory (present-day Oklahoma). It provided the legal basis for the forced removal of the Cherokee in 1838–39, the journey remembered as the Trail of Tears.

Background

Following the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and Georgia's extension of state law over Cherokee territory — accelerated by a gold rush near Dahlonega — the Cherokee majority under Principal Chief John Ross resisted removal, winning a favourable Supreme Court ruling in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) that the federal government declined to enforce. A minority "Treaty Party", led by Major Ridge, his son John Ridge, Elias Boudinot and Stand Watie, concluded that removal was inevitable and that negotiating terms was the only way to preserve the nation.

The treaty and its ratification

The treaty was negotiated with federal commissioner John F. Schermerhorn and signed by the Treaty Party without authorisation from the Cherokee national government; nearly the entire Cherokee population signed petitions against it. Despite this, the United States Senate ratified it in May 1836 by a single vote. Cherokee law prescribed death for anyone ceding land without authority, and in 1839 Major Ridge, John Ridge and Boudinot were assassinated.

Removal

When the deadline for voluntary emigration passed in May 1838, federal troops under General Winfield Scott rounded up some 16,000 Cherokee into internment camps and removed them west in detachments through the winter of 1838–39. Roughly 4,000 people died of disease, exposure and hardship — a catastrophe the Cherokee call nu na da ul tsun yi, "the place where they cried".
