Tax refund

A tax refund is a payment made by a tax authority to a taxpayer who has paid more tax during the year than their final assessed liability, most commonly because withholding or estimated payments exceeded the tax computed on the annual return. Refunds may also arise from refundable tax credits such as the Earned Income Tax Credit, which can pay out more than the taxpayer's liability.

Process

In the United States, most individual taxpayers file a return in the first months of the year; the IRS issues most refunds within about 21 days of accepting an electronically filed return, typically by direct deposit. Refunds for returns claiming the EITC or Additional Child Tax Credit are delayed by law (the PATH Act) until mid-February as an anti-fraud measure. In the United Kingdom, PAYE overpayments are refunded automatically or after a claim, often flagged by a P800 calculation. Many countries similarly reconcile withholding against annual assessments.

Debate

Although refunds are popular — in the US the average federal refund has recently been around $3,000 — economists note that a large refund represents an interest-free loan to the government, and some taxpayers could instead reduce withholding to receive the money during the year. Others treat over-withholding as a form of forced saving. Refund anticipation products and identity-theft refund fraud are recurring policy concerns.
