James Dewar

Born20 September 1842, Kincardine, Scotland
Died27 March 1923, London, England
Known forDewar flask (vacuum flask); liquefaction of gases
AwardsRumford Medal, Copley Medal, knighthood (1904)

Sir James Dewar FRS (20 September 1842 – 27 March 1923) was a Scottish chemist and physicist. He is best known for his invention of the Dewar flask — the vacuum-insulated vessel commercialised as the Thermos flask — and for his pioneering work in low-temperature physics.

Career

Dewar became professor of experimental philosophy at Cambridge in 1875 and Fullerian Professor of Chemistry at the Royal Institution in London in 1877, a post he held for 46 years. His Friday Evening Discourses at the Royal Institution were famous demonstrations of experimental chemistry and physics.

Low-temperature work

Working with ever more efficient refrigeration, Dewar liquefied oxygen and air in quantity and, in 1898, became the first person to liquefy hydrogen, reaching temperatures near 20 kelvin. In 1891 he built apparatus to produce liquid oxygen commercially. He also studied the properties of matter at extreme cold, investigated spectroscopy, and with Henri Moissan attempted (unsuccessfully) to liquefy helium before Heike Kamerlingh Onnes achieved it in 1908.

Other work

With Frederick Abel, Dewar developed cordite, a smokeless propellant, leading to a celebrated patent dispute with Alfred Nobel. He proposed a ring structure for benzene (Dewar benzene) and studied high-temperature chemistry with the oxy-hydrogen flame.
