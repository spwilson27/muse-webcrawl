Ancient Canaanite religion

Ancient Canaanite religion encompasses the polytheistic religious beliefs and practices of the Canaanites of the southern Levant during the Bronze and Iron Ages (roughly 3500–637 BCE). It was the indigenous religion of the region whose city-states included Ugarit, Ebla, and Byblos, and it profoundly influenced the Phoenician religion, Aramaean religion, and the early development of ancient Israelite religion.

Sources

Knowledge of Canaanite religion derives from archaeological evidence — temples, stelae, figurines, and cult objects — and from cuneiform tablets discovered at Ugarit (modern Ras Shamra) from 1929 onward. The Ugaritic texts, including the Baal Cycle, the Legend of Keret, and the Tale of Aqhat, preserve myths, rituals, offering lists, and incantations. The Hebrew Bible, though polemical toward Canaanite cults, also preserves valuable information about their gods, festivals, and practices.

Pantheon

The supreme god was El, the aged and benevolent creator, father of gods and humanity, who presided over the divine council. His consort was Asherah, mother goddess associated with sacred trees and poles. El's son Baal (Hadad), the storm god who brings rain and fertility, became increasingly prominent and is celebrated in the Baal Cycle for his victories over Yam (Sea) and Mot (Death). Other deities included Anat, the violent maiden goddess of war; Astarte, goddess of sexuality and war; Dagon, god of grain; Resheph, god of plague and war; and Shapash, the sun goddess.

Worship and practice

Worship centred on temples staffed by priests, sacrifices of animals and produce, seasonal festivals tied to the agricultural cycle, and cultic installations such as high places (bamot), standing stones (massebot), and sacred groves. Ritual texts from Ugarit describe royal sacrifices and liturgies lasting days. Practices such as divination, votive offerings, and funerary cults honouring deceased ancestors (the Rephaim) were also important.

Legacy

Canaanite religion was the matrix from which Israelite monotheism emerged: El was identified with Yahweh, and much Canaanite poetic and cultic vocabulary entered the Hebrew scriptures, while biblical writers simultaneously waged fierce polemics against Baal and Asherah worship. Through the Phoenicians, Canaanite deities spread across the Mediterranean world.
