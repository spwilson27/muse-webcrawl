Unwanted catch being returned to the sea from a fishing vessel.

Discards are the portion of a fishing catch that is not retained on board but is thrown back into the sea, often dead or dying. Discarding is part of the broader problem of bycatch — the incidental capture of non-target species and undersized or unmarketable individuals of target species.

Causes

Fishers discard catch for several reasons: the fish may be below the legal minimum landing size; quota for that species may already be exhausted ("quota discards"); the species may have low market value; the hold may be reserved for higher-value fish ("high grading"); or the catch may be damaged or prohibited. Fishing gear selectivity strongly influences discard rates — bottom trawls generally discard far more than passive gears such as pots and longlines.

Scale and impact

Estimates by the Food and Agriculture Organization have put global discards at roughly 9 to 10 million tonnes annually in recent assessments, down from estimates of around 27 million tonnes in the 1990s as gear, regulation and market practices improved. Most discarded fish do not survive, so discards represent a waste of marine resources and can distort stock assessments and hinder recovery of depleted populations. Survival rates vary widely by species — many crustaceans, sharks, skates and some flatfish survive discarding at appreciable rates.

Policy

Norway and Iceland pioneered discard bans, requiring vessels to land all catch. The European Union's reformed Common Fisheries Policy introduced a "landing obligation", phased in between 2015 and 2019, requiring catches of regulated species to be landed and counted against quota. Complementary approaches include more selective gear (such as sorting grids and larger mesh sizes), real-time area closures, remote electronic monitoring with cameras, and markets for formerly discarded species.
