Trans-Saharan trade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trans-Saharan trade is trade between sub-Saharan Africa and North Africa that requires a crossing of the Sahara desert. Although it existed in prehistoric times, the trade peaked from the 8th century until the early 17th century, when camel caravans linked the Mediterranean coast and North African entrepôts with the kingdoms of the West African savanna and forest zones.

Commodities

The principal goods carried north were gold, ivory, and enslaved people, while southward caravans brought salt — mined in slabs at desert deposits such as Taghaza and Taoudenni — together with cloth, beads, copper, horses and manufactured goods. Salt was so valuable in the savanna that it sometimes exchanged weight-for-weight with gold dust. West African gold from Bambuk, Bure and the Akan forest reached Europe and the Middle East largely through these routes, supplying much of the medieval world's gold.

Routes and entrepôts

Major routes crossed the desert to oasis towns such as Sijilmasa, Ghadames, Agadez and the salt mines, and terminated in the great trading cities of the western Sudan, including Timbuktu, Gao and Djenné on the Niger Bend, and later Kano and the Hausa cities. Caravans of hundreds — sometimes thousands — of camels travelled between staging posts, guided by desert peoples such as the Tuareg.

Camel and Islam

The spread of the dromedary camel, well established by late antiquity, made regular desert crossings feasible. Trade carried Islam south with it: merchants and scholars spread the religion through the western Sudan, underpinning the rise of the Ghana, Mali and Songhai empires and making Timbuktu a centre of Islamic learning.

Decline

The trade declined after the Moroccan invasion of Songhai in 1591, the diversion of gold flows to European ships on the Guinea coast, and the growth of the Atlantic slave trade, though caravan trade in salt and other goods continued on a reduced scale into the colonial era.
