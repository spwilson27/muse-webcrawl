Tokyo Story

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The elderly Hirayama couple seated quietly in their children's Tokyo apartment, waiting with their suitcases packed for kindness that comes late]

Tokyo Story (Japanese: 東京物語, Tōkyō Monogatari) is a 1953 Japanese drama film by Yasujirō Ozu, widely considered the director's masterpiece and one of the greatest films ever made — it topped the Sight and Sound directors' poll in 2012. An elderly couple from the port town of Onomichi travel to Tokyo to visit their grown children, who are too busy for them; only their widowed daughter-in-law Noriko (Setsuko Hara) makes time. After the journey home, the mother falls ill and dies, and the family gathers — briefly — too late.

Ozu's 'tatami shot' (camera at the height of a person seated on a mat), his ellipses (the wedding, the death happen off-screen), his still-life 'pillow shots' and his 50mm domestic geometry create cinema's most devastating quietness: nothing much happens, and everything happens.

Reception

Too 'Japanese' for export in its day (the studio withheld it abroad), Tokyo Story reached the West in the 1970s to immediate canonisation. Its treatment of ageing, filial neglect and acceptance has made it the reference point for family drama from Edward Yang to contemporary slow cinema; the 2012 directors' poll placed it above Citizen Kane.

The Noriko trilogy

Tokyo Story completes the informal Noriko trilogy with Late Spring (1949) and Early Summer (1951), all starring Setsuko Hara as Noriko — differently written each time. Ozu, who never married, said he understood families by observing them; his grave in Kamakura bears a single character: mu, nothingness.
