Neoplasm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A neoplasm is a type of abnormal and excessive growth of tissue. The growth of a neoplasm is uncoordinated with that of the normal surrounding tissue, and persists in growing abnormally, even if the original trigger is removed. This abnormal growth usually forms a mass, which may be called a tumour or tumor. The World Health Organization classifies neoplasms into four main groups: benign neoplasms, in situ neoplasms, malignant neoplasms, and neoplasms of uncertain or unknown behavior. Malignant neoplasms are also simply known as cancers and are the focus of the field of oncology.

Prior to the abnormal growth of tissue, such as neoplasia, cells often undergo an abnormal pattern of growth, such as metaplasia or dysplasia. However, metaplasia or dysplasia does not always progress to neoplasia and can occur in other conditions as well. The word neoplasm is from Ancient Greek νέος- neo, 'new', and πλάσμα plasma, 'formation, creation'.

Types

  • Benign neoplasms — self-limited growths that do not invade surrounding tissue or spread to distant sites; examples include uterine fibroids (leiomyomas), skin moles (melanocytic nevi), lipomas and adenomas. They can still cause harm by compressing organs or secreting hormones.
  • In situ neoplasms — abnormal cells confined to their site of origin, without invasion through the basement membrane; a precancerous stage.
  • Malignant neoplasms (cancers) — characterised by invasion of surrounding tissues and the capacity to metastasise to distant organs via blood or lymph, causing the great majority of tumour-related deaths.
  • Neoplasms of uncertain behaviour — growths whose future behaviour cannot be predicted from their current appearance.

Neoplasms are further named by their tissue of origin: carcinomas arise from epithelium, sarcomas from connective tissue, lymphomas and leukaemias from blood-forming tissues, and so on.

Causes

Neoplasms arise from genetic and epigenetic alterations — mutations in oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, chromosomal abnormalities and viral infections (such as HPV, hepatitis B and C, and EBV) — often promoted by carcinogens including tobacco smoke, ultraviolet radiation, asbestos and alcohol. Most neoplasms are clonal, deriving from a single mutated progenitor cell, though the resulting tumour is typically genetically heterogeneous.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on imaging, endoscopy and, definitively, histopathological examination of a biopsy. Treatment depends on type and stage and includes surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, hormonal therapy and immunotherapy.
