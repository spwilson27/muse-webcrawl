Alpine tundra

Alpine tundra is a type of natural region or biome that occurs on high mountains throughout the world, above the altitude at which trees can grow (the treeline). Unlike arctic tundra, which is defined by latitude, alpine tundra is defined by elevation, and it is not underlain by permafrost over most of its extent. The alpine zone's harsh climate — low temperatures, strong winds, intense ultraviolet radiation, and a short growing season — limits plants to low, slow-growing, cold-adapted forms.

Climate

Temperatures fall roughly 1 °C for every 150 metres of altitude gained, so alpine areas experience long, cold winters and cool summers regardless of latitude; even tropical peaks such as those of the Andes and East Africa have alpine tundra. Precipitation is often low and much of it falls as snow. Strong winds desiccate plants and redistribute snow, scouring exposed ridges while filling sheltered hollows.

Flora and fauna

Alpine plants typically grow as cushions, mats, or rosettes close to the ground, which shelters them from wind and traps warmth; many are covered in hairs that reduce water loss and UV damage. Characteristic plants include cushion saxifrages, moss campion, alpine avens, sedges and dwarf willows, and in the tropical Andes the giant rosette plants (espeletias). Animal life includes pikas, marmots, mountain goats, ibex, chamois, ptarmigans, and the American pika; many species migrate downslope or hibernate in winter.

Human use and threats

Alpine tundra has traditionally been used for summer grazing of livestock. It is highly sensitive to disturbance: vegetation grows so slowly that damage from trampling, roads, or mining can persist for decades. Climate warming is pushing the treeline upward and allowing lowland species to invade, squeezing the alpine zone against the summits — a special threat to species found nowhere else.
