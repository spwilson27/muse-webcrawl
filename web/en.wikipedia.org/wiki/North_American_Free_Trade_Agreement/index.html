The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA; Spanish: Tratado de Libre Comercio de América del Norte, TLCAN; French: Accord de libre-échange nord-américain, ALÉNA) was an agreement signed by Canada, Mexico and the United States that created a trilateral trade bloc in North America. It came into force on 1 January 1994, superseding the 1988 Canada–United States Free Trade Agreement, and gradually eliminated most tariffs and other barriers to trade and investment among the three countries.

Provisions

NAFTA eliminated tariffs on most goods over transition periods of up to 15 years, opened government procurement and services markets, protected intellectual property, and created dispute-resolution mechanisms, including the controversial Chapter 11 investor-state provisions allowing companies to sue governments. Side agreements on labour and the environment (the NAALC and NAAEC) were added to secure ratification in the United States.

Effects and debate

Trade among the three countries roughly quadrupled during NAFTA's lifetime, and North American supply chains — especially in the automotive industry — became deeply integrated. The agreement remained politically contentious: critics in the United States blamed it for manufacturing job losses and factory relocations to Mexico, while supporters credited it with lower consumer prices and increased competitiveness; in Mexico it was criticised for exposing small farmers to subsidised US corn imports. Economists generally found its aggregate effects on the US economy modest but significant for particular sectors and regions.

Replacement

Following renegotiation demanded by the administration of US President Donald Trump, NAFTA was replaced on 1 July 2020 by the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA, known as CUSMA in Canada and T-MEC in Mexico), which updated rules on automobiles (higher regional content requirements), digital trade, labour and dairy access while retaining the basic structure of North American free trade.
