Banach fixed-point theorem


Successive iterates of a contraction converge to its unique fixed point.

The Banach fixed-point theorem, also known as the contraction mapping theorem or Banach–Caccioppoli theorem, is an important result in metric space theory and functional analysis. It guarantees the existence and uniqueness of fixed points of certain self-maps of metric spaces, and provides a constructive method — Picard iteration — for finding them. It was first stated by Stefan Banach in his 1922 doctoral dissertation; Renato Caccioppoli gave an independent formulation in 1930.

Statement

Let (X, d) be a non-empty complete metric space, and let T : X → X be a contraction mapping, that is, there exists a constant q with 0 ≤ q < 1 such that d(T(x), T(y)) ≤ q · d(x, y) for all x, y in X. Then T admits exactly one fixed point x*, and for every starting point x0, the sequence defined by xn+1 = T(xn) converges to x*.

The proof shows that the iterates form a Cauchy sequence (the distances between successive terms shrink geometrically by the factor q), and completeness of the space ensures the limit exists. Standard error estimates bound the distance from the n-th iterate to the true fixed point, making the theorem a practical tool in numerical analysis as well as a theoretical one.

Applications

  • Differential equations — the Picard–Lindelöf theorem on existence and uniqueness of solutions to initial value problems is proved by applying the theorem to an integral operator on a space of continuous functions.
  • Integral equations — Volterra and Fredholm equations can be solved by contraction arguments.
  • Fractals — the existence of attractors of iterated function systems (e.g. the Barnsley fern) follows from the theorem applied to the Hausdorff metric.
  • Economics and game theory — convergence of iterative optimisation and the solution of dynamic programming (Bellman) equations, which are contraction mappings in the sup norm.

Generalisations

Many generalisations relax the contraction condition (Edelstein, Meir–Keeler, and Caristi-type theorems), or replace the metric space by other structures. A converse due to Bessaga (1959) shows that if every iterate of a map has a unique fixed point, then a metric can be defined making the map a contraction.
