Property tax

Property taxes are assessed largely by local governments.

A property tax (whose rate is often expressed as a millage rate) is an ad valorem tax on the value of a property. The tax is levied by the governing authority of the jurisdiction in which the property is located: depending on the country, this may be a national government, a state or province, a county or region, or a municipality. Multiple jurisdictions may tax the same property.

Property taxes are most commonly imposed on real estate, but in some jurisdictions also apply to personal property such as vehicles, boats, and business equipment. Land and improvements (buildings) may be taxed at different rates, and special-purpose levies can fund schools, fire districts, water systems, and other local services.

Assessment and collection

Tax assessors determine the value of property, typically by market comparison, income, or cost approaches, and owners usually may appeal assessments. The tax owed equals the assessed value multiplied by the millage rate (one mill equals one dollar per thousand dollars of value). Failure to pay can result in liens and eventually tax sales of the property.

By country

In the United States property tax is the main source of revenue for local governments and public schools and is generally levied at the county or municipal level. In the United Kingdom, domestic property is subject to Council Tax and business premises to business rates. Many economists regard land value taxation as an efficient alternative because land supply is fixed, an argument popularized by Henry George.
