Placentalia is one of the three major divisions of living mammals, the others being the monotremes (egg-layers) and the marsupials. Placental mammals carry their young in the mother's uterus for a relatively long gestation period, nourishing the fetus through an organ called the placenta, and give birth to relatively completely developed young.

Evolution

Molecular clock studies suggest the placental crown group originated in the Late Cretaceous, though undisputed placental fossils appear mainly after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, after which the group underwent an explosive adaptive radiation into the ecological niches vacated by the non-avian dinosaurs. Whether the deepest divergences among the placental orders predate or postdate the extinction is a long-standing debate between "explosive" and "long-fuse" models.

Major groups

Placentals are divided into four superorders: Xenarthra (armadillos, sloths, anteaters), Afrotheria (elephants, hyraxes, aardvarks, tenrecs and others), Euarchontoglires (primates, rodents, lagomorphs, treeshrews, colugos), and Laurasiatheria (carnivorans, bats, ungulates, whales, insectivores and others). Humans are placental mammals of the order Primates.

Anatomy

Distinguishing anatomical traits include the absence of epipubic bones, the presence of a corpus callosum connecting the cerebral hemispheres, and a chorioallantoic placenta. Gestation ranges from about two weeks in some rodents to 22 months in African elephants.
