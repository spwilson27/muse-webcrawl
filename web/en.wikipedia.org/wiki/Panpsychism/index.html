Panpsychism is the view in the philosophy of mind that mind, mentality or consciousness is a fundamental and pervasive property of the physical world — that all things, down to the most basic particles, possess some form of experience. The word derives from the Greek pan ("all") and psyche ("soul" or "mind").

History

Panpsychist ideas appear in ancient Greek philosophy (Thales held that "all things are full of gods") and in many of the world's religious and animist traditions. Early modern versions were defended in different forms by Spinoza and Leibniz, whose monads are each endowed with perception. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, thinkers such as William James, Charles Strong and Alfred North Whitehead developed systematic panpsychist or "panexperientialist" views, though the position fell out of favour during the ascendancy of behaviourism and early physicalism.

Contemporary revival

Panpsychism has been revived in contemporary philosophy of mind as a response to the "hard problem of consciousness": if consciousness cannot be explained by physical processes alone, perhaps experience is built into matter itself. Thomas Nagel, David Chalmers, Galen Strawson and Philip Goff have argued for versions of the view, and "Russellian monism" — the idea that physics describes matter's behaviour but not its intrinsic nature, which may be experiential — has become a prominent framework.

Criticism

The chief objection is the combination problem, posed by William James: how do the many micro-experiences of particles combine into the single unified consciousness of a human subject? Critics also charge panpsychism with being empirically untestable and with lacking a clear account of what it means for an electron to have experience.
