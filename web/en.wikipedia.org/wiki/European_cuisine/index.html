European cuisine

European cuisine (also called Western cuisine) comprises the cuisines of Europe and, by extension, those of the European-settler societies of the Americas, Australia and elsewhere. It is enormously diverse, but shares common foundations: wheat-based breads, dairy products, meat cookery, and — since the Columbian exchange — New World staples such as the potato, tomato, maize and capsicum peppers.

Regional traditions

Mediterranean cuisines (Italian, Spanish, Greek, southern French) emphasise olive oil, wine, tomatoes, garlic and seafood. French cuisine, codified in the 19th and 20th centuries by chefs such as Auguste Escoffier, became the template for fine dining worldwide. Central and Eastern European cuisines feature rye breads, cured and smoked meats, dumplings, cabbage and sour cream — kielbasa sausages in Poland, goulash in Hungary. Northern European traditions rely on preserved fish, root vegetables and dairy; British and Irish cooking on roasts, pies and puddings; Scandinavian smörgåsbord and New Nordic cuisine have gained international attention.

History

Medieval European food, documented in works such as Le Viandier and The Forme of Cury, used spices lavishly among elites and relied on preservation by salting and smoking. The Columbian exchange transformed European diets after 1492. Court cuisine in France developed the sauce-based haute cuisine, while industrialisation, restaurants (a Parisian invention of the late 18th century) and eventually nouvelle cuisine reshaped both professional and home cooking. In the 20th and 21st centuries, European food absorbed global influences — pasta, pizza, kebab and curry are now everyday fare across the continent.
