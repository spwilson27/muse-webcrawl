Sheriff

A sheriff is a government official, with varying duties, existing in some countries with historical ties to England where the office originated. The word derives from the Old English scīrgerefa — "shire reeve" — the royal official charged with keeping the peace in a county (shire) on behalf of the king. Today the office is most prominent in the United States, where most county sheriffs are elected officials who lead a sheriff's office responsible for law enforcement, courts and jails; in England and Wales the surviving office of High Sheriff is largely ceremonial.

History

The office dates to Anglo-Saxon England, where the shire reeve collected taxes, raised the militia (fyrd), presided over the shire court and enforced royal writs. After the Norman Conquest the sheriff became the crown's chief local officer, at times so powerful that Magna Carta (1215) included clauses restraining sheriffs. Over the centuries, functions were progressively transferred to justices of the peace, coroners, lord lieutenants and professional police, leaving the English High Sheriff with mainly ceremonial duties such as attending royal visits and supporting the judiciary.

United States

American sheriffs are typically elected by county voters for four-year terms — the United States is unusual in electing its chief local law-enforcement officers — and are constitutional officers in most states. A sheriff's office (distinct from a municipal police department) commonly:

  • Provides law-enforcement patrol, especially in unincorporated areas of the county.
  • Operates the county jail.
  • Provides court security and serves legal process, warrants and evictions.
  • May conduct foreclosure sales and enforce court judgments.

Notable exceptions exist: New York City's sheriff is an appointed finance official, Connecticut abolished county sheriffs in 2000, and Alaska has no counties or sheriffs. The sheriff of the frontier era — immortalised in Western films, from Wyatt Earp to the fictional Sheriff of Nottingham's American cousins — remains a potent figure in American political culture; Nottingham's own Sheriff is the famous villain of the Robin Hood legends.

Other countries

  • Scotland: sheriffs are professional judges who preside over sheriff courts, hearing civil and criminal cases.
  • Canada: sheriffs mainly provide court security, prisoner transport and process service; some provinces also give them enforcement roles.
  • Australia: the sheriff is an officer of the court responsible for enforcing judgments, serving documents and managing juries.
  • South Africa and elsewhere: sheriffs are court-appointed officers who serve process and execute judgments.
