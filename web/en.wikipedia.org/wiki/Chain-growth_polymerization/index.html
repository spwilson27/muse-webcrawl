Chain-growth polymerization

Chain-growth polymerization proceeds by initiation, propagation and termination.

Chain-growth polymerization (also called addition polymerization) is a polymerization technique in which unsaturated monomer molecules add onto the active growing site of a polymer chain one at a time. It contrasts with step-growth polymerization, in which chains grow by reactions between functional groups of molecules of all sizes.

Mechanism

The process involves three fundamental steps:

  • Initiation — an initiator generates a reactive centre (a free radical, cation, anion or coordination complex) that attacks the first monomer.
  • Propagation — the active centre adds monomer molecules in rapid succession; a chain may add thousands of monomers within seconds.
  • Termination — the active centre is destroyed by combination, disproportionation or reaction with another species. Alternatively, chain transfer can hand the activity to a new molecule, limiting molecular weight.

Because only monomer adds to growing chains, high-molecular-weight polymer forms at low overall conversion, and the product mixture contains polymer and unreacted monomer but few intermediate oligomers — the opposite of the step-growth case.

Types

Chain-growth methods include free-radical polymerization (used for polystyrene, poly(methyl methacrylate) and PVC), anionic and cationic polymerization, ring-opening polymerization, and coordination polymerization with Ziegler–Natta or metallocene catalysts (used for most polyethylene and polypropylene). Controlled ("living") techniques such as ATRP and RAFT suppress termination, giving narrow molecular-weight distributions and block copolymers.
