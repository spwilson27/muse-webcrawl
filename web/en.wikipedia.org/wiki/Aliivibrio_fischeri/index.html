Aliivibrio fischeri (formerly Vibrio fischeri) is a Gram-negative, rod-shaped bacterium found in marine environments worldwide. It is best known for its bioluminescence and for its symbiosis with marine animals such as the Hawaiian bobtail squid (Euprymna scolopes), in whose light organ it lives.

Bioluminescence and quorum sensing

A. fischeri produces light through the action of the enzyme luciferase, encoded by the lux operon. Light production is controlled by quorum sensing: the bacterium secretes an autoinducer molecule (an acyl homoserine lactone), and only when the population density — and hence autoinducer concentration — is high enough does the population switch on light production. A. fischeri was the organism in which quorum sensing was first characterised, in work by Kenneth Nealson and Terry Platt in the 1970s.

Symbiosis with the bobtail squid

Free-living in seawater at low densities, A. fischeri colonises the light organ of newly hatched Hawaiian bobtail squid. The squid uses the bacterial light for counter-illumination camouflage: by matching the intensity of moonlight from above, the squid avoids casting a shadow visible to predators below. Each dawn the squid expels most of the bacteria, and the population regrows during the day.

Genomics and taxonomy

The species was reclassified from Vibrio to the new genus Aliivibrio in 2007 on the basis of genetic analyses. Its genome, about 4.3 million base pairs across two chromosomes, was sequenced in 2005. The species is named in honour of German microbiologist Bernhard Fischer.
