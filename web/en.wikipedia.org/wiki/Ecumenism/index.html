Ecumenism — also spelled oecumenism — is the movement among Christian Churches and denominations to promote unity and cooperation, in response to the divisions of Christianity into Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant and other traditions. The word derives from the Greek oikoumene, "the whole inhabited world", the same root as ecumenical council.

History

The modern ecumenical movement is conventionally dated to the World Missionary Conference held in Edinburgh in 1910, chaired by John R. Mott, where competition between Protestant missions overseas prompted calls for cooperation. Bishop Charles Brent helped inspire the Faith and Order movement, which addressed doctrinal questions, while the Life and Work movement pursued practical cooperation. These streams converged in the formation of the World Council of Churches (WCC) at Amsterdam in 1948.

Catholic and Orthodox involvement

The Roman Catholic Church long kept its distance but embraced ecumenism at the Second Vatican Council, whose 1964 decree Unitatis redintegratio recognised genuine Christian elements in other churches. The Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity conducts dialogues with Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran and other bodies. The Eastern Orthodox Church is a WCC member and engages in bilateral dialogues, notably with Catholics since the lifting of the mutual anathemas of 1054 in 1965.

Achievements and limits

Notable fruits include the Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry convergence text (Lima, 1982) and the 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between Catholics and Lutherans, which resolved a central dispute of the Reformation era. Full visible unity — shared communion and ministry — remains elusive: disagreements over the papacy, ordination of women, and moral teaching persist, and conservative churches often view the movement with suspicion. In recent decades ecumenical energy has partly shifted toward interfaith dialogue and common social action, including shared work on poverty, peace and the environment.
