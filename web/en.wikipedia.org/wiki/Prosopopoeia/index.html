Prosopopoeia

Prosopopoeia (from Ancient Greek προσωποποιία, prosōpopoiía, "making a face/person") is a rhetorical device in which a speaker or writer communicates by speaking as another person or object, or gives voice to an absent, dead, abstract or inanimate entity. It is a form of personification — related to but distinct from apostrophe (addressing an absent entity) — in which the imagined speaker actually delivers a speech in the first person.

Classical rhetoric

Prosopopoeia was a staple of classical oratory and rhetorical training (the progymnasmata exercises). Cicero famously used it in the First Catilinarian, where he makes the Roman fatherland itself rebuke Catiline, and in the Pro Caelio, where the stern ancestor Appius Claudius Caecus is summoned from the dead to chastise his descendant Clodia. Quintilian discussed the figure as a powerful means of bringing opponents, cities and even abstract qualities "before the eyes" of an audience. Ethopoeia, a closely related exercise, required students to compose speeches in character for historical or mythical figures.

Literary uses

The device pervades literature: the speaking cross in the Old English poem The Dream of the Rood; Lady Philosophy in Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy; personified virtues and vices in medieval allegory; Death's speech in morality plays; and the imagined voices of the dead in epitaphs and elegies. Paul de Man influentially analysed prosopopoeia as the master trope of autobiography and lyric address — the figure that "gives a face" (and a voice) to what is absent or voiceless, most poignantly in epitaphs for the dead.

Examples

  • Boethius: Philosophy converses with the imprisoned author.
  • John Donne addresses — and is answered in kind by the conceit of — "Death, be not proud".
  • In courtroom rhetoric, lawyers may speak "for" a victim who cannot testify.
  • Political cartoons routinely use prosopopoeia, letting nations, parties or abstractions speak in captions.
