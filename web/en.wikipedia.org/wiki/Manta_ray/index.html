Manta rays are large rays belonging to the genus Mobula (formerly Manta). The two species, the giant oceanic manta ray (Mobula birostris) and the reef manta ray (M. alfredi), are among the largest rays in the world: the oceanic manta reaches up to 7 metres in disc width. Mantas are filter feeders that consume large quantities of zooplankton, which they funnel into their mouths with distinctive cephalic fins. Despite their size they are harmless to humans, having lost the defensive tail sting of their ancestors.

Description

Mantas have triangular pectoral fins, horn-like cephalic fins that give them the alternative name "devilfish", and wide, terminal mouths. Each individual bears a unique pattern of spots on its underside, allowing researchers to identify animals photographically. They possess the largest brain of any fish and show notable intelligence and social behaviour.

Distribution and behaviour

They are found in warm temperate, subtropical and tropical waters worldwide. Reef mantas frequent cleaning stations on coral reefs, while oceanic mantas range further offshore and migrate over long distances. Mantas are ovoviviparous, giving birth to usually a single pup after a gestation of over a year.

Conservation

Both species are threatened by targeted fisheries for their gill plates, used in traditional medicine, and by bycatch. The giant oceanic manta ray is listed as endangered and the reef manta as vulnerable; both are protected under CITES Appendix II and many national laws. Ecotourism centred on manta diving is economically significant in places such as the Maldives, Hawaii and Indonesia.
