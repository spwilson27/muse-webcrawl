Tenement

A tenement is a type of multi-family residential building, typically a multi-storey structure divided into separate apartments rented to working-class families. In common usage the term is especially associated with the crowded, often poorly maintained housing built for immigrants and industrial workers in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century cities such as New York, as well as with the stone-built tenements of Scottish cities like Glasgow and Edinburgh.

New York tenements

As New York City's population exploded with immigration, landlords built narrow tenements on standard 25-by-100-foot lots, often six storeys tall with four apartments per floor and minimal light, air, and sanitation. Early "railroad flats" had almost no windows in interior rooms and shared privies in the yard. Reformers such as Jacob Riis, whose photojournalism in How the Other Half Lives (1890) exposed tenement conditions, campaigned for change.

Successive Tenement House Acts in New York (1867, 1879, and 1901) progressively imposed standards. The 1879 law produced the ubiquitous "dumbbell tenement", indented at the sides to create air shafts; the 1901 "New Law" required outward-facing windows in every room, indoor plumbing, and fireproofing in larger buildings, effectively reshaping subsequent construction.

Scotland

In Scotland, "tenement" simply denotes a flatted block of stone-built housing, traditionally three or four storeys with a shared close (entry) and stair, housing families across the social spectrum. Glasgow's Victorian tenements — many built in red or blonde sandstone with bay windows — remain the dominant housing form in much of the city and are now valued rather than stigmatised. Scottish tenement law gives the word a distinct legal meaning: under the Tenements (Scotland) Act 2004, a tenement is any building divided into two or more flats on separate floors.

Legal meaning

In older legal usage, a tenement was anything that could be held by tenure from a feudal superior — land, houses, rents, or rights — a sense surviving in the phrase "messuages and tenements" in historic deeds. The residential meaning developed from buildings held by multiple tenants on one plot.
