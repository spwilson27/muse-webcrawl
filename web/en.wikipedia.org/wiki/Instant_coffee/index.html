Instant coffee

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Instant coffee dissolves in hot water, requiring no brewing equipment.

Instant coffee, also called soluble coffee, coffee crystals, coffee powder, or powdered coffee, is a beverage derived from brewed coffee beans that enables people to quickly prepare hot coffee by adding hot water or milk to coffee solids in a powdered or crystallised form and stirring. It is made from regular roasted coffee that has been brewed and then dehydrated by spray-drying or freeze-drying.

History

New Zealander David Strang patented a soluble coffee powder in 1890, and Japanese chemist Satori Kato demonstrated a version in Chicago in 1901. George Constant Louis Washington's Red E Coffee (from 1909) was the first mass-marketed product. Nescafé, launched by Nestlé in 1938 at the request of Brazil — which had a coffee surplus — became ubiquitous through Second World War military rations and postwar consumer marketing.

Production

Beans are roasted, ground, and brewed at high concentration. In spray-drying, the extract is misted into hot air and falls as fine powder; in freeze-drying, the extract is frozen, broken into granules, and dried under vacuum — costlier, but preserving more aroma. Instant coffee has a long shelf life and is lighter to transport than beans, though it typically contains less caffeine per cup and fewer aromatic compounds than freshly brewed coffee.

Consumption

Instant coffee accounts for a large share of consumption in the United Kingdom, Australia, and much of Asia and Latin America; it is also the base of drinks such as Greek frappé and whipped dalgona coffee, and an ingredient in baking and in "3-in-1" sachets with sugar and creamer.
