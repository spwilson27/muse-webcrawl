Lionel George Logue (26 February 1880 – 12 April 1953) was an Australian speech and language therapist and amateur stage actor who successfully treated King George VI of the United Kingdom, who had a severe stammer, most famously helping him deliver his wartime radio broadcasts during the Second World War.

Lionel Logue, speech therapist to King George VI.

Early life and career

Logue was born in Adelaide, South Australia, and studied elocution. After treating shell-shocked Australian soldiers returning from the First World War, he emigrated to England in 1924 and set up a practice at 146 Harley Street, London. In 1926 he was consulted by the then Duke of York, the future George VI, whose stammer made public speaking an ordeal. Logue's regime of breathing exercises, physical relaxation and psychological encouragement produced rapid improvement: the Duke opened the Australian parliament in Canberra in 1927 with a speech delivered almost without hesitation.

Royal service and later life

Logue remained the King's coach and confidant through the abdication crisis of 1936 and the Second World War, rehearsing the King before each major broadcast, including the declaration of war speech of 3 September 1939. He was appointed a Member (and later Commander) of the Royal Victorian Order. He died in London in 1953.

In popular culture

Logue was portrayed by Geoffrey Rush in the 2010 film The King's Speech, which dramatises his treatment of George VI and won the Academy Award for Best Picture. His grandson Mark Logue co-wrote a companion book, The King's Speech: How One Man Saved the British Monarchy, based on Logue's diaries and patient cards.
