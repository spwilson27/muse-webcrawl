Russian traditional music

Russian traditional music encompasses the folk music traditions of ethnic Russians, including vocal genres such as the bylina (epic narrative song), lyrical songs, dance songs, ritual calendar songs, and work songs, as well as a distinctive instrumental tradition centred on instruments such as the balalaika, domra, gusli, garmon (accordion), and spoons.

Vocal traditions

Russian folk song is characterised by podgolosnaya polyphony, a heterophonic style in which a main melody is accompanied by improvised subordinate voices. Songs accompanied every part of peasant life: weddings, funerals, sowing and harvest, conscription, and imprisonment. The chastushka, a short, often humorous rhymed quatrain sung to accordion accompaniment, became widespread in the late 19th century.

Epic byliny recount the deeds of heroes such as Ilya Muromets, Dobrynya Nikitich, and Alyosha Popovich. They were preserved longest in the Russian North, around Lake Onega and the White Sea, where singers such as Marfa Kryukova maintained the tradition into the Soviet era.

Instruments

  • Balalaika — triangular-bodied, three-stringed instrument, developed in its modern form by Vasily Andreyev in the 1880s.
  • Domra — round-bodied lute with three or four strings.
  • Gusli — one of the oldest East Slavic instruments, a plucked zither.
  • Garmon and bayan — free-reed accordions central to village dance music.
  • Zhaleika and svirel — traditional wind instruments.
  • Lozhki — wooden spoons used as percussion.

Church and choral music

The Russian Orthodox tradition of znamenny chant, a monophonic liturgical chant notated with special signs called znamena, dates to the medieval period. Sacred choral singing later became a major influence on Russian folk and composed music alike.

Soviet and modern era

In the Soviet period, state folk ensembles such as the Pyatnitsky Choir (founded 1910) and the Beryozka dance ensemble presented stylised versions of folk material. Andreyev's Great Russian Orchestra of folk instruments, with its families of balalaikas and domras, also became a model for amateur and professional ensembles across the country. Since 1991, revival movements and ethnomusicological fieldwork have sought to document surviving village traditions.
