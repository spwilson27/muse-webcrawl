The East India Company (EIC) was an English, and later British, joint-stock company founded in 1600 by royal charter from Elizabeth I. It was formed to trade in the Indian Ocean region, initially with the East Indies (South and Southeast Asia), and later with East Asia. The company eventually came to rule large areas of India, exercising military power through its own armies and assuming administrative functions; its rule in India effectively began in 1757 and lasted until 1858, when the British Crown took direct control after the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

Early trade

The company established trading posts ("factories") at Surat, Madras, Bombay and Calcutta, competing with the Portuguese and the Dutch East India Company (VOC). Its early trade centred on spices, cotton textiles, silk and saltpetre; in the 18th century tea from China became enormously profitable, and the company notoriously exported opium from Bengal to China, contributing to the Opium Wars. The company's monopoly of British trade with Asia was progressively dismantled by the Charter Acts of 1813 and 1833.

Company rule in India

Victory under Robert Clive at the Battle of Plassey (1757) and the grant of the diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal in 1765 transformed the company into a territorial power. Through wars and annexations under governors-general such as Warren Hastings and Lord Dalhousie, company rule expanded across the subcontinent; at its height the company maintained private armies of some 260,000 soldiers, twice the size of the British Army. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 led the British government to abolish company rule with the Government of India Act 1858, inaugurating the British Raj. The company was formally dissolved in 1874.

Legacy

The East India Company is often described as the first multinational corporation and a pioneer of the joint-stock model, shareholder governance and the modern office; it is also criticised for exploitation, famine policy in Bengal, and the opium trade.
