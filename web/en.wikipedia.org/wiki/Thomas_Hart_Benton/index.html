Thomas Hart Benton

Thomas Hart Benton
Born: 15 April 1889, Neosho, Missouri, US
Died: 19 January 1975, Kansas City, Missouri, US
Movement: Regionalism

Thomas Hart Benton (April 15, 1889 – January 19, 1975) was an American painter and muralist. Along with Grant Wood and John Steuart Curry, he was at the forefront of the Regionalist movement in American art. His fluid, sculpted figures in his murals and paintings showed everyday people in scenes of life in the United States, particularly the Midwest.

Early life

Benton was born in Neosho, Missouri, into a prominent political family; his great-uncle, also Thomas Hart Benton, was a US Senator, and his father Maecenas Benton was a congressman. He studied at the Art Institute of Chicago and the Académie Julian in Paris, and after early experiments with modernist styles he developed the recognisable figurative style that made him famous.

Regionalism and murals

In the 1930s Benton became a leading voice for Regionalism, arguing for an American art rooted in native subjects rather than European abstraction. His major mural commissions included America Today (1930–31, now at the Metropolitan Museum of Art), murals for the Missouri State Capitol (1936), and the Indiana Murals (1933). His work appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1934, and his self-portrait appeared there in 1942. He taught at the Art Students League of New York and later at the Kansas City Art Institute, where his most famous pupil was Jackson Pollock — who went on to repudiate Benton's style as a founder of Abstract Expressionism.

Later life

Benton remained a prolific and sometimes controversial figure, quarrelling publicly with the art establishment. He continued painting murals into his eighties, including the Independence and the Opening of the West mural for the Truman Library. He died in Kansas City in 1975 while working in his studio.
