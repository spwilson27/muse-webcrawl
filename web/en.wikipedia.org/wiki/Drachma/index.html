Drachma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A silver tetradrachm of Athens, c. 449 BC, worth four drachmae

The drachma (Greek: δραχμή, plural drachmae or drachmas) was the currency used in Greece during several periods in its history. In ancient Greece it was one of the standard units of silver coinage, and in modern times it served as the Greek national currency from its reintroduction in 1832 until it was replaced by the euro in 2001 (coins and banknotes circulated until 2002).

The name derives from the Greek verb δράσσομαι (drássomai, "to grasp"), and originally denoted a handful of six iron spits (oboloí), which served as an early form of money. The drachma was subdivided into six obols and 100 drachmae made up one mina.

Ancient drachma

The ancient drachma was struck by many Greek city-states, each with its own weight standard. The most famous was the Attic drachma of Athens, weighing roughly 4.3 grams of silver; four of these made a tetradrachm. Athenian "owls" — tetradrachms bearing the head of Athena and her owl — became one of the most widely used trade coins of the ancient world. Other standards included the Aeginetic drachma (about 6.1 g) and the lighter Corinthian issues.

Drachmae were issued in denominations including the decadrachm (10), tetradrachm (4), didrachm (2) and smaller fractions down to the obol and chalkous. Charon's obol — the coin placed with the dead to pay the ferryman of Hades — reflects how deeply the currency was embedded in Greek culture.

Modern drachma

After Greek independence from the Ottoman Empire, the drachma was reintroduced in 1832, replacing the short-lived phoenix. It initially circulated at par with the French franc and joined the Latin Monetary Union in 1868. The modern drachma was subdivided into 100 lepta. Over the 20th century it suffered repeated devaluations and, during the Axis occupation of 1941–1944, extreme hyperinflation; a new drachma was introduced in 1944 at a rate of 50 billion old drachmae to one, and again revalued in 1954 at 1,000 to one.

Greece joined the euro area on 1 January 2001 at a fixed conversion rate of 340.75 drachmae to the euro. Euro coins and notes were introduced in 2002 and drachma cash ceased to be legal tender shortly afterwards, ending nearly three millennia of the currency's use.
