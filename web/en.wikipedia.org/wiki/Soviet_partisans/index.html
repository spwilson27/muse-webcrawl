The Soviet partisans were members of the resistance movement that fought behind German lines in the occupied territories of the Soviet Union during the Second World War (the Great Patriotic War). Composed of Red Army soldiers cut off by the German advance, Communist Party cadres, and local volunteers and conscripts, the movement grew from scattered, desperate detachments in 1941 into a centrally directed strategic arm by 1943–44.

Organisation

After early chaos, the Central Headquarters of the Partisan Movement, created in May 1942 under Panteleimon Ponomarenko, coordinated operations with the Red Army. In Belarus — whose forests and marshes sheltered "partisan zones" amounting to a state within a state — the movement was strongest; large formations under commanders such as Sydir Kovpak conducted long raids deep into Ukraine. Partisans derailed trains, destroyed track and bridges (notably in Operation Rail War and Operation Concert, timed with the offensives of 1943–44), gathered intelligence, and administered liberated zones.

Occupation and reprisals

Nazi anti-partisan warfare ("Bandenbekämpfung") was extraordinarily brutal, burning hundreds of Belarusian villages with their inhabitants — the Khatyn massacre became its emblem — and killing civilians on a vast scale, with the partisans' presence often used as pretext. Relations between partisans and local populations were complex, involving requisitioning, conscription and clashes with Ukrainian and Polish nationalist guerrillas and with collaborators.

Legacy

Soviet historiography celebrated the partisans as an all-people's war; modern scholarship gives a more mixed picture of their military effect and conduct, but their role in tying down German security forces and supporting the 1944 Operation Bagration is widely acknowledged. Belarus officially honours them as national heroes.
