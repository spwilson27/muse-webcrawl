Thatching

Thatching is the craft of building a roof with dry vegetation such as straw, water reed, sedge, rushes, heather, or palm fronds, layering the material so as to shed water away from the inner roof. Because most plant matter remains dry and densely packed, trapping air, a thatched roof is insulating as well as weatherproof. Thatch is among the oldest roofing materials still in use and is found in both temperate and tropical climates around the world.

A thatcher at work on a water-reed roof in England.

Materials

The principal materials in Britain and northern Europe are water reed (often imported from wetlands such as the Norfolk Broads or continental Europe), long straw (wheat straw prepared so the stems remain unbroken), and combed wheat reed. Elsewhere, materials are whatever suitable vegetation is local: palm leaves in the tropics, heather in parts of Scotland, and various grasses and reeds worldwide. A well-laid water-reed roof may last 40–60 years; long straw typically lasts 20–40 years. The ridge, the most exposed part, is renewed more often, in 10–15 years, and is often finished in a decorative pattern of hazel rods (liggers) that is a signature of the individual thatcher.

Technique

Thatch is laid in courses from the eaves upward, each course overlapping the one below, on a steeply pitched roof (usually at least 45–50 degrees) so that rain runs quickly off the surface. Only the outer few centimetres of a coat — typically 30–40 cm thick — weather; the stems are fixed with spars, wires, or screws to battens or the coat below. Water penetrates only the surface layer, so the underside remains dry.

History and prevalence

Thatching is ancient and was the standard roofing of rural buildings across much of the world. In England it declined from the late 19th century as industrially produced tiles and Welsh slate became cheap to transport, and thatch became associated with poverty. From the late 20th century, however, thatch enjoyed a revival as a desirable, sustainable, and characterful material for historic and high-value homes; more new thatched houses have been built in recent decades than for many years before. In Japan, thatched farmhouses (minka), such as those of Shirakawa-gō, are UNESCO-listed. In the developing world, thatch remains a common, affordable roofing.

Fire risk

Thatch is combustible, and insurers treat thatched properties as higher risk; chimney design and maintenance, spark arrestors, and fire barriers beneath the coat mitigate the danger. Contrary to popular belief, thatched roofs are not markedly more likely to catch fire than other roofs, but fires that do take hold are harder to extinguish and more destructive.
