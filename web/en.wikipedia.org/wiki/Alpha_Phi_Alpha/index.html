Alpha Phi Alpha Fraternity, Inc. (ΑΦΑ) is an American intercollegiate fraternity and the first Greek-letter organisation founded by African American men. It was established on 4 December 1906 at Cornell University in Ithaca, New York, by seven undergraduates known within the fraternity as the "Seven Jewels": Henry Arthur Callis, Charles Henry Chapman, Eugene Kinckle Jones, George Biddle Kelley, Nathaniel Allison Murray, Robert Harold Ogle, and Vertner Woodson Tandy.

History

The fraternity grew out of a literary and social study group formed by Black students at Cornell, who faced isolation and exclusion on campus. The founders resolved to create a brotherhood that would support its members academically and socially and, after graduation, serve the wider community. Alpha Phi Alpha expanded rapidly to other campuses and became the model for the other historically Black Greek-letter organisations that followed; it is a founding member of the National Pan-Hellenic Council (the "Divine Nine").

Programs and advocacy

Alpha Phi Alpha has long combined social fellowship with public service and political advocacy. Its national programs include "Go-to-High-School, Go-to-College", the voter-education drive "A Voteless People Is a Hopeless People", and Project Alpha, a youth mentoring and education initiative run with the March of Dimes. During the civil rights movement, members were prominent in campaigns for voting rights, desegregation, and fair housing; the fraternity was a driving force behind the creation of the Martin Luther King Jr. Memorial in Washington, D.C., which it first proposed in 1984 and which was dedicated in 2011.

Notable members

Prominent members have included W. E. B. Du Bois (honorary), Thurgood Marshall, the first African American justice of the U.S. Supreme Court; Martin Luther King Jr.; Jesse Owens; Duke Ellington; Lionel Richie; and numerous members of Congress, mayors, judges, and university presidents.
