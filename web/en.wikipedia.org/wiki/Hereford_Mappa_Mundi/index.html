The Hereford Mappa Mundi is a medieval map of the known world (a mappa mundi) created around 1300 and preserved at Hereford Cathedral in England. Drawn on a single large sheet of vellum, it is the largest medieval map still in existence. The map is a T and O map in form but greatly elaborated: the circular world is divided among the three known continents — Asia above, Europe and Africa below — ringed by ocean, with Jerusalem at its centre and the Day of Judgement depicted at the top.

Contents

The map contains roughly 500 drawings: cities and towns (including a detailed depiction of Hereford itself), biblical episodes such as Noah's Ark and the Exodus, classical myths, plants, animals and monstrous races believed to inhabit the edges of the world, such as the Blemmyes, men with faces in their chests. The British Isles appear at the lower-left edge, distorted by the circular frame. Its inscriptions are mainly in Latin, with some Anglo-Norman French.

Authorship and history

An inscription asks prayers for "Richard of Holdingham, or of Sleaford", who is generally credited with the map's making, probably in Lincoln and then Hereford around 1300. The map hung in the cathedral for centuries, at one time apparently forming part of an altarpiece or display near the tomb of St Thomas Cantilupe. In 1988 the cathedral chapter, facing repair costs, controversially proposed selling the map; a public campaign and a donation from Sir Paul Getty enabled it to be retained, and in 1996 it was installed in a purpose-built exhibition centre alongside the cathedral's chained library. The map is inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World register.
