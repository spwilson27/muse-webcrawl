Populate or perish

"Populate or perish" was a slogan and policy imperative in Australia, most prominent in the decades after the Second World War, expressing the belief that the country — a vast, thinly populated continent with a small population concentrated in coastal cities — had to increase its numbers substantially through immigration and natural growth or risk being unable to defend and develop itself, potentially succumbing to invasion or pressure from the more populous nations of Asia.

Origins

Anxieties about Australia's small population long predated the slogan. The "yellow peril" fears of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the experience of Japanese attacks on northern Australia in 1942, and the wartime slogan's use by figures such as Billy Hughes all contributed to the idea. The phrase became famous in the 1940s when Arthur Calwell, the first Minister for Immigration in Ben Chifley's post-war Labor government, promoted mass immigration with the declaration that Australians had to "populate or perish."

Post-war immigration programme

From 1945 Australia launched one of the largest planned migration programmes of the 20th century. The government set targets of adding about 2 percent to the population annually, half from natural increase and half from immigration. British migrants were preferred and subsidised as "Ten Pound Poms," but the shortage of British applicants led to successive agreements with European countries, bringing displaced persons from Eastern Europe and then large numbers of Italians, Greeks, Dutch, Germans, Yugoslavs, and others. Calwell's slogan captured the programme's rationale: migrants were needed for defence manpower, for great nation-building projects such as the Snowy Mountains Scheme, and for the labour force of a rapidly industrialising economy.

Later usage and legacy

The phrase retained currency into the late 20th century, invoked in debates over immigration, defence, and the "baby bonus" policies of the 2000s aimed at raising fertility. Historians note how the slogan intertwined strategic, economic, and racial assumptions of its era: the programme it justified initially operated within the framework of the White Australia policy, which was progressively dismantled between 1966 and 1973. Australia's population grew from about 7.4 million in 1945 to over 26 million by the early 21st century, with overseas-born residents comprising roughly 30 percent of the total.
