Permian Basin

The Permian Basin is a large sedimentary basin in western Texas and southeastern New Mexico in the United States, and one of the most prolific oil and gas producing regions in the world. It is named for the Permian period, the last period of the Paleozoic era, because its rocks were deposited in a sea that existed during that time — the basin contains one of the world's thickest deposits of Permian-aged rock.

Geology

The basin covers roughly 86,000 square miles (220,000 km²) and consists of several sub-basins and platforms, principally the Delaware Basin in the west, the Midland Basin in the east, and the Central Basin Platform between them. Its sedimentary fill includes organically rich shales and siltstones — formations such as the Spraberry, Wolfcamp, and Bone Spring — that are the source and reservoir rocks for its hydrocarbons.

Oil and gas production

Commercial oil production began in the 1920s, and the basin became a mainstay of American oil output through the twentieth century, with the cities of Midland and Odessa as its service hubs. Conventional production declined late in the century, but from around 2010 the combination of horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing in its tight shale formations produced a dramatic revival. By the late 2010s and early 2020s the Permian Basin was producing several million barrels of oil per day — more than most OPEC countries — and was the largest oil-producing region in the United States and among the largest in the world. Its output underpins much of the revenue of Texas's Permanent University Fund, which owns extensive mineral lands in the basin.
