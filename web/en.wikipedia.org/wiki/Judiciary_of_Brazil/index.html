The judiciary of Brazil is the system of courts that exercises judicial power in the Federative Republic of Brazil. Organised under the Constitution of 1988, it comprises federal and state courts, specialised branches for electoral, labour and military justice, and at its apex the Supreme Federal Court (Supremo Tribunal Federal, STF), which acts as the guardian of the Constitution.

Structure

The principal organs are the Supreme Federal Court; the Superior Court of Justice (STJ), which harmonises the interpretation of federal law; the regional federal courts and federal judges; the labour courts headed by the Superior Labour Court; the electoral courts headed by the Superior Electoral Court; military courts; and the courts of the states and the Federal District, each state maintaining its own Tribunal de Justiça. The National Council of Justice, created in 2004, supervises the administrative and financial management of the judiciary.

The Supreme Federal Court

The STF has eleven justices, appointed by the president with Senate approval, serving until the mandatory retirement age of 75. It tries direct actions of unconstitutionality, hears criminal cases against the highest authorities, and since the introduction of the "general repercussion" filter and binding precedents (súmulas vinculantes) manages one of the world's largest constitutional dockets. Its televised plenary sessions have made its justices prominent public figures, and the court has played a conspicuous role in major political investigations, including the mensalão and Lava Jato cases.

Features and criticism

Brazilian justice is known for its size — tens of millions of cases are pending at any time — its high costs, and the privileged forum (foro privilegiado) enjoyed by senior officials. Entry to the career judiciary is by competitive public examination. Reforms have promoted electronic process (PJe), conciliation centres and precedent-based management to address delay.
