Aberration of light

Stellar aberration
Discovered byJames Bradley, 1725–1728
Maximum displacementAbout 20.5 arcseconds
CauseFinite speed of light combined with observer's motion
Also calledAstronomical aberration; Bradley aberration

The aberration of light (also called astronomical aberration or stellar aberration) is an astronomical phenomenon in which celestial objects appear to shift position by up to about 20.5 arcseconds over the year, because the finite speed of light combines with the motion of the Earth in its orbit. Discovered by James Bradley in the 1720s, it provided the first direct observational proof that the Earth moves around the Sun, and an early measure of the speed of light.

Explanation

Because light travels at finite speed, a telescope must be tilted slightly forward — in the direction of the observer's motion — for starlight to pass straight down it, just as a walker tilts an umbrella into vertically falling rain. As the Earth's velocity vector rotates through the year, each star describes a small annual ellipse on the sky: a circle of radius 20.5″ for a star at the ecliptic pole, flattening to a line for stars in the ecliptic plane. The effect depends only on the observer's velocity, not on the star's distance, which distinguishes aberration from parallax.

Discovery

Bradley and Samuel Molyneux were searching for the parallax of Gamma Draconis with a zenith telescope in 1725 when they found an annual displacement of the wrong phase for parallax. Bradley arrived at the explanation in 1728, reportedly after watching a flag on a boat shift as the boat turned, and announced the discovery to the Royal Society in 1729. From the size of the effect he computed the speed of light at about 301,000 km/s, remarkably close to the modern value.

Later developments

Classical attempts to reconcile aberration with the luminiferous aether produced difficulties (notably Airy's 1871 water-filled telescope experiment) that were resolved by special relativity, in which aberration follows from the relativistic transformation of light direction. Bradley's observations also led him to discover the nutation of the Earth's axis.
