Fifth Generation (film)

Fifth Generation refers to a movement in Chinese cinema comprising directors who graduated from the Beijing Film Academy in 1982 — the first class to enrol after the Academy reopened following the Cultural Revolution. Their films, made from the mid-1980s onward, broke with the socialist-realist conventions of earlier Chinese cinema, favouring striking visual composition, rural settings, and oblique, often allegorical treatments of Chinese history.

Background

The "Class of '82" included directors Zhang Yimou, Chen Kaige, Tian Zhuangzhuang and Wu Ziniu, and cinematographers such as Gu Changwei. Their studies were shaped by the upheavals of the Cultural Revolution, during which many had been sent down to the countryside or served in the army; these experiences informed the movement's fascination with land, ritual and collective history.

Key films

The movement is conventionally dated from Chen Kaige's Yellow Earth (1984), photographed by Zhang Yimou in the loess landscapes of Shaanxi, which won international attention at festivals. Other landmark works include Zhang's Red Sorghum (1987), winner of the Golden Bear at Berlin; Raise the Red Lantern (1991); Chen's Farewell My Concubine (1993), which shared the Palme d'Or at Cannes; and Tian's The Blue Kite (1993), banned in China. These films brought Chinese cinema to global prominence and launched the international careers of actors such as Gong Li.

Legacy

By the early 1990s the Fifth Generation's leading directors moved toward bigger-budget historical epics and commercial productions, while a "Sixth Generation" of independent, urban-focused filmmakers emerged in reaction. Film historians credit the Fifth Generation with transforming the visual language of Chinese cinema and re-establishing it as a major force in world cinema.
