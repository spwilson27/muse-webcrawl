Michael Porter

Born: Michael Eugene Porter, 23 May 1947, Ann Arbor, Michigan, U.S.

Occupation: Academic, economist

Institution: Harvard Business School

Known for: Porter's five forces; competitive strategy; value chain

Michael Eugene Porter (born May 23, 1947) is an American academic known for his theories on economics, business strategy, and social causes. He is a Bishop William Lawrence University Professor at Harvard Business School and one of the world's most influential thinkers on management and competitiveness. He is widely regarded as the founder of the modern strategy field.

Work on competitive strategy

Porter's 1980 book Competitive Strategy introduced the five forces framework for analyzing industry attractiveness: the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers, the bargaining power of suppliers, the threat of substitute products or services, and the intensity of competitive rivalry. The same book defined three generic strategies — cost leadership, differentiation, and focus. In Competitive Advantage (1985) he introduced the value chain, a model of a firm's activities, and the concept of strategic "fit" among activities, developed further in his influential 1996 Harvard Business Review article "What Is Strategy?", which distinguished strategy from operational effectiveness.

Competitiveness and later work

In The Competitive Advantage of Nations (1990), Porter proposed the "diamond model" of national competitiveness, based on factor conditions, demand conditions, related and supporting industries, and firm strategy, structure, and rivalry; the work led to his concept of business clusters. He co-founded the consulting firm Monitor Group (1983) and the Initiative for a Competitive Inner City (1994). With Mark Kramer, he developed the concept of "creating shared value" (2011), arguing that companies can create economic value by addressing societal needs.

Porter has also applied his frameworks to healthcare (with Elizabeth Teisberg, Redefining Health Care, 2006, advocating value-based competition) and led the U.S. Competitiveness Project and the Social Progress Index. He earned his BSE in aerospace and mechanical engineering from Princeton University (1969), an MBA from Harvard Business School (1971) as a Baker Scholar, and a PhD in business economics from Harvard (1973).
