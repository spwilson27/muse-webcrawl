Opsonin

Opsonins are extracellular proteins that, when bound to substances or cells, induce phagocytes to phagocytose the substances or cells bearing the opsonins. The process is called opsonisation (or opsonization). The term derives from the Greek opsōnein, meaning to prepare for eating.

Types

The principal opsonins of the immune system are:

  • Antibodies, especially IgG, whose Fc regions are recognised by Fc receptors on macrophages and neutrophils;
  • Complement proteins C3b, C4b and iC3b, deposited on microbial surfaces during complement activation and recognised by complement receptors;
  • Collectins and other pattern-recognition molecules, such as mannose-binding lectin, ficolins, surfactant proteins and C-reactive protein, which tag pathogens and apoptotic cells for clearance.

Mechanism

By coating a particle, opsonins bridge it to the phagocyte: receptors on the phagocyte membrane engage the opsonin, triggering engulfment into a phagosome. Filamentous actin rearrangements drive the wrapping process, which has been described in "zipper" and "trigger" models. Opsonisation with C3b is a major defence against encapsulated bacteria; people lacking C3, or lacking antibody, suffer recurrent infections with such organisms.

History

The phenomenon was described in 1903 by Almroth Wright and Stewart Douglas, who showed that blood serum contained a factor — which they named opsonin — that made bacteria palatable to phagocytes. The observation helped unite the cellular and humoral theories of immunity.
