Bon Festival

The Bon Festival (Japanese: お盆, Obon, or simply Bon in its shorter form) is a Japanese Buddhist custom honouring the spirits of one's ancestors. It has been observed in Japan for more than 500 years and has evolved from the Mahayana Buddhist Ghost Festival, originating in the Ullambana Sutra, in which the disciple Mokuren (Maudgalyayana) rescued his mother from the realm of hungry ghosts through offerings to the monks.

Observance

Obon is held around the 15th day of the seventh month — in mid-August in most of Japan since the adoption of the Gregorian calendar, and in July in parts of the Kantō region; some areas still follow the lunar calendar. Families return to their hometowns, clean ancestral graves, and set up altars (butsudan or temporary shōryō-dana) with offerings of food and flowers. Fires called mukaebi welcome the spirits on the first day, and okuribi send them off on the last; Kyoto's famous Gozan no Okuribi lights great characters on five mountainsides.

Bon Odori

The best-known feature of the festival is the Bon Odori, a communal folk dance performed in a circle around a raised scaffold (yagura) with musicians and singers. Dances and songs vary widely by region — famous examples include the Awa Odori of Tokushima, the Gujō Odori of Gifu, and the Nishimonai Bon Odori of Akita. According to tradition, the dancing began with Mokuren's joyful dance on saving his mother.

Outside Japan

Japanese diaspora communities celebrate Obon in Hawaii, the mainland United States, Brazil and elsewhere, often as summer festivals at Buddhist temples featuring dancing, food stalls and memorial services. The season is one of Japan's three major holiday periods, alongside New Year and Golden Week, and trains and roads are crowded with returning families.
