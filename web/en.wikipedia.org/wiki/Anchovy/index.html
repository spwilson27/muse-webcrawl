Anchovy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A small open tin of anchovy fillets packed in olive oil]
A small open tin of anchovy fillets packed in olive oil

Anchovies are small forage fish of the family Engraulidae, found in temperate oceans worldwide; the European anchovy (Engraulis encrasicolus) and the Peruvian anchoveta (E. ringens) — the world's largest single-species fishery by volume — are the most important commercially. Eaten fresh locally, anchovies are best known preserved: salted and packed in oil, they are a pungent seasoning across Mediterranean and Southeast Asian cuisines, and the backbone of Fish sauce, Worcestershire sauce and Caesar salad.

Biology and fisheries

Anchovies are filter-feeding schooling fish of upwelling zones, converting plankton directly into food for predators — seabirds, tuna, whales — and for the fishmeal industry. The Peruvian anchoveta catch fluctuates violently with El Niño events (collapsing in 1972–73 and 1997–98, a canonical case of fishery–climate interaction), supplying much of the world's fishmeal and fish oil for aquaculture.

Culinary role

Curing in salt for months dissolves the flesh's mild fresh taste into concentrated umami; a single fillet seasons puttanesca, tapenade, anchoïade, bagna càuda, Jansson's temptation and the original 1924 Caesar salad. Their polarising reputation — especially as a pizza topping — belies their role as the invisible bass note of countless sauces, the modern descendant of Roman Garum.
