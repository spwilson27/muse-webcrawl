Preferred habitat theory

The preferred habitat theory is a theory of the term structure of interest rates which holds that investors have a preference for bonds of one maturity over another — a particular segment of the market, or "preferred habitat" — and will move away from that habitat only if they are compensated by a sufficiently higher expected return, usually in the form of a term premium.

Content

The theory, developed in a form associated with Franco Modigliani and Richard Sutch (1966), sits between the pure expectations hypothesis — which assumes investors care only about expected return and are indifferent to maturity — and market segmentation theory, which assumes investors will not leave their habitat at any price. Under preferred habitat, bond prices at each maturity are determined mainly by supply and demand within that segment, but arbitrage across segments links the structure together; yield curves can therefore take any shape depending on expectations and the size of term premiums.

Implications

Preferred habitat helps explain why yield curves are usually upward-sloping (longer maturities carry a positive term premium demanded by investors who prefer shorter habitats) and why large purchases of long-term bonds by central banks — quantitative easing — can compress long-term yields by removing supply from the segment where investors' habitat demand is less than perfectly elastic.
