Essen Minster (Essener Münster)

Location: Essen, North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany
Founded: Abbey founded c. 845; present church mainly 1275–1316 (Gothic) over Ottonian predecessors
Status: Cathedral of the Diocese of Essen since 1958
Notable treasures: Golden Madonna (c. 980); seven-branched candelabrum; Cross of Otto and Mathilda

Essen Minster (German: Essener Münster), since 1958 the cathedral church of the Diocese of Essen, is a Gothic hall church in the centre of Essen, Germany. It stands on the site of the church of Essen Abbey, a convent for noble women founded around 845, and preserves one of the oldest and most important church treasuries in Germany.

History

Essen Abbey was founded about 845 by Altfrid, later Bishop of Hildesheim, as a house for daughters of the Saxon nobility. The abbey church burned down several times. The great age of the abbey came under Abbess Mathilde II (971–1011), granddaughter of Emperor Otto I, and Abbess Theophanu (1039–1058), granddaughter of the Byzantine-born Empress Theophanu; both enriched the church with masterpieces of Ottonian goldsmith work. After a fire in 1275 the church was rebuilt as a Gothic hall church, consecrated in 1316, retaining the Ottonian westwork and crypt. The abbey was secularised in 1803. Badly damaged in the Second World War, the minster was rebuilt by 1957, and in 1958 became the cathedral of the newly created Diocese of Essen.

Architecture

The church is a three-aisled Gothic hall church with a double choir. Its most distinctive element is the octagonal westwork, modelled ultimately on the Palatine Chapel at Aachen, which still embodies substantial Ottonian fabric. The eastern crypt contains the tombs of Saint Altfrid and Abbess Mathilde. The adjoining church of St. Johann Baptist stands immediately west of the minster.

Treasury

The Essen cathedral treasury (Domschatz) ranks among the finest collections of Ottonian art to survive. Its highlights include:

  • The Golden Madonna (c. 980) — the oldest fully three-dimensional sculpture of the Virgin Mary in Western art, a wooden figure sheathed in gold leaf.
  • The Cross of Otto and Mathilda (c. 973–982), a processional cross set with enamels and gems, bearing portraits of the donors.
  • The seven-branched candelabrum (c. 1000), a monumental bronze candlestick over 2 metres tall, donated by Abbess Mathilde.
  • The Cross of Theophanu, the Cross with the large enamels, and the bejewelled cover of the Theophanu Gospels.
  • Four Ottonian processional crosses, among the largest surviving groups of their kind.
