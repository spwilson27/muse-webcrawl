Conglomerate discount

[Image placeholder: sum-of-the-parts valuation chart]

The conglomerate discount is the tendency of markets to value a diversified conglomerate at less than the combined value of its constituent businesses if they were traded separately (the "sum of the parts"). Financial studies have commonly estimated the discount in the range of roughly 10–15 percent for diversified firms, though estimates vary by period, country and method.

Explanations

Several explanations are offered. Internal capital markets may allocate investment inefficiently, propping up weak divisions with cash from strong ones ("corporate socialism"). Diversification can mask the performance of individual units, reducing transparency for investors, who can diversify their own portfolios more cheaply. Agency problems may lead managers to build empires through value-destroying acquisitions. Offset against these are possible benefits: financing advantages, tax benefits, and the ability to shift resources and talent between divisions — a potential "conglomerate premium" in economies with underdeveloped external capital markets, where business groups often trade at less of a discount or even a premium.

Implications

A persistent discount creates pressure for break-ups, spin-offs and divestitures, as management or activist investors seek to unlock the sum-of-the-parts value. Many famous conglomerates — including General Electric, which announced a split into three companies in 2021 — have eventually been dismantled in favour of focused firms.
