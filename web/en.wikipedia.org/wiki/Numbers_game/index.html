Numbers game

The numbers game, also known as the numbers racket, the Italian lottery, Mafia lottery or the daily number, is a form of illegal gambling or illegal lottery played mostly in poor and working-class neighbourhoods in the United States, wherein a bettor attempts to pick three digits to match those that will be randomly drawn the following day.

How it works

Players bet small amounts — historically as little as a penny — on a three-digit number from 000 to 999. The winning number was taken from a publicly verifiable, hard-to-manipulate source, such as the last three digits of the United States Treasury balance published in newspapers, totals from pari-mutuel betting at race tracks, or figures from stock exchanges. Because the true odds are 999-to-1 against while payouts were typically 600-to-1, the house kept a large margin.

Organisation

The racket was run through networks of runners who collected bets and slips from shops, bars and street corners, passing them up to controllers and bankers who held the money and paid winners. In Harlem, figures such as Casper Holstein and Stephanie St. Clair ("the Numbers Queen") built large operations in the 1920s; later, organised-crime families taxed or took over numbers banks, and the game was a major revenue source investigated by prosecutors including Thomas E. Dewey.

Legacy

State lotteries adopted the format legally from the 1970s onward: games such as "Pick 3" are direct descendants of the numbers game, and the legal versions were promoted partly to draw revenue away from organised crime. Numbers terminology — dream books for interpreting dreams as lucky numbers, "bolita" in Cuban-American communities, and "policy" in Chicago (where the related policy game used wheels) — remains part of American urban culture.
