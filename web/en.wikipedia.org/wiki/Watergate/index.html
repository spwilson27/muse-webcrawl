The Watergate scandal was a major American political scandal in the 1970s stemming from the Nixon administration's attempts to cover up its involvement in the 17 June 1972 break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. It led to the resignation of President Richard Nixon on 9 August 1974 — the only resignation of a US president.

Break-in and cover-up

Five burglars linked to Nixon's re-election committee (CREEP) were caught wiretapping and photographing documents. The White House denied knowledge and paid hush money; the FBI investigation was obstructed. Washington Post reporters Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, guided by the source "Deep Throat" (revealed in 2005 as FBI associate director Mark Felt), connected the burglary to a wider campaign of political espionage and sabotage.

Unravelling

Senate hearings (1973) revealed Alexander Butterfield's disclosure of Nixon's secret Oval Office taping system. Special prosecutor Archibald Cox was fired in the "Saturday Night Massacre". In United States v. Nixon (July 1974) the Supreme Court ordered the tapes released; the "smoking gun" tape proved Nixon had approved the cover-up days after the break-in. Facing certain impeachment, Nixon resigned; Vice President Gerald Ford succeeded him and pardoned him a month later. Dozens of officials were convicted. The suffix "-gate" for scandals, and campaign-finance and ethics reforms, are lasting legacies. Its era is also chronicled in works like The Federalist Papers' warnings about executive power.
