Snorkeling

Snorkeling (British and Commonwealth English spelling: snorkelling) is the practice of swimming on or through a body of water while equipped with a diving mask, a shaped breathing tube called a snorkel, and usually swimfins. In cooler waters, a wetsuit may also be worn. The primary appeal is the opportunity to observe underwater life in a natural setting without the complicated equipment and training required for scuba diving, and without exhaled bubbles disturbing the environment — making it popular with tourists and used by spearfishermen, underwater hockey players and surface support for freedivers.

Equipment

  • Mask — seals around the eyes and nose, providing an air space for clear vision and allowing pressure equalisation via the nose.
  • Snorkel — a J-shaped tube about 30–40 cm long with a mouthpiece; modern versions include splash guards and purge valves. Tube length is physiologically limited: longer tubes increase dead-space breathing and pressure difference.
  • Fins — full-foot or open-heel swimfins for propulsion.
  • Exposure protection and flotation — wetsuits or rash guards, snorkeling vests, and weight systems for duck-diving.

Technique and safety

Snorkelers breathe at the surface through the tube and may make short breath-hold "duck dives", clearing the snorkel on surfacing by a sharp exhalation ("blast clearing"). Fogging is countered with defog preparations. Principal hazards include sunburn, dehydration, boat traffic, currents, marine stings, and shallow water blackout if the snorkeler hyperventilates before breath-hold dives; drownings of tourists, sometimes associated with full-face snorkel masks and rebreathing, have prompted safety investigations in destinations such as Hawaii. Medical authorities advise snorkeling with a buddy, within one's ability, and never after alcohol.

Popularity

Snorkeling is a major component of tourism at coral-reef destinations — the Great Barrier Reef, the Red Sea, the Caribbean, Hawaii and Southeast Asia — and is practised over reefs, kelp forests, wrecks and even in flooded quarries. Because no certification is required, instruction typically consists of a short briefing; tour operators may require flotation for weak swimmers.
