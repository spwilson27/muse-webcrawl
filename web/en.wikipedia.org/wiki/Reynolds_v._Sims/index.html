Reynolds v. Sims

Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533 (1964), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision holding that districts for both houses of a state legislature must be apportioned substantially according to population. The ruling established the principle popularly summarized as “one person, one vote.”

Citation
377 U.S. 533
Decided
June 15, 1964
Holding
The Equal Protection Clause requires state legislative districts to be as nearly equal in population as practicable.

Background

Alabama had not reapportioned its legislature since 1901 despite enormous population shifts. Urban residents challenged districts that gave a vote in a sparsely populated county far more weight than a vote in a populous county. The case followed Baker v. Carr, which allowed federal courts to hear apportionment disputes, and Wesberry v. Sanders, which required population equality for congressional districts.

Decision

Chief Justice Earl Warren’s majority opinion reasoned that legislators represent people, not trees or acres. Because the right to vote is fundamental, substantial population disparities diluted the votes of citizens in larger districts and violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court rejected the argument that one legislative house could be based on geography by analogy to the United States Senate; states, unlike the federal union, were not formed by sovereign political units in the same way.

Dissent and debate

Justice John Marshall Harlan II dissented, criticizing the majority’s reading of the Fourteenth Amendment and warning that the Court was imposing a single political theory on the states. Supporters regarded the decision as essential democratic equality; critics argued it reduced flexibility for regional representation.

Impact

Reynolds triggered redistricting across the United States and shifted political power toward urban and suburban areas. Together with later cases, it remains a foundation of American election law and a constraint whenever district populations diverge.
