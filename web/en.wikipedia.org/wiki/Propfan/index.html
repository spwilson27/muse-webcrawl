Propfan

A propfan, also called an open rotor engine, is a type of aircraft engine related in concept to both the turboprop and turbofan. It uses a gas turbine to drive an unducted propeller-like fan with a large number of short, highly twisted blades, giving it a bypass ratio far higher than a conventional turbofan and propulsive efficiency superior to turboprops at high subsonic speeds. The design is also known as an unducted fan (UDF) or open rotor.

History

Propfan research was driven by the fuel price shocks of the 1970s. NASA and industry studies, including Hamilton Standard's propfan work, culminated in flight demonstrators in the 1980s: the General Electric GE36 unducted fan flew on a Boeing 727 and a McDonnell Douglas MD-80 in 1986–87, and the Pratt & Whitney–Allison 578-DX was also flight-tested. Falling fuel prices at the end of the 1980s removed the economic incentive, and no Western propfan entered production.

Production and modern developments

The only propfan to reach production is the Progress D-27 powering the Ukrainian Antonov An-70 military transport, with contra-rotating propellers of 14 blades. Interest revived with rising fuel costs and emissions concerns: Safran flight-tested an open rotor demonstrator under the EU Clean Sky programme in 2017, and in 2021 CFM International announced the RISE (Revolutionary Innovation for Sustainable Engines) programme, targeting an open-fan architecture for entry into service in the mid-2030s with around 20% lower fuel burn.

Design

Propfan blades are thin, swept and scimitar-shaped to delay shock formation at cruise Mach numbers around 0.8. Configurations include single-rotation and contra-rotating propellers, usually mounted at the rear fuselage ("pusher") or on the wing. Locating the unducted fan raises issues of cabin noise, blade containment, and ground clearance, which modern designs address with advanced blade aerodynamics and acoustic treatment.
