The Windrush scandal was a British political scandal that began in 2018 concerning people who were wrongly detained, denied legal rights, threatened with deportation, and in at least 83 cases wrongly deported from the UK by the Home Office. Many of those affected had arrived in the UK from Caribbean countries as children in the 1950s and 1960s and were members of the "Windrush generation", named after the ship Empire Windrush, which brought one of the first large groups of West Indian migrants to Tilbury in 1948.

Background

Under the British Nationality Act 1948, citizens of the UK and Colonies had the right to settle in Britain. Those who arrived before the Immigration Act 1971 came into force were granted indefinite leave to remain, but many were never given documents proving their status, and the Home Office kept no records. In 2010 the Home Office destroyed the landing cards that recorded arrivals, removing a key source of evidence. From 2012, under the "hostile environment" policy associated with Home Secretary Theresa May, landlords, employers, banks, and the NHS were required to check immigration status, and long-term residents unable to document their right to be in the UK lost jobs, homes, benefits, and medical care, or were detained and removed.

Exposure and response

Journalistic investigations—notably by Amelia Gentleman of The Guardian—brought individual cases to public attention in late 2017 and early 2018. The scandal broke into a national crisis in April 2018 during the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in London. The government apologised; Home Secretary Amber Rudd resigned on 29 April 2018 after misleading Parliament about the existence of removal targets. A compensation scheme was established in 2019, though it was widely criticised for slowness and low payments. An independent review by Wendy Williams, the Windrush Lessons Learned Review (2020), concluded the Home Office had shown "ignorance and thoughtlessness" toward the race and history of the Windrush generation and that the scandal was foreseeable and avoidable.

Legacy

Windrush Day is marked on 22 June. The scandal prompted wider scrutiny of the hostile environment policies and of the Home Office's culture, and several victims' cases remained unresolved years later; advocacy groups have continued to press for faster compensation and for those wrongly deported to be brought home.
