Roman society

Roman society was the social system of ancient Rome, structured around a steep hierarchy of status, wealth, and legal privilege. Although formally divided into classes, Roman society prized the network of personal obligations — patronage, family, and citizenship — that bound its members together, and its structures evolved considerably between the early Republic and the late Empire.

Classes and orders

Roman tradition divided citizens into patricians, the hereditary aristocracy, and plebeians, the common citizens. The Conflict of the Orders (roughly 494–287 BC) won plebeians political rights, including their own magistrates, the tribunes. By the late Republic, status was organised by wealth-based orders: the senatorial order at the top, requiring property of a million sesterces and supplying magistrates and governors; the equestrian order below it, a business and administrative class; and the mass of ordinary citizens (plebs), ranging from prosperous tradesmen to the urban poor dependent on subsidised grain (the annona) and the patronage of the rich.

Around and beneath the citizen body stood non-citizens: resident foreigners (peregrini), freedpeople, and slaves. Citizenship spread steadily through Italy and the provinces until the Edict of Caracalla (AD 212) granted it to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire. In late antiquity the formal distinction that mattered most was between the privileged honestiores and the humble humiliores, who faced harsher legal penalties.

Slavery and freedpeople

Rome was a slave society: enslaved people worked in households, farms, mines, workshops, and the civil service, and at times formed a substantial fraction of Italy's population. Manumission was frequent, and freedmen and freedwomen (liberti) became citizens, though barred from magistracies; their freeborn children faced no such bar. Some imperial freedmen rose to great power in the emperor's administration. A freedperson owed continuing obligations of respect and service (obsequium and operae) to their former owner, now their patron.

Family and patronage

The household (familia) was headed by the pater familias, who in law held extensive power over children, slaves, and property, though in practice custom moderated it. Marriage, adoption, and inheritance were instruments of alliance and property strategy among the elite. The patron–client relationship structured daily life: clients attended their patron's morning reception (salutatio), offered political support and services, and received protection, legal help, and gifts of food or money in return.

Women

Women could not vote or hold office, but elite women managed property, influenced politics through family networks, and participated in religion, including the Vestal Virgins. Over time legal guardianship over women weakened, and by the Empire many women acted independently in business and law.

Daily life

Most city dwellers lived in rented rooms in multi-storey apartment blocks (insulae), ate at cookshops, bathed in public baths, and attended games and races provided by magistrates and emperors. Bread, circuses, religious festivals, and collegia — guilds and burial societies — structured communal life. Roman society's values emphasised virtus, pietas (duty to gods, family, and state), gravitas, and the pursuit of honour and reputation.
