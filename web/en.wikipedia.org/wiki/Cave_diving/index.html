Cave diving is underwater diving in water-filled caves. It is a form of technical diving conducted in an overhead environment, meaning the diver cannot ascend directly to the surface because the cave ceiling blocks the way. Cave diving is widely regarded as one of the most hazardous forms of recreational diving, and it requires specialised training, redundant equipment, and strict adherence to procedures such as continuous guideline use and conservative gas management.

History

Exploration of flooded caves began in the United Kingdom and France in the early twentieth century, initially using standard diving dress. In 1935, members of the Cave Diving Group in Britain began penetrating sumps in Wookey Hole and Swildon's Hole. After the Second World War, Jacques Cousteau's divers explored French resurgences such as the Fontaine de Vaucluse, where a fatal attempt in 1946 highlighted the dangers. In Florida, the vast flooded limestone systems of the Floridan aquifer became a centre of cave diving from the 1960s onward.

The high fatality rate among untrained divers in Florida caves during the 1960s and 1970s led to the development of formal training. Sheck Exley, a pioneer who logged thousands of cave dives, analysed cave diving accidents and codified the safety rules still taught today, including training, guideline use, the rule of thirds for gas, depth limits, and adequate lighting. Exley died in 1994 while attempting a deep dive in Zacatón, Mexico.

Equipment and techniques

Cave divers use redundant life-support systems: two independent regulators and usually a twin-cylinder backmount set or a sidemount configuration. A continuous guideline is laid from open water to the point of penetration so the team can exit even in zero visibility caused by a silt-out, in which fine sediment disturbed by fins or bubbles reduces visibility to nothing. Divers use specialised finning techniques such as the modified frog kick to avoid disturbing silt.

Gas is managed by the rule of thirds: one third of the supply for penetration, one third for exit, and one third reserved for emergencies. At least three lights are carried. Decompression and mixed-gas techniques are added when cave dives exceed recreational depth or time limits.

Hazards

Accident analyses consistently find that most cave diving fatalities involve divers without cave training. Common factors include failure to use a guideline, exceeding gas reserves, silt-outs, and becoming lost. Because direct ascent is impossible, even minor problems such as a failed light or a lost line can become fatal.

Notable systems

Famous cave systems include Sistema Ox Bel Ha and Sistema Sac Actun in Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula — among the longest underwater caves in the world — the Woodville Karst Plain systems including Wakulla Springs in Florida, and the deep blue holes of the Bahamas. In Europe, the Lot region of France and Spain's Pozo Azul are well known.
