Penance

TraditionsRoman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, some Anglican and Lutheran practice
ElementsContrition, confession, satisfaction, absolution

Penance is repentance of sins as well as an alternate name for the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Oriental Orthodox sacrament of Reconciliation or Confession. It also plays a part in confession among Anglicans and Methodists, in which it is a rite, as well as among other Protestants.

Sacrament of Penance

In the Roman Catholic Church, the Sacrament of Penance (commonly called Confession, Reconciliation, or Penance and Reconciliation) is one of the seven sacraments and one of two sacraments of healing, alongside the Anointing of the Sick. Its traditional elements are contrition (sorrow for sin and resolve to amend), confession of sins to a priest, absolution pronounced by the priest, and satisfaction — the "penance" in the narrower sense, acts of prayer, fasting, almsgiving, or restitution assigned by the confessor to help repair the harm of sin.

Historical development

In the early Church, reconciliation after grave sins such as apostasy, murder, or adultery involved a public and often lengthy order of penitents, with readmission typically once in a lifetime. From the 6th and 7th centuries, Irish and British monastic practice spread a repeatable, private form of confession guided by penitential books that assigned specified penances for specified sins. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required annual confession to one's own priest. The Council of Trent (1551) defined the sacrament against Reformation critiques, and the 1983 Code of Canon Law continues to require annual confession of grave sins.

Other traditions

Eastern Orthodoxy treats confession as a mystery of healing, typically conducted before an icon with the priest as witness and spiritual physician, and often without fixed assigned penances. Many Protestant traditions rejected sacramental confession and auricular confession to a priest, while retaining repentance, public confession of sin, and in some cases private pastoral confession; the Anglican formularies permit individual confession as an optional rite ("all may, some should, none must"). Concepts resembling penance — repentance and acts of expiation — appear in Judaism (teshuvah), Islam (tawbah), and dharmic traditions (prāyaścitta).
