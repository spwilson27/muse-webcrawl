Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores, Inc., 573 U.S. 682 (2014), is a landmark decision by the United States Supreme Court allowing privately held for-profit corporations to be exempt from a regulation its owners religiously object to, if there is a less restrictive means of furthering the law's interest. The case concerned the contraceptive mandate in the Affordable Care Act, which required employer health plans to cover certain contraceptives.

Background

The Green family, owners of the arts-and-crafts chain Hobby Lobby, and the Hahn family, owners of Conestoga Wood Specialties, objected on religious grounds to providing coverage for four of the mandated contraceptive methods, which they considered abortifacients. They sued under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993 (RFRA).

Decision

In a 5–4 decision written by Justice Samuel Alito, the Court held that RFRA applies to closely held for-profit corporations, that the mandate substantially burdened the plaintiffs' religious exercise, and that it was not the least restrictive means of guaranteeing cost-free access to contraception. Justice Ruth Bader Ginsburg's dissent warned the ruling could open the door to widespread religious exemptions from generally applicable laws.

Significance

The decision was the first in which the Court recognised a for-profit corporation's right to religious exercise under RFRA, and it remains a major precedent in debates over religious liberty, corporate personhood, and reproductive rights.
