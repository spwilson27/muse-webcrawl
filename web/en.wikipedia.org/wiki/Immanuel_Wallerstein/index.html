Immanuel Maurice Wallerstein (1930–2019) was an American sociologist and economic historian, best known as the founder of world-systems analysis, an approach to history and social change that treats the modern world as a single capitalist "world-economy" structured into core, semiperipheral and peripheral zones.

Career

Wallerstein studied at Columbia University, taking his PhD in 1959 with work on African politics and decolonisation. He taught at Columbia and McGill before joining Binghamton University (SUNY) in 1976, where he founded the Fernand Braudel Center for the Study of Economies, Historical Systems, and Civilizations. He served as president of the International Sociological Association (1994–1998) and ended his career as a senior research scholar at Yale.

World-systems analysis

His four-volume The Modern World-System (1974–2011) traced the emergence of a capitalist world-economy in the "long sixteenth century" and its expansion to cover the globe. Drawing on the Annales school, Marxism and dependency theory, Wallerstein argued that development and underdevelopment are two faces of a single historical process, and that nation-states cannot be understood as independent units of analysis. In later work he predicted a prolonged structural crisis and systemic transition of the capitalist world-system.
