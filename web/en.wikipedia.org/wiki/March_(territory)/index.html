March (territory)

In medieval Europe, a march (from Old Frankish marka, "border") was a frontier territory created as a buffer zone and military district between a kingdom and potentially hostile neighbours. Marches were governed by a margrave (German Markgraf, "march count"), who held broader military authority than an ordinary count.

Carolingian marches

The Frankish kings, especially Charlemagne, organised marches along their frontiers: the Spanish March (Marca Hispanica) against al-Andalus, ancestor of Catalonia; the Breton March; the Saxon and Avar marches in the east; and the March of Friuli facing the Adriatic. These districts combined defence with gradual expansion and colonisation.

Later examples

In the Holy Roman Empire, border marches such as the March of Brandenburg, the Margraviate of Austria (Ostmark), and the March of Meissen grew into major principalities. In Britain, the Welsh Marches and the Anglo-Scottish border marches were ruled by Marcher Lords with quasi-royal powers. Similar frontier institutions existed elsewhere, from the Byzantine themes to the krajina of the Habsburg Military Frontier.
