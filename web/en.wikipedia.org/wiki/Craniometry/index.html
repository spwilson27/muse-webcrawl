Craniometry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Craniometry is the measurement of the cranium (the main part of the skull), usually the human cranium. It differs from phrenology, which claimed to read character from the external shape of the head, and from craniology, the broader study of skull form. Craniometry was developed in the 19th century as a branch of physical anthropology and anthropometry, and its measurements are still used in anatomy, archaeology, palaeoanthropology and forensic science, although its historical use to rank human populations racially has been discredited.

Standard craniometric landmarks used in measuring the skull.

Measurements and indices

Craniometrists defined standard landmarks on the skull — such as glabella, bregma, lambda, nasion and basion — and standard measurements between them, including maximum cranial length and breadth, cranial height, facial lengths and cranial capacity (measured by filling the braincase with shot or seed, or estimated from external dimensions). From these were derived indices, most famously the cephalic index (breadth ÷ length × 100), used by Anders Retzius to classify skulls as dolichocephalic (long-headed), mesocephalic or brachycephalic (short-headed).

History

Systematic skull measurement began with comparative anatomists such as Pieter Camper (whose "facial angle" was meant to compare species, not rank races) and Johann Friedrich Blumenbach, who assembled a famous skull collection. In the 19th century Samuel George Morton measured hundreds of skulls to compute cranial capacities, which he interpreted as evidence of racial hierarchy — an interpretation later criticised by Stephen Jay Gould in The Mismeasure of Man (1981) and debated by subsequent re-measurements. Paul Broca and the Paris school developed precision instruments and methods (craniophores, goniometers, calipers) that professionalised the field. In the 20th century Franz Boas showed that cranial form in immigrants' descendants changed within a generation, undermining the idea of fixed racial types, and craniometry's role in racial classification declined.

Modern applications

Today craniometric data are used in palaeoanthropology to compare fossil hominins, in bioarchaeology to study past populations' health, diet and migration, in forensic anthropology (with appropriate population references and stated error rates), and in clinical contexts such as assessing cranial deformities in infants. Modern practice uses three-dimensional landmark data from CT scans and geometric morphometrics rather than simple indices.
