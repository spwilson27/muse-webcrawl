Languages of the Caucasus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The languages of the Caucasus are among the most diverse in the world for so small an area: medieval Arab geographers called the region the "mountain of tongues". Some fifty languages are spoken between the Black Sea and the Caspian. They comprise three indigenous families found nowhere else — Kartvelian, Northeast Caucasian (Nakh-Dagestanian) and Northwest Caucasian (Abkhaz-Adyghe) — together with languages of wider families: Indo-European (Armenian, Ossetian, Russian, Greek), Turkic (Azerbaijani, Kumyk, Nogai, Karachay-Balkar) and others.

Indigenous families

Kartvelian

The Kartvelian (South Caucasian) family of Georgia includes Georgian — written in its own unique script and the literary language of the region's oldest state tradition — and its relatives Mingrelian, Laz and Svan.

Northeast Caucasian

The Nakh-Dagestanian family includes the Nakh languages (Chechen, Ingush, Bats) and the many languages of Dagestan, such as Avar, Dargin, Lezgian, Lak and Tabasaran. Dagestan alone hosts dozens of distinct languages, some spoken in a handful of villages. These languages are famed for enormous consonant inventories and rich systems of noun classes and cases.

Northwest Caucasian

The Abkhaz-Adyghe family includes Abkhaz, Abaza, Circassian (Adyghe and Kabardian) and the extinct Ubykh. Ubykh, whose last native speaker, Tevfik Esenç, died in Turkey in 1992, was famous for having one of the largest consonant inventories of any language (84 or more consonants) and only two or three vowels.

Languages of wider distribution

Armenian, an independent branch of Indo-European with its own alphabet (created c. 405 AD), dominates the southern Caucasus, alongside Turkic Azerbaijani. Ossetian, an Iranian language, is spoken astride the central range; its speakers descend in part from the medieval Alans. Russian spread as the imperial and Soviet lingua franca and remains widely understood.

Characteristics

The indigenous Caucasian families share areal features: ejective consonants, ergative alignment in many languages, large case systems (some Dagestanian languages mark dozens of locative cases), and vigesimal counting in places. The extreme diversity is attributed to mountainous terrain isolating valleys, combined with the region's position as a crossroads of empires, which repeatedly pushed refugee communities into the highlands.
