Minoan eruption

The Minoan eruption was a catastrophic volcanic eruption that devastated the island of Thera (modern Santorini) in the Aegean Sea in the mid-second millennium BC. One of the largest volcanic events in recorded history, it ejected an estimated 30–60 km³ of material (VEI 6–7), burying the Minoan town of Akrotiri under metres of pumice and ash and generating tsunamis that struck the coast of Crete.

Dating

The eruption's date is famously disputed. Radiocarbon dating of an olive tree buried by the eruption and of seeds from Akrotiri points to the late 17th century BC (around 1620s BC), while conventional archaeological chronologies, synchronized with Egyptian records, favor a date around 1500 BC. Ice-core and tree-ring evidence has been invoked for several candidate dates; the discrepancy remains unresolved and matters greatly for eastern Mediterranean chronology.

Effects and legend

Akrotiri, preserved like a Bronze Age Pompeii, yielded elaborate multi-storey houses and vivid frescoes. The eruption weakened Minoan civilization, though Crete's palaces continued for decades afterward. Ash fell as far as the Nile Delta, and the event has often been proposed as a source for Plato's legend of Atlantis and for traditions behind the biblical Exodus plagues, though both connections are speculative. The eruption created much of the present crescent shape of Santorini.
