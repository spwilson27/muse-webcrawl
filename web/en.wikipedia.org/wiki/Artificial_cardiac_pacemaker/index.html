Artificial cardiac pacemaker

Artificial cardiac pacemaker
Type: Implantable medical device
First human implant: 1958 (Arne Larsson; device by Åke Senning and Rune Elmqvist)
Function: Electrical regulation of heartbeat
Power: Lithium–iodine battery (typically 6–15 years)

An artificial cardiac pacemaker, commonly just pacemaker, is an implanted medical device that generates electrical pulses to prompt the heart to contract, controlling abnormal heart rhythms (arrhythmias), especially bradycardia — a heartbeat that is too slow.

How it works

A pulse generator (a sealed titanium case containing a battery and circuitry) is implanted under the skin near the collarbone, with one or more insulated leads threaded through a vein into the heart's chambers. The device senses the heart's own activity and delivers a pacing pulse only when needed ("demand" pacing). Modern devices are rate-responsive, adjust to activity, store diagnostic data, and may pace both ventricles (cardiac resynchronisation therapy) or be entirely leadless, implanted inside the right ventricle.

History

External pacing was demonstrated in the 1950s by Paul Zoll. The first fully implantable pacemaker was implanted in Stockholm in 1958 by surgeon Åke Senning using a device designed by Rune Elmqvist; the recipient, Arne Larsson, outlived both and received over twenty replacement devices. Wilson Greatbatch's work in the US led to practical long-lived implants from 1960, and lithium batteries in the 1970s extended device life to a decade or more.

Indications and risks

Pacemakers are indicated for symptomatic bradycardia, heart block, and some forms of heart failure. Risks include infection, lead dislodgement, and interference concerns, largely reduced in modern MRI-conditional devices.
