Compass-and-straightedge construction

In geometry, compass-and-straightedge construction – also known as ruler-and-compass construction, Euclidean construction, or classical construction – is the construction of lengths, angles, and other geometric figures using only an idealized ruler and a pair of compasses.

The idealized ruler, known as a straightedge, is assumed to be infinite in length, have only one edge, and no markings on it. The compass is assumed to have no maximum or minimum radius, and is assumed to "collapse" when lifted from the page, so it may not be directly used to transfer distances. (This restriction is of no practical significance since, even with a collapsing compass, distances can still be transferred using Euclid's Proposition 2 in Book 1 of the Elements.) More formally, the only permissible constructions are those granted by the first three postulates of Euclid's Elements.

History

The ancient Greek mathematicians first attempted straightedge-and-compass constructions and discovered how to construct sums, differences, products, and quotients of lengths, and the square root of a given length. They could construct half of a given angle, the square whose area is twice that of another square, the square having the same area as a given polygon, and regular polygons of 3, 4, 5, and 15 sides, as well as polygons with double those numbers of sides.

Impossible constructions

Three classical problems, posed in antiquity, were proven impossible in the 19th century using algebraic methods:

  • Squaring the circle – constructing a square with the same area as a given circle. Impossible because π is a transcendental number, proved by Ferdinand von Lindemann in 1882.
  • Doubling the cube – constructing a cube with twice the volume of a given cube; requires constructing the cube root of 2, which is not a constructible number.
  • Trisecting the angle – dividing an arbitrary angle into three equal parts; impossible in general, as shown by Pierre Wantzel in 1837.

Constructible numbers and polygons

A point is constructible if it can be produced from given points using the allowed operations; equivalently, the coordinates of a constructible point are constructible numbers, obtained from the rationals by addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and the extraction of square roots. By the Gauss–Wantzel theorem, a regular polygon with n sides is constructible if and only if n is the product of a power of 2 and any number of distinct Fermat primes.

Extensions

By relaxing the rules – for example by allowing a marked ruler (neusis construction) or paper folding (origami, governed by the Huzita–Hatori axioms) – angle trisection and cube doubling become possible. Jakob Steiner showed that constructions possible with compass and straightedge are also possible with a straightedge alone given a single circle and its center (the Poncelet–Steiner theorem), and Lorenzo Mascheroni showed that the compass alone suffices (the Mohr–Mascheroni theorem).
