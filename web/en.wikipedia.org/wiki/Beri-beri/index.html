Beriberi is a disease caused by a deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). The name is generally said to derive from a Sinhalese phrase meaning "weak, weak" — "I cannot, I cannot". Thiamine is essential for carbohydrate metabolism and for the function of nerves and the heart; the body stores little of it, so deficiency can develop within weeks on a thiamine-free diet.

Forms and symptoms

Wet beriberi primarily affects the cardiovascular system: peripheral vasodilation and high-output heart failure, with fluid retention and oedema of the legs, rapid heartbeat and breathlessness; a fulminant variant, shoshin beriberi, can be rapidly fatal. Dry beriberi primarily affects the nervous system: a symmetrical peripheral neuropathy with pain, numbness and weakness of the legs, loss of reflexes and wasting of muscles, sometimes progressing to wrist drop and foot drop. In alcohol-related deficiency the related Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome affects the brain. Infantile beriberi strikes breastfed babies of thiamine-deficient mothers and can cause sudden cardiac death.

History

Beriberi was endemic wherever polished white rice was the staple food, the milling removing the thiamine-rich husk and bran. In the 1880s the Japanese naval surgeon Takaki Kanehiro dramatically reduced it in the Imperial Japanese Navy by adding meat and barley to sailors' diets. In the Dutch East Indies, Christiaan Eijkman found that chickens fed polished rice developed a beriberi-like polyneuritis cured by rice bran; his colleague Gerrit Grijns proposed that rice lacked a vital protective substance. Casimir Funk isolated the "vitamine" (thiamine) concept from these findings in 1912, and Eijkman shared the 1929 Nobel Prize for the discovery of vitamins. The disease remains a public-health problem in parts of Asia and in refugee and prison populations, and occurs in the West mainly with alcoholism, bariatric surgery and hyperemesis gravidarum.

Treatment

Treatment is prompt thiamine replacement by mouth or injection, which can reverse cardiac symptoms within hours to days, together with a balanced diet; neurological recovery is slower and may be incomplete.
