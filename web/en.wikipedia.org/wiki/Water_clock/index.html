Water clock

A water clock, or clepsydra (from the Greek for "water thief"), is a timepiece in which time is measured by the regulated flow of liquid into or out of a vessel. Water clocks are among the oldest time-measuring instruments, second only to sundials and gnomons.

History

The earliest surviving water clock, from the reign of Amenhotep I (c. 1500 BC), was found in Egypt; Babylonian and Indian examples are also ancient. In Greece and Rome, clepsydrae timed speeches in law courts — hence the expression "his water has run out". The Hellenistic inventor Ctesibius (3rd century BC) added feedback regulators and geared indicators. Chinese engineers developed elaborate water-driven clocks, culminating in Su Song's astronomical clock tower (1094). In the Islamic world, al-Jazari's castle clock (1206) combined water power with automaton figures.

Types

Outflow clocks empty a vessel through a small orifice, with marks inside indicating the hours; inflow clocks fill a vessel, with a float rising past graduations. Their chief weaknesses are temperature-dependent viscosity, clogging, and freezing — drawbacks that kept them secondary to mechanical clocks from the 14th century, though refined water clocks remained in use into the 17th century.
