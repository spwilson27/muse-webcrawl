In graph theory, two graphs G and G′ are homeomorphic if there is a graph isomorphism from some subdivision of G to some subdivision of G′. A subdivision of a graph is obtained by inserting new vertices of degree two into its edges; the reverse operation, removing a degree-two vertex and merging its two incident edges, is called smoothing. If two graphs are homeomorphic, they can each be obtained from a common graph by a sequence of subdivisions, and they have essentially the same topological structure when viewed as one-dimensional topological spaces.

Formally, an edge subdivision replaces an edge (u, v) with a path u–w–v through a new vertex w. Two graphs are homeomorphic if one can be transformed into the other by a sequence of subdivisions and smoothings. Every graph is homeomorphic to a unique (up to isomorphism) graph with no vertices of degree two, obtained by smoothing them all away, except in special cases such as cycle graphs.

Relation to planarity

Homeomorphism is central to the classical characterization of planar graphs. Kuratowski's theorem (1930) states that a finite graph is planar if and only if it does not contain a subgraph homeomorphic to K5 (the complete graph on five vertices) or to K3,3 (the complete bipartite graph on two sets of three vertices). Such subgraphs are called Kuratowski subgraphs. This is related to, but distinct from, the notion of graph minors used in Wagner's theorem: a homeomorphic copy is a topological minor, a stronger requirement than an ordinary minor obtained by edge contractions.

Embedding on surfaces

The concept extends to embeddings of graphs on other surfaces: two embeddings may be compared via homeomorphisms of the surface itself. When a graph is regarded as a topological space (a 1-dimensional CW complex), homeomorphism of graphs coincides with homeomorphism of the corresponding spaces, provided vertices of degree two are disregarded appropriately.
