Stamp Act 1765

The Stamp Act 1765, also known as the Duties in American Colonies Act 1765, was an Act of the Parliament of Great Britain that imposed a direct tax on the British colonies in America. It required that many printed materials in the colonies be produced on stamped paper made in London, carrying an embossed revenue stamp. The tax applied to legal documents, licences, newspapers, pamphlets, playing cards and other printed matter.

A one-penny stamp issued under the Stamp Act

Background

After the Seven Years' War, Britain sought revenue to pay for the defence of the colonies and its enlarged empire. Prime Minister George Grenville's ministry introduced the Stamp Act, which was passed in March 1765 and was to take effect on 1 November 1765. Unlike earlier measures regulating trade, it was an internal tax levied directly on the colonists.

Colonial resistance

The Act provoked widespread protest under the slogan "no taxation without representation", since the colonies had no members in Parliament. Colonial assemblies passed resolutions against it, mobs forced stamp distributors to resign, and the Stamp Act Congress of October 1765 — the first united colonial gathering of its kind — petitioned against it. Non-importation agreements by colonial merchants hurt British trade.

Repeal

Under pressure from merchants and a changed ministry, Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in March 1766, simultaneously passing the Declaratory Act, which asserted Parliament's authority to legislate for the colonies "in all cases whatsoever". The crisis is regarded as a key step on the road to the American Revolution.
