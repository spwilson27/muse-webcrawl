Aromatic compound

An aromatic compound is an organic compound containing one or more planar rings of atoms joined by delocalised pi electrons, conferring unusual stability known as aromaticity. The archetype is benzene, whose six-carbon ring was first explained by August Kekulé in 1865.

Aromaticity

According to Hückel's rule, a planar, cyclic, conjugated system is aromatic when it contains 4n + 2 pi electrons. Aromatic rings are unusually stable and typically undergo electrophilic substitution rather than addition reactions, preserving the pi system.

Examples

Common aromatic compounds include benzene, toluene, naphthalene, anthracene and phenol, as well as heterocycles such as pyridine, furan, thiophene and pyrrole. Many biologically important molecules — including the amino acids phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan, and the nucleobases of DNA — contain aromatic rings.

Applications

Aromatic compounds are feedstocks for dyes, pharmaceuticals, polymers such as polystyrene and nylon, and solvents. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are also environmental pollutants formed by incomplete combustion.
