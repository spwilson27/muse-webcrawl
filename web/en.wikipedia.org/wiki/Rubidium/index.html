Rubidium is a chemical element with symbol Rb and atomic number 37. It is a very soft, whitish-grey alkali metal that oxidises almost instantly in air and reacts violently with water. Rubidium was discovered spectroscopically in 1861 by Robert Bunsen and Gustav Kirchhoff in the mineral lepidolite, and is named from the Latin rubidus, "deepest red", for the bright red lines in its emission spectrum.

Properties and occurrence

Rubidium melts at 39.3 °C, just above room temperature, and its compounds impart a purple colour to flames. Although not rare — it is about as abundant in the crust as zinc — it forms no minerals of its own and is obtained as a by-product of lepidolite and pollucite processing. Natural rubidium contains the stable isotope Rb-85 and the slightly radioactive Rb-87 (half-life ~49 billion years), whose decay to strontium-87 underpins rubidium–strontium dating of rocks.

Applications

Applications are specialised: rubidium vapour is used in atomic clocks (the secondary standard for the second) and in magnetometers; rubidium-82 is used as a tracer in cardiac PET imaging; the metal and its compounds serve in research on Bose–Einstein condensates (the first condensate, in 1995, was made with Rb-87), in getter tubes, photocells and speciality glasses.
