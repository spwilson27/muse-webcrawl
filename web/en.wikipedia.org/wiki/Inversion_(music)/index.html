Inversion (music)

In music theory, an inversion is a type of change to intervals, chords, voices (in counterpoint), or melodies. There are four main kinds of inversion: interval, chord, melodic, and contrapuntal.

Intervals

An interval is inverted by raising the lower note or lowering the upper note by an octave, so that the higher becomes the lower and vice versa. Inverting an interval changes its size and quality: the sum of an interval and its inversion is nine (counting letter names), so a third becomes a sixth, a second a seventh, and so on. Perfect intervals remain perfect, major intervals become minor, minor become major, augmented become diminished, and diminished become augmented.

Chords

A chord is in an inversion when a note other than the root is in the bass. A triad with the third in the bass is in first inversion; with the fifth in the bass it is in second inversion. Seventh chords admit a third inversion with the seventh in the bass. Inversions are labelled with figured bass symbols. When the root is in the bass, the chord is said to be in root position.

Melodic inversion

In melodic inversion, the intervals of a melody are flipped: ascending intervals become descending intervals of the same size, and vice versa, as if the melody were reflected in a mirror held horizontally at some axis pitch. Melodic inversion is a central device in twelve-tone technique and in fugal writing, where a subject may appear inverted as a countersubject.

Contrapuntal inversion

In double (invertible) counterpoint, two voices are written so that each can serve as the upper or lower voice; the voices are exchanged, usually at the octave, tenth, or twelfth.
