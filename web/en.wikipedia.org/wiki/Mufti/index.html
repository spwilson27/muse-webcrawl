Mufti

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mufti: an Islamic jurist qualified to issue fatwas.

A mufti (Arabic: مفتي, muftī) is an Islamic jurist qualified to issue a fatwa — a nonbinding legal opinion on a point of Islamic law (sharia). A person who formulates a fatwa is drawing on the Qur'an, the hadith, consensus (ijma), and analogical reasoning (qiyas) within one of the recognized schools of law (madhhab). Though fatwas are formally advisory rather than binding judgments, in practice a respected mufti's opinion carries great religious and social weight.

Role and qualifications

Traditionally a mufti had to be a mature Muslim of sound judgment, trained in Arabic, the Qur'anic sciences, hadith criticism, legal theory (usul al-fiqh), and the rulings of his school, ideally capable of independent reasoning (ijtihad). Petitioners (mustafti) bring questions ranging from ritual practice to finance and family law. Unlike a qadi (judge), whose rulings are enforceable, a mufti answers the question put to him and leaves implementation to the questioner and society.

Institutions

In the Ottoman Empire, the chief mufti of Istanbul, the Shaykh al-Islam, stood at the head of the religious hierarchy and could legitimize or undermine sultanic policy. In many modern states the office is institutionalized: a Grand Mufti heads official fatwa bodies such as Egypt's Dar al-Ifta al-Misriyyah (founded 1895) or Saudi Arabia's Council of Senior Scholars, and national muftis issue opinions for their country's Muslims. Since the late 20th century, fatwas have proliferated on television call-in shows and websites ("cyber muftis"), raising debates about credentialing and authority.

Other usage

In British and Commonwealth military slang, "mufti" also means ordinary civilian clothing worn by someone who usually wears a uniform — as in "mufti day" in schools. This usage derives from the 19th-century British Army in India, where off-duty officers relaxed in dressing gowns and caps resembling the robes of a mufti.
