Solar oven

A solar oven or solar cooker is a device which uses the energy of direct sunlight to heat, cook, or pasteurise food and drink. Because they use no fuel, solar ovens are promoted as a cheap, low-impact cooking method, especially in sunny developing regions where firewood collection contributes to deforestation and indoor smoke harms health.

Designs

  • Box cookers — an insulated box with a transparent lid and reflective panels that concentrate light into the interior; they typically reach 90–150 °C and cook slowly like a slow cooker.
  • Panel cookers — foldable reflective panels direct sunlight onto a pot enclosed in a clear heat-resistant bag.
  • Parabolic cookers — a parabolic reflector focuses sunlight onto a small spot, reaching temperatures comparable to a conventional stove, but requiring frequent re-aiming and supervision.
  • Evacuated tube cookers — use a borosilicate vacuum tube as the cooking chamber, retaining heat efficiently even in cold or windy weather.

History

The Swiss naturalist Horace-Bénédict de Saussure built early solar hot boxes in the 1760s, reaching temperatures above 100 °C. Modern designs were developed in the mid-20th century and popularised by organisations such as Solar Cookers International from the 1980s onward.

Uses and limitations

Solar ovens can bake, roast, and boil, and can pasteurise water. They depend on clear skies, cook most effectively around midday, and generally cannot fry at high speed; many households use them alongside conventional stoves.
