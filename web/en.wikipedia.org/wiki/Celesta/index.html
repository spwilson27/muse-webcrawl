The celesta is a keyboard percussion instrument invented in 1886 by the Parisian harmonium builder Auguste Mustel. It looks like a small upright piano, but its sound is produced by hammers striking steel plates suspended over wooden resonators, giving a soft, bell-like, ethereal tone. The celesta sounds one octave higher than written and is a transposing instrument in orchestral scores.

History and construction

Mustel's celesta developed from his earlier "typophone" and replaced Victor Mustel's dulcitone concept with metal plates and resonating chambers. The firm Schiedmayer of Stuttgart became the principal maker; the standard modern instrument has a range of five and a half octaves, though many orchestras use a four-octave model. A damper pedal operates much like a piano's sustain pedal.

Repertoire

Tchaikovsky introduced the celesta to international fame in the "Dance of the Sugar Plum Fairy" from The Nutcracker (1892), having heard the instrument in Paris and arranged for one to be smuggled to Russia before rivals could use it. Mahler used it extensively, notably in the Sixth and Eighth Symphonies and Das Lied von der Erde. Other famous parts include Bartók's Music for Strings, Percussion and Celesta (1936), Holst's "Neptune" from The Planets, and the celesta writing in Harry Potter film scores, which made its bell-like sound familiar to modern audiences.
