Shepard tone

A Shepard tone, created by Roger Shepard, is a sound consisting of a superposition of sine waves separated by octaves. When played with the bass pitch of the tone moving upward or downward, it is referred to as the Shepard scale. This creates the auditory illusion of a tone that continually ascends or descends in pitch, yet which ultimately seems to get no higher or lower.

Construction

Each sine wave component is weighted by an amplitude envelope fixed in pitch space, typically a bell-shaped curve plotted on a logarithmic frequency scale. As the components glide upward, high components fade out while new low components fade in, so the overall pitch range of the sound stays constant while the perceived pitch appears to rise endlessly—an audio analogue of a barber's pole illusion or a Penrose staircase.

Variants

The Shepard–Risset glissando, developed by Jean-Claude Risset, applies the illusion to a continuous glissando rather than discrete scale steps. A related illusion uses discrete tones that seem to fall endlessly. The tritone paradox, discovered by Diana Deutsch, concerns pairs of Shepard tones separated by a tritone, which different listeners hear as ascending or descending.

Uses

Shepard tones have been used in music and film sound design to create a sense of ever-rising tension; examples include the music of Hans Zimmer for the film Dunkirk and the endless staircase music of the video game Super Mario 64.
