Theory of value (economics)

FieldEconomics, political economy
Central questionWhat determines exchange value and price?
Major approachesLabour, utility, cost of production, subjective/marginalist, monetary and institutional theories

In economics and political economy, a theory of value is an account of what makes goods exchangeable, why they command the prices they do, and how value relates to usefulness, scarcity, cost and social relations. The subject is older than modern economics: Aristotle distinguished use from exchange, medieval writers debated the just price, and classical political economists made value the starting point for theories of wages, profit and rent.

Use value and exchange value

A recurring distinction separates use value—the capacity of a thing to satisfy a want—from exchange value, the ratio at which it trades for other goods. Water is useful but often cheap; diamonds are less necessary but expensive. This “paradox of value” pushed theorists to explain price not by usefulness alone. Solutions appealed to scarcity, labour required for production, costs, and later to marginal utility: price reflects the importance of one additional unit under given conditions, not the total usefulness of the class of goods.

Classical and labour theories

Adam Smith used labour both as a measure and, in early societies, as a source of value, while also describing market prices gravitating around natural prices composed of wages, profit and rent. David Ricardo sharpened the labour theory: relative prices in competitive conditions tend to reflect relative quantities of embodied labour, modified by capital intensity and time. Karl Marx transformed the framework by distinguishing labour from labour-power and arguing that surplus value arises because workers create more value than the value of their labour-power; for Marx, value is a social relation expressed in commodities rather than a physical property.

Marginalism and neoclassical price theory

In the 1870s William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras placed subjective valuation and marginal analysis at the centre. Consumers allocate income until marginal utility per unit of money is equalized across goods; producers and factor markets transmit those valuations into costs and prices. In mature neoclassical theory, “value theory” often becomes price theory: equilibrium prices coordinate supply and demand under constraints. This approach explains many market phenomena but is criticized for backgrounding power, institutions, money, uncertainty and the social reproduction of labour.

Other approaches

Cost-of-production theories emphasize the expenses that must be covered for supply to continue. Monetary theories stress that values are expressed and validated through money and credit. Institutional and feminist economists note that unpaid household labour, legal rules and bargaining power shape what counts as valuable. Ecological economics argues that market prices omit energy, materials and waste sinks, so value theory should include biophysical limits. Contemporary debates continue over whether value is best treated as labour embodied, utility, a monetary phenomenon, or an emergent result of pricing practices.

Why it matters

Theories of value are not scholastic. They influence how economists interpret profit, exploitation, taxation, rent, intellectual property, care work and environmental damage. Even when modern models avoid the word “value”, they rely on assumptions about preference, scarcity, production and distribution that descend from these older disputes.
