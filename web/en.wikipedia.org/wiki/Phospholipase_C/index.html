Phospholipase C

Phospholipase C (PLC) is a class of membrane-associated enzymes that cleave phospholipids just before the phosphate group. In cell signalling, the term usually refers to the eukaryotic phosphoinositide phospholipase C enzymes that hydrolyse phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into two second messengers: inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG). PLC enzymes thereby play a central role in signal transduction downstream of many cell-surface receptors.

Reaction and signalling

IP3 diffuses into the cytosol and opens calcium channels on the endoplasmic reticulum, releasing Ca2+ into the cell, while DAG remains in the membrane, where it recruits and activates protein kinase C and other targets. Together, the IP3/Ca2+ and DAG/PKC branches regulate secretion, contraction, metabolism, gene expression, and cell growth.

Families

Mammalian PLCs are grouped into six families by sequence homology — PLC-β, PLC-γ, PLC-δ, PLC-ε, PLC-ζ, and PLC-η — comprising more than a dozen isoforms. They share catalytic X and Y domains and typically contain PH, EF-hand, and C2 domains that mediate membrane targeting and calcium regulation. Different families are activated differently: PLC-β by G proteins of the Gq family downstream of G protein-coupled receptors; PLC-γ by receptor tyrosine kinases such as the epidermal growth factor receptor and by immune-receptor pathways (PLC-γ1 and PLC-γ2 are central to T-cell and B-cell signalling); and PLC-ζ, found in sperm, triggers the calcium oscillations that activate the egg at fertilisation.

Other phospholipase C enzymes

Bacteria secrete their own PLCs, some of which are virulence factors — for example, the alpha-toxin of Clostridium perfringens is a zinc-dependent phospholipase C (a lecithinase) that damages cell membranes in gas gangrene. Plants also possess PI-specific and non-specific PLCs involved in stress and hormonal signalling.
