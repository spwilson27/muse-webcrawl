The Lemkos are an East Slavic ethnic group traditionally inhabiting Lemkivshchyna, a region of the northern Carpathian Mountains straddling present-day southeastern Poland, northeastern Slovakia, and western Ukraine. They are generally considered part of the broader Rusyn people, though the question of Lemko identity — Rusyn, Ukrainian, or distinct — has been debated for over a century. Their name is often said to derive from the characteristic word lem ("only"), common in their dialect.

History

Lemko villages grew along Carpathian valleys from the 14th to 16th centuries under Wallachian law, practising transhumant shepherding and farming marginal mountain soils. The region's wooden churches, in Greek Catholic and Orthodox traditions, are among the finest examples of Carpathian sacral architecture. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, poverty drove mass emigration to the United States and Canada; the family of artist Andy Warhol, born to Lemko immigrants in Pittsburgh, is the most famous product of this diaspora.

Deportations and revival

The Second World War and its aftermath devastated the community. In 1944–1946 many Lemkos were resettled to Soviet Ukraine, and in 1947 the Polish communist government's Operation Vistula (Akcja Wisła) deported the remainder — about 30,000 people — from their homeland to Poland's western and northern territories, emptying Lemko villages almost entirely. After 1989, a cultural revival brought language teaching, festivals such as the Lemko Vatra, and official recognition of Lemko as an ethnic minority in Poland, though only a fraction of the population returned to the Carpathians.
