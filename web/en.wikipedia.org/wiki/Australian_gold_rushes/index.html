Australian gold rushes

The Australian gold rushes were a series of gold rushes in the 19th century that transformed the Australian colonies economically, politically, and demographically. Payable gold was first officially announced near Bathurst, New South Wales, in 1851; much richer fields followed in the newly separated colony of Victoria at Ballarat and Bendigo, making Melbourne for a time one of the world's great boom cities. Later rushes occurred in Queensland, and in Western Australia at Coolgardie and Kalgoorlie in the 1890s.

Discovery and the Victorian rush

Edward Hargraves, fresh from California, announced gold at Ophir near Bathurst in February 1851, triggering Australia's first rush. Discoveries in Victoria later that year dwarfed it: the colony's population exploded from about 77,000 in 1851 to over 500,000 by 1861, and Victoria produced a large share of the world's gold. Melbourne's wealth built banks, mansions, and the young city's great public buildings.

Eureka and democracy

Resentment at the expensive miner's licence and its enforcement culminated in the Eureka Stockade at Ballarat on 3 December 1854, when diggers under Peter Lalor rose against colonial troops; at least 27 people died. The rebellion is widely credited with accelerating democratic reform: the licence was replaced by the cheaper "miner's right", which carried the vote, and Victoria soon adopted manhood suffrage and the secret ballot.

Social impact

The rushes drew migrants from Britain, Europe, America, and China; tens of thousands of Chinese diggers faced discrimination that foreshadowed the White Australia policy. Gold earnings funded infrastructure, pastoral expansion, and the "Marvellous Melbourne" boom of the 1880s, while spectacular nuggets — including the Welcome Stranger (1869), the largest alluvial gold nugget ever found — entered legend.
