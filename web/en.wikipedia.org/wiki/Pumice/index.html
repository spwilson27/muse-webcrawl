Pumice

Pumice is a volcanic rock that consists of highly vesicular, rough-textured volcanic glass. It is typically light in colour and so porous that many pieces have a density less than that of water and float. Pumice forms when super-heated, highly pressurized lava is violently ejected from a volcano; as the magma decompresses and cools rapidly, dissolved gases exsolve and become trapped as countless tiny bubbles, producing a frothy solid.

Properties and formation

Pumice is most commonly of felsic composition (rhyolitic or dacitic) because viscous, silica-rich magmas trap gas most effectively, although basaltic pumices occur. The vesicles may be spherical, elongate, or tube-like, and the glassy walls between them are extremely thin. Large eruptions can produce pumice rafts that drift across oceans — famously after the 2006 eruption near Tonga and the 2012 Havre eruption — carrying marine organisms over great distances.

Uses

Pumice has been used since antiquity as an abrasive: pumice stones remove dead skin, ground pumice polishes and cleans, and pumice is used in heavy-duty hand soaps, pencil erasers, and in the stonewashing of denim. It is also a lightweight aggregate in concrete (used by the Romans, notably in the Pantheon's dome), a soil amendment and growing medium in horticulture, and a filtration aid. Major producers include Italy (Lipari Islands), Turkey, Greece, and the United States.
