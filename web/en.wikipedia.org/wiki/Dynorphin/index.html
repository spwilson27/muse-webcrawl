Dynorphin

Dynorphins (DYN) are a class of opioid peptides that arise from the precursor protein prodynorphin. When prodynorphin is cleaved during processing by proprotein convertase 2 (PC2), multiple active peptides are released: dynorphin A, dynorphin B, and α/β-neo-endorphin. These peptides share the same N-terminal sequence, Tyr-Gly-Gly-Phe-Leu (Leu-enkephalin), but differ in their C-terminal extensions.

Dynorphins exert their effects primarily through the κ-opioid receptor (KOR), though they have some affinity for the μ-opioid receptor (MOR), δ-opioid receptor (DOR), and NMDA receptors as well. Depending upon the region of the nervous system and the receptor engaged, dynorphin can produce analgesia, dysphoria, and modulation of stress and reward pathways.

History

Dynorphin was first isolated from porcine pituitary glands by Avram Goldstein and colleagues in 1979. Its name derives from the Greek dynamis (power), reflecting its extraordinary potency — dynorphin A (1–17) was found to be roughly 200 times more potent than morphine and 50 times more potent than β-endorphin in the guinea-pig ileum assay. The full 17-amino-acid sequence of dynorphin A was published in 1981.

Function

Dynorphins are widely distributed in the central nervous system, with high concentrations in the hypothalamus, striatum, hippocampus, spinal cord, and periaqueductal grey. The dynorphin/KOR system is best known as a counterweight to the reward-promoting effects of dopamine and μ-opioid signalling: stress causes dynorphin release, which activates KORs, suppresses dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens, and produces aversion and dysphoria. This system is implicated in stress-induced relapse to drug seeking, in the negative affective states of withdrawal, and in mood disorders, making KOR antagonists a target of antidepressant and anti-addiction drug research.

Dynorphin also functions in pain processing, particularly in the spinal cord, where its upregulation contributes to chronic and neuropathic pain states, and it plays roles in appetite, thermoregulation, and neuroendocrine control.

Clinical significance

Mutations in the prodynorphin gene (PDYN) cause spinocerebellar ataxia type 23. Altered dynorphin expression has been reported in epilepsy, Alzheimer's disease, chronic pain, and addiction. KOR agonists such as difelikefalin are used clinically (for uraemic pruritus), and KOR antagonists such as aticaprant have been investigated in depression and substance-use disorders.
