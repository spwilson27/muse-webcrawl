Hymn of the Pearl

The Hymn of the Pearl is a celebrated allegorical poem preserved within the third-century Syriac Acts of Thomas, one of the apocryphal Acts of the Apostles. Traditionally attributed to the apostle Thomas singing in prison, the hymn is widely regarded as one of the masterpieces of early Syriac literature and a key text for the study of Gnosticism and early Christian mysticism.

Summary

The narrator, a young prince from the East (Parthia), is sent by his parents, the King of Kings and the Queen of the East, to Egypt to retrieve a pearl guarded by a fierce serpent. Stripped of his glorious robe, he travels in disguise, but the Egyptians feed him their food, and he falls into a forgetful sleep, losing memory of his mission and identity. His parents send a letter that flies to him like an eagle and awakens him; he charms the serpent by invoking his father's name, seizes the pearl, casts off his filthy garments, and returns home, where his splendid robe — bearing the image of his true self — comes out to meet him and clothes him once more.

Interpretation

The hymn is read as an allegory of the soul: the prince is the soul (or the savior) sent into the material world, forgetting its divine origin, awakened by revelation, and restored to glory. Parallels with the parable of the Prodigal Son, Manichaean myth, and the later Persian Tale of the Western Exile by Suhrawardi have been noted, and the poem influenced C. G. Jung's writings on individuation.
