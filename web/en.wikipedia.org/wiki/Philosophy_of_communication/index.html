Philosophy of communication

Philosophy of communication is the area of philosophy that deals with questions concerning communication. It examines the nature of communication itself, the relationship between language, meaning, and the self, and the ethical and existential dimensions of human interaction. Philosophy of communication draws on traditions including phenomenology, hermeneutics, pragmatism, and existentialism, and contributes to the broader field of communication theory.

Central questions

Philosophers of communication ask what happens when human beings address one another: how meaning arises between speakers, whether understanding is ever complete, and how communication constitutes community and identity. A recurring theme is the distinction between communication as the transmission of information and communication as dialogue — a relational event in which persons encounter one another.

Key figures and traditions

Martin Buber's I and Thou (1923) distinguished the dialogical "I–Thou" relation from the instrumental "I–It" relation, making encounter foundational to human existence. Jürgen Habermas developed a theory of communicative action, in which speech aimed at mutual understanding, rather than strategic success, coordinates social life and grounds the public sphere. Emmanuel Levinas placed the ethical demand of the face-to-face encounter at the origin of philosophy. Hans-Georg Gadamer's hermeneutics treated conversation as a "fusion of horizons" in which understanding emerges. In the pragmatist tradition, John Dewey and George Herbert Mead emphasised communication as the basis of society and of the self. Marshall McLuhan, though more often read in media studies, raised philosophical questions about how media reshape perception — "the medium is the message".

Themes

Contemporary philosophy of communication addresses dialogue and difference, the ethics of listening, communicative ethics and discourse ethics, narrative and identity, embodiment and nonverbal communication, and the philosophical implications of digital and mediated communication.
