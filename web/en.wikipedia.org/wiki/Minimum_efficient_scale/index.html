Minimum efficient scale

The minimum efficient scale (MES) or efficient scale of production is the lowest point where a plant or firm can produce such that its long-run average costs are minimized. It is the point at which economies of scale have been exhausted and constant returns to scale commence, before diseconomies of scale may begin to raise average costs at still higher output levels.

Significance

MES relative to the size of market demand helps determine the structure of an industry. If MES is small compared with total demand, many firms can operate efficiently and the industry will be competitive. If MES is large relative to demand, only a few firms — or in the extreme case of a natural monopoly, one firm — can produce at minimum cost, and the market tends toward oligopoly or monopoly.

Measurement

Economists estimate MES using cost surveys, engineering estimates, and the "survivor technique" developed by George Stigler, which infers efficient scale from which plant sizes survive and grow in a competitive industry over time. MES is usually expressed as a range rather than a precise output, because long-run average cost curves are often L-shaped, with a flat bottom over a wide range of output.
