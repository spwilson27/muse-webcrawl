Gangrene

Gangrene is a type of tissue death (necrosis) caused by a critically insufficient blood supply, often compounded by bacterial infection. It most commonly affects the extremities — toes, feet, fingers, and hands — but can also occur in internal organs. Left untreated, gangrene can spread and become life-threatening through sepsis.

Types

Dry gangrene results from gradual arterial insufficiency, as in peripheral arterial disease; the tissue becomes dry, shrunken, and dark. Wet gangrene involves bacterial infection of necrotic tissue, with swelling, blistering, and rapid spread. Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis), usually caused by Clostridium perfringens, produces gas within tissues and is a surgical emergency. Fournier gangrene affects the perineum and genitalia, and necrotizing fasciitis is a related rapidly spreading soft-tissue infection.

Causes and risk factors

Any condition that compromises circulation can lead to gangrene: atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, vasculitis, severe trauma, frostbite, and embolism. People with diabetes are at particular risk because of combined vascular and nerve damage, especially in the feet.

Treatment

Treatment requires urgent removal of dead tissue (debridement), antibiotics for infection, and restoration of blood flow by angioplasty or bypass surgery where possible. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is an adjunct in some cases. When tissue loss is extensive or infection uncontrolled, amputation of the affected part may be necessary to save the patient's life.
