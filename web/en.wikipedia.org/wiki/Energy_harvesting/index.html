Energy harvesting

Energy harvesting (also known as energy scavenging) is the process by which energy is derived from external sources — such as solar power, thermal energy, wind energy, salinity gradients and kinetic energy — captured, and stored for small, wireless autonomous devices, like those used in wearable electronics and wireless sensor networks. Energy harvesters generally supply very small amounts of power, measured in microwatts to milliwatts.

Sources and technologies

  • Light – photovoltaic cells convert ambient or direct light, indoors or outdoors.
  • Vibration and motion – piezoelectric, electromagnetic and electrostatic transducers convert mechanical movement into electricity.
  • Thermal – thermoelectric generators exploit temperature differences via the Seebeck effect.
  • Radio frequency – antennas and rectennas capture ambient RF energy from broadcast and communications signals.
  • Human body – body heat and biomechanical motion can power implants and wearables.

Applications

Energy harvesting enables battery-free or battery-assisted sensor nodes in buildings, industrial plants, transport infrastructure and environmental monitoring, reducing maintenance where battery replacement is difficult or costly. It is also used in consumer devices such as self-powered switches and some wristwatches.

Challenges

Ambient energy is often intermittent and low in density, so practical harvesters require power-management circuitry, energy storage in supercapacitors or thin-film batteries, and ultra-low-power electronics designed around the available energy budget.
