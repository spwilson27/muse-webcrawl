Daedalus

In Greek mythology, Daedalus (Greek: Δαίδαλος, "cunningly wrought") was a skillful architect and craftsman, seen as a symbol of wisdom, knowledge and power. He is the father of Icarus, the uncle and teacher of Perdix, and possibly also the father of Iapyx, although this is unclear. He was the creator of the Labyrinth on Crete and of the wings with which he and Icarus escaped imprisonment by King Minos.

Myths

Daedalus, an Athenian, was exiled (in some accounts for murdering his nephew and apprentice Perdix out of jealousy of the boy's inventions) and came to Crete, where he entered the service of King Minos. He built the wooden cow by which Pasiphaë mated with the Cretan Bull, fathering the Minotaur, and then constructed the Labyrinth to imprison the monster. Later imprisoned himself by Minos — in a tower, so he could not reveal the Labyrinth's secrets — Daedalus fashioned wings of feathers and wax for himself and his son Icarus.

The flight of Icarus

Daedalus warned Icarus neither to fly too low, lest the sea's moisture clog the wings, nor too high, lest the sun melt the wax. Icarus, ignoring the warning, flew too close to the sun; the wax melted, he fell into the sea near an island later called Icaria, and drowned. Daedalus reached Sicily, where he was sheltered by King Cocalus; when Minos pursued him there, Cocalus's daughters killed Minos.

Legacy

The Greeks credited Daedalus with many inventions and with the first statues that seemed lifelike. "Daedalic" sculpture is the name given to an early Greek sculptural style. Daedalus remains a byword for the ingenious craftsman, and his story with Icarus a cautionary tale about hubris and ingenuity.
