Public–private partnership

A public–private partnership (PPP or P3) is a long-term arrangement between a government and private-sector companies to finance, build and operate infrastructure or deliver public services — such as toll roads, bridges, hospitals, schools, water systems and prisons. Typically a private consortium designs, constructs and maintains an asset over a concession of 25–30 years, paid either by users or by government availability payments, after which the asset reverts to public ownership.

Models

Common structures include design-build-finance-operate (DBFO), build-operate-transfer (BOT) and concessions. Britain's Private Finance Initiative (PFI), launched in 1992, became an influential — and controversial — model adopted and adapted in Canada, Australia and many other countries.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argue PPPs transfer construction and operating risks to the party best able to manage them, mobilise private capital and deliver projects on time. Critics contend that private borrowing costs more than public borrowing, that long inflexible contracts can burden public finances, and that claimed risk transfer is often illusory when failing projects are rescued by the state. Several governments have scaled back or ended PFI-style programmes while retaining other forms of partnership.

Scale

PPPs have been used in more than 130 countries and account for a significant share of infrastructure investment in transport and social infrastructure worldwide.
