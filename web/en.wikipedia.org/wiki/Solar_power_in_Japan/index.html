Solar power in Japan

Solar power in Japan has been expanding since the late 1990s, and Japan is one of the world's largest markets for photovoltaics, with cumulative installed capacity exceeding 100 GW in the mid-2020s. Solar generation supplies roughly a tenth of Japan's electricity, placing it among the leaders in solar share of national consumption despite limited flat land.

History

Japan was an early leader in solar cell manufacturing; companies such as Sharp, Kyocera, and Sanyo dominated global production in the early 2000s, and government subsidy programmes (notably the "Sunshine Project" dating to 1974) promoted rooftop systems. Deployment accelerated dramatically after the 2011 Fukushima nuclear accident and the introduction in July 2012 of a generous feed-in tariff, which triggered a boom in large-scale "mega solar" plants. Subsequent tariff reductions shifted policy toward auctions and self-consumption.

Deployment

Installations range from residential rooftops to utility-scale plants built on reservoirs (floating solar), former golf courses, and reclaimed land. Land scarcity, complex grid-connection rules across Japan's ten regional utilities, and curtailment in regions such as Kyushu are persistent constraints. Notable plants include large installations in Okayama, Hyogo, and Kagoshima prefectures.

Industry and policy

Although Chinese manufacturers overtook Japanese firms in module production, Japanese companies remain significant in inverters, materials, and project development. Government targets under Japan's Strategic Energy Plan call for renewables to form a much larger share of the mix by 2030, with solar the largest single renewable source. Perovskite solar cells — a technology pioneered by Japanese researcher Tsutomu Miyasaka — are a national research priority for lightweight, flexible deployment on buildings.
