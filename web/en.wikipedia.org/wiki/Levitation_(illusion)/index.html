Levitation (illusion)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Levitation, in the context of stage magic, is an illusion in which a magician appears to make a person or object float in mid-air, defying gravity. Levitation illusions have been staples of magic performances since the 19th century and range from intimate parlour tricks to large stage productions.

History

One of the earliest famous levitations was performed by John Henry Pepper and Henry Dircks in the 1860s, and the French magician Jean Eugène Robert-Houdin presented a celebrated "etherial suspension." Harry Kellar and later Howard Thurston popularized large-scale levitations in American theatres; Thurston's levitation of Princess Karnac, in which a reclining woman floated and a hoop was passed around her body, became a classic of stage magic. In the 1990s, David Copperfield's televised "flying" illusion, in which he soared around the stage and through hoops, brought the levitation to a global television audience.

Methods

Stage levitations are achieved by concealed supports. Common approaches include hidden rigid armatures extending from behind drapery or through the magician's body position, platforms of clear glass or acrylic, very fine but strong wires or threads, and black-art techniques in which supports are camouflaged against dark backgrounds. The Balducci levitation, a street-magic effect, uses angled body positioning and a concealed partial foot lift so that spectators, viewing from a controlled angle, perceive both of the performer's feet leaving the ground. Magician's code discourages revealing methods, and explanations traditionally focus on publicly documented or expired-patent mechanisms.

Street levitation

Living-statue style levitation is performed by street artists around the world, often in the guise of a seated mystic or "floating man," in which one hand resting on a staff — actually a steel support connected to a concealed platform and harness — appears to be the performer's only contact with the ground.
