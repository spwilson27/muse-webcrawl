Coat of arms of Lithuania

Vytis, the coat of arms of Lithuania

Armiger: Republic of Lithuania

Adopted: 1991 (origins in the 14th century)

Shield: Gules, an armoured knight on horseback salient Argent, holding a sword Or and a shield Azure bearing a double cross Or

The coat of arms of Lithuania is a mounted and armoured knight holding a sword and shield, known as Vytis (pronounced [ˈviːtɪs]). The coat of arms has been used by the state of Lithuania since the early 15th century in its current broad form, making it one of the oldest national emblems in Europe still in use. The knight charges on a red field; the shield he carries bears the golden Columns of Gediminas-related double cross associated with the Jagiellonian dynasty.

History

The mounted knight first appeared on the seals of the Grand Dukes of Lithuania in the late 14th century, during the reign of Algirdas and Kęstutis, and was firmly established under Vytautas the Great. It served as the arms of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania within the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth, where it was displayed alongside the Polish eagle. The earliest depictions show the knight's shield bearing a patriarchal (double) cross, a symbol connected with Jogaila's baptism and marriage to Jadwiga of Poland.

After the partitions of the Commonwealth in the late 18th century, the symbol survived in the arms of cities and governorates under the Russian Empire. Following Lithuania's declaration of independence in 1918, Vytis was restored as the state arms. During the Soviet occupation (1940–1990) it was suppressed and replaced by a Soviet-style emblem, but it continued to be used by the Lithuanian diaspora and resistance. On 11 March 1990, the day independence was restored, Vytis was reinstated; the present version, designed by heraldist Arvydas Každailis, was officially approved in 1991.

Symbolism

The charging knight represents the defence of the homeland and the nation's readiness to fight for freedom. In folk interpretation, the rider is seen as a defender who pursued the invaders from Lithuanian soil. The name Vytis derives from the Lithuanian verb vyti, "to chase, to pursue".

Usage

The arms appear on Lithuanian euro coins, passports, state seals, and the flags of government institutions. A stylised Vytis forms part of the insignia of the Lithuanian Armed Forces, and the Grand Duke's palace banner in Vilnius displays the historical form.
