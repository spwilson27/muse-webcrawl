Cherokee (ᏣᎳᎩ, Tsalagi) is an Iroquoian language spoken by the Cherokee people. It is the only Southern Iroquoian language and differs significantly from the Northern Iroquoian languages of the Haudenosaunee. Cherokee is polysynthetic, with complex verb morphology in which a single verb can convey information that would require a whole sentence in English. The language is considered endangered, and UNESCO classifies its dialects as definitely to severely endangered.

Dialects

Historically there were several dialects, of which two survive. The Kituwah (Eastern) dialect is spoken at the Qualla Boundary in North Carolina, and the Overhill (Western) dialect is spoken in northeastern Oklahoma. The Lower dialect, once spoken in South Carolina and Georgia, is extinct. The two surviving dialects differ in phonology and vocabulary but remain partly mutually intelligible.

The syllabary

Cherokee is written with a syllabary invented by Sequoyah (c. 1770–1843), a Cherokee silversmith who, though initially illiterate in any language, completed his writing system around 1821 after twelve years of work. Each of the 85 characters represents a syllable. Literacy spread rapidly among the Cherokee; by 1828 the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix was being published at New Echota. The syllabary is included in the Unicode standard and supported on modern operating systems and phones.

Phonology and grammar

Cherokee has six vowels (a, e, i, o, u, and the nasalised v, /ə̃/) and a modest consonant inventory. Unlike most Iroquoian languages it lacks labial consonants other than /m/. Cherokee is tonal, with up to six tones, though tone is not marked in the syllabary. Verbs are the heart of the language: a verb stem combines with pronominal prefixes encoding both subject and object, aspect suffixes, and other morphology to form long, information-dense words.

Revitalisation

The Cherokee Nation and the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians run immersion schools, master-apprentice programmes, and language classes. The Cherokee Nation declared a state of emergency for the language in 2019 and established a language department under a Secretary of Language and Culture. Digital tools, including a Cherokee keyboard and online courses, support learners.
