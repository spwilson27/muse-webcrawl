The Ulm School of Design (German: Hochschule für Gestaltung Ulm, HfG Ulm) was a college of design founded in Ulm, West Germany, in 1953 by Inge Aicher-Scholl, Otl Aicher, and Max Bill, with funding from the Geschwister-Scholl Foundation. Though it operated for only fifteen years, closing in 1968, it is considered one of the most influential design schools of the twentieth century and the principal heir of the Bauhaus.

The HfG Ulm building, designed by Max Bill

History and philosophy

The school was created in memory of Hans and Sophie Scholl, the executed members of the White Rose resistance group, and was conceived as an institution for democratic education in postwar Germany. Its building, designed by Max Bill — the school's first rector and a Bauhaus alumnus — is regarded as a landmark of postwar modernist architecture. Bill modelled the early curriculum on the Bauhaus, but a younger faculty led by Tomás Maldonado pushed the school toward a rigorous, science-based methodology: design as a systematic, interdisciplinary practice informed by mathematics, sociology, semiotics, and operations research, rather than art or intuition.

Work and legacy

The HfG's development groups produced landmark industrial designs, including the Braun SK 4 radio-phonograph (with Hans Gugelot) that set the template for Dieter Rams's Braun designs, Otl Aicher's corporate identity programme for the airline Lufthansa, and the iconic Ulm stool (Ulmer Hocker) by Max Bill. Its approach — often called the "Ulm model" — shaped design education worldwide, especially in Latin America and India, and its staff and students helped define modern graphic and industrial design. The building now houses the HfG Archive, which preserves the school's records and collections.
