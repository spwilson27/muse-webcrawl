Alans

The Alans (Latin: Alani; Greek: Alanoi) were an ancient and medieval Iranian nomadic pastoral people of the North Caucasus and the Pontic Steppe. Speaking an Eastern Iranian language related to the language of the Scythians and Sarmatians, the Alans emerged in the 1st century AD and became a major power on the steppe, famed as heavy cavalry. Groups of Alans migrated westward with the Huns during the Migration Period, reaching Gaul, Hispania and North Africa, while those remaining in the Caucasus became the ancestors of the modern Ossetians.

Origins

The Alans are generally identified with the Yancai of Chinese sources and were closely related to the Sarmatians, from whom they may have split. Their name is thought to derive from the Iranian word aryāna, "of the Aryans". Early Alans occupied the steppe between the Don River and the Caspian Sea, and Roman authors such as Josephus describe their raids through the Caucasus passes into Media and Armenia in the 1st century AD.

The Migration Period

Defeated by the Huns around 375 AD, the Alans divided: some submitted to the Huns and fought alongside them, while others fled west. On the last day of 406, Alans joined Vandals and Suebi in crossing the frozen Rhine into Gaul. In 409 they entered Hispania, where Alan settlers in Lusitania were later crushed by the Visigoths. Other Alans under King Goar settled in Gaul and were granted lands around Orléans by the Roman commander Aëtius; an Alan king, Sangiban, held Orléans during Attila's invasion in 451. Alans who crossed to North Africa with the Vandals gave the Vandal kingdom its full royal style, "King of the Vandals and Alans".

The Alans of the Caucasus

Alans who remained in the North Caucasus formed the medieval kingdom of Alania, a Christian state allied at times with Byzantium and Georgia. Alania flourished between the 9th and 13th centuries before being destroyed by the Mongol invasions and later campaigns of Timur. Survivors retreated into the mountain valleys, where their language and culture survive among the Ossetians of North Ossetia–Alania and South Ossetia.

Language and legacy

The Alan language, known from a handful of phrases and inscriptions such as the Zelenchuk inscription, is the direct ancestor of modern Ossetian. Alan mercenaries served in Byzantine, Mongol and even Yuan Chinese armies, and Alan personal names and cavalry traditions influenced peoples from France to Spain.
