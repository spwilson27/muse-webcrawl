Suture (anatomy)

In anatomy, a suture is a fairly rigid joint between two or more hard elements of an organism, with or without significant overlap of the elements. Sutures are found in the skeletons and exoskeletons of a wide range of animals, in both invertebrates and vertebrates. The best-known examples are the cranial sutures, the fibrous joints between the bones of the skull.

Cranial sutures

The major sutures of the human skull include the coronal suture, between the frontal and parietal bones; the sagittal suture, between the two parietal bones; the lambdoid suture, between the parietal and occipital bones; and the squamosal sutures, between the temporal and parietal bones. In infants, the sutures and the fontanelles between them allow the skull to deform during birth and to expand as the brain grows.

Growth and fusion

Cranial sutures are sites of bone growth throughout childhood. They normally fuse progressively in adulthood, a process called synostosis; the pattern and timing of fusion can be used in forensic anthropology to estimate age at death. Premature fusion in infancy, known as craniosynostosis, restricts skull growth and may require surgical correction.

In other organisms

Sutures also join the shell plates (thecae) of turtles and other armoured animals, the segments of ammonite shells—whose suture patterns are important in classification—and the exoskeletal plates of arthropods such as trilobites.
