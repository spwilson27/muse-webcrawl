Breaking the line

Breaking the line was a celebrated manoeuvre in naval tactics during the Age of Sail, in which a fleet sailing in line ahead pierced the enemy's line of battle, cutting it into segments that could be engaged from both sides and preventing mutual support. The manoeuvre defeated the rigid tactical orthodoxy of the line of battle and is associated above all with Admirals George Rodney at the Battle of the Saintes (1782), Richard Howe on the Glorious First of June (1794), John Jervis at Cape St Vincent (1797), and Horatio Nelson at the Battle of Trafalgar (1805).

Background

From the mid-17th century, the Fighting Instructions of the Royal Navy and other navies required fleets to fight in a single line ahead, exchanging broadsides while preserving formation. The system minimised confusion and friendly fire but often produced indecisive actions, since a beaten enemy could withdraw along its line. Commanders chafed at the restriction, and the Articles of War — under which Admiral John Byng was executed in 1757 after failing to relieve Minorca — made initiative professionally dangerous.

The manoeuvre

To break the line, an attacking fleet approached in one or more columns and steered through gaps in the enemy's line. Ships passing through could fire devastating raking broadsides along the length of enemy vessels (through the vulnerable stern or bow), then engage the severed portion of the enemy line from both sides — "doubling" it — while the detached segment, usually the van, struggled to tack back into the fight against the wind. Success required the weather gage (the upwind position), close action, and captains trained to act on a commander's intent rather than on precise signals.

Notable instances

A form of the manoeuvre occurred at the Battle of Malaga (1704) and was described in John Clerk of Eldin's tactical essays. Rodney's victory at the Saintes in 1782 resulted when shifts of wind opened gaps in the French line — French tradition holds the breach partly accidental. Howe deliberately ordered each of his ships to cut the French line on the Glorious First of June 1794. At Trafalgar in 1805 Nelson attacked in two columns perpendicular to the Franco-Spanish line, accepting exposure to raking fire during the approach in exchange for a close-range melee that destroyed the allied centre and rear, capturing or destroying some 20 ships.

Legacy

Breaking the line exemplified the transition from formal, signal-bound tactics to mission command at sea. The concept survived the age of sail in altered forms: crossing an enemy fleet's "T" with steam warships achieved a similar concentration of fire, and the phrase remains a shorthand for audacious concentration against part of an enemy force.
