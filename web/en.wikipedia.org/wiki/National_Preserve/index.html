A national preserve is a type of protected area in the United States, designated by Congress and usually administered by the National Park Service. National preserves are managed much like national parks, but with a crucial difference: activities that are prohibited in parks — such as hunting, fishing, trapping and, in some cases, oil and gas exploration or off-road vehicle use — may be allowed under regulation.

Origin and purpose

The category was created in 1974 with the establishment of Big Cypress National Preserve in Florida and Big Thicket National Preserve in Texas, as a political compromise: local residents and members of Congress wanted park-grade protection for the landscapes but insisted that traditional hunting, fishing and some resource uses continue. The formula was applied on a grand scale in the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980, which created many of the largest preserves — among them Denali, Gates of the Arctic, Wrangell–St. Elias and Yukon–Charley Rivers — as preserve lands adjoining or surrounding national park cores, permitting sport and subsistence hunting by local residents.

Examples

Well-known national preserves include Big Cypress (Florida), Big Thicket (Texas), Great Sand Dunes (Colorado), Tallgrass Prairie (Kansas), Craters of the Moon (Idaho, jointly managed with the Bureau of Land Management), Mojave (California) and the preserve portions of Alaska's park units. Management rules vary unit by unit according to each preserve's enabling legislation, but all are held to the Park Service's overarching duty to conserve scenery, wildlife and historic objects unimpaired for future generations.
