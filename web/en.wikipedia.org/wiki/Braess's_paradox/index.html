Braess's paradox

Braess's paradox
FieldGame theory, transport networks
Named afterDietrich Braess
Published1968
ClaimAdding capacity to a network can worsen its equilibrium performance

Braess's paradox is the observation that adding one or more roads to a road network can slow overall traffic flow through it, and that removing roads can sometimes improve it. First presented by the German mathematician Dietrich Braess in 1968, the paradox arises because selfish route choices by individual drivers lead to a Nash equilibrium that extra capacity can make worse for everyone.

The example

Consider a network in which 4,000 drivers travel between two points by two routes, each consisting of one congestible segment (travel time = number of cars / 100 minutes) and one fixed 45-minute segment. Equilibrium splits traffic evenly and the journey takes 65 minutes. Adding a very fast shortcut link between the midpoints makes the shortcut route dominant: every driver switches, both congestible segments now carry all 4,000 cars, and the trip takes 80 minutes — worse for all, though no one can gain by deviating alone.

Real-world instances

Road closures that unexpectedly improved traffic have been reported in Stuttgart (after a new road's partial closure in 1969), in New York City (the 1990 closure of 42nd Street) and in Seoul, where removal of the Cheonggyecheon elevated freeway improved both traffic and the urban environment. Analogous effects occur in electrical power grids, computer networks and mechanical systems of springs and strings; in queueing theory the related "price of anarchy" quantifies the loss from selfish routing.
