The judiciary of India is a single integrated system of courts that administers both central and state laws, inherited from the British colonial legal system and structured by the Constitution of India (1950). At its apex is the Supreme Court in New Delhi, followed by High Courts in each state and a hierarchy of subordinate district and sessions courts.

Supreme Court

The Supreme Court consists of the Chief Justice of India and up to 33 other judges appointed by the President, in practice on the recommendation of the collegium of senior judges. It has original, appellate, and advisory jurisdiction, and is the final interpreter of the Constitution. Landmark doctrines include the basic structure doctrine from Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), which limits Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

High and subordinate courts

There are 25 High Courts, some serving multiple states or union territories. Below them, district judges, civil judges, and magistrates handle the bulk of litigation. Specialised tribunals cover tax, company, environmental, and armed-forces matters. India also promotes alternative dispute resolution through Lok Adalats (people's courts).

Issues

The judiciary is noted for public interest litigation and judicial activism, and criticised for enormous backlogs — tens of millions of pending cases — vacancies, and the opacity of the collegium appointments system, which Parliament attempted to replace with the National Judicial Appointments Commission, struck down in 2015.
