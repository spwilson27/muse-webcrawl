New River (London)

The New River is an artificial waterway in England, opened in 1613, that carries drinking water from springs and the River Lea in Hertfordshire to London. It was one of the most significant early civil engineering projects in Britain and still supplies part of the capital's water today.

History

The project was begun by Edmund Colthurst and completed by the goldsmith Sir Hugh Myddelton with financial backing from King James I. The channel followed the contours of the land for some 38 miles (61 km) from Chadwell and Amwell springs near Ware to the New River Head reservoir at Clerkenwell, dropping only a few metres over its whole length—a gentle gradient achieved with loops that were later shortened by aqueducts and cuttings. The New River Company, chartered in 1619, was one of the first joint-stock utility companies and remained in existence until 1904, when it was absorbed into the Metropolitan Water Board.

Today

The northern sections still convey raw water to reservoirs in northeast London, while the southern section through Islington is an ornamental and recreational waterway. The New River Path follows its course through Hertfordshire and London.
