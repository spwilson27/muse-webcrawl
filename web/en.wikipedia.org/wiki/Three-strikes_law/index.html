Three-strikes law

Three-strikes laws are statutes enacted by governments in the United States requiring state courts to impose a life sentence on persons convicted of a felony who have been previously convicted of two or more serious felonies in state or federal courts. The term takes its name from baseball, where a batter is out after three strikes.

History

Washington state adopted the first modern three-strikes law in 1993 by voter initiative, followed in 1994 by California, whose law — approved as Proposition 184 — became the largest in scope and effect. The federal Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 included a federal three-strikes provision, and roughly half of U.S. states adopted some form of habitual-offender law in the 1990s. California's version was unusually broad, counting certain nonviolent felonies as third strikes, and led to cases in which offenders received 25-years-to-life sentences for minor theft offences, a result upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court in Ewing v. California (2003).

Effects and criticism

Studies of the laws' effect on crime rates have produced mixed findings; some attribute part of the 1990s crime decline to them, while others find little marginal deterrent effect and emphasise their contribution to prison population growth and ageing inmate populations, with associated costs. Critics argue the laws are disproportionate and applied unevenly; defenders argue they incapacitate repeat serious offenders. California voters substantially narrowed the law with Proposition 36 in 2012, requiring the third strike to be a serious or violent felony and allowing resentencing for many inmates.
