Cleavage (crystal)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cleavage, in mineralogy and materials science, is the tendency of crystalline materials to split along definite crystallographic structural planes where atomic bonds are weakest. These planes of relative weakness are a diagnostic property used to identify minerals.

Types

  • Basal (pinacoidal): one direction — e.g., mica splits into sheets.
  • Prismatic: two directions at ~90° (orthoclase) or ~56°/124° (amphibole), a key distinction from pyroxene.
  • Cubic: three directions at right angles — halite, galena.
  • Rhombohedral: three non-perpendicular directions — calcite.
  • Octahedral: four directions — fluorite, diamond.
  • Dodecahedral: six directions — sphalerite.

Quality

Cleavage is described as perfect, good, distinct, or imperfect. It is distinct from parting (breaking along twin planes or defects) and from fracture (conchoidal, uneven, or hackly breaks in minerals like quartz that lack cleavage).

Use and cutting

Gem cutters exploit cleavage to split stones (e.g., cleaving rough diamonds), while engineers must account for cleavage planes in crystal processing and geology.
