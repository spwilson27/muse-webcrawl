Ada Salter (née Brown; 20 July 1866 – 4 December 1942) was an English socialist, pacifist, environmentalist and social reformer. A pioneer of ethical socialism and of urban beautification, she became in 1922 the first female mayor of a London borough (Bermondsey) and one of the first women mayors in Britain.

Early life and activism

Born in Raunds, Northamptonshire, she moved to London and worked at the Bermondsey Settlement, a Methodist social mission in the slums of south London. There she met the doctor Alfred Salter, whom she married in 1900. Both were Quakers and committed socialists; they joined the Independent Labour Party. Ada was active in the Women's Labour League and in campaigns against sweated labour, and campaigned for the Liberal Party and then Labour in municipal politics, winning election to Bermondsey Borough Council in 1919.

Mayor of Bermondsey and the beautification campaign

As mayor (1922–23) and as a long-serving councillor and later alderman, Salter championed public health, slum clearance, municipal housing and baths. She is best remembered for her "Bermondsey Beautification Committee", which planted hundreds of trees and thousands of flowers in one of London's poorest and most industrial boroughs, filled window boxes and open spaces, and promoted the idea that beauty in the environment was a right of the poor, not a luxury — work that made her nationally known as a pioneer of urban greening. She also served on the London County Council from 1925.

Later life and legacy

The Salters' only daughter, Joyce, died of scarlet fever in 1910, a loss that deepened their commitment to public health. Ada died in 1942 during the Second World War, refusing to leave London during the Blitz. She is commemorated by a statue on the Thames Path at Bermondsey, part of a group depicting the Salters, and by the Ada Salter Garden in Southwark Park.
