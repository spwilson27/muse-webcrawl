The 6 February 1934 riots were an anti-parliamentarian street demonstration in Paris organized by multiple far-right leagues that culminated in a riot on the Place de la Concorde, near the seat of the French National Assembly. The police shot and killed 17 people. The crisis brought down the government of Édouard Daladier and remains a pivotal episode in the history of the French Third Republic.

Background

France in the early 1930s suffered political instability aggravated by the Great Depression and the Stavisky affair, a financial scandal implicating figures close to the ruling Radical Party. On 7 January 1934 the swindler Serge Alexandre Stavisky was found dead, and right-wing newspapers denounced an alleged cover-up. The dismissal of Paris police prefect Jean Chiappe, popular with the far right, by the new Daladier government triggered the demonstrations of 6 February, when the Chamber of Deputies was to vote confidence in the cabinet.

The riot

Tens of thousands of demonstrators — members of leagues such as Action Française, the Croix-de-Feu, and the Jeunesses Patriotes, along with some communist veterans — converged on the Place de la Concorde and attempted to cross the bridge toward the Palais Bourbon. Police and Mobile Guards, fearing being overwhelmed, opened fire. Fighting continued into the night, leaving at least 16 rioters and one police officer dead and over two thousand injured.

Aftermath

Daladier resigned the next day — the first Third Republic government to fall under street pressure — and was replaced by a national union government under former president Gaston Doumergue. The events polarized France: the left, convinced that a fascist coup had been narrowly averted, moved toward unity, culminating in the Popular Front of 1936, while the leagues grew in strength. Historians continue to debate whether the riot was a genuine attempt to seize power or a protest that spiralled out of control.
