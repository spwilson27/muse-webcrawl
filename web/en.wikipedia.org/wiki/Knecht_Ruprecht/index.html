Knecht Ruprecht (German pronunciation: [ˈknɛçt ˈʁuːpʁɛçt]; "Farmhand Rupert" or "Servant Rupert") is a companion of Saint Nicholas in the folklore of Germany. He is traditionally depicted as a rough, dark-clad figure who accompanies Saint Nicholas on the eve of his feast day, 5 or 6 December, visiting children to reward the well-behaved and punish the naughty.

Description

Ruprecht is commonly described as wearing dark robes or furs, with a soot-blackened face, and carrying a switch or birch rod and a sack. In many traditions he wears bells and carries a staff, and his sack is said to hold ashes, switches, or — in darker tellings — the means to carry away disobedient children. Unlike the more demonic Krampus of Alpine folklore, Ruprecht is generally portrayed as a servant figure, subordinate to Saint Nicholas, who questions children about their prayers or lessons.

Traditions

In the traditional visitation, Saint Nicholas asks children whether they have been good during the year, while Ruprecht stands by as a threatening presence. Children who could recite their prayers were rewarded with apples, nuts, and gingerbread; those who could not risked a swat from Ruprecht's switch or, in milder modern versions, a lump of coal. The figure is first documented in written sources from the 17th century, including accounts of Nuremberg's Christkindlesmarkt processions.

Theodor Storm's well-known poem "Knecht Ruprecht" (1862), with its opening lines "Von drauß' vom Walde komm ich her" ("From out of the forest I come"), helped fix the character in the German literary imagination. Martin Luther is sometimes credited with popularising the figure as part of efforts to shift Christmas gift-giving away from Saint Nicholas, though Ruprecht remained tied to the December visitations.

Regional variants

Similar companions appear throughout northern and central Europe: Hans Trapp in Alsace, the Belsnickel among Pennsylvania Dutch communities, Père Fouettard in France, and Zwarte Piet in the Low Countries. In some regions Ruprecht has merged with or been replaced by other figures, and in the modern era his role has softened considerably, with the punitive aspects played down in family celebrations.
