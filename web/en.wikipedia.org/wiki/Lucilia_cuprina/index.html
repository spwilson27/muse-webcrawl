Lucilia cuprina, known as the Australian sheep blowfly, is a member of the family Calliphoridae (blowflies). Females lay their eggs in carrion and in the soiled wool of live sheep, where the larvae feed and develop. It is a major agricultural pest in Australia and New Zealand.

Biology

Adults are metallic coppery-green flies. Females are attracted to sheep by odours of bacterial decay in damp fleece and deposit egg batches near skin lesions or soiled wool. The first-instar larvae move to the skin and feed on exudates and tissue, causing myiasis — commonly called flystrike — which can kill untreated sheep within days.

Economic significance

Flystrike costs the Australian wool industry hundreds of millions of dollars annually in lost production, treatment, and sheep deaths. Control measures include insecticides, crutching and shearing schedules, tail docking and mulesing (controversial), and breeding sheep with less wrinkle and fleece rot susceptibility. The species has been a prominent target of insecticide-resistance research and of genetic control programs such as the sterile insect technique and experimental female-killing transgenic strains.

Like other blowflies, L. cuprina also appears in forensic entomology, where its development rates help estimate post-mortem intervals.
