The presidency of Franklin D. Roosevelt began on 4 March 1933, when he was inaugurated as the 32nd president of the United States, and ended with his death on 12 April 1945. Roosevelt, a Democrat from New York, took office in the depths of the Great Depression after defeating President Herbert Hoover in the 1932 election. He was the only president to serve more than two terms, winning election in 1932, 1936, 1940, and 1944 and leading the country through the New Deal and most of World War II.

The New Deal

In his celebrated "First Hundred Days", Roosevelt launched the New Deal, a flurry of programs for relief, recovery, and reform: a bank holiday and the Emergency Banking Act stabilized the financial system; the Civilian Conservation Corps and Public Works Administration put the unemployed to work; the Agricultural Adjustment Act raised farm prices; and the Tennessee Valley Authority brought power and development to the Tennessee Valley. A "Second New Deal" (1935) added the Works Progress Administration, the National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act protecting union organizing, and the Social Security Act, which created old-age pensions and unemployment insurance — the foundation of the American welfare state. The Supreme Court struck down several early programs; Roosevelt's 1937 proposal to expand ("pack") the Court failed politically, though subsequent rulings upheld the new regulatory order.

Foreign policy and World War II

Roosevelt's foreign policy began with the Good Neighbor Policy toward Latin America and recognition of the Soviet Union. As war spread in Europe and Asia, he moved the initially isolationist United States toward aiding the Allies — through the Lend-Lease Act of 1941 and an undeclared naval war in the Atlantic — while promising to keep the country out of the war. After the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, the United States entered the war. Roosevelt directed the grand strategy of the Allied coalition with Winston Churchill and Joseph Stalin, meeting them at Tehran and Yalta, prioritized the defeat of Germany, and presided over a massive industrial and military mobilization that transformed the American economy. His administration also interned some 120,000 Japanese Americans, a policy widely condemned in retrospect and formally apologized for in 1988.

Death and legacy

Re-elected in 1944 with Harry S. Truman as vice president, Roosevelt's health declined sharply; he died of a cerebral haemorrhage at Warm Springs, Georgia, on 12 April 1945, weeks before the German surrender, and Truman succeeded him. The Twenty-second Amendment (1951) subsequently limited presidents to two terms. Historians consistently rank Roosevelt among the greatest American presidents, crediting him with reshaping the federal government's role, creating the modern presidency, and leading the nation through its two gravest 20th-century crises, while debates continue over the economic effects of the New Deal, the court-packing plan, and the internment of Japanese Americans.
