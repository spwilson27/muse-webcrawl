Zooid

A zooid is a single animal that forms part of a colonial organism. Colonial animals consist of numerous zooids produced by asexual budding from a single founding individual, and the zooids remain physically connected and often share tissues. In many groups the zooids become specialised for different functions — such as feeding, defence, or reproduction — so that the colony functions as an integrated whole resembling a single organism.

Types and specialisation

In bryozoans, the typical feeding zooid (autozooid) has a retractable crown of tentacles called a lophophore, while specialised heterozooids include avicularia (defensive, jaw-like structures) and vibracula. In hydrozoans such as the Portuguese man o' war — a siphonophore — the colony comprises zooids specialised as a float (pneumatophore), feeding polyps (gastrozooids), defensive tentacles armed with stinging cells (dactylozooids), and reproductive structures (gonozooids). Colonial tunicates and corals likewise consist of interconnected zooids or polyps.

Development

A colony typically begins when a sexually produced larva settles and metamorphoses into a founding zooid (such as the bryozoan ancestrula). Further zooids are then budded asexually, so all zooids in a colony are usually genetically identical clones, though colonies can sometimes fuse or compete. The degree of physiological integration varies from loose aggregations to colonies with shared nervous and circulatory systems.

Significance

Zooid specialisation raises questions about biological individuality: whether selection acts on the zooid, the colony, or both. Colonial organisation has evolved independently in many lineages and allows division of labour without the developmental complexity of a single large body plan.
