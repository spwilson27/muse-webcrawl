Roger Newland Shepard (January 30, 1929 – May 30, 2022) was an American cognitive scientist and psychologist, often called the father of research on spatial relations. His inventions and discoveries include Shepard tones (an auditory illusion of endlessly rising pitch), the experimental study of mental rotation, nonmetric multidimensional scaling, and the universal law of generalization. He was Ray Lyman Wilbur Professor Emeritus at Stanford University.

Life and career

Shepard was born in Palo Alto and earned his bachelor's degree at Stanford in 1951 and his PhD at Yale in 1955 under Carl Hovland. He worked at Bell Telephone Laboratories (1958–1966), taught at Harvard, and joined Stanford's faculty in 1968, where he remained until retirement.

Contributions

In 1964 Shepard described tones composed of octave-spaced harmonics whose pitch seems to ascend forever — the Shepard scale or Shepard tones, an auditory analogue of the Penrose stairs (he also produced famous optical illusions such as the "Turning the Tables" tabletops). His 1971 experiment with Jacqueline Metzler, measuring reaction time as a linear function of the angular difference between two 3D block figures, founded the study of mental rotation and mental imagery. His 1957–1962 work on generalization and his 1987 Science paper proposed that generalization falls off exponentially with distance in a psychological space — the "universal law of generalization." He also developed nonmetric multidimensional scaling (with Joseph Kruskal's related work), enabling recovery of spatial structure from similarity judgments.

Honors

Shepard was elected to the National Academy of Sciences (1977) and received the National Medal of Science in 1995 and the Rumelhart Prize in 2006. He died in Tucson, Arizona, in 2022, aged 93.
