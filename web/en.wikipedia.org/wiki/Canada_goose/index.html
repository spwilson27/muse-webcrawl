The Canada goose (Branta canadensis) is a large wild goose native to North America, easily recognised by its black head and neck, white cheeks and "chinstrap" patch, and brown body. It breeds in Canada and the northern United States and migrates in characteristic V-shaped flocks, announcing itself with loud honking calls. Introduced populations thrive in Europe, New Zealand, and elsewhere.

Description

Canada geese range widely in size among their seven recognised subspecies, from the 3 kg "lesser" forms to the giant Canada goose (B. c. maxima), which can exceed 6.5 kg. Males and females look alike, males being somewhat larger. The smaller cackling goose, formerly lumped with this species, was recognised as separate (Branta hutchinsii) in 2004.

Behaviour and ecology

Canada geese are herbivores, grazing on grasses, sedges, and aquatic plants and raiding grain fields. They mate for life and nest on the ground near water, the female incubating 2–9 eggs while the male stands guard; goslings can fly at about 6–9 weeks. Families migrate together, and young birds learn traditional routes and staging areas from their parents. In many temperate areas — including parks, golf courses, and suburban lawns — populations have become year-round residents, aided by abundant food, few predators, and warm winters.

Relationship with humans

Hunted heavily in the early 20th century, the species recovered spectacularly under protection and management; the giant subspecies, once feared extinct, was rediscovered in 1962. Today Canada geese are abundant — in places a nuisance, fouling water and grass, behaving aggressively in the nesting season, and posing a birdstrike hazard: a strike by Canada geese caused the 2009 ditching of US Airways Flight 1549 in the Hudson River. The species is an unofficial symbol of Canada, though — counter to popular belief — it does not appear on the Canadian one-dollar coin (which bears a loon).
