Martyrs of Japan

The Martyrs of Japan were Christians who were persecuted and executed for their faith in Japan, chiefly between 1597 and the 1630s, during the suppression of Christianity under the Tokugawa shogunate. The Roman Catholic Church has canonized or beatified hundreds of them in several groups.

The monument to the Twenty-six Martyrs at Nishizaka Hill, Nagasaki.

Background

Christianity arrived in Japan in 1549 with Francis Xavier and spread rapidly, winning perhaps 300,000 converts by 1600, including several daimyo. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, suspicious of foreign influence, issued an edict against the missionaries in 1587, and in 1597 had twenty-six Christians — Franciscans, Jesuits, and Japanese laypeople, including three boys — crucified at Nagasaki. They were canonized in 1862.

Tokugawa persecution

The Tokugawa shogunate banned Christianity outright in 1614, expelled missionaries, and required citizens to register at Buddhist temples and to trample on Christian images (fumi-e) to prove they were not believers. Executions by crucifixion, burning, and the torture pit (ana-tsurushi) killed thousands of Japanese Christians and foreign missionaries. Notable groups include the 205 Martyrs beatified in 1867, the Martyrs of Unzen, and the sixteen martyrs of Nagasaki canonized in 1987, among them Lorenzo Ruiz of Manila.

Legacy

Driven underground, Japan's "hidden Christians" (Kakure Kirishitan) preserved their faith in secret for over two centuries until religious toleration in the Meiji era. The sites associated with the martyrs and the hidden Christians of the Nagasaki region were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2018.
