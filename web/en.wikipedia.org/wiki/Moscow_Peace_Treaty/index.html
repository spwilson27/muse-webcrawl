Moscow Peace Treaty

Moscow Peace Treaty
SignedMarch 12, 1940
LocationMoscow, Soviet Union
EffectiveMarch 13, 1940
SignatoriesFinland; Soviet Union
ContextEnd of the Winter War

The Moscow Peace Treaty was signed by Finland and the Soviet Union on March 12, 1940, and the ratifications were exchanged on March 21. It marked the end of the 105-day Winter War, in which the Soviet Union had invaded Finland on November 30, 1939. The treaty ceded large areas of Finnish territory to the Soviet Union — more land than the USSR had demanded before the war — while preserving Finland's independence, unlike the fate of the Baltic states.

Terms

Finland ceded approximately 11 percent of its territory, including:

  • The entire Karelian Isthmus, including Viipuri (Vyborg), Finland's second-largest city;
  • Parts of Finnish Karelia north of Lake Ladoga, the town of Sortavala and the Salla area;
  • The Kalastajasaarento (Rybachy) peninsula and islands in the Gulf of Finland;
  • A 30-year lease of the Hanko Peninsula to the Soviet Union for a naval base.

More than 400,000 Finns — about 12 percent of the population — were evacuated from the ceded areas and resettled in the remainder of Finland under a government compensation programme. Finland was also required to build a railway across its territory linking the Murmansk line with Sweden's border, though this was never completed.

Aftermath

The harsh terms embittered Finnish opinion and shaped the "interim peace": Finland turned toward Germany for support and, in June 1941, joined the Continuation War in an attempt to recover the lost territories. The eventual settlement of 1944–47 confirmed and extended the cessions of 1940 in the Moscow Armistice and the Paris Peace Treaty, and Finland paid reparations to the Soviet Union.
