Betws-y-Coed (pronounced roughly "bet-oos-uh-COYD"; literally "prayer house in the wood") is a village and community in Conwy County Borough, Wales, lying in the Snowdonia (Eryri) National Park where the rivers Conwy, Lledr and Llugwy meet. Despite a small resident population of only a few hundred, it is one of the most popular inland tourist villages in Wales, a honeypot for walkers and visitors to Snowdonia.

History

A religious cell founded by St Michael in the late sixth century gave the settlement its name and its church, St Michael's, parts of which date to the fourteenth century. The village remained a small farming community until the nineteenth century, when Thomas Telford's London–Holyhead road (the A5) crossed the Conwy here on the cast-iron Waterloo Bridge of 1815. Artists of the Betws-y-Coed artists' colony, led by David Cox from the 1840s, made the scenery famous, and the arrival of the railway in 1868 cemented its role as a resort. The station now houses the Conwy Valley Railway Museum.

Attractions

Nearby sights include the Swallow Falls (Rhaeadr Ewynnol) on the Llugwy, the Miners' Bridge, the Fairy Glen gorge, the Pont-y-Pair bridge in the village, and the extensive Gwydir Forest surrounding it. The village is a base for climbing, mountain biking and walking, with outdoor-equipment shops clustered on the main street, and for reaching the Ogwen valley and the higher Snowdon group.

Geography

Betws-y-Coed sits at about 30 metres above sea level amid steep, wooded hills. Its sheltered site and high rainfall support lush vegetation; it is sometimes called the "gateway to Snowdonia".
