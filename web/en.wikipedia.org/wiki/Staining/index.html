Staining

Staining is a technique used in microscopy and histology to enhance contrast in samples, usually at the microscopic level. Stains and dyes are used to highlight structures in biological tissues, cells, and microorganisms, and to define and examine bulk tissues or cell populations. Because most cells and many tissue components are nearly colourless and transparent, staining is often essential to making them visible and distinguishable under the microscope.

Principles

Stains work because different chemical components of a sample have different affinities for particular dyes. Acidic dyes (such as eosin) bind to basic structures, while basic dyes (such as haematoxylin in its oxidised, mordanted form) bind to acidic structures such as nucleic acids. Some staining is performed on living material (in vivo or vital staining, and intravital staining of living organisms); most histological staining is performed on fixed, sectioned tissue (in vitro).

Common techniques

  • Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) — the standard stain of histopathology: nuclei blue-purple, cytoplasm and connective tissue pink.
  • Gram stain — the fundamental bacteriological stain (1884) dividing bacteria into Gram-positive (violet) and Gram-negative (pink) groups, guiding antibiotic choice.
  • Giemsa stain — used for blood films, bone marrow and the detection of parasites such as Plasmodium.
  • Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) — stains carbohydrate-rich structures magenta.
  • Masson's trichrome — distinguishes collagen and muscle in connective tissue studies.
  • Silver stains — used for reticular fibres, fungi and certain neurons (Golgi's method).
  • Negative staining — in electron microscopy, heavy-metal salts darken the background around viruses and macromolecules, leaving them light.

Applications

Staining underpins diagnostic histopathology, cytology (for example the Pap stain of the cervical smear test), microbiology, and much research in cell biology. Related techniques extend the idea beyond visible dyes: immunohistochemistry uses antibody-linked enzymes or fluorophores, and fluorescent stains such as DAPI label specific molecules for fluorescence microscopy.
