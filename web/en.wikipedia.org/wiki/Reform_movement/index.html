Reform movement

A reform movement is a type of social movement that aims to bring about a system of gradual change, or change in certain aspects of society, rather than a rapid or fundamental change. A reform movement can be distinguished from a revolutionary movement in that it works within the existing system, seeking to modify institutions and laws rather than overthrow them.

Types of reform movements

Historians and sociologists have classified reform movements by their goals: moral reforms (temperance, abolition of capital punishment), political reforms (suffrage, anti-corruption, electoral reform), social reforms (labour rights, prison and asylum reform, public education), and economic reforms (land reform, monetary reform, regulation of industry). The theory of "reformist" versus "revolutionary" change was debated by socialist thinkers such as Eduard Bernstein and Rosa Luxemburg in Reform or Revolution (1899).

Historical examples

  • The British Reform Acts (1832, 1867, 1884) and the Chartist movement, which gradually widened the franchise.
  • The abolitionist movement against slavery in the 18th and 19th centuries.
  • The temperance movement and women's suffrage movements in Europe and North America.
  • The Progressive Era reforms in the United States (c. 1890s–1920s): antitrust, food safety, direct election of senators, and municipal reform.
  • The Tanzimat reforms in the Ottoman Empire and the Self-Strengthening and Hundred Days' Reform movements in Qing China.

Methods

Reform movements typically use petitioning, pamphleteering and journalism, lobbying, litigation, electoral campaigning and peaceful protest to pressure governments and public opinion toward incremental change.
