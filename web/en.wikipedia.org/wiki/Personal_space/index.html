Personal space

Personal space is the region surrounding a person which they regard as psychologically theirs. Most people value their personal space and feel discomfort, anger, or anxiety when it is encroached upon. The study of interpersonal distances is called proxemics, a field established by anthropologist Edward T. Hall in the 1960s.

Distance zones

Hall described four concentric zones around a person in Western cultures: intimate distance (0–45 cm), reserved for partners, close family, and children; personal distance (45 cm–1.2 m) for friends and good acquaintances; social distance (1.2–3.6 m) for formal interactions and colleagues; and public distance (beyond 3.6 m) for speeches and lectures. These distances vary by culture, relationship, gender, age, and situation: they tend to be smaller in Latin America, the Middle East, and southern Europe, and larger in East Asia and northern Europe.

Psychology

Violations of expected distance trigger measurable arousal and avoidance behaviour. Research links personal space regulation to the amygdala; one well-known case involved a patient with amygdala damage who felt no discomfort at very close distances. Crowding — a subjective sense of spatial restriction, as on packed trains — differs from mere density and can produce stress. Digital analogues of personal space arise in online communication and virtual environments.

Applications

Proxemic principles inform office and classroom design, retail layouts, architecture, robot and avatar design, and cross-cultural training.
