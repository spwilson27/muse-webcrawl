Second Intermediate Period of Egypt

Periodc. 1700 – c. 1550 BC
Dynasties13th–17th (partially overlapping)
Key powersHyksos (Avaris); Theban kingdom; Kingdom of Kush
Ends withReunification under Ahmose I; start of the New Kingdom

The Second Intermediate Period (c. 1700–1550 BC) is the era of ancient Egyptian history between the Middle Kingdom and the New Kingdom, when central authority collapsed and Egypt was divided among competing powers. It is conventionally taken to span the Thirteenth through Seventeenth Dynasties, which overlap in time: a weakening native line at Itjtawy and then Thebes, the foreign Hyksos Fifteenth Dynasty ruling the north from Avaris, and the Nubian kingdom of Kush pressing from the south.

Political fragmentation

The Thirteenth Dynasty (c. 1803–1649 BC) began in orderly succession from the Twelfth but deteriorated into rapid turnover of short-reigned kings and the loss of control over the eastern Delta, where Canaanite settlers at Tell el-Dab'a (Avaris) formed the Fourteenth Dynasty. Around 1650 BC the Hyksos — western Semitic rulers whose name derives from the Egyptian heqa-khasut, "rulers of foreign lands" — took Memphis and established the Fifteenth Dynasty, ruling Lower and Middle Egypt with the aid of superior military technology, including the horse-drawn chariot, the composite bow, and new bronze weaponry. A native Seventeenth Dynasty held Thebes in the south, caught between the Hyksos and Kush, with which the Hyksos corresponded about encircling the Thebans.

The war of liberation

The Theban king Seqenenre Tao died violently, apparently in battle against the Hyksos; his son Kamose campaigned north and left stelae boasting of his advance toward Avaris. His brother Ahmose I completed the struggle, capturing Avaris and Sharuhen in Canaan, expelling the Hyksos, and reunifying Egypt around 1550 BC. Ahmose founded the Eighteenth Dynasty, opening the New Kingdom, in which Egypt — having learned the danger of a divided state — built a professional army and an empire in the Levant and Nubia.

Sources and chronology

The period is poorly documented compared with the kingdoms before and after it. Sources include the Turin King List, scarab seals naming otherwise obscure kings, the Kamose stelae, and later, hostile New Kingdom tradition, which remembered the Hyksos as hated foreign occupiers — a picture modern archaeology at Tell el-Dab'a has substantially moderated. The absolute chronology remains debated, with dates varying by several decades between reconstructions.
