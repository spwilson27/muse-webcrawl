Modernity

Modernity refers to the set of social, cultural, economic, and political conditions and ideas associated with the modern era, generally understood to have emerged in Europe from the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance onward and to be characterized by rationalization, industrialization, urbanization, secularization, and the nation-state.

Characteristics

Writers on modernity typically point to a shift toward reason and science as sources of authority, the growth of capitalism and industrial production, the rise of individualism, democracy and bureaucratic administration, and a distinctive sense of time oriented toward progress and the new. Sociologists such as Max Weber described modernity in terms of rationalization and "disenchantment," while Karl Marx analyzed it through the dynamics of capital.

Periodization

There is no single agreed start date: proposed markers include the printing press, the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment, the French Revolution, and the Industrial Revolution. The term is distinguished from modernism, the cultural and artistic movements that responded to these conditions in the late 19th and 20th centuries.

Critiques

Modernity has been criticized from many directions: postmodern thinkers questioned its universal narratives of progress; postcolonial scholars emphasized its entanglement with colonialism; and theorists of "multiple modernities" argue that different societies have produced distinct forms of modern life.
