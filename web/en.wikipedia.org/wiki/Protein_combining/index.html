Protein combining

Rice and beans, a classic complementary combination.

Protein combining (or protein complementation) is a dietary theory for protein nutrition that has been largely superseded. It held that vegetarians and vegans must combine plant foods in each meal so that their mixtures of amino acids would supply all essential amino acids — for example, grains (low in lysine) with legumes (low in methionine).

History

The idea was popularised by Frances Moore Lappé in her 1971 book Diet for a Small Planet, which advised careful meal-by-meal complementation. In the book's 1981 tenth-anniversary edition, Lappé retracted the strict requirement, explaining that it had been intended mainly to dispel the myth that meat is the only source of high-quality protein.

Current scientific view

Position statements of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics hold that protein from a variety of plant foods eaten over the course of a day supplies enough of all essential amino acids, and that complementary proteins do not need to be consumed at the same meal, because the body maintains a pool of free amino acids drawn on as needed. Almost all plant foods contain all essential amino acids; they differ in proportions rather than entirely lacking any one.

Traditional combinations

Many traditional cuisines nonetheless pair grains and legumes — rice and beans in Latin America, rice and dal in India, corn and beans in Mesoamerica (the "Three Sisters" with squash), and bread with hummus in the Middle East. Such dishes remain nutritionally sound and economical even though deliberate combining is no longer considered necessary.
