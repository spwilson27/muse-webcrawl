The Northrop YF-17 (nicknamed "Cobra") was a prototype lightweight fighter aircraft designed by Northrop for the United States Air Force's Lightweight Fighter (LWF) program. It was a twin-engine, single-seat design that competed against the General Dynamics YF-16.

Development

The YF-17 grew out of Northrop's internal P-530 "Cobra" project, begun in 1965, which itself drew on the successful F-5 light fighter family. Northrop emphasized maneuverability at transonic speeds and high angles of attack, employing prominent leading-edge root extensions (LERX) that generated vortex lift and gave the aircraft its "Cobra" nickname and appearance.

Competition and outcome

Two YF-17 prototypes flew in 1974 and competed in the LWF fly-off. In January 1975 the Air Force selected the YF-16, which became the F-16 Fighting Falcon. However, the U.S. Navy, seeking a replacement for its A-7 Corsair II and F-4 Phantom II, preferred a twin-engine design and chose the YF-17 as the basis for its new fighter-attack aircraft. Northrop teamed with McDonnell Douglas to navalize the design, enlarging it and adding carrier equipment; the result was the F/A-18 Hornet, which first flew in 1978 and became one of the most widely used carrier fighters in the world.

Design features

  • Two General Electric YJ101 (later F404) turbofan engines
  • Large leading-edge root extensions enabling controlled flight at angles of attack above 50 degrees
  • Conventional mechanical flight controls (unlike the YF-16's fly-by-wire)
  • Armament: 20 mm cannon and two AIM-9 Sidewinders on wingtip rails in prototype form

One surviving YF-17 is displayed at the Western Museum of Flight in California, and the other at the Battleship Memorial Park in Mobile, Alabama.
