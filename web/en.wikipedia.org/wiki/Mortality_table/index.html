Mortality table

In actuarial science and demography, a life table (also called a mortality table or actuarial table) is a table which shows, for each age, what the probability is that a person of that age will die before their next birthday (the probability of death). In other words, it represents the survivorship of a cohort of people from a certain population.

Structure

A typical life table starts from a radix — often 100,000 hypothetical births — and tabulates for each age x:

  • lx — the number of survivors to age x;
  • dx — deaths between age x and x+1;
  • qx — the probability of dying between age x and x+1;
  • Lx and Tx — total person-years lived; and
  • ex — remaining life expectancy at age x.

Types and uses

Period life tables use the mortality rates of a single time period, while cohort (generation) tables follow an actual birth cohort. Life tables underpin life insurance pricing, pension valuation, epidemiology and public policy. The first modern table was constructed by John Graunt (1662) from the London Bills of Mortality; Edmond Halley (1693), using Breslau data, and Abraham de Moivre developed the mathematics further. Widely used standard tables include those of the World Health Organization, national statistical offices, and the US Social Security Administration.
