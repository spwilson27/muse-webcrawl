Oil field

An oil field is a region with an abundance of petroleum (oil) extracted from below the Earth's surface. Because oil reservoirs typically extend over large areas — potentially several hundred square kilometres — a field is usually developed with multiple wells distributed across it and connected by gathering pipelines to processing and storage facilities. Oil fields may be located on land or offshore, and are often associated with natural gas, which may occur in solution in the oil or as a separate gas cap.

Formation and structure

Petroleum forms from buried organic-rich source rock heated over geological time. The oil migrates upward through porous rock until trapped by an impermeable cap rock in a geological structure such as an anticline or fault trap. The reservoir rock — commonly sandstone or fractured limestone — stores the hydrocarbons in its pore spaces. Exploration combines geological mapping, seismic surveys, and exploratory drilling to locate and appraise accumulations before development.

Development and production

Developing a field involves drilling production and injection wells, installing surface facilities for separating oil, gas, and water, and planning the recovery strategy. Primary recovery relies on natural reservoir pressure; secondary recovery injects water or gas to maintain pressure; tertiary or enhanced oil recovery methods (steam, chemicals, CO2 injection) extract additional oil. The largest conventional field in the world by reserves is Ghawar in Saudi Arabia; other giant fields include Burgan (Kuwait), Kashagan (Kazakhstan), and the offshore fields of Brazil's pre-salt province.

Lifecycle and economics

Production typically rises to a plateau and then declines as the reservoir depletes, giving rise to analysis of "peak oil" at field and global scales. Late-life fields may be operated for decades at low rates, and decommissioning — plugging wells and removing infrastructure — is a major final cost, particularly offshore.
