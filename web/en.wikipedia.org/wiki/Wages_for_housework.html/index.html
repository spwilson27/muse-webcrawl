Wages for housework

Wages for housework was an international feminist campaign that began in Padua, Italy, in 1972, demanding that the state pay a wage for unpaid domestic and care work performed overwhelmingly by women. The campaign grew out of the Italian workerist (operaismo) tradition and the writings of Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James, whose 1972 book The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community argued that housework reproduces labor power and therefore underpins the capitalist wage system while remaining unwaged.

Ideas and organization

The International Feminist Collective, founded in 1972, coordinated groups in Italy, Britain, the United States, and elsewhere. Thinkers associated with the movement include Silvia Federici, whose 1975 pamphlet Wages Against Housework framed the demand not as a goal in itself but as a political perspective: demanding wages would expose housework as work, undermine its naturalization as an act of love, and give women leverage to refuse it. The demand was thus understood as "wages against housework" rather than a reform to institutionalize domestic labor.

Legacy

Although no state implemented the demand, the campaign profoundly influenced Marxist feminism and later debates about social reproduction theory, care work, and proposals such as basic income. It reframed housework as economically productive labor and remains a reference point in feminist economics.
