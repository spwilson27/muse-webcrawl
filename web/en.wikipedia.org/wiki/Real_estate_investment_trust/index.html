Real estate investment trust

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A real estate investment trust (REIT) is a company that owns, operates or finances income-producing real estate. Modelled on mutual funds, REITs allow individual investors to earn dividends from large-scale property — offices, apartment buildings, warehouses, hospitals, shopping centres, hotels and timberlands — without having to buy or manage the properties themselves. In most jurisdictions a REIT pays little or no corporate tax provided it distributes the great majority of its taxable income to shareholders.

History

REITs were created in the United States by the Real Estate Investment Trust Act of 1960, signed by President Dwight D. Eisenhower, to give small investors access to diversified property portfolios previously open mainly to institutions. The sector grew slowly until the late 1960s mortgage-REIT boom and bust, then expanded rapidly from the early 1990s with the modern "UPREIT" structure and landmark initial public offerings such as Taubman Centers (1992) and Simon Property Group (1993). The model has since spread to more than 40 countries, including the United Kingdom (2007), India (2014) and many European SIIC/G-REIT regimes.

Types and rules

Equity REITs own and manage property and collect rent; mortgage REITs lend money or buy mortgage-backed securities; hybrid REITs combine both. To qualify in the United States a REIT must invest at least 75% of assets in real estate, derive at least 75% of gross income from rents or mortgage interest, distribute at least 90% of taxable income as dividends, and have at least 100 shareholders with no five or fewer individuals owning more than half its shares. Analysts commonly measure performance by funds from operations (FFO) rather than net income, adding back depreciation.

Market significance

Listed REITs form a major sector of equity markets, tracked by indices such as the FTSE Nareit All REITs Index; US listed REITs alone have a combined market capitalisation in the trillions of dollars. Their shares offer liquidity and diversification but correlate with equities more strongly than direct property, and values are sensitive to interest rates.
