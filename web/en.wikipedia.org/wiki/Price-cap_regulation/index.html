Price-cap regulation is a form of regulation designed to set a price ceiling for the services of a regulated firm, most commonly a utility or former monopoly. Rather than regulating the firm's rate of return directly, the regulator caps the overall price the firm may charge, typically using a formula of the form inflation minus X, where X is an expected efficiency factor. The approach was developed in the United Kingdom in the 1980s, following the work of economist Stephen Littlechild, and was applied to privatised industries such as telecommunications, gas, electricity and water.

Mechanism

Under the classic British model, the maximum average price is adjusted each year according to the retail price index (RPI) minus an X factor reflecting the productivity gains the regulator expects the company to achieve: Pt = Pt-1 × (1 + RPI − X). If the firm can reduce its costs by more than X, it keeps the extra profit until the next periodic review, which gives it a strong incentive to operate efficiently. At each review, typically every five years, the regulator resets the cap based on updated information about costs and investment needs.

Comparison with rate-of-return regulation

Price-cap regulation contrasts with the rate-of-return regulation traditionally used in the United States, under which prices are set to allow the firm a specified return on its capital base. Rate-of-return regulation can dull incentives for cost reduction and encourage over-investment in capital (the Averch–Johnson effect). A price cap, by contrast, decouples the firm's allowed revenue from its actual costs between reviews, strengthening efficiency incentives, though it may also create incentives to reduce service quality, which regulators counter with quality standards and penalties.

Variants

Many modern regimes are hybrids. Revenue caps limit total revenue rather than average price, and "totex" regimes such as the UK's RIIO framework combine output targets, incentives and innovation funding with a price or revenue control. Yardstick competition, comparing firms against their peers, is sometimes used to set the X factor.

Criticism

Critics note that setting X requires detailed information the regulator may not have, that firms may game the review process, and that a badly calibrated cap can either bankrupt the firm or hand consumers an unfair deal. Nevertheless, price-cap regulation remains one of the most widely used tools in utility regulation worldwide.
