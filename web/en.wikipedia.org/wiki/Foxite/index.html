Foxite

Foxite is an archaic British political label for a follower of Charles James Fox (1749–1806), the Whig statesman, orator and rival of William Pitt the Younger. In the factional politics of late 18th-century Britain, the Foxites were the wing of the Whig Party loyal to Fox's leadership, as distinct from the Portland Whigs and, later, the Grenvillites.

Character and fortunes

The Foxites championed parliamentary reform, Catholic relief, opposition to the war with revolutionary France, and defence of civil liberties against Pitt's wartime repression (such as the suspension of habeas corpus and the Treasonable Practices Act). Largely in opposition from 1783, except for the Fox–North coalition of 1783 and the "Ministry of All the Talents" in 1806, in which the dying Fox served as Foreign Secretary, the Foxites formed a cohesive circle of aristocratic Whigs and Fox's personal following around Devonshire House and Brooks's club.

After Fox

After Fox's death in 1806, leadership passed to his nephew Lord Holland and to Earl Grey, and the Foxite tradition fed into the 19th-century Whig and Liberal parties. The term is now used mainly by historians of the period, and "Foxite" survives in scholarship on Georgian politics; in the 20th century "Foxite" was very occasionally revived as a jocular label for admirers of Fox's memory.
