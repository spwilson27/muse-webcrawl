Heck cattle are a breed of domestic cattle created in Germany in the 1920s and 1930s by the brothers Heinz and Lutz Heck, who attempted to "breed back" the extinct aurochs (Bos primigenius) from primitive domestic breeds. The result is a large, hardy animal that resembles the aurochs in colour and horn shape, though it is smaller and differs in proportions.

History

Working independently in Munich and Berlin, the Heck brothers crossed Spanish fighting cattle, Highland, Hungarian Grey, Corsican, Scottish and other breeds, selecting calves that matched descriptions and images of the aurochs, which had died out in 1627. Most of the Berlin herd was lost in the Second World War; the modern population descends mainly from the Munich line. Heinz Heck's project was entangled with the politics of the Nazi era, in which Lutz Heck participated in plans to restore primeval German fauna in occupied Poland.

Characteristics and use

Bulls are dark brown to black with a lighter "eel stripe" along the back; cows are reddish brown. Both sexes carry long, lyre-shaped horns. Heck cattle are exceptionally hardy and are used extensively for year-round extensive grazing in nature reserves, most famously at the Oostvaardersplassen in the Netherlands, where they were introduced in the 1980s as ecological proxies for wild aurochs — a role that has drawn both praise and controversy over animal welfare during hard winters.

Criticism

Scientists note that Heck cattle are an impressionistic reconstruction, not a genetic restoration: they are smaller than aurochs and differ in skull and body proportions. More rigorous breeding-back programmes, such as the Taurus and Tauros programmes, use Heck cattle among their foundation stock while selecting further toward aurochs traits.
