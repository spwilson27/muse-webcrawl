History of the English penny (c. 600 – 1707)

The history of the English penny from about 600 to 1707 covers the period from the first Anglo-Saxon silver pennies, modelled on the Frankish denier, to the Acts of Union 1707, after which the English coinage merged with the Scottish into that of Great Britain. For most of this period the penny was England's principal — often only — coin denomination: 240 pennies made one pound sterling, a unit of account long before a pound coin existed. The penny's silver content declined through debasements, above all Henry VIII's Great Debasement, and by the seventeenth century it had become a small silver coin struck alongside gold and larger silver denominations.

A silver penny of Offa of Mercia (757–796), among the finest early English pennies.

Anglo-Saxon pennies

Following the gold thrymsas of the seventh century, broad silver pennies appeared around the 670s (after earlier small, thick sceattas). King Offa of Mercia reformed the coinage around 760–765, striking pennies of high artistic quality bearing his name and portrait. From Edgar's reform of c. 973, a standardised national coinage with local mints and periodic recoinages (the renovatio monetae) produced pennies of consistent weight (about 1.3–1.6 g) and fineness.

Norman and medieval pennies

William the Conqueror continued the Anglo-Saxon system. Henry II's coinage reforms (1158 and the "Short Cross" coinage of 1180) improved quality; the "Long Cross" penny of Henry III (1247) added a cross extending to the rim to deter clipping. Edward I's recoinage of 1279 introduced the groat and halfpenny and farthing as regular denominations, but the penny remained dominant, its weight gradually reduced as silver prices rose.

Tudor debasement and restoration

Henry VIII's Great Debasement (1544–1551) cut the penny's fineness from sterling silver (.925) to as low as one-third silver. Elizabeth I's recoinage of 1560–61 restored sterling fineness, at reduced weight. From 1601 the penny weighed about 0.5 g. Mechanised milling, introduced in 1662 under Charles II, ended hammered coinage; the silver penny continued mainly in Maundy money and as small change.

Union

With the Union of 1707, recoinage of the Scottish money at the Tower Mint ended the separate English series; the British silver penny persisted in tiny form chiefly for Maundy distributions, while copper pennies (from 1797) served everyday commerce.
