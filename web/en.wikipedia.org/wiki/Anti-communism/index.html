Anti-communism

Anti-communism is political and ideological opposition to communist beliefs, groups, and individuals. Organized anti-communism developed after the October Revolution of 1917 in Russia and reached global dimensions during the Cold War, when the United States and the Western Bloc, on one side, and the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc, on the other, engaged in political and ideological confrontation.

History

Opposition to communism arose from many directions: liberals objected to its one-party rule and suppression of civil liberties; conservatives and business interests opposed its programme of abolishing private property; religious groups resisted its official atheism; and rival socialist and anarchist movements disputed Marxist-Leninist doctrine and practice. In the interwar period, anti-communism was a central element of fascist movements in Italy and Germany. After 1945 it shaped Western foreign policy through containment, and in the United States it fuelled McCarthyism and the Second Red Scare.

Forms

Anti-communism has ranged from parliamentary politics and propaganda to violent repression, including mass killings of communists and alleged communists, as in Indonesia in 1965–66 and during Operation Condor in Latin America. Critics argue that anti-communism has also been used as a pretext to suppress legitimate labour, anti-colonial and democratic movements.

After the Cold War

The Revolutions of 1989 and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991 greatly reduced the geopolitical salience of anti-communism, though it remains a significant current in the politics of several countries and in historical debates over the crimes of communist regimes.
