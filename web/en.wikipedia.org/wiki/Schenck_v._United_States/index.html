Schenck v. United States

Schenck v. United States
Court: Supreme Court of the United States
Decided: March 3, 1919
Citation: 249 U.S. 47
Author: Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.

Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), was a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court concerning enforcement of the Espionage Act of 1917 during World War I. A unanimous Court, in an opinion by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., upheld the conviction of Charles Schenck, general secretary of the Socialist Party, for distributing leaflets urging resistance to the military draft. The decision introduced the famous "clear and present danger" test for when speech may be restricted.

The "fire in a theatre" analogy

Holmes wrote that "the most stringent protection of free speech would not protect a man in falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic." He held that the question "is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent."

Legacy

The clear and present danger test was refined in later cases and largely superseded in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), which protected speech unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to produce such action. Holmes himself soon qualified his position, notably in his dissent in Abrams v. United States (1919), which helped shape modern free-speech doctrine.
