Photographic processing

Photographic processing, or photographic development, is the chemical treatment of exposed photographic film or paper to produce a visible, permanent image. Exposure creates an invisible latent image in the silver halide crystals of the emulsion; processing amplifies that image, stabilises it, and removes the unexposed material so the result is no longer sensitive to light.

Photographic processing
Standard black-and-white steps: Develop → Stop → Fix → Wash → Dry
Colour processes: C-41 (negatives), E-6 (slides), RA-4 (prints)

Black-and-white processing

The classic sequence for film is development, a stop bath, fixation, washing, and drying. A developer — typically containing reducing agents such as metol, hydroquinone, or phenidone in alkaline solution — converts exposed silver halide crystals to black metallic silver, while leaving unexposed crystals largely untouched. An acidic stop bath halts development, and the fixer, usually sodium or ammonium thiosulfate ("hypo"), dissolves the remaining unexposed halide. Thorough washing removes residual chemicals that would otherwise stain or fade the image over time.

Colour processing

Colour negative film is processed in the standardised C-41 process, in which a colour developer forms dyes in three emulsion layers alongside the silver image, after which a bleach converts the silver back to a removable form and the fixer removes it, leaving only dyes. Colour reversal (slide) film uses the E-6 process, with a first developer, a reversal step, and colour development. Colour prints on resin-coated paper use the RA-4 process. These processes require tighter temperature control — C-41 is run at about 38 °C (100 °F) — than black-and-white work.

Equipment and practice

Film is loaded in darkness onto reels inside light-tight developing tanks, allowing the chemical steps to proceed in room light. Paper prints are processed in open trays under safelight illumination in a darkroom. One-hour minilabs and large roller-transport machines automated the same chemistry for commercial laboratories. Temperature, time, agitation, and chemical freshness all affect contrast, grain, and colour balance.

History

Development distinguished practical photography from earlier processes: the daguerreotype was developed with mercury vapour, while William Henry Fox Talbot's calotype and the wet-collodion and gelatin dry-plate processes relied on chemical developers such as gallic acid and pyrogallol. Twentieth-century standardisation by manufacturers such as Kodak made home and laboratory processing routine, before digital photography displaced most film processing after 2000. Film processing survives in fine-art, archival, and enthusiast practice.

Environmental and safety considerations

Spent fixer contains dissolved silver, which is recovered commercially and must not be discharged untreated in many jurisdictions. Developers and bleaches are handled with gloves and adequate ventilation.
