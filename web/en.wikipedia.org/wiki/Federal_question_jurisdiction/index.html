Federal question jurisdiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In United States law, federal question jurisdiction is the subject-matter jurisdiction of the federal courts over civil actions "arising under" the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States, conferred by Article III of the Constitution and by statute, principally 28 U.S.C. § 1331. Together with diversity jurisdiction, it forms the main bases on which federal district courts hear civil cases.

The well-pleaded complaint rule

Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, established in Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley (1908), federal question jurisdiction is determined from the plaintiff's own statement of the claim: the federal question must appear on the face of the complaint, and a federal issue raised only in the defendant's anticipated defences does not count. In American Well Works (1916), Justice Holmes stated the classic test: a suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action.

Scope and related doctrines

Most federal-question cases rest on a cause of action created by federal statute or the Constitution. A narrower category, recognised in cases such as Grable & Sons Metal Products v. Darue (2005), allows jurisdiction over state-law claims that necessarily raise a substantial, disputed federal issue. Federal courts may exercise supplemental jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1367) over related state-law claims, and defendants may remove qualifying cases filed in state court to federal court. Unlike diversity jurisdiction, § 1331 imposes no amount-in-controversy requirement (a minimum once applied but was abolished in 1980).
