Atmospheric icing occurs when water droplets in the atmosphere freeze on surfaces exposed to the air. Icing can occur when supercooled liquid droplets — water remaining liquid below 0 °C — strike a structure and freeze on impact. In-cloud icing at altitude affects aircraft; precipitation icing, including freezing rain, affects ground structures.

Types

Rime ice is milky and granular, formed when small supercooled droplets freeze almost instantly, trapping air; it grows into the wind on masts, turbines and trees. Glaze or clear ice is dense and transparent, formed from larger droplets (as in freezing rain) that spread before freezing; it is heavier and harder to remove. Frost forms by direct deposition of water vapour.

Hazards

On aircraft, ice accretion changes airfoil shape, adds weight and can block sensors, and is a recognised cause of accidents; aircraft use pneumatic boots, heated leading edges and chemical de-icing. On power lines, accreted ice combined with wind can collapse towers — the 1998 North American ice storm left millions without power. Wind turbines lose efficiency and throw ice; ships and offshore structures in cold seas suffer dangerous topweight from sea-spray icing.

Measurement and mitigation

Icing rates are measured with dedicated ice detectors and rotating-cylinder instruments, and standards such as ISO 12494 govern design loads for structures. Mitigation includes heating, hydrophobic coatings, mechanical removal and structural design margins.
