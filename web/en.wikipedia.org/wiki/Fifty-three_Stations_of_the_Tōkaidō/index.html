The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō

53 Stations of the Tōkaidō
Artist: Utagawa Hiroshige
Date: 1833–1834 (Hōeidō edition)
Medium: Woodblock prints (ukiyo-e)
Route: Edo (Nihonbashi) to Kyoto (Sanjo Bridge)

The Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō (Tōkaidō Gojūsan-tsugi) are the rest stations (shukuba) along the Tōkaidō, the most important of the Five Routes of Japan's Edo period, running roughly 500 km along the Pacific coast from Edo (modern Tokyo) to Kyoto. The stations provided food, lodging, and fresh horses for travellers, officials, and the great daimyō processions of the sankin-kōtai system.

Hiroshige's series

The stations are famous above all through the woodblock print series by Utagawa Hiroshige, first published in 1833–34 by Hōeidō (the "Hōeidō edition"). Hiroshige had travelled the road in 1832 with an official procession to Kyoto, and his 55 prints — one for each station, plus the starting point at Nihonbashi in Edo and the terminus at Keishi (Kyoto) — depict travellers, landscapes, weather, and local life with a lyricism that made the series a sensation and established Hiroshige as the leading landscape artist of ukiyo-e. Famous images include the driving rain of Shōno, morning mists at Mishima, and snow on the mountains at Kambara.

The stations

From Edo the numbered stations run Shinagawa, Kawasaki, Kanagawa, Hodogaya, Totsuka, Fujisawa, Hiratsuka, Ōiso, Odawara, Hakone, Mishima, Numazu, Hara, Yoshiwara, Kambara, Yui, Okitsu, Ejiri, Fuchū, Mariko, Okabe, Fujieda, Shimada, Kanaya, Nissaka, Kakegawa, Fukuroi, Mitsuke, Hamamatsu, Maisaka, Arai, Shirasuka, Futagawa, Yoshida, Goyu, Akasaka, Fujikawa, Okazaki, Chiryū, Narumi, Miya, Kuwana, Yokkaichi, Ishiyakushi, Shōno, Kameyama, Seki, Sakashita, Tsuchiyama, Minakuchi, Ishibe, Kusatsu, and Ōtsu, before the road ends at Kyoto.

Legacy

The Tōkaidō corridor remains Japan's principal axis: the Tōkaidō Shinkansen and the Tōkaidō Main Line roughly follow the old road, and many station towns preserve Edo-period streetscapes. Hiroshige himself produced numerous later Tōkaidō series, and the imagery influenced Western artists including Van Gogh, who copied two of the prints.
