Assimilation (psychology)

Assimilation is a cognitive process in the theory of development proposed by the Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget. It describes how a person incorporates new experiences into existing mental structures, or schemas, without changing them. Assimilation is paired with its complementary process, accommodation, in which schemas are modified in response to new information; together the two processes produce adaptation and cognitive growth.

Piaget's theory

A schema is an organised pattern of thought or behaviour used to interpret the world — for example, a child's schema for dogs. When a child sees a new animal that fits the schema, such as a different breed of dog, the experience is assimilated: the new object is understood in terms of what is already known. If the child calls a horse a "dog", assimilation has occurred even though the label is wrong; correcting the error requires accommodation, the restructuring of the schema itself.

Piaget held that learners strive for a state of equilibrium. When new experiences fit existing schemas, equilibrium is maintained through assimilation; when they do not, disequilibrium occurs and motivates accommodation, restoring balance at a more advanced level of understanding. This cycle drives the progression through Piaget's stages of cognitive development, from the sensorimotor stage of infancy to the formal operational stage of adolescence.

Examples

A toddler who learns the word "ball" and applies it to oranges and the moon is assimilating. A student who interprets a new economic model through an earlier framework, or a reader who understands a novel by fitting it into familiar story patterns, is likewise assimilating new material into existing structures. In education, the concept underlies constructivist teaching approaches that connect new material to learners' prior knowledge.
