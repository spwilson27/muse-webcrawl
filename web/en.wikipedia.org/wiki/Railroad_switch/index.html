Railroad switch

A railroad switch (known as a turnout in North American industry usage and as a set of points in British English) is a mechanical installation enabling trains to be guided from one track to another, such as at a railway junction or where a siding branches off. It consists of a pair of movable tapered switch rails, fixed stock rails, a frog (crossing) where one rail crosses another, guard rails, and a mechanism to move and lock the switch rails.

Operation

The switch rails are moved together by a switch machine or a hand lever. The closed switch rail guides the wheel flanges so that the train takes the selected route. Because a train following the switch in the trailing direction can force open a switch set against it, switches are fitted with detection and locking, integrated with signalling interlockings, to prevent unsafe movements.

Types

Switches are classified by their geometry — turnout number or frog angle determines the permitted speed on the diverging route. Variants include single and double crossovers, three-way switches, double slips, catch points and movable-point frogs, which eliminate the flangeway gap in the frog to allow smoother high-speed running. In yards, retarders and hump switches sort freight wagons by gravity.

Safety

Switch failures — worn components, obstructions between rail and switch blade, or faults in detection — have contributed to serious derailments, notably the 2002 Potters Bar crash in Britain, and switches are subject to rigorous inspection regimes.
