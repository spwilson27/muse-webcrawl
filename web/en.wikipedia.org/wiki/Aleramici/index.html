Aleramici

The Aleramici were a medieval Italian noble dynasty of Frankish origin, founded by Aleramo, who in the 10th century was invested with a great march in north-western Italy. From Aleramo's inheritance descend the marquesses of Montferrat — the family's most famous line — as well as the marquesses of Saluzzo, Savona, Incisa, and other Piedmontese and Ligurian houses.

Origins and the legend of Aleramo

Aleramo first appears in documents of the 930s–960s as a count in the service of the Italian kings Hugh and Berengar II, and later of Emperor Otto I, who granted him extensive lands between the Po, the Tanaro, and the Ligurian coast. Later chroniclers embroidered his rise into a romance: Aleramo, a German-born orphan raised in Italy, was said to have eloped with Alasia (Adelasia), daughter of Otto I, and to have won the emperor's pardon and the march after proving himself in war. Historians treat the story as legend, though the grant itself is genuine.

Division of the inheritance

Because Aleramo's lands were held jointly and descended through several sons and grandsons, the march fragmented into a constellation of Aleramici lordships. The elder line kept Montferrat, centred on Casale and later Chivasso and Trino; another line, the Del Vasto, took the southern territories and produced the marquesses of Saluzzo, Savona, Busca, and Clavesana, and — through Adelaide del Vasto's marriages — became involved in the Norman kingdom of Sicily and the county of Sicily's royal house. Other branches held Incisa, Occimiano, Ponzone, and Ceva.

The Montferrat line and the Crusades

The Aleramici marquesses of Montferrat rose to international prominence in the 12th and 13th centuries. William V "the Old" campaigned in the Holy Land; his sons included William Longsword, Count of Jaffa and father of King Baldwin V of Jerusalem; Conrad of Montferrat, briefly King of Jerusalem in 1192; and Boniface I, leader of the Fourth Crusade, who after 1204 founded the Kingdom of Thessalonica in conquered Byzantine Greece. Through marriage the marquessate passed in 1305 to the Byzantine Palaiologos family (the Palaeologan Montferrat line), and in 1536 to the Gonzaga of Mantua, ending the direct Aleramici succession, though collateral Piedmontese branches — notably Saluzzo (extinct 1548) and Incisa — persisted longer.
