Conducting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A conductor directing an orchestra

Conducting is the art of directing a musical performance, such as an orchestral or choral concert. It has been defined as "the art of directing the simultaneous performance of several players or singers by the use of gesture." The primary duties of the conductor are to interpret the score in a way that reflects the specific indications in that score, set the tempo, ensure correct entries by ensemble members, and "shape" the phrasing where appropriate.

Technique

Conductors communicate with their musicians primarily through hand gestures, usually with the aid of a baton, and may use facial expressions and body language. Standard beat patterns trace shapes in the air corresponding to the meter: for example, a four-beat pattern moves down, left, right, and up. The right hand generally maintains the tempo and pattern while the left hand cues entries and shapes dynamics and expression. Conductors prepare through score study and lead rehearsals before performance.

History

Ensemble direction existed since at least the Middle Ages, using a staff, hand movements (chironomy), or a leading violinist. The modern conductor emerged in the 19th century as orchestras grew larger and scores more complex; figures such as Mendelssohn, Berlioz, and Wagner established the role, and Hans von Bülow and the Meiningen Court Orchestra set new standards of rehearsal discipline. In the 20th century conductors such as Arturo Toscanini, Herbert von Karajan, and Leonard Bernstein became prominent public figures.

Roles

The conductor of a major orchestra (often titled music director) also selects repertoire, auditions musicians, and serves as the public face of the ensemble. Choral conductors, band conductors, and opera conductors share the same core craft, and many orchestral musicians regard the conductor's interpretive leadership as central to great performance.
