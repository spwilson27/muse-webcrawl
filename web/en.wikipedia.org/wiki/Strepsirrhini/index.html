Strepsirrhini or Strepsirhini is a suborder of primates that includes the lemurs of Madagascar, the galagos ("bushbabies") and pottos of Africa, and the lorises of India and Southeast Asia. Collectively they are known as the strepsirrhines. The name derives from the Greek for "curved" or "twisted" nostrils: strepsirrhines have naked, comma-shaped nostrils and a moist rhinarium (wet nose) like those of dogs and many other mammals, unlike the dry-nosed Haplorhini (tarsiers, monkeys, and apes).

Characteristics

Strepsirrhines retain many ancestral primate traits: a strong reliance on smell, with a long snout and large olfactory bulbs; a reflective tapetum lucidum behind the retina for night vision; eyes that are not fully enclosed in a bony socket; a toothcomb formed by the lower incisors and canines, used in grooming and feeding; and a grooming claw on the second toe. Most females have a bicornuate uterus, and litter sizes of more than one are common among the smaller species. Many strepsirrhines are nocturnal and have relatively small brains compared with haplorhines.

Unlike other primates, strepsirrhines can synthesize vitamin C, and most rely partly on it in their diet less than haplorhines do. Their dental formula and the structure of the ear region are also distinctive.

Classification and distribution

The living strepsirrhines are divided into two infraorders: Lemuriformes (lemurs, lorises, and galagos) and the extinct Adapiformes of the Eocene of North America, Europe, and Asia. Living groups include:

  • Lemurs — over 100 species, all endemic to Madagascar (families Cheirogaleidae, Lemuridae, Lepilemuridae, Indriidae, Daubentoniidae).
  • Lorises and pottos (Lorisidae) — slow-climbing nocturnal forms of Africa and Asia.
  • Galagos (Galagidae) — small, leaping nocturnal primates of sub-Saharan Africa.

Evolution and conservation

Strepsirrhines diverged from haplorhines roughly 55–90 million years ago. Madagascar's lemurs descend from a single colonization event from Africa, probably by rafting, around 50–60 million years ago. Many strepsirrhines, especially lemurs, are threatened by habitat loss and hunting; the IUCN considers lemurs among the most endangered groups of mammals.
