Betalain

Betalains are a class of red and yellow water-soluble pigments found in plants of the order Caryophyllales, where they replace anthocyanins — the two pigment families never occur together in the same plant. They are synthesised from the amino acid tyrosine and are divided into red-violet betacyanins and yellow-orange betaxanthins.

Chemistry

All betalains derive from betalamic acid, which condenses spontaneously with cyclo-DOPA derivatives to give betacyanins (such as betanin, the main pigment of beetroot) or with amino acids and amines to give betaxanthins (such as indicaxanthin of prickly pear). Betanin is used commercially as a natural food colouring, E162, though it is sensitive to heat, light and pH extremes.

Occurrence

Betalains colour the roots of red beet, the flowers and fruit of cacti (including prickly pear and dragon fruit), bougainvillea bracts, amaranth, Swiss chard and pokeberries. Within Caryophyllales, families that produce anthocyanins instead — such as the pink family, Caryophyllaceae — are exceptions that lost betalain production. Fungi such as some Amanita species and the bacterium Gluconacetobacter diazotrophicus also produce betalamic-acid-derived pigments.

Function and uses

In plants, betalains serve as attractants for pollinators and seed dispersers, as sunscreens and antioxidants, and possibly in defence and osmotic stress tolerance. Eating beetroot can colour the urine of some people reddish ("beeturia"). Betalains are studied as dietary antioxidants and are increasingly used as natural alternatives to synthetic food dyes; they are also investigated as sensitizers in dye-sensitized solar cells.

History

The pigments were long confused with anthocyanins; their distinct nitrogenous structure was established in the mid-twentieth century, and the name "betalain" — from Beta, the beet genus — was proposed in 1968 by Mabry and Dreiding.
