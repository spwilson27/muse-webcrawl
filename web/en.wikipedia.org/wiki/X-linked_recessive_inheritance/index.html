X-linked recessive inheritance

X-linked recessive inheritance is a mode of inheritance in which a mutation in a gene on the X chromosome causes the phenotype to be expressed in males (who are necessarily hemizygous for the gene mutation because they have one X and one Y chromosome) and in females who are homozygous for the gene mutation (that is, who have a copy of the gene mutation on each of their two X chromosomes). Females with one copy of the mutation and one normal copy are called carriers; they are usually unaffected, although skewed X-inactivation can occasionally produce mild symptoms.

Characteristics

Because males need only one mutated copy to be affected, X-linked recessive disorders are far more common in males than in females. Affected males cannot pass the trait to their sons (who receive the father's Y chromosome), but all of their daughters are obligate carriers. A carrier female has a 50% chance of passing the mutation to each child: on average half her sons will be affected and half her daughters will be carriers. The trait often appears to skip generations, passing from an affected grandfather through his carrier daughters to his grandsons. Affected females are rare and require an affected father and a carrier (or affected) mother.

Examples

Well-known X-linked recessive conditions include haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) and haemophilia B (factor IX deficiency), historically famous through the descendants of Queen Victoria; Duchenne and Becker muscular dystrophies, caused by mutations in the dystrophin gene—the largest known human gene; red–green colour vision deficiency, which affects roughly 8% of males of Northern European ancestry; and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. Other examples include Lesch–Nyhan syndrome, fragile X syndrome (sometimes classed separately because of its unusual repeat-expansion mechanism), and X-linked agammaglobulinaemia.

Skewed X-inactivation

In females, one X chromosome in each cell is randomly inactivated early in development (lyonisation). If, by chance, the normal X is inactivated in most cells, a carrier may manifest symptoms of the disorder; such women are called manifesting carriers. Skewed X-inactivation explains, for example, why some carriers of Duchenne muscular dystrophy show muscle weakness, and why carriers of haemophilia may have reduced clotting-factor levels and a mild bleeding tendency.
