Haemophilia

Haemophilia (also spelled hemophilia) is a group of hereditary genetic disorders that impair the body's ability to make blood clots, a process needed to stop bleeding. Haemophilia A (factor VIII deficiency) and haemophilia B (factor IX deficiency, or Christmas disease) are X-linked recessive disorders affecting mainly males; a rarer acquired form results from autoantibodies against clotting factors. The disorders cause prolonged bleeding after injury and spontaneous haemorrhage into joints and muscles.

Signs and symptoms

Severity correlates with residual factor level: severe disease (less than 1% of normal) brings spontaneous bleeds, typically into joints (haemarthroses) causing chronic arthropathy, and into muscles and soft tissues; moderate and mild disease bleed mainly after trauma or surgery. Intracranial haemorrhage is the most dangerous complication. Carriers are usually asymptomatic, though some have reduced factor levels and bleeding symptoms.

History

Excessive familial bleeding was noted in ancient texts, including Talmudic rulings exempting boys from circumcision after brothers died of bleeding. The disease became famous as the "royal disease" after Queen Victoria, a carrier, passed the mutation to several European royal houses; her descendants included the haemophiliac Tsarevich Alexei of Russia. In the 1980s, many people with haemophilia were infected with HIV and hepatitis C through contaminated pooled plasma products, a catastrophe that transformed blood-product safety and led to recombinant factor concentrates.

Treatment

Standard care is replacement of the missing factor, given prophylactically or on demand, using plasma-derived or recombinant concentrates. The bispecific antibody emicizumab substitutes for factor VIII function in haemophilia A. Extended-half-life factors reduced infusion frequency, and in the 2020s the first gene therapies for haemophilia A and B (such as valoctocogene roxaparvovec and etranacogene dezaparvovec) were approved, offering durable endogenous factor production after a single infusion.
