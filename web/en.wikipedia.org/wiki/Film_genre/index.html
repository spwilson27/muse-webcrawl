A film genre is a category of motion pictures defined by similarities in narrative elements, aesthetic approach, or emotional response. Films within a genre share conventions of plot, character, setting, theme, and visual style that audiences come to recognise and expect.

Classification

Genres may be defined by setting (the western, the war film), by emotional effect (comedy, horror, melodrama), by narrative structure (the crime film, the mystery), or by style and format (the musical, film noir). Major genres include action, adventure, comedy, drama, fantasy, horror, science fiction, thriller, and the western, each containing numerous subgenres — such as the slasher film within horror or space opera within science fiction. Many films are hybrids combining several genres.

Genre theory

Genre criticism in film studies examines how genres arise, evolve, and reflect the societies that produce them. Scholars note that genres are not fixed: they develop through cycles of repetition and variation, sometimes crystallising into conventions that later films parody or revise, as revisionist westerns did. Industry uses genre as a marketing tool that shapes audience expectations, while critics debate whether genre categories describe real structures or are convenient labels imposed after the fact.

History

Genre filmmaking was systematised by the Hollywood studio system of the 1930s–1950s, when studios specialised in particular kinds of pictures and audiences expected recognisable formulas. Later movements, from Italian neorealism to the French New Wave and New Hollywood, frequently worked by bending or breaking genre conventions.
