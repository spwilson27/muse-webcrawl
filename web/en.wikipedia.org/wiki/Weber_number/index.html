The Weber number (We) is a dimensionless number in fluid mechanics that is often useful in analysing fluid flows where there is an interface between two different fluids, especially for multiphase flows with strongly curved surfaces. It is named after Moritz Weber (1871–1951), a professor of naval engineering in Berlin. It measures the relative importance of a fluid's inertia compared to its surface tension.

Definition

The Weber number is defined as:

We = ρ v² L / σ

where ρ is the density of the fluid, v is its velocity, L is a characteristic length (for example, droplet diameter), and σ is the surface tension of the interface.

Applications

The Weber number is the key parameter in droplet and bubble dynamics. When We is small (below about 1), surface tension dominates and droplets remain nearly spherical. As We increases, aerodynamic forces deform the droplet, and above a critical Weber number (around 12 for low-viscosity droplets in a gas stream) the droplet breaks apart. This behaviour governs fuel injection and atomisation in engines, spray coating and inkjet printing, rain formation, spray drying, and the design of atomisers and sprinklers.

A modified Weber number that includes viscosity effects is often used together with the Ohnesorge number to characterise breakup regimes. The Weber number is also applied to thin-film flows and to the formation of bubbles in liquids.
