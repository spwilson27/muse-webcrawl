Supernova remnant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The expanding shell of a supernova remnant

A supernova remnant (SNR) is the structure resulting from the explosion of a star in a supernova. The remnant consists of the ejected material expanding away from the explosion site, bounded by an outward-moving shock wave that heats and ionises the surrounding interstellar medium.

Stages of evolution

A remnant's evolution is usually described in three stages. In free expansion, the ejecta travel outward almost undecelerated. When the swept-up interstellar mass becomes comparable to the ejecta mass, the remnant enters the Sedov–Taylor phase, an approximately adiabatic expansion whose radius grows as t2/5. Finally, when the shock slows and cools, the remnant enters a radiative or "snowplow" phase, forming a thin, dense shell that gradually disperses into the interstellar medium over tens of thousands of years.

Types and examples

Remnants are classified as shell-like, centre-filled plerions (pulsar wind nebulae), or composite. Famous examples include the Crab Nebula, the remnant of the supernova of 1054, which contains a pulsar; Tycho's remnant (SN 1572); Kepler's remnant (SN 1604); Cassiopeia A, the youngest known Galactic remnant; the Vela remnant; and the remnant of SN 1006.

Significance

Supernova remnants disperse the heavy elements forged in stars into space, stir and heat the interstellar medium, can trigger new star formation by compressing gas clouds, and are believed to accelerate most Galactic cosmic rays at their shock fronts.
