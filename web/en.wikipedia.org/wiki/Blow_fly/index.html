Blow fly

[Hero image: close-up of a blow fly showing its large red compound eyes and bristled thorax]

Blow fly is the common name for flies of the family Calliphoridae, also written blow-fly or blowfly. Adult blow flies are typically 6–14 mm long, stout-bodied and often brightly metallic in colour — blue, green, bronze or violet — with prominent red compound eyes and strong bristles on the thorax. Over 1,500 species are described worldwide.

Life cycle

Females deposit clusters of pale eggs on carrion, faeces, or soiled wool. The eggs hatch within a day into maggots that pass through three larval instars, feeding voraciously before leaving the food source to pupate in the soil. Development is strongly temperature dependent, a fact exploited by forensic scientists to estimate time since death. Adults emerge after one to three weeks in the puparium and live for several weeks, feeding on nectar, honeydew and carrion fluids.

Ecology and significance

Blow flies are key decomposers, accelerating the breakdown of carrion. They are also vectors of pathogens picked up from faeces and corpses, and some species parasitise livestock and occasionally humans (myiasis). Conversely, certain species are managed pollinators, and sterile screw-worm technique has eradicated the parasitic New World screw-worm from North and Central America by releasing sterilised males.
