Flightless bird

Flightless birds are birds that through evolution lost the ability to fly. There are over 60 extant flightless species, including the well-known ratites (ostriches, emus, cassowaries, rheas, and kiwis) and penguins. The smallest flightless bird is the Inaccessible Island rail, and the largest — also the largest living bird of any kind — is the ostrich. Many flightless species are extinct, including the moa of New Zealand, the elephant birds of Madagascar, and the dodo of Mauritius.

Evolution

Flightlessness evolved independently many times, most often on islands lacking mammalian predators, where the high energy cost of flight brought little benefit. Flightless birds tend to show reduced wing bones, a flat sternum without the deep keel (carina) that anchors flight muscles in flying birds, and often larger body size and stronger legs. Genetic studies indicate that the ratites lost flight convergently in several lineages after their flying ancestors dispersed, rather than inheriting flightlessness from a single common ancestor. Penguins took a different path: their wings became flippers for "flying" underwater, propelled swimming being subject to similar aerodynamic constraints.

Characteristics

Flightless birds generally have softer, hair-like feathers lacking the interlocking barbules that create the stiff vanes needed for flight, reduced pectoral musculature, and in many island species, an absence of fear of predators ("island tameness") that proved disastrous when humans, rats, cats, and pigs arrived.

Conservation

Because they evolved without mammalian predators, flightless birds are exceptionally vulnerable to introduced species and habitat change. A large share of historically recorded bird extinctions are flightless island species, and many survivors — kiwis, kakapo, Galápagos flightless cormorant, and numerous rails — are threatened, surviving mainly through intensive conservation management.
