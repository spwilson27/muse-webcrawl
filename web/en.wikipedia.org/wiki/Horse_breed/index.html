A horse breed is a selectively bred population of domesticated horses, often with a pedigree recorded in a breed registry maintained by a breed association or stud book. Breeds are distinguished by consistently heritable characteristics — conformation, size, coat colour patterns, gait, and temperament — developed for particular uses such as racing, riding, driving, or draft work. There are several hundred recognised breeds worldwide, and many more local types and landraces.

Concept of a breed

Unlike the biological subspecies, a "breed" is a human-defined population. Registries may be closed — offspring only of registered parents, as with the Thoroughbred — or open and performance-based, as in many European warmblood studbooks, which approve stallions and mares by inspection and performance testing. Some breeds are defined by colour alone (colour breeds), and others, such as many gaited breeds, by an inherited ambling gait.

Categories

Horses are loosely grouped into hot-blooded light riding breeds of desert or steppe origin (Arabian, Thoroughbred, Barb), cold-blooded heavy draft breeds (Shire, Clydesdale, Percheron, Belgian), and warmbloods, crosses of the two developed for sport. Ponies are defined by height (traditionally under 14.2 hands at the withers) rather than ancestry alone, though many pony breeds, such as the Welsh breeds, are distinct populations.

History

Selective breeding of horses is ancient — Arabian horse pedigrees were preserved orally and in writing for centuries — but formal stud books date from the late 18th century: the General Stud Book for Thoroughbreds began publication in 1791. The great breed-formation era of the 19th century produced most modern breeds as agriculture, cavalry remount programmes, and sport codified type. In the 20th and 21st centuries, motorisation collapsed draft-horse numbers while sport and leisure breeding expanded, and many traditional breeds became rare, prompting conservation by organisations such as the Rare Breeds Survival Trust.

Genetics and health

Closed populations and popular-sire effects can concentrate genetic disease: examples include HYPP in Quarter Horses, SCID in Arabians, and fragile foal syndrome in warmbloods. Modern registries increasingly use DNA testing and genomic tools to manage diversity.
