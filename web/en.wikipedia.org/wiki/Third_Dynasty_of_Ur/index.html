The Third Dynasty of Ur (abbreviated Ur III), c. 2112 – 2004 BC, was an ancient Mesopotamian empire centred on the city of Ur in southern Iraq. Ruled by Sumerian-speaking kings, it brought a "Sumerian Renaissance" of administration, law, literature and monumental building, and it is documented by hundreds of thousands of surviving cuneiform tablets — one of the most richly recorded states of the ancient world.

History

Ur-Namma, originally governor of Ur under Utu-hengal of Uruk, founded the dynasty, expelled the Gutians, reunified Sumer and Akkad, issued one of the earliest known law codes (the Code of Ur-Namma), and began the great Ziggurat of Ur. His son Shulgi (reigned 48 years) transformed the kingdom into a highly centralised bureaucratic empire: he reformed the calendar, weights, taxation (the bala system), the army and the scribal schools, expanded into Elam and the Zagros, and was deified in his lifetime, with hymns sung to him as a god. Amar-Sin and Shu-Sin maintained the system and built defences against Amorite incursions, including a great wall between the Tigris and Euphrates.

Collapse

Under Ibbi-Sin the empire disintegrated: provinces seceded, grain prices soared amid famine, Amorite groups pressed in, and finally the Elamites sacked Ur itself (c. 2004 BC) and carried the king into captivity. The catastrophe was mourned in Sumerian laments such as the Lament for Ur. Power in southern Mesopotamia passed to the rival cities of Isin and Larsa.

Legacy

Ur III is famed for its administrative tablets — receipts, tax records, labour rosters and livestock accounts, especially from Puzrish-Dagan (Drehem) and Umma — which allow extraordinarily detailed reconstruction of an ancient economy, and for its royal hymns and law codes that shaped the Old Babylonian scribal tradition.
