Zoltán Kodály

Zoltán Kodály (Hungarian: Kodály Zoltán, pronounced [ˈkodaːj ˈzoltaːn]; 16 December 1882 – 6 March 1967) was a Hungarian composer, ethnomusicologist, pedagogue, linguist, and philosopher. He is best known internationally as the creator of the Kodály method of music education.

Zoltán Kodály
Born: 16 December 1882, Kecskemét, Austria-Hungary
Died: 6 March 1967 (aged 84), Budapest, Hungary
Occupations: Composer, ethnomusicologist, music educator
Known for: Kodály method, Psalmus Hungaricus, Háry János

Life

Born in Kecskemét, Kodály learned violin as a child and sang in a cathedral choir. He studied at the Franz Liszt Academy of Music in Budapest, where he met Béla Bartók, with whom he collected Hungarian folk songs on phonograph cylinders from 1905 onward. He earned a PhD on the strophic structure of Hungarian folk song and taught theory and composition at the Academy for decades.

Music

Kodály’s compositions include the opera Háry János (1926), from which an orchestral suite became famous — including the humorous musical depiction of a sneeze — the Psalmus Hungaricus (1923), Dances of Marosszék, Dances of Galánta, the Concerto for Orchestra, chamber works, and extensive choral music. His style combines Hungarian folk melody with classical forms.

Kodály method

Believing that music education should begin early, use singing as its foundation, and draw on folk song in the child’s mother tongue, Kodály and his collaborators developed a system using movable-do solfège, hand signs, and rhythm syllables. The approach influenced music teaching worldwide.

Legacy

Kodály received the Kossuth Prize multiple times and is regarded, alongside Bartók, as a founder of modern Hungarian music.
