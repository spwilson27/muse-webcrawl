Münchhausen trilemma

The Münchhausen trilemma, also called Agrippa's trilemma, is a thought experiment in epistemology, coined by the German philosopher Hans Albert in his 1968 Treatise on Critical Reason and named after Baron Münchhausen, who legendarily pulled himself out of a swamp by his own hair. It purports to show that any attempt to justify a claim with certainty must fail.

The three horns

Any chain of justification must end in one of three unsatisfying ways:

  • Infinite regress: each justification requires a further justification, endlessly.
  • Circular reasoning: the chain loops back so that a claim is ultimately supported by itself.
  • Axiomatic termination: the chain stops at some point simply declared self-evident or accepted by dogma.

Responses

Different epistemological schools embrace different horns. Foundationalists accept basic beliefs that terminate the regress; coherentists accept a web of mutual support as virtuously rather than viciously circular; infinitists defend infinite chains; and fallibilists such as Karl Popper reject the demand for certain justification altogether, holding that knowledge grows by conjecture and criticism without ultimate foundations. The problem traces back to the "five modes" of the ancient sceptic Agrippa, recorded by Sextus Empiricus, and to Fries's trilemma, from which Albert developed his version.
