Tamarisk

The tamarisks (genus Tamarix) are a group of about 50–60 species of flowering plants in the family Tamaricaceae, native to dry regions of Eurasia and Africa. The generic name originated in Latin and may refer to the Tamaris River in Hispania Tarraconensis (Spain).

Description

Tamarisks are deciduous or evergreen shrubs and small trees growing to 1–18 m tall, with slender branches and minute scale-like leaves. They produce dense masses of small pink or white flowers in spring and summer. They are adapted to saline and alkaline soils and tolerate drought, deep rooting systems allowing them to tap groundwater in arid environments.

Invasive species

Several species, notably Tamarix ramosissima and Tamarix chinensis (collectively called "saltcedar"), became highly invasive in the southwestern United States and northern Mexico after being introduced as ornamentals and for erosion control. They colonise riparian areas, consume large quantities of groundwater, increase soil salinity through salt-excreting leaves, and displace native cottonwood and willow habitat, prompting extensive control programmes.

Uses and history

Tamarisks have been planted since antiquity as windbreaks and ornamentals and are mentioned in ancient texts, including the Bible, where a tamarisk is planted by Abraham at Beersheba. Their salt tolerance makes them useful for stabilising coastal and desert soils.
