Brute-force attack

In cryptography, a brute-force attack consists of an attacker submitting many passwords or passphrases with the hope of eventually guessing correctly. The attacker systematically checks all possible passwords and passphrases until the correct one is found. Alternatively, the attacker can attempt to guess the key, which is typically created from the password using a key derivation function. This is known as an exhaustive key search.

Theory

The feasibility of a brute-force attack depends on the size of the key space. A 56-bit key, such as that of DES, offers 256 combinations and was broken in under a day by the EFF's Deep Crack machine in 1998. Modern standards such as AES with 128-bit or 256-bit keys are computationally infeasible to exhaust with any foreseeable technology.

Defences

Defences include using long, randomly generated passwords; salting and slow key-derivation functions such as bcrypt, scrypt and Argon2 for stored passwords; rate limiting and account lockouts for online attacks; and multi-factor authentication. Offline attacks, where the attacker has obtained a password hash file, are limited mainly by the speed of the hashing algorithm and the attacker's hardware, often GPU clusters.

Related attacks

A dictionary attack is a refinement that tries likely passwords from a list rather than every combination. Credential stuffing uses breached username–password pairs from other services. The reverse brute-force attack tries one common password against many accounts.
