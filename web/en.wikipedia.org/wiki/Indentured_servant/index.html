Indentured servant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An indentured servant was a person bound by a contract (an indenture) to work for a fixed term of years — commonly four to seven — for a master, in exchange for passage across the ocean, food, clothing, lodging, and sometimes training or a payment of "freedom dues" at the end of service. The system was a principal source of labour in the English colonies of North America and the Caribbean during the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries.

History

Indentured servitude arose as a solution to two problems: the chronic labour shortage in land-rich, cash-poor colonies, and the inability of many would-be emigrants to pay their own passage. Merchants and ship captains advanced the cost of transport and sold the servant's contract on arrival. Perhaps half of the white immigrants to the American colonies arrived as indentured servants. Servants could be bought and sold, were subject to corporal punishment, and could not marry or leave without permission; unlike slaves, however, their servitude was temporary and contractual, and their children were born free.

Decline and later forms

As the price of enslaved African labour fell relative to servant contracts after the late seventeenth century, colonial planters shifted decisively to slavery, and indentured migration to mainland North America dwindled after the American Revolution. In the nineteenth century, a new global system of indentured labour moved more than a million Indian and Chinese workers to plantations in the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, South Africa, and elsewhere after the abolition of slavery; critics called it "a new system of slavery," and it was abolished in the early twentieth century.
