SELEX (Systematic Evolution of Ligands by EXponential enrichment), also referred to as in vitro selection or in vitro evolution, is a combinatorial chemistry technique in molecular biology for producing oligonucleotides of either single-stranded DNA or RNA that specifically bind to a target ligand or ligands. These selected oligonucleotides are known as aptamers.

Process

The process begins with a vast library — often 1013 to 1015 different sequences — of chemically synthesised oligonucleotides with a random central region flanked by fixed primer sites. The library is exposed to the target (a protein, small molecule, cell, or other structure); sequences that fail to bind are washed away, while bound sequences are recovered and amplified by PCR (with transcription steps for RNA libraries), producing an enriched pool for the next round. After typically 5–15 rounds of increasing stringency, the pool is dominated by tight binders, which are then cloned or read out by high-throughput sequencing and characterised.

History and applications

The method was reported independently in 1990 by the laboratories of Larry Gold (Craig Tuerk and Gold, selecting RNA ligands for bacteriophage T4 DNA polymerase) and Jack Szostak (Andrew Ellington and Szostak, who coined the term "aptamer"). Aptamers generated by SELEX have been used as research reagents, biosensors, and affinity tools. The best-known clinical product is pegaptanib (Macugen), an aptamer against vascular endothelial growth factor approved in 2004 for treating wet age-related macular degeneration. Variants of the technique include cell-SELEX, performed against whole living cells, and capillary-electrophoresis SELEX, which can greatly reduce the number of rounds required.
