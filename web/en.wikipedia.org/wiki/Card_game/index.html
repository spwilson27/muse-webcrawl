Card game

Card games have been played worldwide for centuries, using many different decks.

A card game is any game using playing cards as the primary device with which the game is played, whether the cards are of a traditional design or specifically created for the game (proprietary). Countless card games exist, including families of related games such as poker. A small number of card games played with traditional decks have formally standardised rules and international tournaments, but most are folk games whose rules vary by region, culture and person.

History

Playing cards were invented in China, where paper was invented; card games are documented there by the ninth century, during the Tang dynasty. Cards travelled through the Islamic world — the Mamluk decks of Egypt introduced suit systems and court cards — and reached Europe by the late fourteenth century, where regional suit systems (Latin, German, French) developed. The French-suited 52-card deck, with suits of hearts, diamonds, clubs and spades, became the most widespread internationally.

Types of game

Card games are commonly classified by their central mechanism:

  • Trick-taking games (bridge, whist, spades, hearts): players each play a card to a "trick", which is won by the highest card of the suit led or a trump.
  • Matching and shedding games (uno, crazy eights, rummy family): players discard cards matching rank or suit, aiming to empty their hand.
  • Comparing games (poker, baccarat): hands are compared to determine a winner, usually with betting.
  • Banking and gambling games (blackjack): players bet against a house or banker.
  • Solitaire or patience games: single-player puzzles such as Klondike.
  • Collectible card games (Magic: The Gathering, Pokémon): players build decks from large pools of proprietary cards.

Rules and play

Most games proceed through dealing, turns of play in rotation, and the use of shuffling to randomise the deck. Direction of play, the identity of the dealer, and conventions about talking and signals vary by game. Because folk games rarely have official rulebooks, disputes are traditionally settled by local house rules — a fact that prompted Edmond Hoyle's eighteenth-century treatises and the phrase "according to Hoyle".
