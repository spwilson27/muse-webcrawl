Patent of Toleration

The Patent of Toleration (German: Toleranzpatent) was an edict issued on 13 October 1781 by the Holy Roman Emperor Joseph II of Austria, extending religious freedom to non-Catholic Christians living in the Habsburg lands. It was one of the most significant acts of Joseph II's program of enlightened reforms, collectively known as Josephinism, and it substantially reduced the privileged position of the Roman Catholic Church in the monarchy.

Provisions

The patent granted Lutherans, Calvinists (Reformed Christians), and Greek Orthodox Christians the right to practice their religions privately and, under conditions, publicly. Non-Catholics were permitted to build houses of worship, though these were initially required to have no steeple, bells, or street entrance — restrictions that were gradually relaxed. The edict also opened civil service positions, university admission, and many trades to non-Catholics, and removed requirements that converts to Catholicism swear oaths disavowing their former faith.

Related edicts

The Patent of Toleration formed part of a broader series of reforms. In 1782 Joseph issued a separate Edict of Tolerance for the Jews of Vienna and Lower Austria, loosening restrictions on Jewish residence, dress, and occupations, though it also imposed assimilationist measures such as requiring German-language records. Other reforms included the abolition of serfdom (1781), the dissolution of contemplative monastic orders, and state regulation of seminaries.

Significance

Although the patent stopped well short of full religious equality — Catholicism remained the only fully public religion, and conversion from Catholicism to another faith remained restricted — it marked a decisive step toward religious pluralism in Central Europe. Hundreds of new Protestant congregations were founded in the years after 1781, particularly in Bohemia, Moravia, and Hungary, where Protestantism had survived underground since the Counter-Reformation.
