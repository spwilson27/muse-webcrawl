X-ray image intensifier

An X-ray image intensifier (XRII) is an imaging component used in fluoroscopy that converts X-rays into a bright visible-light image. Invented in the late 1940s and commercialised in the 1950s, image intensifiers made real-time X-ray viewing practical at far lower radiation doses than direct fluorescent screens.

Construction and operation

X-rays emerging from the patient strike an input phosphor (typically caesium iodide), which converts them to light. A photocathode converts the light to electrons, which are accelerated across a vacuum tube by a high voltage (about 25–35 kV) and electrostatically focused onto a much smaller output phosphor. The acceleration and the minification of the image together produce a brightness gain of several thousand, yielding a bright image that can be viewed through optics or recorded by a camera.

Applications

Image intensifiers are the core of conventional fluoroscopes and C-arm systems used in angiography, interventional radiology, orthopaedic surgery and gastrointestinal studies. Common nominal input diameters range from about 15 cm to 40 cm, with multiple magnification modes.

Replacement by flat-panel detectors

Since the 2000s, solid-state flat-panel detectors have progressively replaced image intensifiers, offering freedom from geometric distortion (pincushion and vignetting artefacts), wider dynamic range and a more compact form — though intensifiers remain in widespread service.
