Western Marxism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Western Marxism is a label for Marxist theoretical currents that emerged in Western Europe after the First World War, characterized by a focus on philosophy, culture, and ideology rather than on political economy and revolutionary party organization. The term was popularized by Maurice Merleau-Ponty and later analyzed by Perry Anderson in his 1976 book Considerations on Western Marxism.

History

The tradition is usually dated to 1923, the year two foundational works appeared: Georg Lukács's History and Class Consciousness and Karl Korsch's Marxism and Philosophy. Antonio Gramsci, writing his Prison Notebooks under fascism, developed the theory of cultural hegemony. The Frankfurt School — including Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno and Herbert Marcuse — criticized mass culture, bureaucratic capitalism and authoritarianism, producing works such as Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947).

Western Marxism is distinguished from the official Marxism of the Communist International by its engagement with Hegelian philosophy, psychoanalysis and other currents of modern thought, and by its relative pessimism about working-class revolution in advanced capitalist societies. Later figures associated with the broad label include Jean-Paul Sartre, Louis Althusser and members of the Praxis School.
