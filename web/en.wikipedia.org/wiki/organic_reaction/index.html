Organic reaction

A generic organic reaction scheme.

Organic reactions are chemical reactions involving organic compounds. The basic organic chemistry reaction types are addition reactions, elimination reactions, substitution reactions, pericyclic reactions, rearrangement reactions, photochemical reactions and redox reactions. In organic synthesis, organic reactions are used in the construction of new organic molecules, and the production of many man-made chemicals — pharmaceuticals, plastics, food additives, and fabrics — depends on organic reactions.

The oldest organic reactions are combustion of organic fuels and saponification of fats to make soap. Modern organic chemistry starts with the Wöhler synthesis in 1828, in which Friedrich Wöhler prepared urea from inorganic materials, undermining the doctrine of vitalism.

Classification

Reactions are commonly classified by the change in the carbon skeleton: additions join two reactants into one product; eliminations remove a group to form a multiple bond; substitutions exchange one functional group for another; rearrangements shift the connectivity of the molecule itself. Alternatively, reactions can be classified by mechanism — polar (nucleophilic and electrophilic), radical, or pericyclic — and many reactions are known by the names of their discoverers, such as the Grignard reaction, the Diels–Alder reaction, and the Wittig reaction.

Mechanisms

Reaction mechanisms describe the step-by-step sequence of elementary steps by which reactants become products, including the movement of electrons, often drawn with curved ("curly") arrows. Intermediates such as carbocations, carbanions, radicals, and carbenes frequently appear in proposed mechanisms, which are supported by kinetic studies, isotopic labeling, and the stereochemical outcome of reactions.
