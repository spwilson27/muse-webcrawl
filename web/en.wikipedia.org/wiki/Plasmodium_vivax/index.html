Plasmodium vivax is a protozoal parasite and a human pathogen; it is one of the five Plasmodium species that commonly infect humans and cause malaria. P. vivax is the most geographically widespread human malaria parasite, dominant across Asia and the Americas, and is responsible for several million clinical cases each year. Though traditionally labelled "benign tertian malaria", vivax malaria can cause severe illness and death, and it relapses from dormant liver stages.

Life cycle

The parasite is transmitted by the bite of female Anopheles mosquitoes. Sporozoites infect liver cells; some develop at once, while others become dormant hypnozoites that may reactivate weeks, months or even years later to cause relapses — a feature shared with P. ovale and a major obstacle to elimination. In the blood, P. vivax preferentially invades young red blood cells (reticulocytes), using the Duffy blood-group antigen as its classic invasion receptor — although infections in Duffy-negative people, long thought impossible, are now documented in Africa. Infected red cells become enlarged and show Schüffner's dots; the fever paroxysms classically recur every 48 hours (tertian pattern).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by microscopy of blood films or rapid antigen tests. Blood-stage infection is treated with chloroquine (where still effective) or artemisinin combinations; the liver hypnozoites require a course of primaquine or single-dose tafenoquine ("radical cure"), which can cause haemolysis in people with G6PD deficiency and so require testing.

Epidemiology

P. vivax predominates in the Horn of Africa, South and Southeast Asia, Oceania and Latin America. Because of hypnozoite relapses, a large share of vivax cases in endemic areas come from relapses rather than new mosquito bites, making vivax harder to eliminate than falciparum malaria in the same settings.
