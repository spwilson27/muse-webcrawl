Music of the Philippines

The music of the Philippines reflects the country's successive waves of cultural influence: indigenous Austronesian traditions, more than three centuries of Spanish colonisation, American rule in the first half of the twentieth century, and contemporary global pop. Filipino musicians are renowned throughout Asia, and the country has produced both a rich folk repertoire and a distinctive popular music industry known as OPM (Original Pilipino Music).

Indigenous and folk music

Pre-colonial music centred on percussion ensembles, bamboo instruments, and vocal forms tied to ritual and work. The most famous ensemble is the kulintang, a set of horizontally laid knobbed gongs played by the Maranao, Maguindanao, and other peoples of Mindanao and the Sulu Archipelago. Other traditional instruments include the nose flute, the bamboo zither (kudyapi boat lute in its two-string form), and jew's harps. Vocal traditions include the epic chanting of the hud-hud of the Ifugao, recognised by UNESCO.

Spanish and American influence

Under Spanish rule, European harmony, church music, and instruments such as the guitar and the rondalla (string ensemble) took root. Forms like the kundiman — a melancholic art song of unrequited love — and the harana serenade became central to the classical Filipino repertoire. During the American period, jazz, swing, and later rock and roll arrived; after the Second World War, Filipino performers became fixtures in hotels and clubs across Asia.

Original Pilipino Music

In the 1970s, "Manila Sound" bands such as Hotdog blended disco and pop with Tagalog lyrics. The Original Pilipino Music (OPM) movement that followed produced stars including Freddie Aguilar, whose "Anak" (1978) became an international hit, the Apo Hiking Society, Ryan Cayabyab, and Lea Salonga, who won a Tony Award for Miss Saigon. In later decades, rock bands such as Eraserheads and Rivermaya, and today P-pop groups such as SB19, have carried Filipino music to global streaming audiences.
