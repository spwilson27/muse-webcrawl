The Ayutthaya Kingdom (Thai: อาณาจักรอยุธยา) was a Siamese kingdom in the Chao Phraya River basin of present-day Thailand that existed from 1351 to 1767. Founded by King U Thong (Ramathibodi I) on an island at the confluence of the Chao Phraya, Pa Sak, and Lopburi rivers, Ayutthaya grew into one of the largest and most cosmopolitan trading cities in the world, described by 17th-century visitors as a "Venice of the East". It was destroyed by Burmese invasion in 1767 after a 14-month siege.

History

Ayutthaya absorbed the Sukhothai Kingdom by the 15th century, reformed its administration under King Borommatrailokkanat, and codified law and court ritual influenced by the Khmer Empire. Wars with Burma punctuated its history: King Naresuan restored independence after the first fall to the Burmese Toungoo dynasty (1569–1584), famously defeating the Burmese crown prince at Nong Sarai in 1593. The 17th century saw flourishing foreign trade under kings such as Prasat Thong and Narai, who exchanged embassies with Louis XIV of France — an opening that ended with the Siamese revolution of 1688 and the expulsion of the French.

The kingdom operated as a mandala of tributary states rather than a territorially fixed state, exercising influence over Lanna, Lan Xang, Cambodia, and Malay sultanates of the peninsula. Internally, society was organised through the sakdina hierarchy of ranked dignities and corvée labour. After a period of dynastic stability under the Ban Phlu Luang dynasty, renewed Burmese invasion under the Konbaung dynasty culminated in the sack of Ayutthaya on 7 April 1767; the city was burned, its population deported, and its art and records largely destroyed. General Taksin reunified Siam from the new capital at Thonburi, succeeded by the Chakri dynasty at Bangkok.

Trade and foreign relations

Situated between China and India, Ayutthaya exported rice, deerskins, sappanwood, tin, and forest products, and hosted trading communities of Chinese, Persians, Indians, Japanese (including the adventurer Yamada Nagamasa), Portuguese, Dutch, English, and French merchants. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) and the French East India Company maintained major factories; foreign observers estimated the city's population at up to one million around 1700, comparable to London or Paris at the time.

Legacy

The ruins of the old city form the Ayutthaya Historical Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1991, with surviving prangs, chedis, and monasteries such as Wat Phra Si Sanphet, Wat Mahathat, and Wat Chaiwatthanaram. The modern city of Phra Nakhon Si Ayutthaya stands adjacent to the ruins.
