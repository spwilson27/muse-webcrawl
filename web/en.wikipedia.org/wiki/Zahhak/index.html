Zahhak or Zahāk (Persian: ضحّاک) is an evil figure in Persian mythology and folklore. In the Avestan texts he is the three-headed, serpent-like dragon Aži Dahāka, an agent of the evil spirit Angra Mainyu. In Ferdowsi's epic Shahnameh, Zahhak is transformed into a human tyrant of Arab descent, whose shoulders bear two serpents that must be fed daily on human brains.

In the Shahnameh

In the Shahnameh, Zahhak is the son of Merdās, an Arab king. Tempted by the devil Iblis, he murders his father and usurps the throne of Iran from the shah Jamshid, who had lost the divine glory (farr) through pride. Iblis, serving as Zahhak's cook, kisses the king's shoulders, and from each spot a black serpent grows; the serpents cannot be removed and demand a daily meal of the brains of two young men, so that two youths are executed each day.

Zahhak rules tyrannically for a thousand years. Eventually Kaveh the Blacksmith, whose children have been taken for the serpents, raises a revolt, lifting his leather apron as a banner (the Derafsh Kaviani). The hero Fereydun defeats Zahhak, but, warned by an angel not to kill him, chains him beneath Mount Damavand, where he remains imprisoned until the end of the world.

Earlier tradition

In the Zoroastrian Avesta, Aži Dahāka is a three-mouthed, three-headed, six-eyed dragon who sacrifices to obtain the power to depopulate the world and is slain or bound by the hero Thraētaona (Fereydun). Zahhak remains a byword for tyranny in Iranian culture, and his story has often been read as a symbol of foreign oppression and resistance.
