The American white pelican (Pelecanus erythrorhynchos) is a large aquatic soaring bird of the order Pelecaniformes. It breeds in interior North America, moving south and to the coasts, as far as Costa Rica, in winter. It is one of the largest North American birds, with a wingspan of up to about 3 metres, rivalled only by the California condor.

Description

The American white pelican rivals the trumpeter swan for the title of longest bird native to North America. Its plumage is almost entirely bright white, except the black primary and secondary remiges, which are visible only in flight. The bill is long and orange, with a pouch used for scooping fish. During the breeding season, adults grow a distinctive laterally flattened "horn" on the upper bill, which is shed after the eggs are laid.

Distribution and habitat

Colonies breed on isolated islands in shallow lakes across the interior of Canada and the northern and western United States. Wintering birds migrate to the Gulf Coast, California, Mexico, and Central America.

Behaviour

Unlike the brown pelican, the American white pelican does not dive for its food; instead, groups cooperate to herd fish into shallow water and scoop them up. They are strong, graceful soarers, often travelling long distances in V-formations and flapping in synchrony.

Conservation

Populations declined in the mid-20th century from habitat loss and pesticides but have recovered substantially; the species is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
