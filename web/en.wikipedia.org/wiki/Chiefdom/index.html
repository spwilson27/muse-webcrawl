Chiefdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A chiefdom is a political organization of a group of communities united under a hereditary chief, which anthropologists commonly place between egalitarian tribal societies and bureaucratic states in complexity. The term was developed in the mid-20th century by anthropologists such as Kalervo Oberg and Elman Service, whose typology of band, tribe, chiefdom and state became influential in political anthropology and archaeology.

Characteristics

Chiefdoms typically feature:

  • A permanent, hereditary office of chief, with authority over multiple villages
  • Ranked kinship, in which social status depends on genealogical closeness to the chiefly line
  • Redistribution of goods: produce flows to the chief's centre and is reallocated through feasting and patronage
  • Monumental or communal works (mounds, temples, irrigation) coordinated without a true bureaucracy
  • No formal, specialised coercive apparatus — the chief rules by prestige, kin obligation and religious sanction

Examples

Classic ethnographic examples include the Polynesian chiefdoms of Hawaii and Tonga, the Natchez of the lower Mississippi, and the Zulu under Shaka (often described as transitional to a state). Archaeologists have interpreted the Mississippian culture centre of Cahokia, the Hawaiian Islands' polities at European contact, and many Bronze Age societies of Europe and the Near East as chiefdoms.

Theory

Timothy Earle distinguished "simple" from "complex" chiefdoms and emphasised the economic bases of chiefly power — control of subsistence production, craft goods and warfare. Morton Fried, by contrast, treated chiefdoms as "ranked societies" and questioned whether the type is a natural evolutionary stage. Colin Renfrew proposed a distinction between group-oriented chiefdoms, marked by communal monuments, and individualizing chiefdoms, marked by prestige-goods exchange and rich burials.
