Articulatory phonetics

Articulatory phonetics
FieldLinguistics, phonetics
StudiesProduction of speech sounds by the vocal organs
Key conceptsPlace of articulation, manner of articulation, voicing, airstream mechanisms

Articulatory phonetics is the branch of phonetics concerned with how speech sounds are produced by the movements of the speech organs — the lungs, vocal folds, tongue, lips, jaw and other parts of the vocal tract. Together with acoustic phonetics (the study of the physical sound waves) and auditory phonetics (the study of perception), it forms one of the three main branches of the discipline.

The speech production process

Most speech begins with an airstream generated by the lungs (pulmonic egressive airflow). The air passes through the larynx, where the vocal folds may vibrate to produce voicing, and then through the pharynx, oral cavity and nasal cavity, whose shapes are modified by the articulators to produce the individual sounds of language. Some languages also use non-pulmonic airstream mechanisms, such as clicks, implosives and ejectives.

Articulators

Phoneticians distinguish active articulators, which move (typically the tongue and lower lip), from passive articulators, the fixed surfaces against which constrictions are made (the teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, velum and pharynx wall). The tongue is divided into tip, blade, front, back (dorsum) and root for descriptive purposes.

Describing consonants

Consonants are traditionally classified by three parameters:

  • Voicing — whether the vocal folds vibrate (e.g. [z] vs. [s]).
  • Place of articulation — where the constriction occurs: bilabial, labiodental, dental, alveolar, postalveolar, retroflex, palatal, velar, uvular, pharyngeal or glottal.
  • Manner of articulation — how the airstream is modified: plosive, nasal, trill, tap, fricative, approximant, lateral, and so on.

Describing vowels

Vowels are described by the position of the tongue (high–low, front–back), the shape of the lips (rounded or unrounded) and sometimes by tenseness and nasalisation. The resulting vowel space is represented on a quadrilateral diagram used in the International Phonetic Alphabet.

Methods of investigation

Modern articulatory phonetics uses instrumental techniques including palatography, electropalatography, ultrasound tongue imaging, magnetic resonance imaging of the vocal tract, electromagnetic articulography, and laryngoscopy, allowing direct observation of speech movements that earlier phoneticians could only infer.
