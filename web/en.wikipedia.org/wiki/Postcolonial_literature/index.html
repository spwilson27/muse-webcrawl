Postcolonial literature

Postcolonial literature
RegionAfrica, South Asia, the Caribbean, the Pacific, Latin America
Key authorsChinua Achebe, Salman Rushdie, Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o, Jamaica Kincaid
Key criticsEdward Said, Gayatri Spivak, Homi Bhabha

Postcolonial literature is the literature by people from formerly colonised countries, and more broadly writing that engages with the cultural, political and psychological legacies of colonialism and imperialism. It encompasses works produced during and after the period of European colonial rule, often written in the language of the former coloniser — English, French, Spanish or Portuguese — but reshaped by local languages, oral traditions and experience. Central concerns include identity, hybridity, language, displacement, and the recovery of histories suppressed by colonial narratives.

Postcolonial writing spans Africa, South Asia, the Caribbean and beyond.

Emergence

Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart (1958), written in response to colonial depictions of Africa such as Joseph Conrad's Heart of Darkness, is widely regarded as a founding text of modern African literature in English. In the Caribbean, writers such as George Lamming and later Derek Walcott and V. S. Naipaul examined the aftermath of slavery and plantation society. The wave of decolonisation in the 1950s and 1960s brought a flourishing of writing across Africa and Asia.

Theory

Postcolonial studies emerged as an academic field with Edward Said's Orientalism (1978), which argued that Western representations of the East served the project of empire. Homi K. Bhabha developed concepts such as hybridity, mimicry and the "third space"; Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) asked whether the voices of the most marginalised can be heard at all within dominant discourse. The study The Empire Writes Back (1989) by Bill Ashcroft, Gareth Griffiths and Helen Tiffin systematised the field.

Themes and debates

  • Language: Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o's Decolonising the Mind (1986) announced his abandonment of English for Gikuyu; Achebe, by contrast, defended a remade English.
  • Writing back: rewritings of canonical European texts, e.g. Jean Rhys's Wide Sargasso Sea (1966) answering Jane Eyre.
  • Hybridity and diaspora: Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children (1981) exemplifies the migrant, hybrid narration of the postcolonial nation.
  • Nation and memory: recovering suppressed histories and negotiating national identity after independence.

The term itself is contested: critics note that "postcolonial" can flatten very different histories and that colonial power relations did not simply end at independence.
