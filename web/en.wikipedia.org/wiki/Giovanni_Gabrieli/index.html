Giovanni Gabrieli

Born: c. 1554/1557, Venice, Republic of Venice

Died: August 12, 1612, Venice

Occupation: Composer, organist

Era: Venetian School, late Renaissance / early Baroque

Giovanni Gabrieli (c. 1554/1557 – August 12, 1612) was an Italian composer and organist. He was one of the most influential musicians of his era, representing the culmination of the Venetian School and the transition from Renaissance to Baroque style. His music for St Mark's Basilica exploited multiple separated choirs of voices and instruments (cori spezzati), dramatic dynamic contrasts, and increasingly idiomatic instrumental writing.

Life

Gabrieli was born in Venice and studied with his uncle, the composer Andrea Gabrieli. In the 1570s he spent several years in Munich at the court of Duke Albrecht V, working under Orlande de Lassus. After his uncle's death in 1585 he succeeded him as organist of St Mark's Basilica — one of the most prestigious musical posts in Europe — and as composer to the Scuola Grande di San Rocco. He held both positions until his death from kidney stones in 1612. His pupils included Heinrich Schütz, who carried the Venetian style to Germany.

Music

Gabrieli's great collections are the Sacrae symphoniae of 1597 and the posthumous Sacrae symphoniae II (1615) and Canzoni e sonate (1615). These contain motets for up to sixteen vocal and instrumental parts distributed among several choirs, as well as independent instrumental canzonas and sonatas for brass and string ensembles. His Sonata pian' e forte (1597) is famous as one of the first pieces of music to specify dynamics, and his In ecclesiis is a masterpiece of polychoral writing. Although he wrote relatively little purely instrumental music, his works were among the first to assign specific instruments to specific parts, shaping the development of the Baroque orchestra and the concerto.

Legacy

Through his publications and his foreign students, Gabrieli's style spread across Europe, particularly to Germany and Scandinavia. He bridged the polyphonic Renaissance motet and the concertante Baroque, and he remains, with his uncle Andrea and Claudio Monteverdi, a defining figure of Venetian music at its height.
