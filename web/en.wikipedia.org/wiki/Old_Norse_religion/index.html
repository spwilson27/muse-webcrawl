Old Norse religion

Old Norse religion, also known as Norse paganism, was the set of beliefs and practices followed by the Germanic peoples of Scandinavia during the Viking Age and earlier. Centered on a pantheon of gods — chief among them Odin, Thor, and Freyja — it encompassed ritual sacrifice (blót), seership and magic (seiðr), and a mythology preserved centuries later in the Icelandic Poetic Edda and Prose Edda.

Beliefs

The cosmos was structured around the world tree Yggdrasil and divided among the realms of gods, humans, giants, and the dead. The gods themselves were mortal in the long term: Norse eschatology culminated in Ragnarök, a prophesied battle in which many gods would die and the world be submerged and reborn. Fate, personified by the Norns, was a central concern, as were land spirits (landvættir) and ancestral veneration.

Practices

Worship took place at temples (hof), sacred groves, and household shrines. Seasonal blót festivals at midwinter, spring, and midsummer involved feasting and the sacrifice of animals — and, according to some medieval accounts, occasionally humans. The religion declined as Scandinavia was Christianized between the 10th and 12th centuries, though elements survived in folklore. Modern revivals, such as Ásatrú, draw on the medieval sources.
