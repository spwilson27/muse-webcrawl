Combined Bomber Offensive

USAAF B-17s over Europe.

The Combined Bomber Offensive (CBO) was the Allied strategic bombing campaign against Nazi Germany during the Second World War, conducted jointly by the British Royal Air Force Bomber Command and the United States Army Air Forces from 1943 to 1945. Agreed at the Casablanca Conference in January 1943 and elaborated in the Pointblank Directive, it combined round-the-clock bombing: the USAAF attacked specific industrial targets by day while the RAF bombed area targets by night.

Objectives and development

The Casablanca directive set the goal of the "progressive destruction and dislocation of the German military, industrial and economic system" and the undermining of morale. Pointblank prioritized the German aircraft industry, intending to draw the Luftwaffe into battles of attrition; by early 1944, long-range P-51 Mustang escorts allowed US bombers to force those battles, winning air superiority over Europe before D-Day. In 1944–45, priority shifted to oil production and transportation networks, with devastating effects on German fuel supply and logistics.

Results and debate

The offensive absorbed enormous resources on both sides and cost the lives of more than 50,000 RAF Bomber Command aircrew and tens of thousands of USAAF personnel, as well as hundreds of thousands of German civilians. Postwar surveys judged that bombing seriously disrupted German production and diverted vast resources to air defense, though German war production nonetheless expanded until late 1944. The campaign—particularly area bombing of cities such as Hamburg and Dresden—remains the subject of sustained historical and moral debate.
