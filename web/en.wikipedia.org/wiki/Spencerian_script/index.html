Spencerian script is a style of cursive handwriting developed by the American educator Platt Rogers Spencer (1800–1864). From roughly 1850 until about 1925 it was the dominant form of penmanship taught and used in the United States, particularly for business correspondence, bookkeeping, and formal documents, and it is sometimes called the Spencerian method or Spencerian penmanship.

History

Spencer, a teacher from Ohio, developed his system after studying the shapes found in nature — pebbles, waves, and branches — and sought a hand that combined beauty with speed of execution. In 1848 he published Spencer and Rice's System of Business and Ladies' Penmanship, and his copybooks, charts, and teacher-training "Spencerian colleges" spread the style across the country. After his death in 1864, his sons and publishers such as Ivison, Blakeman, Taylor & Co. continued to promote the system, and the name "Spencerian" became a byword for elegant American handwriting.

Characteristics

Spencerian script is written with a flexible pointed pen at a slant of about 52 degrees from the baseline. Its letterforms are oval-based and flowing, with a distinctive contrast between hairline upstrokes and shaded downstrokes produced by varying pen pressure. Capital letters are large, ornate, and looped, while the lowercase letters are comparatively small and rounded. Highly skilled writers elaborated the system into "ornamental penmanship," adding flourishes such as stylized birds and scrollwork.

Decline

From the 1890s the simpler, more upright Palmer Method, which emphasized arm movement and plain, rapid letterforms, displaced Spencerian writing in American schools. The spread of the typewriter further reduced the commercial importance of fine penmanship, and by the mid-1920s Spencerian script had largely fallen out of everyday use.

Legacy

The Spencerian tradition survives most visibly in famous American logotypes: the Coca-Cola Spencerian-style wordmark (designed in the 1880s by Frank Mason Robinson) and the Ford Motor Company script logo are both modelled on the hand. Spencerian copybooks are still reprinted for calligraphers, and the style remains a foundation of modern pointed-pen calligraphy.
