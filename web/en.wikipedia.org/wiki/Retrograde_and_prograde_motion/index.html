Retrograde and prograde motion

Retrograde motion in astronomy is, in general, orbital or rotational motion of an object in the direction opposite the rotation of its primary — that is, the central object (right-hand figure). Prograde or direct motion is motion in the same direction as the primary rotates. In the Solar System, the orbits and rotations of most planets and asteroids are prograde, counterclockwise as seen from above the Sun's north pole.

Apparent retrograde motion

The term also describes the apparent backward movement of a planet against the background stars as observed from Earth. This apparent retrograde motion is an illusion caused by the relative orbital positions and speeds of Earth and the planet: as Earth overtakes an outer planet, the planet appears to slow, stop, and briefly move westward before resuming its eastward course. Explaining this phenomenon was a central problem of ancient astronomy, addressed with epicycles in the Ptolemaic system and naturally accounted for by the heliocentric model of Copernicus.

True retrograde motion

Examples of genuine retrograde motion include Venus, which rotates retrograde (so the Sun rises in the west on Venus), and Uranus, whose axial tilt of about 98° makes its rotation retrograde. Several moons, notably Neptune's Triton, orbit their planets retrograde, which suggests they are captured bodies; many irregular moons of Jupiter and Saturn are also retrograde. Retrograde orbits are comparatively rare in the Solar System and are a clue to the history of gravitational interactions and captures.
