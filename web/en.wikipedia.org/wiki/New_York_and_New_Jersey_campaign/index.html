The New York and New Jersey campaign was a series of battles in 1776 and the winter months of 1777 during the American Revolutionary War. British forces under General Sir William Howe, supported by a fleet under his brother Admiral Lord Richard Howe, sought to seize New York City and split the rebellious colonies. George Washington's Continental Army suffered a string of defeats around New York — at Long Island, Harlem Heights (a partial success), White Plains and Fort Washington — and retreated across New Jersey into Pennsylvania. With his army dwindling and the revolutionary cause near collapse, Washington counter-attacked, winning the Battle of Trenton on 26 December 1776 and the Battle of Princeton on 3 January 1777, which restored American morale and forced the British to consolidate their positions.

Background

After the British evacuated Boston in March 1776, Washington correctly predicted that New York — strategically placed at the mouth of the Hudson River — would be Howe's next objective. The Continental Army fortified Brooklyn Heights and Manhattan, but it was outnumbered, inexperienced and faced an enemy with total command of the sea. In July 1776 British forces landed unopposed on Staten Island, eventually assembling some 32,000 troops, including German auxiliaries, the largest expeditionary force Britain had yet sent overseas.

The New York battles

On 27 August 1776, in the Battle of Long Island (Battle of Brooklyn), Howe outflanked the American position through the unguarded Jamaica Pass and inflicted a heavy defeat. Only a skilful night evacuation across the East River to Manhattan, aided by fog, saved Washington's army. In September the British landed at Kip's Bay and occupied New York City, which was partly destroyed by a great fire days later. After a small American success at Harlem Heights, Howe again outmanoeuvred Washington at White Plains (28 October), and on 16 November Fort Washington on Manhattan fell with nearly 3,000 prisoners — the worst American loss of the campaign. Fort Lee across the Hudson was abandoned days later.

Retreat and counterstroke

Washington retreated across New Jersey, pursued by Lord Cornwallis, crossing the Delaware River into Pennsylvania in December while collecting or destroying boats to delay pursuit. Enlistments were expiring and Congress had fled Philadelphia for Baltimore. Thomas Paine's pamphlet The American Crisis ("These are the times that try men's souls") appeared on 19 December. On Christmas night Washington recrossed the ice-choked Delaware and at dawn on 26 December surprised and defeated a Hessian garrison at Trenton, taking some 900 prisoners. Returning to Trenton, he then slipped around Cornwallis's army on the night of 2–3 January and defeated British forces at Princeton, where General Hugh Mercer was killed. Washington then took winter quarters at Morristown, while the British abandoned most of their New Jersey outposts — in the "Forage War" that followed, American militia harassed British foraging parties through the winter.

Significance

The campaign demonstrated that Britain could occupy cities but not easily destroy the Continental Army or control the countryside. The twin victories at Trenton and Princeton, though small, transformed the political and psychological situation, reviving recruitment and foreign confidence in the American cause.
