Embodied cognition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Embodied cognition is the concept suggesting that many features of cognition, whether human or otherwise, are shaped by aspects of an organism's entire body. The embodied mind thesis challenges other theories, such as cognitivism, computationalism, and Cartesian dualism.

Embodied cognition is closely related to the extended mind thesis, situated cognition, and enactivism — fields collectively grouped as the "4E" approaches to cognition (embodied, embedded, enacted, extended) that stand in contrast to the classical view of the mind as a computer manipulating abstract symbols.

Core claims

Proponents argue that cognitive processes are deeply rooted in the body's interactions with the world: perception, concepts, memory and even abstract reasoning rely on modal simulations in the brain's sensory and motor systems. For example, understanding the concept of "grasping" partly reactivates the motor areas used in actually grasping an object.

Historical roots

Ideas anticipating embodied cognition appear in the work of philosophers such as Maurice Merleau-Ponty, whose phenomenology emphasised the body as the ground of perception, and in J. J. Gibson's ecological theory of perception with its notion of affordances. In the 1980s and 1990s the approach gained force through work in robotics (Rodney Brooks), linguistics (George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory) and psychology.

Evidence and criticism

Experiments often cited include studies showing that bodily states influence social judgements and that motor interference disrupts language comprehension for action words. Critics contend that some prominent embodied effects have proven difficult to replicate, and that strong versions of the thesis struggle to explain offline cognition such as planning and mental arithmetic; others regard embodiment as a useful research programme rather than a single falsifiable theory.

Applications

The perspective has influenced human–computer interaction, education, robotics and artificial intelligence, where morphological computation — letting the body's physical structure do part of the "processing" — is used to simplify control problems.
