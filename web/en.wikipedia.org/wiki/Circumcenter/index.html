Circumcenter

In Euclidean geometry, the circumcenter of a triangle is the point where the three perpendicular bisectors of the triangle's sides meet. It is the centre of the circumcircle, the unique circle passing through all three vertices, and its distance to each vertex is the circumradius, R.

Properties

The circumcenter lies inside the triangle if the triangle is acute, at the midpoint of the hypotenuse if it is right-angled, and outside the triangle if it is obtuse. It is equidistant not only from the vertices but also satisfies central relations with other triangle centres: on the Euler line, the circumcenter O, the centroid G and the orthocenter H are collinear, with HG = 2GO.

Coordinates

In barycentric coordinates the circumcenter is a2 : b2 : c2, equivalently sin 2A : sin 2B : sin 2C, where a, b, c are the side lengths. In trilinear coordinates it is cos A : cos B : cos C. Cartesian coordinates can be computed by solving the linear system formed by two perpendicular-bisector equations.

Relations

The circumradius obeys R = abc / (4Δ), where Δ is the triangle's area, and by the law of sines each side satisfies a = 2R sin A. Euler's formula for the distance d between the circumcenter and the incenter states d2 = R(R − 2r), implying the inequality R ≥ 2r. The circumcenter is the orthocenter of the medial triangle, and its reflection of the orthocenter across each side lies on the circumcircle.

Generalizations

For polygons, a circumcenter exists only if the polygon is cyclic (all vertices concyclic). For tetrahedra and higher-dimensional simplices an analogous circumscribed sphere always exists, whose center is the intersection of the perpendicular bisector planes. In computational geometry, the empty-circumcircle property of circumcenters defines Delaunay triangulations and Voronoi diagrams.
