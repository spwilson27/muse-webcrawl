A crystal radio (or crystal set) is a very simple radio receiver, popular in the early days of radio broadcasting. It uses no battery or mains power: the energy of the received radio waves alone drives a sensitive earphone. Its detector is a crystal of a semiconducting mineral, classically galena, touched by a fine wire called a "cat's whisker".

How it works

A long wire antenna collects radio signals; an LC tuned circuit (a coil and, in later sets, a variable capacitor) selects one station; the crystal detector rectifies the amplitude-modulated signal, recovering the audio; and a high-impedance magnetic earphone converts it to sound. Because there is no amplification, only strong, usually local, stations can be heard, and selectivity is poor.

History

Jagadish Chandra Bose used semiconductor junctions for radio detection in the 1890s; Greenleaf Whittier Pickard patented the silicon crystal detector in 1906. Crystal sets brought broadcasting to a mass audience in the early 1920s because they were cheap and easy to build at home. They were displaced by powered vacuum-tube (and later transistor) radios, but remained popular as a hobby and educational project, and were famously improvised as "foxhole radios" by soldiers in the Second World War using a razor blade as the detector. Crystal radios are still built today and sold as kits; a modern diode usually replaces the temperamental cat's whisker.
