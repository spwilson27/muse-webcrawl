Lead mining is the extraction of lead ores, chiefly galena (lead sulfide), from the earth. Lead has been mined for more than 6,000 years — early workings are known from Anatolia and the Aegean — and was produced on a large scale by the Romans in Britain, Spain and Greece, often as a by-product of silver extraction from argentiferous galena.

Today most lead is recovered together with zinc, silver and copper from polymetallic deposits. China is the world's largest producer of mined lead, followed by Australia and the United States, where the Missouri Lead Belt (the "Lead Belt" and the Viburnum Trend) has been a dominant source since the 18th century.

Methods

Lead ores are typically mined underground by room-and-pillar or cut-and-fill methods, though some deposits are worked in open pits. Crushed ore is concentrated by froth flotation to produce a galena concentrate of 50–70% lead, which is then smelted — traditionally in a blast furnace after sintering — and refined. Because much of the demand for lead (chiefly for lead-acid batteries) is met by recycling, secondary production accounts for a majority of supply in many developed countries.

History

In Britain, lead mining flourished in the Mendips, Derbyshire (the Peak District), the Yorkshire Dales and the North Pennines; Derbyshire's industry was governed by distinctive customary law administered through the barmote courts. In the United States, French colonists mined lead in southeast Missouri from the 1720s, and the Tri-State district (Missouri–Kansas–Oklahoma) and Idaho's Coeur d'Alene district became major 19th- and 20th-century producers.

Environmental and health issues

Lead is highly toxic, and mining and smelting districts have left legacies of contaminated soil and water, as at Broken Hill (Australia), Kabwe (Zambia), and Tar Creek (Oklahoma). Modern operations are subject to controls on dust, effluent and tailings, and occupational exposure limits.
