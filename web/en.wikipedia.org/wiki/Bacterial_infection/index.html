Bacterial infection

A bacterial infection is the proliferation of a harmful strain of bacteria on or inside the body. Bacteria can infect any area of the body, including the skin, lungs, urinary tract, gut, blood and brain. Pneumonia, meningitis, tuberculosis, food poisoning and urinary tract infections are examples of illnesses caused by bacterial infection. Local infection causes localised inflammation, while infection in the bloodstream (bacteraemia) can progress to the life-threatening condition sepsis.

Cause and classification

Pathogenic bacteria cause disease through mechanisms such as producing toxins (for example the cholera and tetanus toxins), invading tissues, and provoking damaging immune responses. Many bacteria, such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, are opportunistic pathogens: they normally live on or in the body harmlessly and cause disease only when defences are breached. Bacteria are classified for clinical purposes by shape, by Gram staining (Gram-positive or Gram-negative) and by oxygen requirement.

Treatment and prevention

Bacterial infections are treated with antibiotics, whose effectiveness is increasingly threatened by antimicrobial resistance. Diagnosis may involve culture, microscopy, antigen tests or molecular methods such as PCR. Prevention includes vaccination (for example against pneumococcus, meningococcus and pertussis), hygiene and sanitation, and safe food handling.
