The Count of Flanders was the ruler of the County of Flanders, one of the most powerful and wealthy principalities of medieval Europe, occupying coastal territory in what is now Belgium, northern France and the southwestern Netherlands. The title was created in the 9th century and was borne successively by the Houses of Flanders, Dampierre, Burgundy, Habsburg and others.

Origins

The county originated in 862, when Charles the Bald granted the frontier march of Flanders to his son-in-law Baldwin I, nicknamed "Iron Arm". His descendants expanded the county southward into Artois and eastward toward the Scheldt, making Flanders a formidable buffer between France and the Empire. The counts were nominally vassals of the French crown for "Crown Flanders" west of the Scheldt, and of the Holy Roman Emperor for "Imperial Flanders" to the east.

Height of power

By the 12th and 13th centuries the Flemish cities of Ghent, Bruges and Ypres were among the largest and richest in northern Europe, based on the cloth trade with England. The counts' power was contested both by the French crown — defeated by Flemish urban militias at the Battle of the Golden Spurs in 1302 — and by their own cities, which repeatedly revolted. Famous counts include Baldwin IX, who became Latin Emperor of Constantinople in 1204, and Margaret II, whose inheritance disputes led to the War of the Succession of Flanders and Hainaut.

Later history

In 1384 Flanders passed by marriage to the Dukes of Burgundy, becoming a core province of the Burgundian Netherlands, and then to the Habsburgs. Under Spanish and later Austrian rule the county survived as an administrative unit of the Southern Netherlands until the French revolutionary annexation of the 1790s. The courtesy title is still reflected in the style of the Belgian royal family: the heir to the Belgian throne traditionally held the title Count of Flanders in earlier generations.
