Aromaticity

In organic chemistry, aromaticity is a property of cyclic, planar, conjugated molecular structures in which the delocalisation of π electrons over the ring confers unusual stability and characteristic reactivity. Benzene is the prototype: despite its formal alternating single and double bonds, all its carbon–carbon bonds are equal in length, and it undergoes substitution rather than the addition reactions typical of alkenes.

CriteriaCyclic, planar, fully conjugated ring; (4n + 2) π electrons (Hückel's rule)
PrototypeBenzene (6 π electrons)
ConsequencesExtra stability; equalised bond lengths; ring current in NMR; substitution chemistry
OppositeAntiaromaticity (4n π electrons, destabilised)

History

The term derives from the fragrant ("aromatic") natural products — benzaldehyde, toluene, and their relatives — among which the property was first recognised. In 1865 August Kekulé proposed the ring structure of benzene; the mystery of its stability and bond equalisation was explained with the advent of quantum mechanics and resonance theory in the 1930s (Linus Pauling), and Erich Hückel's molecular-orbital treatment (1931) produced the celebrated (4n + 2) rule: planar monocyclic conjugated systems with 4n + 2 π electrons are aromatic.

Criteria and evidence

A compound is generally considered aromatic if its ring is cyclic and planar, every ring atom contributes a p orbital to a continuously overlapping conjugated system, and the π-electron count obeys Hückel's rule. Experimental signatures include heats of hydrogenation much lower than expected for isolated double bonds (benzene's "resonance energy" is about 150 kJ/mol), equalised bond lengths from X-ray crystallography, and a diamagnetic ring current that shifts the NMR signals of protons outside the ring strongly downfield. Aromatic systems include heterocycles such as pyridine, pyrrole, furan, and thiophene; charged species such as the cyclopentadienyl anion and the tropylium cation; and polycyclic hydrocarbons such as naphthalene and anthracene.

Antiaromaticity and non-aromaticity

Planar conjugated rings with 4n π electrons, such as cyclobutadiene, are antiaromatic — destabilised by conjugation — and distort or react rapidly to escape that state. Rings that fail the planarity or conjugation criteria (for example cyclooctatetraene, which adopts a tub shape) are simply non-aromatic. Extensions of the concept include Möbius aromaticity (4n electrons in a twisted topology), homoaromaticity, σ-aromaticity, and aromaticity in excited states (Baird's rule).

Importance

Aromatic compounds are central to chemistry and life: the DNA bases, many amino acids and cofactors, most pharmaceuticals, dyes, and bulk chemicals such as styrene and phenol are aromatic. Understanding aromaticity guides synthesis, predicts reactivity (for example in electrophilic aromatic substitution), and underpins materials such as conducting polymers and fullerenes.
