Deixis

In linguistics, deixis is the use of words and phrases whose meaning depends on the context in which they are uttered — who is speaking, and where and when the utterance occurs. Deictic expressions include personal pronouns (I, you), demonstratives (this, that), spatial adverbs (here, there), temporal expressions (now, tomorrow) and phrases such as the following day. The word derives from the Greek deixis, "pointing" or "indicating".

The deictic centre: speaker, place and time of utterance

Categories

Following Karl Bühler and later Charles Fillmore and Stephen Levinson, deixis is usually divided into person deixis (the encoding of speaker, addressee and others: I, you, he), place or spatial deixis (here, there, this, that, and verbs such as come and go), and time deixis (now, then, yesterday, tense). Additional categories include discourse deixis, referring to parts of the discourse itself (in the next chapter), and social deixis, encoding relative social status in honorifics, pronouns of address and similar forms.

The deictic centre

Deictic expressions are interpreted relative to a deictic centre — typically the speaker at the time and place of speaking. In reported speech and narrative, the centre may shift ("deictic projection"), as when a narrator adopts a character's viewpoint. Languages differ in how finely they divide deictic space: some distinguish three distances (near speaker, near addressee, remote), and visibility, elevation and riverine direction can also be encoded.

Significance

Deixis is a central topic in pragmatics and the philosophy of language, where indexicals — Kaplan's "directly referential" expressions — raise questions about how context contributes to what is said. Children's acquisition of deictic terms, and the difficulties of deictic shift for language learners and for machines, make deixis important in psycholinguistics and natural language processing alike.
