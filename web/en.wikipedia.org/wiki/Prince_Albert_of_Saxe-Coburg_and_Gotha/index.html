Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha (Franz August Karl Albert Emanuel; 26 August 1819 – 14 December 1861), later the Prince Consort, was the husband of Queen Victoria of the United Kingdom. Born in the Saxon duchy of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha, he married his first cousin Victoria in 1840; over 21 years of marriage they had nine children, whose marriages into Europe's royal houses made Victoria "the grandmother of Europe" — and spread haemophilia through several dynasties.

Role and legacy

Initially excluded from affairs of state, Albert became Victoria's indispensable private secretary and, in effect, co-monarch: he professionalised the royal household, championed education, science and industry, and masterminded the Great Exhibition of 1851 in the Crystal Palace — a triumph whose profits bought the South Kensington museum district (the Victoria and Albert Museum, Science Museum and Natural History Museum, on land Londoners nicknamed "Albertopolis"). He modernised Cambridge University as its chancellor, pushed army reform, and played a quiet diplomatic role, notably softening a dangerous dispatch during the American Civil War's Trent affair in 1861. His death that December — aged 42, officially of typhoid fever — shattered Victoria, who wore mourning black for her remaining 40 years and withdrew from public life for a decade, damaging the monarchy Albert had laboured to make respectable. The Albert Memorial and Royal Albert Hall in London are his monuments.
