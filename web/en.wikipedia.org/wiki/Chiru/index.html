Chiru

Scientific name: Pantholops hodgsonii

Also called: Tibetan antelope

Conservation status: Near Threatened (IUCN)

The chiru (Pantholops hodgsonii), also called the Tibetan antelope, is a medium-sized bovid native to the Tibetan Plateau. It is the sole member of the genus Pantholops. Males bear long, slender, nearly vertical horns and distinctive dark markings on the face and legs; females are hornless.

Habitat and behaviour

Chiru inhabit high alpine steppe and semi-desert at elevations of roughly 3,700–5,500 m across the Changtang region of Tibet, Qinghai and Xinjiang, with a small population in Ladakh. They feed on grasses, forbs and sedges. Females undertake long seasonal migrations to traditional calving grounds, while males remain in separate herds outside the rutting season in winter.

Shahtoosh and poaching

The chiru's underfur is the source of shahtoosh, an exceptionally fine wool woven into luxury shawls. Because the animals cannot be shorn like domestic stock, they were historically killed for the fibre, and poaching drove the population down from perhaps a million in the early 20th century to around 65,000–72,500 by the 1990s. International trade in shahtoosh is banned under CITES, and intensive anti-poaching patrols in reserves such as Kekexili (Hoh Xil) helped numbers recover to over 200,000, leading the IUCN to downlist the species from Endangered to Near Threatened in 2016.

Cultural note

The chiru, named Yinggeling, was one of the five Fuwa mascots of the 2008 Beijing Olympic Games, symbolising the Olympic Green Ring and the Tibetan Plateau's wildlife.
