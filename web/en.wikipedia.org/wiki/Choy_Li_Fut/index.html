Choy Li Fut (Cantonese: 蔡李佛; Mandarin: Cai Li Fo) is a Chinese martial art founded in 1836 by Chan Heung in King Mui village, Xinhui, Guangdong. It combines techniques drawn from three family styles Chan Heung studied — Choy Gar, known for powerful low stances and palm strikes; Li Gar; and Fut Gar, a Shaolin temple style with Buddhist roots — whose names he joined in tribute to his teachers and to the Buddha (Fut).

History

Chan Heung (1806–1875) first trained under his uncle Chan Yuen-wu in Choy Gar, then under Li Yau-san of Li Gar, and finally under the Shaolin monk Choy Fook, who had withdrawn to a mountain hermitage. Synthesizing these teachings, Chan Heung opened his first school in 1836. The style spread rapidly through Guangdong and overseas Chinese communities; Chan Heung's disciple Cheung Hung-sing (Jeong Yim) founded the influential Hung Sing branch in Foshan (Fut San), which became famous for its association with anti-Qing activism and later with Wong Fei-hung-era martial culture. Today the principal branches include King Mui (the original line), Hung Sing, and Buk Sing, the last developed by Tam Sam and noted for its emphasis on the choy lay fut application in fighting and on the whipping back-fist (gua chui) and other long-bridge techniques.

Techniques and training

Choy Li Fut is known for long, powerful, circular arm movements that generate force from the waist and stance, combined with Northern-style kicks — a blend summarized in the saying "hands from the South, legs from the North." Signature techniques include:

  • Gwa chui — whipping back-fist
  • Sau chui — sweeping horizontal fist
  • Chop chui — thrusting/knuckle punch
  • Pau chui — leopard fist uppercut-like strike
  • Kum jerng — pressing palm
  • Clawing and grabbing hands drawn from Fut Gar, and low stable stances

The curriculum contains an unusually large number of forms — by some counts over 150 hand, weapon, and dummy sets across the branches — along with lion dance, which Choy Li Fut schools traditionally perform. Weapons include the staff, broadsword, spear, butterfly swords, and the bench. The system also preserves two-person sets and wooden-dummy forms influenced by Shaolin practice.

Modern practice

Choy Li Fut is one of the most widely practised southern Chinese styles worldwide, with schools across North America, Europe, and Southeast Asia, promoted by organizations descending from the King Mui, Hung Sing, and Buk Sing lineages. Its fighters have competed in full-contact lei tai and sanda events, and the style features prominently in demonstrations and kung fu cinema.
