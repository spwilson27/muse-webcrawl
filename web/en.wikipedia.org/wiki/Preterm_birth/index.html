Preterm birth
DefinitionBirth before 37 completed weeks of gestation
Frequency~13.4 million births per year (~1 in 10 worldwide)
ComplicationsRespiratory distress, cerebral palsy, developmental delay, vision and hearing problems
Risk factorsPrevious preterm birth, multiple pregnancy, infection, pre-eclampsia, smoking, short cervix

Preterm birth, also known as premature birth, is the birth of a baby at fewer than 37 weeks' gestational age, as opposed to a full-term birth at around 40 weeks. Preterm birth is the leading cause of death in children under five worldwide. About 13.4 million babies—roughly one in ten—are born preterm each year, and rates have changed little in recent decades.

Very preterm infants are cared for in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), often in incubators with respiratory support.

Classification

By gestational age, preterm births are classified as extremely preterm (before 28 weeks), very preterm (28 to under 32 weeks) and moderate-to-late preterm (32 to under 37 weeks). Most preterm births are late preterm. Preterm birth may be spontaneous (preterm labour or preterm prelabour rupture of membranes) or provider-initiated (induction or caesarean delivery for maternal or fetal indications such as pre-eclampsia).

Causes and risk factors

The causes of spontaneous preterm birth are incompletely understood and likely multifactorial: intrauterine infection and inflammation, placental problems, uterine overdistension (as in multiple pregnancy or polyhydramnios), cervical insufficiency and maternal stress all play roles. The strongest risk factor is a previous preterm birth. Others include multiple gestation, short cervix, smoking, low or high maternal age, low body-mass index, and social deprivation.

Outcomes and management

Babies born preterm face higher risks of respiratory distress syndrome (from surfactant deficiency), intraventricular haemorrhage, necrotising enterocolitis, sepsis, retinopathy of prematurity, cerebral palsy and learning difficulties; risks increase steeply with decreasing gestation. Survival has improved dramatically in high-income settings, with most infants born at 28 weeks now surviving, though outcomes vary globally. Management includes antenatal corticosteroids to accelerate fetal lung maturity, magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection, antibiotics where infection or membrane rupture is present, tocolytic drugs to delay labour briefly, and specialised neonatal care including thermal support, nutrition and kangaroo (skin-to-skin) care. Prevention strategies for women at high risk include vaginal progesterone and cervical cerclage.
