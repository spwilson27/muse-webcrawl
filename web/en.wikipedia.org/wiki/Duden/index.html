Duden

The Duden orthography volume, in its familiar yellow binding

The Duden is a series of reference works on the German language, named after the schoolmaster Konrad Duden (1829–1911), whose Vollständiges Orthographisches Wörterbuch der deutschen Sprache ("Complete Orthographical Dictionary of the German Language") appeared in 1880 and laid the foundation for a unified German spelling. The Duden spelling dictionary (volume 1, traditionally in a distinctive yellow cover) is the preeminent authority on German orthography and is published by the Dudenverlag in Berlin.

History and authority

Konrad Duden compiled his dictionary while a headmaster in Schleiz and Hersfeld; the 1880 volume listed some 28,000 words with consistent spellings. In 1901–1902 the Duden's norms were adopted as the basis of official German orthography at the Berlin Orthographic Conference, cementing the book's quasi-official status. That status was formally diluted by the German spelling reform of 1996, which transferred authority to the Council for German Orthography (Rat für deutsche Rechtschreibung), but the Duden remains the most consulted record of German spelling, pronunciation, grammar and usage. From 1956 to 1996, two competing Duden traditions existed — the Leipzig (East German) and Mannheim (West German) editions.

The series

The modern Duden comprises twelve volumes, covering spelling, style, grammar, pronunciation, synonyms, quotations and proverbs, etymology, a picture dictionary, and foreign words, among others; the spelling volume's 28th edition (2020) contains about 148,000 headwords. New words added to the dictionary — from political terms to borrowings and crisis vocabulary — regularly attract press attention in the German-speaking world as an index of cultural change.
