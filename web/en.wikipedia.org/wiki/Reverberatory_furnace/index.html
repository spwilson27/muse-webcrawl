Reverberatory furnace

Reverberatory furnace
TypeMetallurgical furnace
UsesSmelting and refining of copper, tin, lead, aluminium; calcining; glass melting (historical)

A reverberatory furnace is a metallurgical or process furnace that isolates the material being processed from contact with the fuel but not from contact with combustion gases. The term reverberation derives from the way heat is reflected ("reverberated") from the furnace's low, arched roof down onto the material on the hearth below.

Design

In a reverberatory furnace, fuel is burned in a separate firebox at one end. The flame and hot gases pass over a low bridge wall and across the shallow hearth containing the charge; the vaulted roof deflects the flame downward so that the charge is heated by direct radiation from the roof as well as by the passing gases. Exhaust gases leave through a flue at the opposite end. The design keeps ash and fuel contaminants out of the melt while still exposing the charge to the furnace atmosphere, which can be controlled to be oxidising or reducing.

History

Reverberatory furnaces emerged in the seventeenth century, notably in the smelting of lead and later copper, where they allowed coal—which was cheaper than charcoal but contaminated metal on contact—to replace charcoal. The design spread widely during the Industrial Revolution. Henry Cort's puddling process (1784) for making wrought iron employed a reverberatory furnace, in which the pig iron was stirred ("puddled") in the oxidising flame without touching the coal fire.

Applications

  • Copper smelting and refining — historically the principal application; large reverberatory furnaces roasted and melted concentrates and refined blister copper.
  • Aluminium remelting — reverberatory furnaces remain standard for melting scrap and holding molten aluminium.
  • Lead and tin smelting — used where a gentle, oxidising heat is wanted.
  • Calcining and roasting of ores and minerals.

In primary copper smelting, reverberatory furnaces have largely been displaced by flash smelting and other more energy-efficient technologies, but they persist in secondary metal recovery and aluminium processing.

Comparison with other furnaces

Unlike a blast furnace, where fuel and charge are mixed, and unlike a crucible furnace, where the charge is sealed away from furnace gases entirely, the reverberatory furnace places the charge in contact with combustion gases only. Its chief drawbacks are relatively poor thermal efficiency and difficulty in achieving very high temperatures.
