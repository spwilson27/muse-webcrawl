Overview

The Protocols of the Elders of Zion is an antisemitic fabricated text purporting to describe a Jewish plan for global domination. First published in Russia in 1903 and popularized in a 1905 edition by Sergei Nilus, the text is structured as a series of protocols or minutes of meetings of Jewish leaders plotting to control the press, economies, and governments of the world.

The Protocols has been conclusively demonstrated to be a forgery. In 1921, The Times of London showed that large portions were plagiarized from Maurice Joly's 1864 satire Dialogue in Hell Between Machiavelli and Montesquieu, a political work that did not mention Jews, with additional material drawn from Hermann Goedsche's 1868 novel Biarritz.

History

The text first appeared in serialized form in the St. Petersburg newspaper Znamya in 1903. Nilus included it as an appendix to his 1905 book, claiming it documented a conspiracy revealed at the First Zionist Congress, a claim with no basis in fact. After the Russian Revolution, the text spread internationally, promoted by White Russian émigrés and, in the United States, by Henry Ford's newspaper The Dearborn Independent in the 1920s.

In Nazi Germany the Protocols were taught in schools and used as propaganda justifying persecution of Jews. Courts in Switzerland (1934-35) and South Africa ruled the text a forgery. Despite repeated debunking, the Protocols continue to circulate and are widely regarded by historians as one of the most influential works of antisemitic propaganda ever produced.

Scholarly assessment

Scholars classify the Protocols as a work of antisemitic forgery belonging to a long tradition of conspiracy literature. The United States Senate issued a report in 1964 condemning it as fabricated. Historians stress that no credible evidence has ever supported its authenticity.
