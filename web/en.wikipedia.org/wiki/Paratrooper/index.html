Paratrooper

RoleAirborne infantry
DeliveryStatic-line or freefall parachute jump from aircraft
First combat jumps1930s (Soviet Union); large-scale use in the Second World War
Famous unitsUS 82nd and 101st Airborne, British Parachute Regiment, German Fallschirmjäger

A paratrooper or military parachutist is a soldier trained to parachute from aircraft into an operational area, typically as part of an airborne forces unit. Paratroopers are used to seize key objectives, such as airfields or bridges, deep behind enemy lines, exploiting the element of surprise. Because they may be dropped in dispersion and must fight without immediate support, paratroopers are usually volunteer, elite light infantry with intensive training.

Paratroopers exit a transport aircraft during a mass tactical jump.

History

Military parachuting was pioneered in the 1930s by the Soviet Union, which staged large airborne exercises, and by Italy and Germany. German Fallschirmjäger made the first major combat jumps in 1940, in Norway, the Netherlands, and Belgium (including the glider-and-parachute seizure of Fort Eben-Emael), and in the costly airborne invasion of Crete in 1941. The Western Allies built large airborne armies: the American 82nd and 101st Airborne Divisions and the British 1st and 6th Airborne Divisions jumped in Sicily, Normandy (D-Day, 6 June 1944), the Netherlands (Operation Market Garden), and across the Rhine (Operation Varsity, 1945, the largest single-day airborne operation).

Post-war use and role

Large-scale combat jumps have been rare since 1945 — air defences, dispersion, and vulnerability during descent make mass drops risky — but airborne forces remain valued for rapid deployment and strategic deterrence, with notable jumps in Korea, Suez (1956), Panama (1989), and Iraq (2003). Special operations forces additionally train in military freefall (HALO/HAHO) techniques. Distinctive insignia, such as parachute wings and the maroon beret in British service, mark paratrooper status in many armies.

Training

Parachute training typically involves ground training in exit technique and landing falls, jumps from towers, and a series of live jumps (often five) to qualify. Static-line jumping at low altitude (around 300–400 m, sometimes lower) allows mass exits; paratroopers land with weapons and equipment containers and must assemble quickly on the drop zone.
