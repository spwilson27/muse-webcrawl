Jerusalem syndrome

Jerusalem syndrome is a group of mental phenomena involving the presence of religiously themed obsessive ideas, delusions, or other psychosis-like experiences, triggered by a visit to the city of Jerusalem. It is not endemic to one single religion or denomination but has affected Jews, Christians and Muslims of many backgrounds. The syndrome is not recognised in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM).

Presentation

Affected visitors may become convinced that they are biblical figures — prophets, the Messiah, John the Baptist or the Virgin Mary — and may begin preaching in public, wearing makeshift white robes made from hotel bedsheets, or performing ritualistic acts at holy sites. Symptoms usually begin after a few days in the city and typically resolve within a few weeks, often once the person is removed from Jerusalem.

Classification

Psychiatrist Yair Bar-El and colleagues at the Kfar Shaul Mental Health Center, which treats most cases, described three types: (I) cases superimposed on pre-existing psychotic illness; (II) cases superimposed on non-psychotic mental abnormalities such as personality disorders; and (III) a discrete form occurring in tourists with no prior psychiatric history. Between 1980 and 1993 the hospital treated about 1,200 tourists with severe Jerusalem-generated mental problems, of whom roughly 470 were admitted.

Related phenomena

Similar location-specific reactions include Paris syndrome, experienced by some Japanese tourists, and Stendhal syndrome, associated with overwhelming exposure to art in Florence.
