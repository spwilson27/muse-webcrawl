Quest for the historical Jesus

The quest for the historical Jesus consists of academic efforts to reconstruct the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth using historical methods, distinguishing the Jesus who lived in first-century Roman Judea from the Christ of Christian faith as presented in the Gospels and later doctrine. The quest is conventionally divided into several phases, beginning in the Enlightenment.

The first quest

The first quest began with Hermann Samuel Reimarus, whose fragments were published posthumously by Lessing in the 1770s, arguing that Jesus was a failed political messiah whose disciples invented the resurrection. In the nineteenth century, David Friedrich Strauss's The Life of Jesus, Critically Examined (1835) treated many Gospel narratives as myth, and liberal scholars such as Adolf von Harnack presented Jesus as a teacher of ethical religion, the "essence of Christianity".

No quest and the new quest

Albert Schweitzer's landmark The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906) argued that liberal lives of Jesus reflected their authors' own values, and concluded that Jesus was an apocalyptic prophet. Martin Kähler and later Rudolf Bultmann argued that historical reconstruction was both impossible and theologically unnecessary—the era later called the "no quest" period. In 1953 Ernst Käsemann reopened the question, launching the "new quest", which sought continuity between the historical Jesus and the preaching of the early church.

The third quest

From the 1980s, a "third quest" emphasised Jesus' Jewishness and used archaeology, the Dead Sea Scrolls, and social-scientific methods. Figures such as E. P. Sanders, Geza Vermes, John P. Meier, and N. T. Wright placed Jesus firmly within Second Temple Judaism. The Jesus Seminar (founded 1985 by Robert Funk) pursued a parallel, more sceptical programme, voting on the authenticity of sayings attributed to Jesus. Scholars commonly apply criteria of authenticity such as multiple attestation, embarrassment, and dissimilarity, though these criteria are themselves debated.

Consensus results

Nearly all scholars agree that Jesus existed, was baptised by John the Baptist, preached in Galilee, called disciples, and was crucified by order of the Roman prefect Pontius Pilate around AD 30 or 33. Disagreement centres on his self-understanding and the degree to which he was an apocalyptic prophet, wisdom teacher, or social reformer.
