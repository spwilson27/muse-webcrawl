Doctrine of lapse

The doctrine of lapse was a policy of annexation initiated by the East India Company in the Indian subcontinent in the late 1840s, and most aggressively applied by Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General of India from 1848 to 1856. Under the doctrine, any princely state under the suzerainty of the Company would have its territory annexed if its ruler died without a natural male heir; the Company refused to recognize adopted heirs, contrary to long-standing Indian custom.

Application

Among the states annexed under the doctrine were Satara (1848), Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853) and Nagpur (1854). The annexation of Jhansi stripped the widowed Rani Lakshmibai of her adopted son's inheritance, a grievance that made her one of the most famous leaders of the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

Consequences

The doctrine was deeply resented by Indian rulers and subjects and is regarded as one of the contributing causes of the Rebellion of 1857. After the rebellion, the British Crown took over the government of India, and Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1858 renounced further annexation; the doctrine of lapse was formally abandoned, and the rights of princes to adopt heirs were guaranteed.
