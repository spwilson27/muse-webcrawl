Administrative Procedure Act

Image placeholder: the US Capitol

The Administrative Procedure Act (APA; Pub. L. 79-404, 60 Stat. 237) is a United States federal statute enacted on June 11, 1946, that governs the way in which administrative agencies of the federal government propose and establish regulations, and that sets up the process of judicial review of agency action. It is one of the most important pieces of legislation in American administrative law.

Background

The rapid growth of federal agencies during the New Deal prompted concerns about bureaucratic power and calls for uniform procedural safeguards. After a decade of study and debate — including the work of the Attorney General's Committee on Administrative Procedure — Congress passed the APA as a compromise between New Deal supporters and critics of the administrative state.

Main provisions

The APA requires agencies to publish notices of proposed rulemaking in the Federal Register and to give the public an opportunity to comment before rules take effect ("notice and comment" rulemaking). It prescribes procedures for formal adjudication, guarantees rights to a hearing in many licensing and enforcement contexts, and requires agencies to publish their rules, statements of policy, and interpretations.

Judicial review

Under the APA, persons adversely affected by agency action may seek review in federal court. Courts may set aside agency action that is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, contrary to constitutional right, in excess of statutory jurisdiction, or unsupported by substantial evidence. The standards of review elaborated under the APA have shaped landmark doctrines of American administrative law.
