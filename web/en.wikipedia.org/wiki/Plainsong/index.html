Plainsong

Plainsong or plainchant (Latin: cantus planus) is a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church. When referring to the term plainsong, it is those chants that are used within the Roman Catholic Church (though its use is not exclusive to the Roman Catholic Church), which may or may not be accompanied, and may involve solo singing or singing in unison. The best-known form of plainsong is Gregorian chant, the chant tradition of the Roman Rite.

Characteristics

Plainsong is monophonic: it consists of a single melodic line with no harmony or independent accompaniment. It is sung in free rhythm, following the natural accents of the Latin text rather than a regular metre or beat. Melodies move predominantly by step within the framework of the eight church modes (the octoechos). Chant texts are drawn from Scripture and liturgical poetry — psalms, antiphons, hymns, sequences and the fixed and variable parts of the Mass and the Divine Office.

Performance styles range from syllabic chant (one note per syllable, as in much psalmody) through neumatic settings (small groups of notes per syllable) to highly florid melismatic chant, in which a single syllable may carry dozens of notes, as in the great Alleluias and Graduals of the Mass.

History

Christian chant grew out of the psalmody of Jewish worship and the hymnody of the early Church. Regional chant dialects developed across Western Europe: Ambrosian chant in Milan, Mozarabic chant in Spain, Beneventan chant in southern Italy, Gallican chant in Francia, and Old Roman chant in Rome itself. During the Carolingian era, Frankish rulers promoted a standardised Roman-Frankish synthesis that became known as Gregorian chant — named, by legend, after Pope Gregory the Great (r. 590–604), who was said to have received the melodies from the Holy Spirit in the form of a dove dictating into his ear.

Chant was transmitted orally for centuries before systems of notation — first unheighted neumes in the 9th century, then staff notation credited to Guido of Arezzo in the 11th — fixed it in writing. From the late Middle Ages, plainsong was increasingly overshadowed in elaborate settings by polyphony, though it remained the foundation of the liturgy and the cantus firmus of countless compositions. The 19th-century monks of Solesmes Abbey led a scholarly restoration of chant, producing the editions that underlie modern chant books such as the Liber Usualis and the Graduale Romanum. The Second Vatican Council declared that Gregorian chant should be given "pride of place" in the Roman liturgy, though in practice it receded in many parishes after the 1960s.

Other traditions

Plainsong also survives in Anglican practice, where psalms are sung to Anglican chant (a harmonised descendant) and to plainsong tones with English texts, and in Lutheran and monastic communities that retain chant-based offices. The chant of the Eastern churches — Byzantine chant, Znamenny chant and others — is a distinct but related monophonic tradition.
