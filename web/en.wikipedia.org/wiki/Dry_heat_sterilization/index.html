Dry heat sterilization

Dry heat sterilization is a method of sterilization that uses hot air, at temperatures typically between 160 °C and 190 °C, to kill microorganisms, including bacterial spores, on instruments and materials. It is one of the oldest sterilization techniques and is performed in a hot air oven. Because dry heat transfers energy less efficiently than moist heat (steam autoclaving), it requires higher temperatures and longer exposure times.

Process

Standard cycles include approximately two hours at 160 °C, one hour at 170 °C, or 30 minutes at 180 °C, after the load has reached temperature. Killing occurs mainly through oxidation of cellular components and denaturation of proteins. Items must be thoroughly cleaned and dried before processing, and the oven load arranged to allow free circulation of hot air.

Applications

Dry heat is suited to materials that steam would damage or cannot penetrate: anhydrous oils, petroleum jelly, powders, sharp instruments that might corrode or dull, glassware, and metal instruments. It is also used for depyrogenation of glassware for pharmaceutical manufacture, at still higher temperatures. It is unsuitable for most plastics, rubber, fabrics, and aqueous liquids, which may melt, char, or boil.

Comparison with moist heat

Moist heat (steam under pressure) sterilizes faster and at lower temperatures because condensing steam releases latent heat and hydrolyses proteins. Dry heat, however, avoids corrosion and moisture damage, and does not require pressure equipment. Biological indicators for dry heat validation typically use spores of Bacillus atrophaeus.
