Rakı

Rakı (Turkish pronunciation: [ɾaˈkɯ]) is an alcoholic drink made mainly in Turkey. It is an unsweetened, anise-flavoured spirit, similar to other anise-based drinks such as Greek ouzo, French pastis and Levantine arak. Rakı is often considered the national drink of Turkey and is commonly served with seafood or meze. When mixed with water, it turns a milky white colour, earning it the nickname aslan sütü ("lion's milk").

Production

Rakı is traditionally produced by twice distilling grape pomace or raisins fermented with ethanol, and flavouring the distillate with aniseed (Pimpinella anisum). The highest-quality rakı, often labelled yeni rakı, is made from fresh grapes. Turkish law requires rakı to be produced in Turkey from suma (grape or raisin distillate) with aniseed, and to be aged in oak barrels for at least 30 days for some premium styles.

Consumption

Rakı is traditionally drunk mixed with chilled water in a special tall, thin glass, often with ice added after the water to avoid anise crystals forming. It is served at the rakı sofrası ("rakı table"), a leisurely meal of cold and hot meze dishes, salads, grilled fish and fruit, accompanied by conversation, music and fasıl songs. Rakı is also widely consumed in Turkish communities in the Balkans, Germany and elsewhere, and similar drinks are produced in North Macedonia and Albania.

History

Anise-flavoured spirits have a long history in the eastern Mediterranean, descending from arak distilled in the Levant and spread through the Ottoman Empire. Distillation of rakı in Istanbul's taverns grew during the 19th century, when non-Muslim communities operated popular meyhanes. After the founding of the Republic of Turkey, production was a state monopoly under Tekel until privatisation in 2004.
