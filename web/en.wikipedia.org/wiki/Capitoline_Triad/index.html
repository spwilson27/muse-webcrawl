Capitoline Triad

The Capitoline Triad is a group of three deities who were worshipped in ancient Roman religion in the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus Capitolinus on the Capitoline Hill in Rome. The triad consisted of Jupiter, the king of the gods; his wife Juno; and Jupiter's daughter Minerva, goddess of wisdom and strategic warfare. This grouping, of Etruscan inspiration, was central to Roman state religion: temples to the triad, known as Capitolia, were built in Rome and in Roman colonies and municipalities across Italy and the provinces.

The Temple on the Capitoline

The great temple on the Capitoline, traditionally dedicated in 509 BCE, the first year of the Roman Republic, housed the three gods in three parallel cellae, with Jupiter in the centre, Juno on his left, and Minerva on his right. Consuls took their vows there, triumphal processions ended at its steps, and the temple's treasury and archives made it the symbolic heart of the Roman state. The building burned and was rebuilt several times, under Sulla, Vespasian, and Domitian.

Archaic triad

Scholars distinguish the Capitoline Triad from an earlier "archaic triad" of Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus, served by the three major flamines (the flamen Dialis, flamen Martialis, and flamen Quirinalis). The replacement of the war-and-agriculture archaic grouping by the Capitoline family group is generally attributed to Etruscan influence during the period of the Roman kings, reflecting the Etruscan triad Tinia, Uni, and Menrva.

Capitolia in the Roman world

As Rome expanded, temples of the Capitoline Triad were erected in colonies and chartered towns as a mark of Roman civic identity — examples are known from Cosa, Pompeii, Ostia, Verona, and many provincial cities in Gaul, Spain, and North Africa. Within these temples, the three gods received joint cult on the model of the Roman original.
