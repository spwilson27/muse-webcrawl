Deep drawing

Deep drawing: a punch forces a sheet blank into a die, forming a seamless cup.

Deep drawing is a sheet-metal forming process in which a flat blank of metal is drawn into a die by the action of a punch, producing a hollow shape whose depth exceeds its diameter. It is one of the most widely used metalworking processes, employed to manufacture seamless parts such as cans, kitchen sinks, cooking pots, automotive body panels, cartridge cases and gas bottles.

Process

In a typical operation the blank is placed over a die cavity and held lightly by a blank holder. The punch pushes the centre of the blank into the die, and the material of the flange is drawn radially inward, thickening as its circumference decreases. The blank-holder pressure must be carefully controlled: too little allows the flange to wrinkle, while too much restricts flow and tears the wall of the cup. Lubrication reduces friction and tool wear.

The severity of a draw is expressed by the drawing ratio, the ratio of blank diameter to punch diameter; for a single operation this is typically limited to about 2. Deeper parts are made by redrawing through successively smaller dies, sometimes with intermediate annealing to restore ductility. Related techniques include ironing, which thins the cup wall to a uniform thickness — as in the production of aluminium beverage cans — and hydroforming, in which fluid pressure replaces the punch or die.

Materials and defects

Low-carbon deep-drawing-quality steels, aluminium alloys, copper and brass are commonly used. Characteristic defects include wrinkling of the flange, tearing at the punch radius, and "earing" — the formation of a wavy rim caused by planar anisotropy of the rolled sheet, quantified by the Lankford coefficient (r-value). A high normal anisotropy and low planar anisotropy are desirable in sheet intended for deep drawing.
