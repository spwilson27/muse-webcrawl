Tiryns (Ancient Greek: Τίρυνς; Modern Greek: Τίρυνθα) is a Mycenaean archaeological site in Argolis in the Peloponnese, Greece. In ancient times, the city was linked with the myths of Heracles, whose legendary stronghold it was supposed to have been. Tiryns sits 20 km south of Mycenae, on a limestone hill rising up to 30 metres above the surrounding Argolic plain, near the modern town of Nafplio.

History

The site was inhabited from the Neolithic period onwards, but its floruit came in the Late Bronze Age. The famous cyclopean walls — built of huge limestone boulders fitted roughly together — date mainly to the 13th century BC and gave rise to the ancient Greek belief that they had been built by the Cyclopes. The citadel was organised into an upper citadel containing the palace, with its great megaron and frescoed walls, and lower terraces.

Like other Mycenaean palaces, Tiryns was destroyed by fire around 1200 BC, at the close of the Late Bronze Age collapse. Unlike many sites, it continued to be occupied into the Iron Age; a temple was built over the ruins of the palace megaron in the Geometric period.

Mythology

In Greek myth, Tiryns was founded by Proetus and fortified by the Cyclopes. It was the home of the hero Bellerophon, and the seat of Eurystheus, the king who imposed the Twelve Labours on Heracles, who was himself born in Thebes but raised and based at Tiryns in many versions of the myth.

Excavations

The site was excavated by Heinrich Schliemann in 1884–1885, and systematically by the German Archaeological Institute in the early 20th century and again from 1967 onwards. In 1999, Tiryns was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List together with Mycenae.
