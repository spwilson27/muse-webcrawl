Wiretapping

Also calledTelephone tapping, lawful interception
MethodsLine tapping, exchange access, pen registers, metadata collection
Key U.S. lawTitle III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act (1968); FISA (1978)

Wiretapping, also known as telephone tapping, is the monitoring of telephone and Internet-based conversations by a third party, often by covert means. A wiretap originally referred to an electrical connection made to a telegraph or telephone line to obtain access to its signals. When carried out by the state with legal authorization it is often called lawful interception.

Methods

Historically, tapping meant physically attaching equipment to a subscriber's line or gaining access at a telephone exchange. Modern interception typically takes place at the carrier's switch, using built-in lawful-intercept interfaces (in the United States, governed by the Communications Assistance for Law Enforcement Act, CALEA, of 1994). Related tools include pen registers and trap-and-trace devices, which record numbers dialed and other metadata rather than content.

Law in the United States

In Katz v. United States (1967), the Supreme Court held that wiretapping is a search under the Fourth Amendment. Title III of the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act of 1968 then established court-order procedures for criminal wiretaps. The Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act of 1978 governs intelligence-related surveillance. At the state level, jurisdictions differ on whether one party or all parties to a call must consent to recording.

History and controversies

Wiretapping has featured in notable episodes from Prohibition-era bootlegging cases (Olmstead v. United States, 1928) to the Watergate scandal and the post-2001 debates over warrantless surveillance programs. Unauthorized tapping by private parties is generally a criminal offense, and journalistic and corporate phone-hacking scandals have repeatedly prompted legal reform.
