History of England

The history of England covers the development of the land now called England from prehistoric human settlement to the present. England became a unified kingdom in the 10th century, and its legal, parliamentary, and linguistic traditions — most notably the English language and common law — have had a lasting global influence, amplified by the Industrial Revolution, which began there, and by the British Empire, at its height the largest in history.

Prehistory and Roman Britain

Humans have lived in Britain intermittently for hundreds of thousands of years; Neolithic farmers built monuments such as Stonehenge. Celtic-speaking peoples dominated by the Iron Age. Rome invaded in AD 43 and ruled most of the island for nearly four centuries, building roads, towns, and Hadrian's Wall, before withdrawing around AD 410.

Anglo-Saxon and Norman England

Germanic peoples — Angles, Saxons, and Jutes — settled in the following centuries, forming kingdoms such as Wessex, Mercia, and Northumbria. Viking invasions from the late 8th century carved out the Danelaw; Alfred the Great of Wessex resisted, and his descendants unified England by 927 under Æthelstan. In 1066 William of Normandy's victory at Hastings replaced the ruling class, introduced feudalism and Norman French at court, and was recorded in the Domesday Book (1086).

Medieval and Tudor England

Magna Carta (1215) limited royal power; Parliament developed through the 13th–14th centuries; the Black Death (1348–49) killed perhaps a third to a half of the population. After the dynastic Wars of the Roses, the Tudors centralised power: Henry VIII broke with Rome in the 1530s, and Elizabeth I's reign saw the defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588) and a cultural flowering.

Civil war to industrialisation

Conflict between Charles I and Parliament led to the Civil War, the king's execution (1649), Cromwell's Commonwealth, the Restoration (1660), and the Glorious Revolution (1688), which established parliamentary supremacy; the Acts of Union (1707) merged England and Scotland into Great Britain. From the later 18th century, the steam engine, factories, and canals made Britain the first industrial nation, while its empire expanded worldwide.

Modern era

Victorian Britain combined industrial dominance with gradual reform — the franchise expansions of 1832–1928 among them. Two world wars in the 20th century accelerated the end of empire, the creation of the welfare state (1945–51), and Britain's reorientation toward Europe, including EEC/EC membership from 1973 to 2020. England today is a constituent country of the United Kingdom, governed from Westminster, with devolved arrangements for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland but no separate English parliament.
