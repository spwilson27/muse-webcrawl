The Westbury White Horse or Bratton White Horse is a hill figure on the escarpment of Salisbury Plain, located just below Bratton Camp, an Iron Age hillfort, on Bratton Downs near Westbury in Wiltshire, England. It is the oldest of the Wiltshire white horses and measures about 180 feet (55 m) long and 170 feet (52 m) high.

Unlike most other chalk hill figures in the county, the Westbury horse faces left. It was recut in 1778, and legend has long connected an earlier horse on the site with Alfred the Great's victory over the Danes at the Battle of Ethandun (Edington) in 878, although there is no firm documentary evidence for a figure before the 18th century.

History

The origins of the Westbury White Horse are uncertain. The present figure dates from 1778, when it was recut under the direction of a Mr Gee, steward to Lord Abingdon. Local tradition holds that an older, quite different horse previously occupied the site, depicted in an earlier style with a shorter body, and that it commemorated Alfred's defeat of Guthrum's Viking army in 878.

Antiquarians recorded the horse from the early 18th century. Francis Wise mentioned it in 1742, and it appeared in accounts of Wiltshire antiquities thereafter. In the 1850s the local landowner had the horse scoured as part of continuing maintenance.

Restoration and modern era

In the 1950s the horse was covered in concrete and painted white to reduce the cost of repeated scouring (cleaning) of the chalk. This decision remains controversial, but the covering has preserved the figure's outline. In 2003 the horse was cleaned and repainted, and in the spring of that year it was temporarily coloured pink for a charity event.

The figure can be seen from several miles away, including from the A350 road and the railway line through Westbury, and is a well-known local landmark. It appears on the insignia of local organisations and in the names of businesses in the town, including Westbury White Horse Country Park visitor facilities at the foot of the downs.
