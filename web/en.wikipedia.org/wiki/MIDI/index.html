MIDI

MIDI (/ˈmɪdi/; Musical Instrument Digital Interface) is a technical standard that describes a communication protocol, digital interface and electrical connectors for connecting electronic musical instruments, computers and related audio devices for playing, editing and recording music. Unlike digital audio, a MIDI file or stream carries event messages — data about pitch, velocity, tempo and control signals — rather than sound itself, so MIDI recordings are compact and can be re-voiced with any instrument.

History

MIDI was developed in the early 1980s, chiefly by Dave Smith of Sequential Circuits and Ikutaro Kakehashi of Roland, to let synthesisers from competing manufacturers interoperate. The specification was unveiled in 1982–83: at the January 1983 NAMM Show, a Sequential Prophet-600 and a Roland Jupiter-6 performed together over MIDI. The standard was an immediate success, enabling sequencers, drum machines and computers (notably the Atari ST, with built-in MIDI ports) to control whole studios. Smith and Kakehashi received a Technical Grammy Award in 2013 for its creation.

Protocol

Classic MIDI transmits asynchronous serial data at 31,250 bit/s over 5-pin DIN cables using an opto-isolated current loop, with In, Out and Thru ports. Messages include Note On/Off (with velocity), polyphonic and channel aftertouch, Control Change (e.g. modulation, volume, sustain pedal), Program Change, Pitch Bend and System Exclusive (SysEx) for manufacturer-specific data. A single cable carries 16 independent channels, conventionally with percussion on channel 10 under General MIDI, a 1991 refinement that standardised a palette of 128 instrument sounds so files play back consistently across devices. Standard MIDI Files (.mid) store performances; USB-MIDI largely supplanted DIN cables for computer connections from the late 1990s.

MIDI 2.0

MIDI 2.0, adopted by the MIDI Association beginning in 2019, is a bidirectional protocol that remains backward compatible through MIDI-CI (Capability Inquiry) negotiation. It raises note velocity resolution to 16 bits, adds per-note controllers, 32-bit controller resolution and property exchange for device profiles, greatly expanding expressive control for modern instruments.

Applications

Beyond music production and live performance, MIDI controls stage lighting, theatrical effects, and show systems; it underpins video-game music histories, mobile ringtones, digital audio workstations and accessible and experimental instruments.
