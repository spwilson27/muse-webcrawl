The Landlord's Game is a board game patented in 1904 by Elizabeth (Lizzie) Magie and first published by the Economic Game Company in 1906. It is the best-known precursor of Monopoly. Magie, a follower of the economist Henry George, designed the game as an educational tool to demonstrate the effects of land monopolism and to popularise the Georgist proposal of a single tax on land values.

Gameplay

Players move tokens around the perimeter of a square board, buying properties, railways and utilities, paying rent, wages and taxes, and drawing chance cards. Magie's patent described two alternative rule sets: a monopolist version, in which players competed to bankrupt their opponents, and an anti-monopolist "Prosperity" version, in which wealth created was shared and the game ended when the poorest player had doubled their stake. The monopolist rules proved far more popular.

From folk game to Monopoly

The game spread as a folk pastime through Quaker communities and universities, with players making homemade boards named for local streets — most famously in Atlantic City. Charles Darrow encountered one such version in the early 1930s, patented his own variant as Monopoly in 1935 and sold it to Parker Brothers. Parker Brothers bought Magie's patent for $500 without royalties. Her role as inventor was largely forgotten until the 1970s, when litigation over the game Anti-Monopoly and research by Ralph Anspach brought her contribution back to public attention.
