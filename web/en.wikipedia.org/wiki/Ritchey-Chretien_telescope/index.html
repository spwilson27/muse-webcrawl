Ritchey–Chrétien telescope

A Ritchey–Chrétien telescope (abbreviated RCT) is a specialized variant of the Cassegrain reflector that uses two hyperbolic mirrors to eliminate optical errors such as coma and spherical aberration, providing a wider field of view free of those aberrations than a classical Cassegrain design. It was invented in the early 1910s by American astronomer George Willis Ritchey and French astronomer Henri Chrétien.

Design

In the Ritchey–Chrétien design, both the primary and secondary mirrors are hyperboloids. The primary mirror's shape is figured so that, in combination with the hyperbolic secondary, the system is free of third-order coma and spherical aberration. The design still suffers from astigmatism and field curvature at the edges of a wide field, which in professional instruments is corrected with additional field-flattening lenses near the focal plane, sometimes called a gascoigne corrector.

Ritchey built the first successful RCT, a 0.5 m (20 in) telescope, in 1927; it was erected on a 50-acre site near Chula Vista, California, and later moved to the United States Naval Observatory Flagstaff Station.

Professional use

Most large professional research telescopes built since the 1970s use the Ritchey–Chrétien configuration, including the Hubble Space Telescope (2.4 m), the Keck telescopes (10 m), the Very Large Telescope (8.2 m units), and the twin 8.4 m mirrors of the Large Binocular Telescope. The design's ability to deliver sharp images over a comparatively wide field makes it the standard for observatory instruments and for advanced amateur astrophotography telescopes.

Advantages and disadvantages

The principal advantages are the coma-free field and the compact tube length typical of Cassegrain systems. The disadvantages are the cost and difficulty of manufacturing and testing two hyperbolic mirrors, tight collimation tolerances, and the curved focal plane, which usually requires a field flattener for photographic work.
