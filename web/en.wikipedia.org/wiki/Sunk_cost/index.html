Sunk cost

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In economics and business decision-making, a sunk cost (also known as retrospective cost) is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. Sunk costs are contrasted with prospective costs, which are future costs that may be avoided if action is taken. Because a sunk cost will be borne regardless of the decision chosen, classical economics holds that rational decision-makers should ignore sunk costs entirely and decide solely on the basis of future consequences.

The sunk cost fallacy

In practice, people routinely violate this principle. The sunk cost fallacy — also called escalation of commitment — is the tendency to continue an endeavour once time, money or effort has been invested, even when abandoning it would be more beneficial. Classic illustrations include finishing an unenjoyable meal because it was paid for, and governments continuing failing projects because so much has already been spent. The phenomenon is sometimes called the "Concorde fallacy", after the Anglo-French decision to complete the Concorde airliner despite its poor commercial prospects. Behavioural economists link the fallacy to loss aversion: abandoning a project makes the loss feel certain and realised, whereas continuing offers a chance, however slim, of avoiding it.

Bygones principle

Traditional economic teaching summarises the correct treatment as the bygones principle: "bygones are forever bygones", meaning only future costs and benefits are relevant to a current decision. Sunk costs may still matter indirectly — for example through tax deductions, accounting write-offs, or the information they convey — but the expenditure itself is gone whether or not the project proceeds.
