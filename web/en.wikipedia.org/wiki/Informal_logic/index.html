Informal logic

Informal logic is a branch of logic and philosophy that develops non-formal standards, criteria, and procedures for the analysis, interpretation, evaluation, and construction of arguments in everyday language. It arose in North America in the 1970s as educators and philosophers sought better ways to teach reasoning about real arguments — in editorials, advertisements, courtrooms, and public debate — than formal deductive logic alone provided.

Origins

The movement is often dated to the 1970s, with works such as Howard Kahane's Logic and Contemporary Rhetoric (1971) and textbooks by J. Anthony Blair and Ralph H. Johnson, who are regarded as founding figures. The First International Symposium on Informal Logic was held in 1978, and the journal Informal Logic and the Association for Informal Logic and Critical Thinking grew out of the same milieu. Influences included Stephen Toulmin's model of argument layout, Chaim Perelman's "new rhetoric," and C. L. Hamblin's critique of the standard treatment of fallacies.

Subject matter

Informal logic addresses argument identification and reconstruction, the evaluation of premises (acceptability), the relevance and sufficiency of reasons, fallacy theory, argument schemes (recurring patterns such as argument from analogy or from expert opinion), and dialogue types. It is closely connected with the critical thinking movement in education, though its proponents insist it is a theoretical discipline as well as a pedagogical one.

Relation to formal logic

Informal logic does not reject formal logic; rather, it holds that most everyday reasoning is not deductive and cannot be fairly assessed by deductive validity alone. It therefore complements formal methods with tools for inductive, conductive, and defeasible reasoning.
