Appeal to accomplishment

The appeal to accomplishment is an informal logical fallacy in which a claim is defended, or a critic dismissed, on the basis of the accomplishments of the claimant or the lack of accomplishments of the critic, rather than on the merits of the claim itself. It is a form of appeal to authority and is closely related to the appeal to poverty and the courtier's reply.

Form

Typical forms include: "How can you criticise this artist's work when you have never produced anything yourself?"; "She must know what she's doing — look how successful she's been"; and "Achieve something first, then your opinion will count." The fallacy is a red herring: past success in a field does not guarantee the truth of a particular claim, and a lack of personal accomplishment does not make a criticism false — a judgement must stand or fall on evidence and argument.

Related concepts

The reverse fallacy — dismissing a view because its holder is unsuccessful — is sometimes classed with the appeal to poverty. Both differ from legitimate appeals to expertise, where relevant qualifications serve as defeasible evidence rather than as a substitute for argument.
