Stereotactic radiosurgery

Stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) is a distinct discipline that utilizes externally generated ionizing radiation in certain cases to inactivate or eradicate defined targets, typically in the head or spine, without the need for a surgical incision. Imaging modalities (CT, MRI, and angiography) are used to localize the target in a three-dimensional coordinate system, and numerous precisely aimed beams of radiation converge on it, delivering a high dose in a single session while sparing surrounding tissue.

History

The technique was pioneered in the 1950s by the Swedish neurosurgeon Lars Leksell, who coined the term "radiosurgery" and developed the Gamma Knife, first used clinically in 1968. The Gamma Knife uses many cobalt-60 sources arranged in a helmet-like array whose gamma rays intersect at the target. Particle-beam (proton) radiosurgery was developed at Harvard in the same era, and modified linear accelerators (LINACs) were adapted for radiosurgery from the 1980s onward, with frameless robotic systems such as the CyberKnife following.

Applications

SRS is used for benign and malignant brain tumours (including meningiomas, vestibular schwannomas, pituitary adenomas and brain metastases), arteriovenous malformations, and functional disorders such as trigeminal neuralgia. When the dose is divided over several sessions it is called fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy; the analogous treatment of targets elsewhere in the body is known as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT).
