Syncretism

[Image: A home altar in Havana combining a Catholic saint statue with offerings associated with a Yoruba orisha, illustrating Afro-Cuban religious syncretism]

Syncretism is the combination of different, often seemingly contradictory, beliefs and practices — most often the merging of elements from different religions, mythologies or cultures into a new, coherent whole. The term derives from the Greek synkretismos, used by Plutarch for the Cretans' habit of uniting against common enemies, and was applied to religion in the early modern period, sometimes pejoratively, to denote "impure" mixtures.

Religious syncretism

Examples span history: Hellenistic religion fused Greek and Egyptian deities (as in Serapis); Roman practice routinely identified foreign gods with Roman counterparts (interpretatio romana); Manichaeism combined Zoroastrian, Christian and Buddhist elements. Under colonialism and slavery, Afro-Caribbean religions such as Santería, Vodou and Candomblé joined West African orisha worship with Roman Catholic saints and feast days. In Asia, Japan's long fusion of Shinto and Buddhism (shinbutsu-shūgō) and Vietnam's Caodaism are standard examples, and the Baháʼí Faith and many Japanese new religions are consciously syncretic.

Debates

Scholars dispute the term's usefulness: all religions arguably develop through borrowing, so labelling some "syncretic" may reflect the observer's categories more than the tradition itself. Related processes include acculturation, bricolage, religious pluralism and anti-syncretic purist movements that arise in reaction to perceived dilution.
