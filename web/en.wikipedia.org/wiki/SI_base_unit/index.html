SI base unit

The SI base units are the seven units of measurement defined by the International System of Units (SI) from which all other SI units are derived. They are well-defined units for the base quantities of length, mass, time, electric current, thermodynamic temperature, amount of substance, and luminous intensity.

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the base units are defined in terms of fixed numerical values of seven defining constants — including the speed of light, the Planck constant, and the elementary charge — rather than by physical artefacts or specific experimental procedures.

History

The concept of base units descends from the metric system of revolutionary France. The metre and kilogram were originally embodied in physical artefacts; the kilogram prototype, a platinum-iridium cylinder kept near Paris, remained the definition of the kilogram until the 2019 redefinition replaced it with a definition based on the Planck constant.

Derived units

All other SI units — such as the newton, joule, volt, and hertz — are derived units expressed as products of powers of the base units, without additional numerical factors (a property known as coherence).
