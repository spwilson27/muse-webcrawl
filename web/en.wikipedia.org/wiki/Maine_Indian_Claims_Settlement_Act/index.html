Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act
EnactedOctober 10, 1980
Public law96-420
Settlement$81.5 million; up to 300,000 acres
PartiesPenobscot Nation, Passamaquoddy Tribe, Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians

The Maine Indian Claims Settlement Act of 1980 (Public Law 96-420) is a United States federal statute that resolved the land claims of the Penobscot Nation, the Passamaquoddy Tribe, and the Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians against the State of Maine. It ratified a negotiated settlement providing $81.5 million — including a $54.5 million trust fund and $26.8 million for land acquisition — and authority for the tribes to purchase up to 300,000 acres of land in Maine, in exchange for extinguishing the tribes' claims to some 12.5 million acres, roughly two-thirds of the state. It remains one of the largest Indian land claims settlements in U.S. history.

Background

The claims rested on the federal Indian Nonintercourse Act of 1790 and its successors, which prohibited transfers of tribal land without federal approval. In the 1790s and 1818, Massachusetts (which then included Maine) had acquired millions of acres from the Penobscot and Passamaquoddy without that approval. When the tribes sued in the 1970s, the First Circuit's decision in Joint Tribal Council of the Passamaquoddy Tribe v. Morton (1975) held that the Nonintercourse Act applied to the Maine tribes and that a federal trust relationship existed, giving the claims formidable legal force and casting a cloud over land titles across much of Maine.

Negotiation and terms

Negotiations involving the tribes, the state, and the Carter administration produced a settlement embodied in both the Maine Implementing Act (state law, April 1980) and the federal Act (October 1980). Key terms included extinguishment of aboriginal title claims, the monetary settlement and land acquisition fund, federal recognition arrangements, and a unique jurisdictional framework: within their territories the tribes exercise many powers of Maine municipalities, while the state retains broader jurisdiction than in most federal Indian law settlements. The Houlton Band of Maliseet Indians received federal recognition through the Act.

Legacy and disputes

The settlement's jurisdictional compromises have generated decades of litigation and political dispute — over water quality regulation on the Penobscot River, hunting and fishing rights, gaming, and whether later federal Indian legislation applies in Maine. In 2023 the Maine legislature passed, and Governor Janet Mills later supported advancing, measures affecting tribal rights on specific issues, while broader sovereignty bills that would amend the settlement's framework were vetoed in 2023, leaving the 1980 settlement as the governing structure.
