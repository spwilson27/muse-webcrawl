Place cell

A place cell is a type of pyramidal neuron in the hippocampus that becomes active when an animal enters a particular place in its environment, which is known as the place field. Place cells are thought to act collectively as a cognitive representation of a specific location in space, known as a cognitive map.

Place cells were first discovered by John O'Keefe and Jonathan Dostrovsky in 1971 in the hippocampus of the rat. O'Keefe proposed that place cells build an allocentric, or other-centered, cognitive map of the environment. For this work, O'Keefe shared the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with May-Britt Moser and Edvard Moser, who discovered grid cells in the entorhinal cortex.

Place fields

A place field is the particular area of the environment in which a place cell is active. Place fields vary in size depending on the size of the environment, and their arrangement is largely stable over time, though they can reorganize ("remap") when the environment changes substantially.

Role in memory

Place cells exhibit theta phase precession and are replayed during sleep and quiet wakefulness in sequences thought to support memory consolidation and spatial learning.
