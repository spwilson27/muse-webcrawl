CfA2 Great Wall

TypeGalaxy filament (wall)
LengthAbout 600 million light-years
DistanceRoughly 200–300 million light-years
Discovered1989, by Margaret Geller and John Huchra

The Great Wall appears as a prominent horizontal band in the CfA redshift survey.

The CfA2 Great Wall (also called the Coma Wall) is one of the largest known structures in the universe: an immense filament, or "wall", of galaxies roughly 600 million light-years long, 300 million light-years wide and 15 million light-years thick, at a distance of about 200 million light-years. It was discovered in 1989 by Margaret Geller and John Huchra of the Harvard–Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics from data of the CfA Redshift Survey.

Contents and significance

The wall includes the Hercules, Coma and Leo superclusters, and is centred on the rich Coma Cluster of galaxies. Its discovery, following the earlier finding of voids and filaments in the first CfA survey slice, showed that the large-scale structure of the universe is a foam-like web of filaments and voids rather than a uniform distribution, challenging models of structure formation at the time and informing modern cold dark matter cosmology. Still larger structures, such as the Sloan Great Wall and the Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, have since been found.
