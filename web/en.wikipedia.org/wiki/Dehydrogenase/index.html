Dehydrogenase

A dehydrogenase is an enzyme belonging to the group of oxidoreductases that oxidizes a substrate by reducing an electron acceptor, usually NAD+/NADH, FAD, or NADP+. Dehydrogenases transfer two hydrogen atoms from an organic substrate to the electron acceptor in a reaction that can be written in general form as:

Substrate–H2 + NAD+ → Substrate + NADH + H+

Dehydrogenases play central roles in metabolism, in the oxidation of fuels in the citric acid cycle, glycolysis, and the electron transport chain.

Examples

  • Alcohol dehydrogenase — metabolises ethanol to acetaldehyde in the liver.
  • Lactate dehydrogenase — interconverts pyruvate and lactate; its blood levels are a clinical marker of tissue damage.
  • Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase — the rate-limiting enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway; its deficiency is a common human enzyme defect causing haemolytic anaemia.
  • Succinate dehydrogenase — functions both in the citric acid cycle and as Complex II of the electron transport chain.
  • Pyruvate dehydrogenase complex — links glycolysis to the citric acid cycle by converting pyruvate to acetyl-CoA.
  • Glutamate dehydrogenase — important in amino acid metabolism.
  • Malate dehydrogenase and isocitrate dehydrogenase — citric acid cycle enzymes.

Classification

Dehydrogenases are classified in the EC 1 (oxidoreductase) class of the Enzyme Commission system, with subcategories depending on the electron donor group acted upon and the acceptor used.
