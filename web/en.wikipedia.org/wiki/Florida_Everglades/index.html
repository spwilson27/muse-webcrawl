Florida Everglades

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Everglades is a natural region of flooded wetlands in the southern portion of the U.S. state of Florida, comprising the southern half of a large drainage basin within the Neotropical realm. The system begins near Orlando with the Kissimmee River, which discharges into the vast but shallow Lake Okeechobee. Water leaving the lake in the wet season forms a slow-moving river 60 miles (97 km) wide and over 100 miles (160 km) long, flowing southward across a limestone shelf to Florida Bay at the southern end of the state.

The Everglades is often described as a "river of grass" — a phrase popularised by Marjory Stoneman Douglas in her 1947 book The Everglades: River of Grass. Its ecosystems include sawgrass marshes, cypress swamps, mangrove forests, hardwood hammocks, pinelands and the marine environment of Florida Bay. The region supports American alligators, West Indian manatees, wading birds and the endangered Florida panther.

Human impact and restoration

Beginning in the late 19th century, and accelerating after 1948 with the Central and Southern Florida Project, roughly half of the original Everglades was drained or diverted for agriculture — notably sugarcane in the Everglades Agricultural Area — and for urban development along the Atlantic coastal ridge. Altered water flow, nutrient pollution and invasive species such as the Burmese python severely damaged the ecosystem. The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan, authorised in 2000, is one of the largest ecosystem restoration projects in the world, aiming to restore more natural water quantity, quality and timing to the remaining wetlands. Everglades National Park, established in 1947, protects the southern portion and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
