Overproduction

In economics, overproduction (or oversupply) refers to an excess of supply of a good or service over the demand for it at the prevailing price. When producers collectively produce more than consumers are willing and able to buy, unsold inventories accumulate, prices come under downward pressure, and producers may be forced to cut output, lay off workers or sell at a loss. Persistent or economy-wide overproduction — a "general glut" — has been a central topic in theories of economic crisis since the early 19th century.

Say's law and the general glut controversy

Classical economists such as Jean-Baptiste Say and David Ricardo argued, in what became known as Say's law, that production itself generates the income with which output is purchased, so that a general overproduction of all goods was impossible — only temporary mismatches between particular sectors could occur. Thomas Malthus and Sismondi dissented, arguing that insufficient consumption demand could produce widespread gluts. John Maynard Keynes later revived the argument in The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), holding that a shortfall of aggregate demand could leave an economy stuck with idle capacity and unemployment, a condition that markets would not automatically correct.

Marxist theory

In Marxist economics, crises of overproduction are a fundamental feature of capitalism. Because production is driven by profit rather than need, and because the purchasing power of workers is limited by wages held below the value they produce, the system periodically produces more commodities than can be sold at profitable prices. Such crises manifest as unsold goods, bankruptcies, factory closures and mass unemployment, and — in this analysis — are resolved only through the destruction of capital values, the opening of new markets, or war. The apparent paradox that scarcity and want coexist with surplus production ("poverty in the midst of plenty") was highlighted during the Great Depression, when agricultural surpluses were destroyed while millions went hungry.

Underconsumption theories

Closely related are underconsumption theories, associated with writers such as J. A. Hobson, which attribute crises to inequality: if income is concentrated among those who save rather than spend, consumption demand will chronically lag behind productive capacity. Remedies proposed on this basis include redistribution, welfare provision and public investment.

Sectoral overproduction

Overproduction may be confined to particular industries. Agricultural overproduction has been a recurrent policy problem: advances in productivity repeatedly outrun demand (which is price-inelastic for food), depressing farm incomes and prompting interventions such as price supports, production quotas and the European Community's notorious "butter mountains" and "wine lakes" of the 1980s. In manufacturing, production cycles and the "bullwhip effect" in supply chains can cause gluts of goods such as steel, ships, semiconductors and solar panels. Environmental critics have extended the term to the fashion, plastics and electronics industries, where overproduction generates waste rather than crisis in the classical sense.
