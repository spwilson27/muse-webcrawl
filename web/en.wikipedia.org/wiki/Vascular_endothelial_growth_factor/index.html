Vascular endothelial growth factor

Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a signal protein produced by many cells that stimulates the formation of blood vessels. Specifically, VEGF is a sub-family of growth factors within the platelet-derived growth factor family. It is a key mediator of angiogenesis — the growth of new vessels from pre-existing ones — and of vasculogenesis, the de novo formation of the embryonic circulatory system.

VEGF dimers bind receptors on endothelial cells to trigger vessel growth.

Function

VEGF is released by cells in response to hypoxia: the transcription factor HIF-1α, stabilised when oxygen is scarce, drives VEGF expression. VEGF binds to receptor tyrosine kinases (VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2 and VEGFR-3) on endothelial cells, promoting their proliferation, migration and survival, increasing vascular permeability, and recruiting endothelial progenitor cells. The family includes VEGF-A (usually meant by "VEGF"), VEGF-B, VEGF-C, VEGF-D and placental growth factor (PlGF).

Clinical significance

Tumours exploit VEGF to build the blood supply they need to grow and metastasise, making VEGF a major target of anticancer therapy. The monoclonal antibody bevacizumab (Avastin) neutralises VEGF-A and is used in several cancers, while small-molecule VEGFR inhibitors include sunitinib and sorafenib. In the eye, excess VEGF drives the abnormal vessel growth and leakage of wet age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy; anti-VEGF drugs such as ranibizumab and aflibercept, injected into the eye, have transformed the treatment of these diseases. Conversely, insufficient VEGF signalling is implicated in pre-eclampsia, where excess soluble VEGFR-1 (sFlt-1) scavenges circulating VEGF.

History

An activity that increased vascular permeability was described by Senger and colleagues in 1983 as "vascular permeability factor". In 1989 Napoleone Ferrara and co-workers at Genentech isolated and cloned VEGF, and Ferrara's group subsequently developed bevacizumab.
