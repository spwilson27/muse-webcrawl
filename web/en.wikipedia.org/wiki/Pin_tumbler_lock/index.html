Pin tumbler lock

The pin tumbler lock is a locking mechanism that uses pins of varying lengths to prevent the lock from opening without the correct key. It is the most common type of cylinder lock in the world, found in most doors, padlocks, and ignition switches.

Mechanism

Inside the cylinder, a rotatable plug sits within a housing. A series of vertical chambers, each containing a spring, a driver pin, and a shorter key pin, cross the shear line between plug and housing. When no key is present, the driver pins bridge the shear line and block rotation. Inserting the correct key lifts each key pin so that the gaps between key pins and driver pins align exactly at the shear line, allowing the plug to turn.

History

Ancient Egyptian wooden locks used a similar pin principle over 4,000 years ago. The modern design was developed by Linus Yale Sr. in the 1840s and perfected by Linus Yale Jr., who patented his smaller, flat-key pin tumbler lock in 1861 and 1865; it remains the basis of most cylinder locks.

Security

Pin tumbler locks can be defeated by lock picking, bumping, or drilling, though security pins (spool, serrated, or mushroom-shaped) and tighter tolerances raise resistance. High-security variants add sidebars or secondary locking elements.
