Spaying and neutering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Placeholder image — neutering is a routine veterinary surgical procedure.

Spaying and neutering are surgical procedures performed by veterinarians to sterilise animals. Spaying removes the ovaries (and usually the uterus) of a female animal; neutering, in common usage, refers to castration — removal of the testicles — of a male animal, though technically it applies to either sex. The procedures render the animal unable to reproduce.

Reasons

The most common reason for spaying and neutering pet animals is population control: preventing unwanted litters reduces the number of animals entering shelters. The procedures also have health effects, including eliminating the risk of some reproductive cancers and infections such as pyometra, and behavioural effects such as reduced roaming, spraying and aggression in some animals. Against these benefits, studies have found associations with increased risk of certain other conditions, including obesity and some joint disorders and cancers in dogs, which has led to debate about the optimal age for the procedure.

Terminology and alternatives

A spayed female is sometimes called a "sprite" in older usage, and castrated livestock have special terms such as steer, gelding and wether depending on species. Non-surgical alternatives include hormonal contraception and immunocontraception, used mainly in wildlife and feral animal management; trap–neuter–return programmes manage feral cat colonies.
