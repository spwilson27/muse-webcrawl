The ackee (Blighia sapida), also known as ankye, akee or akee apple, is a fruit-bearing tree in the soapberry family Sapindaceae, related to the lychee and longan. Native to tropical West Africa, it was carried to the Caribbean in the eighteenth century and became the national fruit of Jamaica, where — paired with salt cod — it forms the national dish, ackee and saltfish.

A ripe ackee pod opened naturally, showing the cream-coloured arils and black seeds.

Description

The ackee is an evergreen tree growing up to about 10 metres. Its fruit is pear-shaped and bright red to yellow-orange when ripe, splitting open to reveal three large, glossy black seeds, each partly surrounded by a soft, creamy to yellow fleshy aril — the only edible portion.

Toxicity

Unripe or improperly prepared ackee contains the toxin hypoglycin A, concentrated in the arils before the fruit opens naturally and also present in the seeds. Eating it can cause "Jamaican vomiting sickness," characterised by vomiting, hypoglycaemia and potentially fatal encephalopathy. For this reason imports of raw ackee into some countries, including the United States, have historically been restricted, and only fruit that has opened on the tree should be eaten.

Culinary use

In Jamaica, ackee arils are boiled and sautéed with salted cod, onions, tomatoes and peppers to make ackee and saltfish, traditionally served at breakfast with dumplings, breadfruit or fried plantain. Ackee is also eaten in West Africa and in Haitian cuisine.
