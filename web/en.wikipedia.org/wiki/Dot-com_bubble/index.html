Dot-com bubble

Periodc. 1995–2001
PeakNASDAQ Composite at 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000
CrashNASDAQ fell about 78% from peak to October 2002
Notable failuresPets.com, Webvan, eToys, WorldCom

The dot-com bubble (or dot-com boom) was a stock market bubble in the late 1990s driven by speculative investment in Internet-based companies. Following the launch of the World Wide Web and the 1995 Netscape IPO, venture capital poured into "dot-com" startups, and stock valuations soared on expectations of future profits rather than earnings. The bubble burst in 2000–2001: the technology-heavy NASDAQ Composite peaked at 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000, and then lost nearly four-fifths of its value by October 2002, wiping out trillions of dollars in market capitalisation.

The NASDAQ Composite more than quintupled between 1995 and its March 2000 peak, then gave back nearly all its gains.

Rise

Cheap capital, enthusiasm for the commercial Internet, and a "get big fast" strategy drove companies to spend heavily on marketing and infrastructure while deferring profits. IPOs routinely doubled or tripled on their first day of trading; firms such as theGlobe.com soared on debut. Alan Greenspan had famously warned of "irrational exuberance" in December 1996, yet the market climbed for three more years.

Burst and aftermath

Rising interest rates, disappointing earnings, and the realisation that many companies would never be profitable triggered the collapse beginning in March 2000. High-profile online retailers such as Pets.com and Webvan went bankrupt; telecom giants that had overbuilt fibre networks, most infamously WorldCom, collapsed amid accounting scandals. Thousands of companies folded and technology employment contracted sharply. Some firms survived the shakeout — including Amazon, eBay, and Google (founded during the boom) — and went on to dominate the mature Internet economy, leading many historians to conclude that the bubble's underlying technological vision was sound even if its valuations were not.
