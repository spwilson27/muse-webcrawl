The occipital lobe is the smallest of the four paired lobes of the human cerebral cortex and sits at the very back of the skull, above the cerebellum, from which it is separated by the tentorium cerebelli. It is the brain's main visual processing centre, containing most of the anatomical region of the visual cortex.

Anatomy and function

The occipital lobe receives input from the retina via the thalamus (lateral geniculate nucleus) and the optic radiations. Its core is the primary visual cortex (V1, also called the striate cortex), arranged around the calcarine sulcus on the medial surface; V1 maps the visual field retinotopically, with a disproportionately large area devoted to the central (foveal) field. Surrounding association areas (V2, V3, V4, V5/MT) progressively analyse colour, form and motion, feeding two great pathways: the ventral ("what") stream toward the temporal lobe and the dorsal ("where/how") stream toward the parietal lobe.

Clinical significance

Damage to one occipital lobe — for example from a posterior cerebral artery stroke — causes loss of the opposite half of the visual field (homonymous hemianopia, often with macular sparing). Bilateral damage can cause cortical blindness; in Anton syndrome patients deny their blindness. Irritative lesions may produce simple visual hallucinations (flashes, zigzags), distinguishing them from the complex formed hallucinations of temporal-lobe origin. Occipital seizures and the visual aura of migraine (spreading across the striate cortex) also originate here.

Boundaries

On the lateral surface the lobe is bounded by an imaginary line from the parieto-occipital sulcus to the preoccipital notch; medially, the parieto-occipital sulcus separates it from the parietal lobe and the calcarine sulcus divides its superior (cuneus) and inferior (lingual gyrus) parts.
