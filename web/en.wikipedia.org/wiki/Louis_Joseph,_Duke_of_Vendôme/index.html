Louis Joseph, Duke of Vendôme

Louis Joseph de Bourbon, Duke of Vendôme (1 July 1654 – 11 June 1712), often simply known as Vendôme, was a French general and one of the most successful commanders of King Louis XIV during the Nine Years' War and the War of the Spanish Succession. He was a great-grandson of King Henry IV of France and his mistress Gabrielle d'Estrées, and thus a member of the legitimised Bourbon-Vendôme line.

Early life

Louis Joseph was born in Paris, the son of Louis de Bourbon, Duke of Vendôme, and Laura Mancini, a niece of Cardinal Mazarin. Orphaned young, he entered military service as a teenager and distinguished himself in the campaigns of the 1670s under commanders such as Turenne and Luxembourg. He inherited the dukedom of Vendôme on his father's death in 1669.

Nine Years' War

During the Nine Years' War Vendôme campaigned in the Low Countries and Germany. In 1695 he was given command in Catalonia, where he captured Barcelona in 1697, one of the last major French successes of the war. His energy and willingness to fight won him a reputation as one of France's most aggressive generals.

War of the Spanish Succession

In Italy, Vendôme replaced the captured Marshal Villeroy and checked Prince Eugene of Savoy at the Battle of Cassano in 1705 and defeated him at Calcinato in 1706. Recalled to Flanders in 1708 to serve alongside the Duke of Burgundy, he quarrelled with the prince, and the French were defeated at Oudenarde and lost Lille; Vendôme was disgraced and retired to his estates.

Recalled in 1710 to rescue the Bourbon cause in Spain, he won decisive victories at Brihuega and Villaviciosa in December 1710, securing the Spanish throne for Philip V. He died suddenly at Vinaròs in 1712, reportedly from food poisoning, and was buried in the royal pantheon at El Escorial.

Character

Vendôme was admired for courage and rapid decision-making but criticised for indolence in quarters, carelessness about logistics and logistics discipline, and periods of neglect between bursts of energy. Saint-Simon's memoirs paint a vivid, ambivalent portrait. In 1710 he married Marie Anne de Bourbon, daughter of the Prince of Condé, but the marriage was brief and childless.
