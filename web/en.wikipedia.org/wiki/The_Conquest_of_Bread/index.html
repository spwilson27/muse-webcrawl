The Conquest of Bread (French: La Conquête du pain) is a book by the Russian anarchist and scientist Peter Kropotkin, first published in French in 1892 and in English in 1906. Originally a series of articles in the anarchist journals Le Révolté and La Révolte, it became one of the foundational texts of anarcho-communism, arguing that the tendential causes of poverty are the private ownership of the means of production and the wage system, and that society should be reorganized on the basis of voluntary communes and free distribution of goods.

Summary

Kropotkin contends that all wealth is the collective product of generations — "everything belongs to all" — so no individual has a legitimate exclusive claim to it. He proposes expropriation of land, factories, housing, and foodstuffs by the people during a revolution; the abolition of wages in favor of distribution according to need; a short working day made possible by ending wasteful production; and the integration of manual and intellectual labor, with agriculture and industry combined in self-sufficient communes federated freely. He answers the objection that people will not work without compulsion by pointing to the universal appetite for freely chosen, varied activity.

Influence

The book was translated into many languages, serialized widely, and read by generations of labor and revolutionary movements; its vision of food security and shortened toil influenced syndicalists, the Spanish collectives of 1936, and later writers on post-scarcity economics. Together with Kropotkin's Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution (1902), it defines his scientific case for anarchist communism, standing in the tradition of foundational political-philosophical works like Stirner's The Ego and Its Own as a classic of radical thought.
