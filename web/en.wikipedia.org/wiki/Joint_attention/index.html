Joint attention

Joint attention
FieldDevelopmental psychology, cognitive science
Key behavioursGaze following, pointing, showing, shared gaze
Typical emergenceFrom about 6–18 months of age in infants

Joint attention, or shared attention, is the shared focus of two individuals on an object or event, achieved when one individual alerts another to something by eye-gazing, pointing or other verbal or non-verbal means. It is a foundational capacity for social learning, language development and understanding others' minds.

Development

Abilities develop in stages across the first two years of life. By around six months infants can follow another's line of regard crudely; between nine and fourteen months come gaze following, social referencing, declarative pointing (pointing to share interest rather than to request) and showing objects to others. Michael Tomasello has argued that this "nine-month revolution" in shared intentionality distinguishes human social cognition from that of other apes and makes cumulative culture possible.

Forms

Researchers distinguish responding to joint attention (following another's gaze or point) from initiating joint attention (spontaneously directing another's attention), as well as lower-level gaze alternation from episodes in which the child appears to monitor the adult's attention itself.

Clinical significance

Impairments in joint attention are among the earliest detectable signs of autism spectrum disorder: autistic children often show reduced declarative pointing and gaze following, and screening instruments such as the CHAT assess joint-attention behaviours. Joint attention is also studied in relation to language delay, Down syndrome and parent–child interaction interventions.
