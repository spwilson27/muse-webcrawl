Structural failure

The 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge, a classic structural failure.

Structural failure is the loss of the load-carrying capacity of a component or member within a structure, or of the structure itself. It is initiated when a material is stressed beyond its strength limit, causing fracture or excessive deformation; a structure thereby fails when it no longer can support the intended loads. Structural failure can range from local damage (a cracked beam) to partial or total collapse.

Modes of failure

  • Strength failure — yielding, rupture, or crushing under excessive stress.
  • Instability — buckling of columns and shells, lateral-torsional buckling of beams, overturning.
  • Fatigue — crack growth under repeated loading, as in the de Havilland Comet accidents (1954) and countless bridge and machinery failures.
  • Fracture — brittle fracture, promoted by low temperature, as in the Liberty ship hull failures of World War II and the Silver Bridge collapse (1967, stress corrosion and fatigue of an eyebar).
  • Progressive collapse — a local failure triggers disproportionate collapse, as at Ronan Point (London, 1968, gas explosion) and the Champlain Towers South condominium (Surfside, Florida, 2021).
  • Foundation failure — settlement, bearing failure, or scour undermining supports, e.g. bridge pier scour.

Notable examples

  • Quebec Bridge collapse during construction (1907) — buckling of compression chords; 75 workers died.
  • Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) — aeroelastic flutter destroyed the suspension span.
  • Hyatt Regency walkway collapse (Kansas City, 1981) — a design change to hanger rods doubled connection loads; 114 died.
  • I-35W Mississippi River bridge (Minneapolis, 2007) — undersized gusset plates plus construction loads.
  • Morandi Bridge (Genoa, 2018) — corrosion and degradation of stays.

Investigation and prevention

Failure investigations combine document review, site inspection, materials testing, and structural re-analysis to identify root causes — typically combinations of design error, construction defects, deterioration, and extreme loading. Prevention relies on codes and standards, safety factors, redundancy, inspection and maintenance regimes, and peer review; many modern code provisions (progressive-collapse resistance, fracture control plans) arose directly from famous failures.
