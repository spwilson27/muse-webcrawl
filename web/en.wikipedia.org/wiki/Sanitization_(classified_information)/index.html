Sanitization (classified information)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Redaction — blacking out sensitive text — is the most visible form of sanitization.

Sanitization is the process of preparing classified, sensitive or proprietary information for release at a lower level of sensitivity by removing or concealing the elements that require protection. In government, military and intelligence usage, a sanitized document, report or product can be circulated to a wider audience — for example, to foreign partners, to personnel without the relevant security clearances, or to the public — while the sources, methods or details behind it remain protected.

Methods

Common sanitization techniques include:

Redaction — the removal or blacking-out of names, dates, figures and passages. Paraphrasing and generalisation — rewriting material so that specific sources ("a clandestine agent in X ministry") become generic ("reporting indicates"). "Tearline" reporting — intelligence reports are written with a perforation-like division: the detailed, highly classified portion above the tearline, and a sanitized summary below it that can literally be torn off and shared. Downgrading and declassification review — formally reassessing whether information still needs protection.

Sanitization protects human sources and technical collection methods: even when the fact learned can be shared, the way it was learned often cannot, because revealing it could endanger a source or cause an adversary to close a vulnerability being exploited.

Uses

Sanitized products are used when intelligence must be shared with allies under "third party rule" constraints, when evidence derived from secret sources is needed in court or public inquiries, when governments publish white papers or dossiers supporting a policy, and when historians and journalists obtain documents under freedom-of-information laws. The released Iraq and intelligence dossiers of various governments, and large declassification programmes for historical records, all involve systematic sanitization.

Problems and failures

Sanitization can fail in both directions. Over-sanitization can strip a document of meaning or mislead readers about the confidence of the underlying reporting. Under-sanitization can expose sources. Digital redaction has repeatedly failed when black boxes were drawn over text in PDF files while the underlying text remained copyable — a flaw exposed in several high-profile legal and government releases — leading to standards requiring true removal of the underlying data. Sanitization of electronic media (wiping or destroying storage devices before disposal or reuse) is a related but distinct technical discipline governed by standards such as NIST SP 800-88.
