Radical Reformation

The Radical Reformation represents the response to perceived corruption in both the Roman Catholic Church and the expanding Magisterial Protestant movement led by Martin Luther and Huldrych Zwingli. Beginning in Germany and Switzerland in the 1520s, it gave rise to Anabaptists and other movements outside state churches.

Radical Reformation
Period: 1520s onwards
Regions: Germany, Switzerland, Netherlands, Moravia
Groups: Anabaptists, Hutterites, Mennonites, Spiritualists, anti-Trinitarians
Key event: Münster rebellion (1534–35)

Beliefs

Radicals typically taught believer's baptism (rejecting infant baptism), separation of church and state, pacifism or non-resistance, community of goods (Hutterites), and freedom of conscience — positions for which Catholic and Protestant authorities both persecuted them; thousands were executed.

Main branches

Historian George Huntston Williams classified Anabaptists, Spiritualists, Evangelical Rationalists (anti-Trinitarians such as the Polish Brethren), and others. The Schleitheim Confession (1527) defined early Anabaptism; Menno Simons reorganised Dutch Anabaptists into Mennonites; Jakob Hutter's followers formed communal Hutterite colonies in Moravia.

Legacy

Descendants include Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites and Baptists (in part), and its ideas anticipated modern religious liberty.
