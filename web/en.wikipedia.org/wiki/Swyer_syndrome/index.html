Swyer syndrome, or 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis, is a rare condition in which a person has XY chromosomes (the pattern usually associated with male development) but develops as a phenotypic female with normally formed female external genitalia and a uterus and fallopian tubes, while the gonads are undeveloped "streak gonads" rather than testes or ovaries. The syndrome is named after Gerald Swyer, the endocrinologist who described it in 1955.

Cause

Sexual development depends on a cascade of genes. In typical development, the SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers the embryonic gonads to become testes, which then produce hormones that direct male development. In Swyer syndrome, this pathway fails — in about 15–20% of cases because of a mutation or deletion in SRY itself, and in other cases because of mutations in genes such as MAP3K1, DHH, NR5A1, or SOX9. Without functioning testes, no anti-Müllerian hormone or testosterone is produced, so the internal and external anatomy develops along typically female lines, but the gonads remain as fibrous streaks.

Presentation and diagnosis

People with Swyer syndrome are usually raised as girls and develop normally in childhood. The condition most often comes to attention in adolescence, when puberty does not occur: breast development is absent and menstruation does not begin (primary amenorrhoea). Investigation typically shows a 46,XY karyotype, a present uterus, elevated gonadotropin levels, and streak gonads.

Management

Because streak gonads carry a significantly increased risk of developing tumours (gonadoblastoma and dysgerminoma), they are usually removed surgically once the diagnosis is made. Hormone replacement therapy with oestrogen (and later progesterone) is given to induce puberty, support bone health, and maintain female secondary sexual characteristics. Although affected individuals cannot produce eggs, the uterus is functional, so pregnancy is possible using donated eggs with assisted reproduction.
