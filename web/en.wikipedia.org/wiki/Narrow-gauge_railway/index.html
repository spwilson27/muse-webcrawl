Narrow-gauge railway
Track gauge: less than 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in)
Common gauges: 1,067 mm (3 ft 6 in), 1,000 mm (metre gauge), 762 mm (2 ft 6 in), 610 mm (2 ft)

A narrow-gauge railway (or narrow-gauge railroad in American terminology) is a railway with a track gauge narrower than the 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in) standard gauge. Narrow-gauge lines are cheaper to build, can follow tighter curves, and require smaller structures and tunnels, making them well suited to mountainous terrain, lightly trafficked routes, and industrial applications such as mines, logging railways, and plantations.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because narrow-gauge track can be laid with lighter rails, smaller sleepers, and sharper curves, construction costs are substantially lower than for standard gauge, especially in difficult terrain. The trade-offs are lower speed and capacity, reduced stability, and incompatibility with the standard-gauge network, which forces transshipment of goods at break-of-gauge points.

Common gauges

The 1,067 mm (3 ft 6 in) "Cape gauge" is used extensively in southern and central Africa, Japan, parts of Australia, and New Zealand. The 1,000 mm metre gauge is widespread in Southeast Asia, parts of South America and Europe, and on many mountain railways in Switzerland. Narrower gauges such as 762 mm (2 ft 6 in) and 610 mm (2 ft) were common for industrial, military, and colonial light railways; the Darjeeling Himalayan Railway in India, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, uses 610 mm.

History

Narrow gauges were used in early wagonways; the Ffestiniog Railway in Wales, opened in the 1830s to carry slate, pioneered narrow-gauge steam traction. In the 1870s a narrow-gauge boom swept the United States, where 3 ft gauge lines such as the Denver and Rio Grande spread across the Rocky Mountains. Many lines were later converted to standard gauge as traffic grew, but narrow gauge remains the mainline standard in several countries and is widely preserved by heritage railways.
