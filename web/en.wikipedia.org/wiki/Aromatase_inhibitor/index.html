Aromatase inhibitors (AIs) are a class of drugs used in the treatment of breast cancer in postmenopausal women and gynecomastia in men. They work by inhibiting the enzyme aromatase, which converts androgens into estrogens by a process called aromatization. As breast tissue is stimulated by estrogens, decreasing their production is a way of suppressing the recurrence of the breast tumour tissue.

Mechanism of action

In postmenopausal women, most estrogen is produced not by the ovaries but by the aromatization of adrenal androgens in peripheral tissues such as adipose tissue, muscle and the breast itself. Aromatase inhibitors block this conversion, reducing circulating estradiol levels by 85–90% or more. Unlike tamoxifen, which blocks the estrogen receptor, AIs have no estrogen-agonist activity.

Types

Third-generation AIs fall into two groups: irreversible steroidal inhibitors such as exemestane, which forms a permanent bond with the aromatase enzyme and is therefore called a "suicide inhibitor", and reversible nonsteroidal inhibitors such as anastrozole and letrozole, which bind reversibly to the haem group of the enzyme. Earlier agents such as aminoglutethimide were less selective and are now rarely used.

Medical uses

Aromatase inhibitors are a standard adjuvant therapy for estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer in postmenopausal women, typically given for five to ten years; large trials such as ATAC and BIG 1-98 showed advantages over tamoxifen in reducing recurrence. They are also used off-label for ovulation induction, endometriosis, and in men for conditions such as gynecomastia. Because suppression of estrogen can be counterproductive in premenopausal women, AIs are in that group combined with ovarian suppression.

Side effects

Common side effects include hot flushes, arthralgia (joint pain), myalgia, and accelerated bone loss leading to osteoporosis and increased fracture risk; bone density monitoring and bisphosphonates may be recommended. AIs do not carry tamoxifen's risks of endometrial cancer and venous thromboembolism.
