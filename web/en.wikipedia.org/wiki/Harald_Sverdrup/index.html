Harald Ulrik Sverdrup (15 November 1888 – 21 August 1957) was a Norwegian oceanographer and meteorologist who is widely regarded as a founder of modern physical oceanography. He directed the Scripps Institution of Oceanography from 1936 to 1948, co-authored the landmark textbook The Oceans: Their Physics, Chemistry and General Biology (1942), and lent his name to the Sverdrup, the unit of volume transport in ocean currents (1 Sv = 106 m³/s).

Early life and the Maud expedition

Sverdrup was born in Sogndal, the son of a theologian, and studied astronomy and theoretical physics at the University of Oslo, taking his doctorate in 1917 with a thesis on the North Atlantic trade winds. He served as scientific leader of Roald Amundsen's Maud expedition (1918–1925), which drifted in the Arctic Ocean along the Siberian coast; his tidal and current measurements from this voyage occupied him for years and produced a large body of Arctic research. In 1926 he spent a summer studying the dynamics of the Greenland ice cap with a submarine attempt to reach the North Pole region under Sir Hubert Wilkins later abandoned.

Scripps Institution

In 1936 Sverdrup became director of the Scripps Institution of Oceanography in La Jolla, California, transforming it into a leading centre of ocean research. With Martin W. Johnson and Richard H. Fleming he wrote The Oceans (1942), for decades the standard synthesis of the science. During the Second World War he applied oceanography to military problems, notably the prediction of surf conditions for amphibious landings. His theory of wind-driven ocean circulation — the Sverdrup balance relating wind-stress curl to meridional transport — published in 1947, became a cornerstone of dynamical oceanography and underlies later theories of western boundary currents by Henry Stommel and Walter Munk.

Return to Norway

Sverdrup returned to Norway in 1948 to direct the Norwegian Polar Institute, and in 1949 became professor of geophysics at the University of Oslo. He led the planning of the Norwegian–British–Swedish Antarctic Expedition (1949–1952) and chaired the Norwegian committee for the International Geophysical Year. He died in Oslo in 1957.

Legacy

The Sverdrup remains the standard unit for large-scale ocean and river volume transport. The Sverdrup Gold Medal of the American Meteorological Society, the icebreaker and research facilities bearing his name, and Sverdrup Island in the Kara Sea commemorate him.
