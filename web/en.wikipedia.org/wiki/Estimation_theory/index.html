Estimation theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Estimation theory is a branch of statistics that deals with estimating the values of parameters based on measured, empirical data that has a random component. The parameters describe an underlying physical setting in such a way that their value affects the distribution of the measured data. An estimator is a rule or algorithm that computes an estimate of a parameter from the observations.

Approaches

In the classical (frequentist) approach, the parameter is treated as fixed but unknown, and estimators are evaluated by their sampling properties over repeated data. In the Bayesian approach, the parameter is itself a random variable described by a prior distribution, and estimates are derived from the posterior distribution. Classical examples include the maximum likelihood estimator, the method of moments, and least squares.

Properties of estimators

Desirable properties include unbiasedness (the estimate's expected value equals the true parameter), consistency (convergence to the true value as the sample size grows), and efficiency (low variance). The Cramér–Rao bound gives a lower limit on the variance of any unbiased estimator, expressed through the Fisher information; an estimator attaining it is efficient. The mean squared error, combining variance and squared bias, is the standard measure of overall accuracy. Estimation theory underpins signal processing techniques such as the Kalman filter and is central to econometrics, communications and control.
