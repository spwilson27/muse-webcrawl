Back-pass rule

A goalkeeper must play a deliberate back-pass with his feet
SportAssociation football
Governing lawLaw 12 (Fouls and misconduct)
Introduced1992
SanctionIndirect free kick

The back-pass rule is a provision in the Laws of the Game of association football that prohibits a goalkeeper from handling the ball when it has been deliberately kicked to them by a team-mate. Introduced in 1992, the rule is codified in Law 12 and is punishable by an indirect free kick awarded from the place where the goalkeeper handled the ball.

History

Before 1992, goalkeepers could pick up back-passes from defenders at will, a tactic widely used by teams to waste time and kill the momentum of a match. The dour, low-scoring 1990 FIFA World Cup, in which time-wasting by goalkeepers was especially prevalent, prompted FIFA to act. The rule was adopted in 1992 and first applied at the 1992 Summer Olympics and in domestic leagues from the 1992–93 season. It is widely credited with speeding up the game, forcing goalkeepers to develop foot skills, and encouraging pressing by forwards.

Details

The offence occurs only when the ball is deliberately kicked to the goalkeeper by a team-mate and the goalkeeper then touches it with the hand or arm. Deflections, headers, chest passes, and other plays involving parts of the body other than the foot are not covered. Since 1997 the rule has also applied when the goalkeeper handles the ball directly from a team-mate's throw-in. A related provision forbids a goalkeeper from handling the ball if a team-mate uses a deliberate trick — for instance, flicking the ball up to head it back — to circumvent the rule; the player performing the trick is cautioned.

Effects

The rule transformed the role of the goalkeeper: modern keepers are expected to act as a "sweeper-keeper", comfortable receiving passes under pressure and distributing the ball with their feet. It also reduced time-wasting and increased the effective playing time of matches.
