Spacecraft propulsion

Spacecraft propulsion

Spacecraft propulsion is any method used to accelerate spacecraft and artificial satellites. Because there is no medium to push against in space, nearly all methods rely on reaction mass — expelling propellant in one direction to gain momentum in the other, as described by the Tsiolkovsky rocket equation. Performance is characterised by thrust and by specific impulse, the efficiency with which propellant produces velocity change (delta-v).

Chemical propulsion

Chemical rockets burn fuel and oxidiser in a combustion chamber and expel the hot gas through a nozzle. Solid motors are simple and storable; liquid engines (using combinations such as kerosene/LOX, hydrogen/LOX, or methane/LOX) offer higher performance and throttling. Monopropellant and bipropellant thrusters provide small impulses for attitude control and station-keeping.

Electric propulsion

Electric thrusters use electrical power — from solar arrays or nuclear sources — to accelerate propellant to much higher exhaust velocities than chemical rockets, giving far greater specific impulse at very low thrust. Ion thrusters (such as NASA's NEXT and the engines on the Dawn probe), Hall-effect thrusters, and electrothermal devices are used for deep-space missions and satellite station-keeping.

Other methods

Proposed and experimental methods include nuclear thermal rockets, solar sails driven by radiation pressure, electrodynamic tethers, mass drivers, and beamed-energy propulsion. Gravity assists exchange momentum with planets and require no propellant at all.
