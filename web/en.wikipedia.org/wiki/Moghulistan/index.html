Moghulistan, also spelled Mughalistan or Moghul Khanate (from Persian Mughūlistān, "land of the Moghuls"), was a steppe khanate that formed from the eastern part of the Chagatai Khanate in the mid-14th century. It occupied the region north of the Tarim Basin — the Ili valley, the Jetisu (Semirechye) area around Lake Balkhash and Lake Issyk-Kul, and the lands eastward toward Hami and Turpan — corresponding roughly to northern Xinjiang and southeastern Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan today. Its Turkic-Mongol nomadic inhabitants, the "Moghuls", preserved a nomadic way of life longer than their western Chagatai cousins in Transoxiana, who became sedentary and gave rise to the Timurid world; ironically, the term "Mughal" later attached itself to the Indian empire founded by Babur, a Timurid prince who married into the Moghul royal house.

Formation

When the Chagatai Khanate split in the 1340s, the eastern tribes enthroned Tughlugh Timur (c. 1347) as khan of the east. Tughlugh Timur converted to Islam around 1354 — the first Chagatai khan of the east to do so — and twice invaded Transoxiana (1360–61), briefly reuniting the khanate. After his death (1363), his son Ilyas Khoja was defeated by Timur (Tamerlane) at the Battle of Tashkent (1365), and Moghulistan thereafter fought repeated wars with Timur, who campaigned against the Moghuls into the 1390s but never subdued their steppe heartland.

Division and the eastern khanates

In the 15th century the khanate weakened through succession struggles and pressure from the Oirats (Kalmyks). Under Yunus Khan (1462–1487), grandfather of Babur, Moghulistan split: Yunus ruled the west from Tashkent while his son Ahmad Alaq held the east. In the early 16th century, Said Khan — a grandson of Yunus — conquered the Tarim Basin cities and founded the Yarkent Khanate (1514–1705), based at Yarkand and Kashgar, while his brother Mansur Khan ruled the eastern Turpan Khanate. "Moghulistan" thereafter refers chiefly to the northern steppe zone; the Moghul khans of Turpan survived into the late 17th century, when the region was absorbed amid the rise of the Khojas and then the Dzungar Khanate.

Society and sources

The principal historical source is the Tarikh-i Rashidi (c. 1546) of Mirza Muhammad Haidar Dughlat, himself a Moghul prince and cousin of Babur, which chronicles the khans and the powerful Dughlat emirs who dominated Kashgaria. Moghul society combined Mongol imperial traditions, Turkic language (Chagatai), and an increasingly thorough Islamisation led partly by Naqshbandi Sufi networks. The Moghuls' conversion and their wars with Timur and the Oirats are central to the history of Central Asia between the Mongol and early modern periods.
