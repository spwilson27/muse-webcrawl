National Assembly (French Revolution)

National Assembly
French: Assemblée nationale
Existed: June 17, 1789 – July 9, 1789 (then as National Constituent Assembly to September 30, 1791)
Created from: Estates-General of 1789
Seat: Versailles, then Paris

The National Assembly (French: Assemblée nationale) was the revolutionary assembly formed during the French Revolution by the representatives of the Third Estate of the Estates-General. It existed briefly under that name from June 17 to July 9, 1789, after which it added "Constituent" to its title and governed France alongside the monarchy until September 1791.

Formation

When the Estates-General convened at Versailles in May 1789, deadlock arose over whether votes would be counted by order (giving the clergy and nobility a permanent majority) or by head (favoring the Third Estate, which represented the vast majority of the population). On June 17, 1789, the Third Estate, joined by some members of the other orders, declared itself the National Assembly, claiming to represent the nation as a whole. Locked out of their meeting hall on June 20, the deputies swore the Tennis Court Oath not to disperse until France had a constitution. King Louis XVI eventually ordered the remaining deputies of the clergy and nobility to join the Assembly.

Achievements

Sitting as the National Constituent Assembly, it abolished feudalism and noble privilege on the night of August 4, 1789, adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen on August 26, confiscated church lands, enacted the Civil Constitution of the Clergy, reorganized France into departments, and promulgated the Constitution of 1791, which established a constitutional monarchy and the unicameral Legislative Assembly that succeeded it.

Leading figures associated with the Assembly included the Abbé Sieyès, author of the pamphlet What Is the Third Estate?, Honoré Gabriel Riqueti, comte de Mirabeau, and the Marquis de Lafayette.
