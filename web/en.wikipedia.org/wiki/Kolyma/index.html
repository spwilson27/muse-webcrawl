Kolyma

The Kolyma Highway, the "Road of Bones" (image placeholder)

Kolyma (Russian: Колыма́) is a region located in the Russian Far East, corresponding broadly to the basin of the Kolyma River and the territory now divided between Magadan Oblast, the Sakha Republic (Yakutia) and Chukotka. In the 20th century the name became infamous worldwide as the site of one of the largest and most lethal complexes of Gulag forced-labour camps, where prisoners mined gold and other minerals under conditions of extreme cold; the writer Varlam Shalamov's Kolyma Tales is the classic literary testimony of life there.

Geography

Kolyma occupies the far north-east of Siberia between the Arctic Ocean and the Sea of Okhotsk. The Kolyma River flows some 2,129 km northwards from the Kolyma Mountains to the East Siberian Sea. The climate is among the harshest inhabited on earth: winters are long and frigid, with temperatures falling below −50 °C in the interior, while summers are brief and cool. The landscape is tundra in the north and larch taiga in the south, over continuous permafrost. The region is rich in gold, tin and other minerals; placer gold mining around the port city of Magadan (founded 1929) remains central to the economy.

Dalstroy and the camps

From 1931 to 1953 the region was administered by Dalstroy, the Far North Construction Trust, which ran the Sevvostlag camp system with prisoner labour shipped through Magadan. Gold output was the overriding goal; prisoners also mined tin, built roads and towns, and logged timber. Estimates of the number who passed through the Kolyma camps run into the millions, and deaths — from cold, starvation, disease and execution — are estimated in the hundreds of thousands. After Stalin's death in 1953 the system was wound down; Dalstroy was dissolved in 1957 and free labour gradually replaced prisoner labour, though mining towns such as Susuman and Seimchan retained the imprint of the camps.

The Kolyma Highway

The R504 Kolyma Highway, running about 2,000 km from Nizhny Bestyakh near Yakutsk to Magadan, was built largely by prisoner labour in the 1930s–1950s. It is popularly called the "Road of Bones" because of the many prisoners who died building it and were said to have been buried in the roadbed. Long unpaved stretches have been progressively upgraded; the road was opened to year-round travel in the 2000s and remains one of the world's great adventure-driving routes.

Legacy and memorial

Shalamov's Kolyma Tales (written 1954–1973, published in the West in translation) stands alongside Solzhenitsyn's works as an account of the Soviet camps, and unlike Solzhenitsyn, Shalamov insisted the camps were a purely destructive experience. In Magadan, the "Mask of Sorrow" memorial by Ernst Neizvestny (unveiled 1996) commemorates the victims above the site of a transit camp. "Kolyma" remains a byword in Russian for the Gulag at its most brutal.
