1960 Valdivia earthquake

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The 1960 Valdivia earthquake (Spanish: Terremoto de Valdivia), or the Great Chilean earthquake, occurred on 22 May 1960 at 15:11 local time off south-central Chile. With a moment magnitude of 9.4–9.6, it is the most powerful earthquake ever recorded.

The earthquake and tsunami

The rupture, caused by the Nazca Plate subducting beneath South America, extended for roughly 1,000 km of the Chilean coast. Shaking and subsidence destroyed vast areas of Valdivia, Puerto Montt, Concepción and other cities. The quake generated a trans-Pacific tsunami that devastated Hilo, Hawaii (61 deaths), and killed well over a hundred people in Japan, as well as damaging coasts in the Philippines and elsewhere. Two days later, the Cordón Caulle volcano erupted, compounding the disaster.

Death toll and damage

Estimates of the death toll range from about 1,000 to 6,000, with many sources citing roughly 1,655 dead, thousands injured, and some two million people left homeless. Damage was estimated at hundreds of millions of dollars. River courses changed, new lakes and wetlands formed, and the city of Valdivia was partly inundated by subsidence that persists in today's Cruces River wetlands.

Scientific significance

The event provided key evidence for the young theory of plate tectonics: measurements showed the Earth's free oscillations ringing for days, and the scale of the rupture helped scientists establish the concept of megathrust earthquakes and moment magnitude.
