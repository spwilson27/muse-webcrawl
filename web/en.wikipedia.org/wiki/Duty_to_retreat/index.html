Duty to retreat
Legal areaCriminal law; self-defence

In law, a duty to retreat is a condition of the defence of self-defence: before using force — especially deadly force — against an attacker, a person must attempt to retreat or escape if it is safe to do so. Where a duty to retreat applies, a claim of self-defence may fail if the defendant could safely have avoided the confrontation. The doctrine is the counterpart of "stand-your-ground" laws, which expressly remove any such duty.

United States

American jurisdictions are divided. A minority of states impose a duty to retreat before using deadly force in public places; the majority either follow the castle doctrine only, or have adopted stand-your-ground statutes extending no-retreat rules beyond the home. Nearly all jurisdictions recognise an exception, the castle doctrine, under which there is no duty to retreat from an attacker in one's own home. A person who is not the initial aggressor, or who is in a place they have a legal right to be, is treated differently under different states' laws, making the doctrine a recurring topic in debates over self-defence shootings.

Other jurisdictions

In England and Wales there is no strict duty to retreat, but the possibility of retreat is a factor the jury may consider in assessing whether force was reasonable; a person is not generally expected to flee their own home. Many European legal systems require force to be a last resort and proportionate, which in practice imposes similar expectations without a formal retreat rule.
