Whetstone

A whetstone (also sharpening stone or water stone) is a fine-grained stone used to sharpen the edges of steel tools and implements such as knives, chisels, scissors, and razors, by grinding and honing. The word "whet" is an old form of "sharpen" and has nothing to do with water, although many stones are used with water or oil as a lubricant.

Types

Natural stones are quarried from sedimentary or metamorphic rock. Famous examples include Arkansas stones (novaculite), Belgian coticule, and Japanese natural stones. Synthetic stones are made from bonded abrasives — aluminium oxide, silicon carbide, or ceramic — and are more uniform and faster-cutting. Diamond plates bond industrial diamond grit to a steel plate and can flatten other stones as well as sharpen.

Grit and use

Stones are graded by grit size: coarse stones (roughly 200–800 grit) repair chips and reshape edges; medium stones (1000–3000) do routine sharpening; fine stones (4000–12000 and above) polish and hone a razor edge. Water stones are soaked or splashed with water, forming an abrasive slurry; oil stones are used with honing oil. The blade is drawn across the stone at a constant angle, alternating sides, raising a burr that is then refined on progressively finer stones and finished on a strop.

History

Sharpening stones are as old as ground stone tools themselves, and whetstones were prized trade goods in the Viking Age — schist stones from Eidsborg in Norway were exported across northern Europe. The pendant whetstones found in Viking graves may also have had symbolic status.
