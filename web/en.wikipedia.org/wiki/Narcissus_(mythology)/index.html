In Greek mythology, Narcissus (Greek: Νάρκισσος, Nárkissos) was a hunter from Thespiae in Boeotia, the son of the river god Cephissus and the nymph Liriope, who was renowned for his beauty. According to legend, he rejected all romantic advances, including those of the nymph Echo, and was punished by the goddess Nemesis, who made him fall in love with his own reflection in a pool of water. Unable to leave the image of himself, he died beside the pool, and in the place where he rested grew the flower that bears his name — the narcissus, or daffodil. The story is the origin of the term narcissism, a fixation with oneself and one's physical appearance or public perception.

The myth

When Narcissus was born, the seer Tiresias prophesied that the boy would live a long life "if he never knows himself". As he grew into a beautiful youth, he scorned all who loved him. The nymph Echo, cursed by Hera to repeat only the last words spoken to her, fell in love with him; rejected, she wasted away until only her voice remained. Another rejected suitor, Ameinias, killed himself at Narcissus's door after being mockingly sent a sword, calling on the gods to avenge him.

Nemesis, goddess of retribution, answered: she lured Narcissus to a still pool in the woods where, stooping to drink, he saw his own image and fell hopelessly in love with it. Realising at last that the beloved was himself — "I am that one" — he grieved that he could neither possess nor leave his reflection, and wasted away (in some versions stabbing himself, his blood colouring the flower). Where he died sprang the white-and-gold narcissus flower, which nods beside water.

Versions

The best-known telling is Ovid's in Book 3 of the Metamorphoses, which combines the Echo episode with the pool. An earlier version by Conon set the story at Thespiae with Ameinias as the rejected lover, and Pausanias recorded a rationalising variant in which Narcissus gazed into the water to remember a beloved dead twin sister. A Boeotian local tradition treated the flower as native to the region, and the narcissus was also sacred to Persephone and Demeter.

Legacy

The myth has been retold in art and literature for two millennia — in Caravaggio's Narcissus, Waterhouse's Echo and Narcissus (1903), poems by Keats and Rilke, and Oscar Wilde's prose poem "The Disciple". In psychology, Havelock Ellis (1898) and Freud ("On Narcissism", 1914) borrowed the name for self-directed love, giving rise to the modern concept of narcissism and, later, narcissistic personality disorder.
