Margaret Thatcher

Margaret Hilda Thatcher, Baroness Thatcher (née Roberts; 13 October 1925 – 8 April 2013), was a British stateswoman who served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from 1979 to 1990 and Leader of the Conservative Party from 1975 to 1990. She was the longest-serving British prime minister of the 20th century and the first woman to hold the position. As prime minister, she implemented policies that came to be known as Thatcherism. A Soviet journalist dubbed her the "Iron Lady", a nickname that became associated with her uncompromising politics and leadership style.

Early life and education

Thatcher was born in Grantham, Lincolnshire, the daughter of a grocer. She studied chemistry at Somerville College, Oxford, and worked briefly as a research chemist before training as a barrister. She was elected Member of Parliament for Finchley in 1959 and served as Secretary of State for Education and Science in the government of Edward Heath. In 1975 she defeated Heath in the Conservative Party leadership election and became Leader of the Opposition.

Prime Minister

After the Conservative victory in the 1979 general election, Thatcher became prime minister. Her governments pursued monetarist economic policies, deregulation, privatisation of state-owned industries, and reductions in the power of trade unions. In 1982 she led Britain to victory in the Falklands War, which contributed to her re-election in 1983. She won a third term in 1987.

Her premiership saw major events including the miners' strike of 1984–85, the Anglo-Irish Agreement, and closer integration with Europe tempered by increasing Euroscepticism within her party. Opposition to the Community Charge (the "poll tax") and divisions over Europe led to a leadership challenge in November 1990, and she resigned, succeeded by John Major.

Legacy

Thatcher remains a polarising figure in British politics. Supporters credit her with revitalising the British economy and restoring national confidence, while critics blame her policies for increased unemployment and social inequality during her tenure. She died in London on 8 April 2013 and received a ceremonial funeral.
