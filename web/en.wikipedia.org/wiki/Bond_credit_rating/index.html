Bond credit rating

A bond credit rating is an assessment, expressed as a letter grade, of the creditworthiness of a corporation, government, or other issuer of bonds, and by extension of a particular bond issue. The rating expresses the agency's opinion of the issuer's ability and willingness to meet its financial obligations in full and on time. High ratings indicate low perceived credit risk and allow issuers to borrow at lower interest rates.

Rating agencies and scales

The industry is dominated by three agencies — Moody's, Standard & Poor's (S&P), and Fitch Ratings — which together assign the great majority of ratings worldwide. Ratings are divided into investment grade (BBB−/Baa3 and above), considered relatively safe, and speculative grade or "junk" (BB+/Ba1 and below), which carry higher default risk and higher yields.

Uses and significance

Ratings influence the interest rates issuers pay, are embedded in investment mandates (many pension and money-market funds may hold only investment-grade debt), and are used by regulators to set bank capital requirements. Sovereign credit ratings similarly affect governments' borrowing costs and can trigger wider market effects.

Criticism

The "issuer-pays" business model — in which issuers pay for their own ratings — has been criticised as a conflict of interest. The agencies were widely blamed for assigning inflated AAA ratings to complex mortgage-backed securities before the 2008 financial crisis, and for slow downgrades of sovereign debt during the euro-area crisis. Reforms in the United States (Dodd–Frank Act) and the European Union increased oversight and reduced statutory reliance on ratings.
