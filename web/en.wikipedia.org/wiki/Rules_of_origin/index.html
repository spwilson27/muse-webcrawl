Rules of origin

Rules of origin are the criteria used to define where a product was made — its "economic nationality" — for the purposes of international trade. They determine whether an imported good qualifies for preferential tariffs under a free trade agreement, and are also used for anti-dumping measures, quotas, government procurement and origin marking.

Types of rules

Non-preferential rules of origin apply where no trade preference is involved, for example in applying most-favoured-nation tariffs or trade remedies. Preferential rules of origin determine whether goods qualify for the lower duties of a free trade agreement or preference scheme such as the Generalised System of Preferences.

Criteria

A good is generally originating if it is wholly obtained in one country (minerals extracted, animals born and raised, plants harvested there), or, where inputs from several countries are used, if it has undergone substantial transformation. Substantial transformation is commonly tested by:

  • Change of tariff classification — the processing shifts the product into a different heading of the Harmonized System;
  • Value-added (ad valorem) tests — a minimum percentage of the product's value must originate in the preference-giving area;
  • Specific processing rules — particular operations must (or must not) be performed.

Cumulation provisions allow materials from partner countries to count as originating, which can be bilateral, diagonal or full in scope.

Issues

Divergent and complex rules of origin can act as a barrier to trade, raising compliance costs and causing traders to forgo preferences. The WTO Agreement on Rules of Origin aims at harmonising non-preferential rules, though that work remains incomplete. High-profile disputes, such as those over automotive regional value content in NAFTA and its successor USMCA, illustrate their economic significance.
