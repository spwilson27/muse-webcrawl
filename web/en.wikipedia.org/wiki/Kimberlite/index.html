Kimberlite is a rare type of potassic ultramafic igneous rock, best known as the principal source rock of diamonds. It is named after the town of Kimberley in South Africa, where the discovery of an 83.5-carat diamond in 1871 triggered a diamond rush and the excavation of the "Big Hole". Kimberlites occur in the Earth's crust in vertical structures known as kimberlite pipes, together with dykes and sills.

Origin

Kimberlite magmas originate deep in the mantle, from depths of 150 to 450 kilometres — far deeper than most magmas — and rise to the surface with extraordinary speed, entraining fragments (xenoliths) of mantle rock such as peridotite and eclogite. Diamonds form in the mantle at these depths and pressures and are carried up rapidly enough to avoid converting to graphite or resorbing; only about one kimberlite pipe in a hundred contains diamonds in economic quantities, and typically only a fraction of a carat per tonne of rock.

Mineralogy

Fresh kimberlite is a dark bluish rock rich in olivine, with characteristic minerals including phlogopite mica, carbonate, serpentine and the "indicator minerals" used by prospectors — pyrope garnet, chrome diopside, chromite and ilmenite. Near the surface, weathered kimberlite oxidises to a soft yellow-brown material historically called "yellow ground" over the harder unweathered "blue ground".

Distribution

Kimberlites are found mainly in the ancient, stable cores of continents (cratons). Major fields occur in South Africa, Botswana (the Jwaneng and Orapa mines, among the world's richest), Russia's Yakutia, Canada (the Ekati and Diavik mines in the Northwest Territories, discovered in the 1990s), Angola, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Australia (Argyle — a lamproite rather than a kimberlite) and Brazil.
