Luciferin

Luciferin (from Latin lucifer, "light-bearer") is a generic term for the light-emitting compounds found in organisms that generate bioluminescence. Luciferins typically undergo an enzyme-catalysed oxidation — the enzyme being a luciferase — producing an excited-state intermediate that emits light as it returns to the ground state. Different classes of luciferins evolved independently in many lineages.

Types

Firefly luciferin is a benzothiazole compound (D-luciferin) found across beetle families; its oxidation by firefly luciferase requires ATP, magnesium, and oxygen, emitting yellow-green light around 550–570 nm. Coelenterazine is widespread in marine organisms — jellyfish such as Aequorea, shrimp, and many fish — and underlies the photoproteins aequorin and obelin; it requires no ATP but does need calcium ions in some systems. Other classes include bacterial luciferin (a reduced riboflavin phosphate reacting with a long-chain aldehyde), dinoflagellate luciferin (a tetrapyrrole related to chlorophyll), and the fungal luciferin 3-hydroxyhispidin, identified in 2015.

History

In the 1880s, French physiologist Raphaël Dubois extracted from a luminous bivalve a heat-stable substance he named "luciférine" and a heat-labile enzyme he named "luciférase", demonstrating that light production was a chemical reaction rather than a vital force. Firefly luciferin was isolated and its structure determined by William McElroy and colleagues in the mid-20th century, and it was first synthesised in 1961.

Applications

The luciferin–luciferase reaction is a mainstay of biotechnology: reporter-gene assays, ATP-based hygiene and contamination tests, and in-vivo imaging of tumours and gene expression all exploit its sensitivity and low background in mammalian tissue.
