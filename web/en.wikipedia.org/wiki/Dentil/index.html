In classical architecture, a dentil (from Latin dens, a tooth) is a small block used as a repeating ornament in the bedmould of a cornice. Dentils are found in classical Greek and Roman architecture, and also in later styles such as Neoclassical, Federal, Georgian Revival, and Beaux-Arts architecture. A row of dentils is said to form a dentil course, and the regular spacing of the blocks with gaps between them creates a distinctive tooth-like rhythm of light and shadow under the cornice.

History

The Roman architect Vitruvius (c. 80–15 BC) advised that dentils should never be placed below mutules, since the dentils were understood to represent the ends of wooden rafters or beams translated into stone — a petrified echo of earlier timber construction. Dentils are characteristic of the bedmould of the Ionic order and were also taken up in Corinthian and Composite cornices.

Use

Beyond stone architecture, dentil moulding is widely used in interior joinery, cabinet making and furniture, where it appears as a carved or applied strip of small rectangular blocks along cornices, mantels and casework.
