Basal-cell carcinoma

Basal-cell carcinoma (BCC), also known as basal-cell cancer, is the most common type of skin cancer. It often appears as a painless raised area of skin, which may be shiny with small blood vessels running over it, or as a raised area with ulceration. Basal-cell cancer grows slowly and can damage tissue around it but is unlikely to spread to distant areas (metastasize) or result in death.

Cause and diagnosis

Risk factors include exposure to ultraviolet light, having lighter skin, radiation therapy, long-term exposure to arsenic, tanning beds and poor immune function. Childhood sun exposure is particularly harmful. Diagnosis is generally by skin examination confirmed by biopsy; the tumour arises from basal cells of the epidermis, with most cases driven by mutations in the hedgehog signalling pathway, especially the PTCH1 gene — defective in the inherited Gorlin syndrome, which predisposes to numerous BCCs.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment is typically by surgical removal: standard excision, Mohs micrographic surgery (preferred for facial and recurrent lesions), curettage and electrodesiccation, cryotherapy, topical agents such as imiquimod or 5-fluorouracil for superficial tumours, and, for rare advanced cases, hedgehog pathway inhibitors such as vismodegib. Prognosis is generally excellent after complete removal, though people with one BCC are at high risk of further skin cancers.

Epidemiology

BCC is the most common cancer worldwide in fair-skinned populations; at least 32% of white people in some studies develop it during their lifetime. Incidence is highest in Australia and rises with age and cumulative sun exposure.
