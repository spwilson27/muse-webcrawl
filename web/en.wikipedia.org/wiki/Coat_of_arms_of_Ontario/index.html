Coat of arms of Ontario

The coat of arms of Ontario

Armiger: His Majesty the King in Right of Ontario

Adopted: Shield 1868; full achievement 1909

Shield: Vert three maple leaves slipped Or on a chief Argent the Cross of St. George

Supporters: A moose and a deer

Motto: Ut Incepit Fidelis Sic Permanet ("Loyal she began, loyal she remains")

The coat of arms of Ontario is the heraldic symbol of the Canadian province of Ontario. The shield was assigned by royal warrant of Queen Victoria on 26 May 1868, less than a year after Confederation. The crest, supporters and motto were added by royal warrant of King Edward VII on 27 February 1909.

Shield

The shield bears three golden maple leaves, conjoined on a single stem, on a green field — green and gold being Ontario's provincial colours. On a silver chief (upper band) is the red Cross of St. George, the patron saint of England, signifying the province's early ties to England and the importance of the United Empire Loyalists who settled Upper Canada after the American Revolutionary War.

Crest, supporters and motto

The crest is a black bear standing on a gold and green wreath, representing the province's wildlife and northern forests. The supporters are a moose on the dexter side and a deer (whitetail) on the sinister side, both native animals. The motto, Ut Incepit Fidelis Sic Permanet, translates as "Loyal she began, loyal she remains" and refers to the province's origin as a refuge for Loyalist settlers.

History and use

The simple shield of 1868 was among the first arms created for the new Dominion, alongside those of Quebec, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick. These four were initially combined in the first Great Seal of Canada. Today Ontario's arms appear on the provincial flag (in shield form), government buildings, the mace of the Legislative Assembly, and official publications. Under the provincial trademark programme, use of the arms is restricted to official purposes.
