Videoconferencing

Videoconferencing (or video teleconferencing) is the two-way or multipoint reception and transmission of audio and video signals by people in different locations for real-time communication. A videoconference allows individuals and groups to hold face-to-face meetings without travelling to a single location, and typically adds features such as screen sharing, text chat, file transfer, and collaborative whiteboards.

Early videotelephony was demonstrated in the 1920s and 1930s, and AT&T's Picturephone was exhibited at the 1964 New York World's Fair, but high costs limited adoption for decades. Dedicated room systems from companies such as Polycom and Tandberg spread through corporations in the 1990s using ISDN lines and the H.323 standard. Cheap webcams, broadband internet, and software clients — Skype (2003), FaceTime (2010), Zoom (2013), Microsoft Teams, and Google Meet — moved videoconferencing to personal computers and phones.

The remote-work era

During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, videoconferencing became a primary medium for work, education, healthcare (telemedicine), government, and social life, with daily meeting participants on leading platforms numbering in the hundreds of millions. The phenomenon of "Zoom fatigue" — exhaustion attributed to prolonged video meetings — became a subject of psychological research. Videoconferencing has durably reduced business travel and underpins hybrid work, while raising ongoing questions about privacy, security (such as "Zoombombing"), accessibility, and the digital divide.

Technology

A videoconferencing system combines cameras, microphones, displays, and loudspeakers with codecs that compress audio and video (standards such as H.264, H.265, VP9, and AV1) for transmission over IP networks, typically using protocols including SIP, WebRTC, and RTP. Cloud-based multipoint control units mix or switch streams for group calls, and echo cancellation, noise suppression, and bandwidth adaptation maintain call quality.
