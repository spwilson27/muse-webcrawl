Éclair

An éclair is a long, thin individual pastry made with choux pastry, filled with a cream and topped with icing. The dough, which is the same as that used for profiteroles, is piped into an oblong shape with a pastry bag and baked until it is crisp and hollow inside. The pastry is typically filled with custard (crème pâtissière), whipped cream, or chiboust cream, and topped with fondant icing, usually chocolate or coffee flavoured, though fruit, caramel, and other varieties are common.

History

The éclair is believed to have been created in the early 19th century in France, and is often associated with Antonin Carême, the celebrated chef who refined choux pastry and many classic French pastries. Before the mid-19th century, a similar pastry was known as pain à la duchesse. The word éclair is French for "flash of lightning" — folk explanations hold either that the pastry is eaten "in a flash" or that its glossy icing glints like lightning. The Oxford English Dictionary records the word in English from the 1860s.

Preparation

Choux dough made from butter, water, flour, and eggs is piped into fingers on a baking sheet and baked; steam leavens the pastry, leaving a hollow centre. After cooling, the shells are filled with flavoured crème pâtissière and dipped in fondant. Classic flavours are chocolate and coffee; modern pâtissiers produce éclairs in many flavours with elaborate decoration. In North America, the similar Long John doughnut is sometimes informally called an éclair.
