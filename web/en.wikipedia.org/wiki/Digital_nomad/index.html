Digital nomads are people who use telecommunications technologies to earn a living and conduct their lives in a nomadic manner, working remotely from foreign countries, coffee shops, public libraries, co-working spaces, or recreational vehicles rather than from a fixed workplace. The lifestyle grew with cheap air travel, ubiquitous Wi-Fi and laptops, and expanded rapidly after the COVID-19 pandemic normalised remote work.

Profile and hubs

Digital nomads work disproportionately in software development, design, online marketing, writing, consulting and other knowledge work, mixing employment, freelancing and online businesses. Popular destinations combine low living costs, good internet, pleasant climate and visa convenience; long-standing hubs include Chiang Mai (Thailand), Bali (Indonesia), Lisbon (Portugal), and Mexico City. Estimates of the global population run to the tens of millions, though definitions vary — US survey firms estimated around 17–18 million American digital nomads in the early 2020s.

Legal and social issues

Nomads often navigate grey areas of immigration and tax law: many work while on tourist visas, prompting more than 50 countries and territories (including Estonia, Barbados, Croatia, Portugal and Spain) to introduce dedicated digital-nomad visas with income thresholds and tax terms. Critics highlight effects on host communities — rising rents and gentrification in popular neighbourhoods — as well as nomads' own challenges: loneliness, healthcare access, and insecure legal status.
