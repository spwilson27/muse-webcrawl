Amateur telescope making is the activity of designing and constructing telescopes — most often Newtonian reflectors — as a hobby, rather than buying finished instruments. Makers may grind and polish their own mirrors, build tubes and mountings, and test the optics themselves. The craft combines optics, woodworking, and machining, and it has historically given amateurs access to apertures far beyond what they could afford commercially.

History

The modern movement began in the 1920s when Albert G. Ingalls, an editor at Scientific American, promoted the work of Russell W. Porter, who had taught telescope making in Springfield, Vermont. Ingalls's columns and the three volumes of Amateur Telescope Making (from 1926) spread the craft worldwide. The Springfield Telescope Makers' annual Stellafane convention, begun in 1926, remains a centre of the hobby. In the 1960s John Dobson revolutionised the field with the Dobsonian design — a large Newtonian on a simple, inexpensive altazimuth mount of plywood and Teflon bearings — popularised through the San Francisco Sidewalk Astronomers.

Mirror making and testing

The heart of the craft is figuring a parabolic primary mirror: a glass blank is ground against a tool with successively finer abrasives, polished with pitch laps, and tested for a figure accurate to a fraction of a wavelength of light using the Foucault knife-edge test or a Ronchi grating, before being coated with aluminium. Amateurs also build refractors, Schmidt cameras, binocular telescopes, and computerised (GoTo) mounts, and in recent decades have adopted CNC machining, 3D printing, and commercial optics in otherwise home-built instruments.
