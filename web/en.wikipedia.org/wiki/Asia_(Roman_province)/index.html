Asia was a Roman province covering the western, Aegean-facing part of Anatolia in what is now Turkey. It was one of Rome's richest and most urbanized provinces, and its name — originally that of the Attalid kingdom of Pergamon — was later extended to the whole continent of Asia. The province is also notable in early Christian history as the location of the seven churches addressed in the Book of Revelation.

Formation

In 133 BC, King Attalus III of Pergamon died and bequeathed his kingdom to the Roman people. After the revolt of Aristonicus (who claimed the throne as Eumenes III) was suppressed, the consul Manius Aquillius organized the territory as the province of Asia in 129 BC, with Ephesus as its capital. The province encompassed Mysia, Lydia, Caria, and part of Phrygia, containing famous Greek cities such as Ephesus, Pergamon, Smyrna, Sardis, Miletus, and Laodicea.

Republican exploitation

Under the Republic, taxation of Asia was farmed out to Roman publicani (tax-farming companies), whose exactions made the province a byword for exploitation and generated deep resentment. Governor Quintus Mucius Scaevola and his legate Rutilius Rufus were praised for restraining them. In 88 BC, during the First Mithridatic War, the cities of Asia joined Mithridates VI of Pontus in the "Asiatic Vespers," a coordinated massacre of Romans and Italians in the province, with ancient sources giving figures as high as 80,000 killed. Sulla recovered the province and imposed a crushing indemnity of 20,000 talents.

Imperial period

Under the Empire, Asia became a senatorial province governed by a proconsul of consular rank — one of the most prestigious posts in a senatorial career. Its cities prospered, competing in titles and temple-building; Ephesus, Smyrna, and Pergamon rivalled each other for the honour of being "first of Asia" and for imperial temples (neocorates). The provincial council (koinon) of Asia organized the imperial cult. Diocletian's reforms subdivided the province into smaller units within the diocese of Asiana.
