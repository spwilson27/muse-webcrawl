Linear logic

Linear logic is a substructural logic proposed by Jean-Yves Girard in 1987 as a refinement of classical and intuitionistic logic, joining the dualities of the former with many of the constructive properties of the latter. It treats propositions as resources that, unlike truths in classical logic, can be consumed only once: the structural rules of contraction and weakening are restricted.

Connectives

Because resources matter, linear logic splits the classical conjunction and disjunction into multiplicative and additive pairs: tensor (⊗) and par (⅋), plus (⊕) and with (&). The exponential modalities ! and ? mark resources that may be reused or discarded freely. Linear implication (⊸) consumes its premise to produce its conclusion.

Applications

Linear logic has influenced computer science, notably in the semantics of concurrent and resource-sensitive computation, the design of type systems such as Rust's ownership model (related to affine logic), quantum computing, and linguistics.
