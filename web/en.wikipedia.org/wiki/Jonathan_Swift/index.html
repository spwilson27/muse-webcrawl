Jonathan Swift

Jonathan Swift (30 November 1667 – 19 October 1745) was an Anglo-Irish satirist, author, essayist, political pamphleteer, poet, and Anglican cleric who became Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin. He is remembered for Gulliver's Travels, A Modest Proposal, A Tale of a Tub, and An Argument Against Abolishing Christianity, and is regarded as the foremost prose satirist in English.

Life

Born in Dublin to English parents, Swift was educated at Kilkenny College and Trinity College Dublin, then worked in England as secretary to Sir William Temple at Moor Park, where he met Esther Johnson ("Stella"). Ordained in the Church of Ireland, he wrote for Tory ministries, edited The Examiner, and befriended Pope, Gay, and Arbuthnot in the Scriblerus Club. After the Tory fall in 1714 he returned to Ireland as Dean of St Patrick's.

Works

A Tale of a Tub (1704) satirised religious excess. The Drapier's Letters (1724) helped defeat Wood's halfpence patent and made him an Irish patriot. Gulliver's Travels (1726), a satire on human nature and travel literature, became a classic read in abridged form as a children's book. A Modest Proposal (1729) ironically suggested the poor sell their children as food, a masterpiece of Juvenalian satire.

Later life and legacy

Plagued by Ménière-like vertigo and mental distress, Swift endowed St Patrick's Hospital for imbeciles in his will. He is buried in St Patrick's Cathedral beside Esther Johnson.
