Levetiracetam, sold under the brand name Keppra among others, is an anticonvulsant medication used to treat epilepsy. It is used for partial-onset, myoclonic, and tonic-clonic seizures, and is taken by mouth in immediate-release or extended-release formulations or by injection into a vein. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is available as a generic medication. Levetiracetam belongs to the racetam family and is chemically related to the nootropic piracetam; it was developed by the Belgian company UCB and approved in the United States in 1999.

Medical uses

Levetiracetam is effective as adjunctive therapy and, in many jurisdictions, as monotherapy for focal (partial-onset) seizures in adults and children, and as adjunctive therapy for myoclonic seizures in juvenile myoclonic epilepsy and for primary generalised tonic-clonic seizures. Intravenous levetiracetam is widely used in hospital practice for acute repetitive seizures and as an alternative agent in status epilepticus; a large trial (ESETT, 2019) found it comparably effective to fosphenytoin and valproate as second-line therapy. Typical adult doses range from 500 mg to 1,500 mg twice daily, adjusted downward in renal impairment.

Side effects

Common side effects include sleepiness, dizziness, weakness, and irritability. Behavioural adverse effects — irritability, agitation, and mood change, sometimes nicknamed "Keppra rage" — are a notable reason for discontinuation, particularly in children and people with prior psychiatric history; a small minority develop depression or, rarely, psychosis. Serious effects are uncommon. Unlike many antiepileptic drugs, levetiracetam is not significantly metabolised by the liver's cytochrome P450 system and has minimal drug interactions, a major reason for its popularity. It is excreted largely unchanged by the kidneys.

Mechanism of action

The precise mechanism is not fully understood. Levetiracetam binds to synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A (SV2A), a protein involved in neurotransmitter release; this binding is believed to modulate synaptic transmission and dampen abnormal neuronal synchronisation. Its much more potent analogue brivaracetam targets the same protein. Levetiracetam does not act through the classical anticonvulsant mechanisms of sodium-channel blockade or GABA potentiation.
