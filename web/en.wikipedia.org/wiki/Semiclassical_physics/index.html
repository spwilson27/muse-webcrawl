Semiclassical physics

Semiclassical physics, or simply semiclassical, refers to a theory in which one part of a system is described quantum-mechanically while another part is treated classically. Semiclassical approximations are widely used where full quantum-mechanical or quantum-field-theoretic calculations are intractable, and they often capture important physical effects while remaining computationally manageable.

Examples

Well-known semiclassical methods include the WKB approximation for wavefunctions in slowly varying potentials; semiclassical gravity, in which spacetime is classical while matter fields are quantised; the treatment of atoms interacting with a classical electromagnetic field in quantum optics; and molecular dynamics schemes in which nuclei move on classical trajectories while electrons are treated quantum-mechanically (as in the Born–Oppenheimer approximation). In condensed-matter physics, the Boltzmann transport equation with band-theoretic inputs is a semiclassical description of electron motion in solids.

Applications and limits

Semiclassical methods are used in atomic and molecular physics, quantum chaos, chemical dynamics, and astrophysics. Their validity depends on the separation of scales between the classical and quantum degrees of freedom; where quantum coherence of the "classical" part matters, the approximation breaks down and a fully quantum treatment is required.
