Hazing

Hazing
DefinitionAbusive or humiliating initiation practices imposed on newcomers to a group
Common settingsFraternities and sororities, sports teams, military units, schools
Legal statusCriminal offence in most US states and other jurisdictions

Hazing (American English; in British English, related practices are sometimes called ragging, fagging, or bullying) refers to rituals and other activities involving harassment, abuse, or humiliation used as a way of initiating a person into a group, organisation, or team. Hazing is typically imposed on pledges, rookies, or newcomers regardless of their willingness to participate, and is distinct from legitimate initiation or team-building because it involves a power imbalance and the risk of physical or psychological harm.

Forms

Hazing ranges from relatively mild practices — demeaning names, forced servitude, sleep deprivation, scavenger hunts, and compulsory dress — to dangerous ones, including forced consumption of alcohol or other substances, paddling and beatings, branding, exposure to weather, kidnapping, and sexual acts. Forced alcohol consumption is the most common cause of hazing deaths in colleges.

Prevalence and harm

Studies in the United States have found that a majority of students who belong to campus groups experience some form of hazing, and that deaths and serious injuries recur annually, particularly in fraternities and athletics. Defenders argue that shared ordeals build group loyalty; psychologists note that the severity of an initiation does increase attachment to a group (an effort-justification effect), but that positive, inclusive activities achieve cohesion without the dangers.

Law and policy

Most US states have anti-hazing statutes, and the federal Stop Campus Hazing Act (2024) requires colleges to report hazing incidents and publish prevention policies. Universities and national fraternities prohibit hazing, and organisations and individuals have faced criminal prosecutions, civil suits, chapter closures, and expulsions after hazing deaths, such as those of Tim Piazza (Penn State, 2017) and Max Gruver (LSU, 2017).
