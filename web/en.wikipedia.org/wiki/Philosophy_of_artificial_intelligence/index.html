Philosophy of artificial intelligence

Philosophy of artificial intelligence
Key questions: Can machines think? Can a machine have a mind, consciousness, and mental states? Is the brain a computer?
Key figures: Alan Turing, John Searle, Hubert Dreyfus, Daniel Dennett, David Chalmers
Related fields: Philosophy of mind, cognitive science, computer science, ethics of AI

The philosophy of artificial intelligence is a branch of the philosophy of technology that explores questions about artificial intelligence and its implications for knowledge, mind, ethics and society. It asks whether machines can genuinely think and understand, or only simulate thought, and what the creation of intelligent machines would show about human cognition.

Can a machine think?

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" reframed the question "Can machines think?" in terms of the imitation game (the Turing test): if a machine's conversation is indistinguishable from a human's, we may reasonably call it intelligent. Positions in the ensuing debate include strong AI — the claim, associated with functionalism and computationalism, that a suitably programmed computer literally has a mind — and weak AI, on which machines merely model aspects of cognition.

Arguments against machine minds

John Searle's Chinese room argument (1980) contends that manipulating symbols according to rules — as a computer following a program does — produces no understanding or intentionality, so strong AI is false. Hubert Dreyfus, drawing on Heidegger and phenomenology, argued that human expertise is embodied and intuitive rather than rule-based, and criticised symbolic AI's assumption that the world can be fully represented as facts. Other objections invoke consciousness and qualia: even a perfect behavioural duplicate, critics argue, might lack subjective experience.

Responses and related issues

Defenders reply with the systems reply, robot reply and connectionist approaches, and philosophers such as Daniel Dennett argue that intentionality is a matter of predictive stance rather than inner magic. The field also encompasses the mind–body problem as applied to machines, machine consciousness, superintelligence and existential risk, and the ethics of artificial agents — including questions of moral status, responsibility and bias.
