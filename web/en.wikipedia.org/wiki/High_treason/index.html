High treason

High treason is the crime of disloyalty to one's state or sovereign, traditionally regarded as the gravest offence against the state. The concept originated in English law, where treason was divided into high treason — offences against the monarch and the security of the realm — and petty treason, the killing of a lawful superior by an inferior.

English law

The Treason Act 1351 remains the principal statute defining high treason in the United Kingdom. It covers compassing or imagining the death of the sovereign, levying war against the sovereign in the realm, and adhering to the sovereign's enemies, giving them aid and comfort. Historically the penalty was to be hanged, drawn and quartered for men, and death remains the maximum penalty in some jurisdictions, though in the United Kingdom the death penalty for treason was abolished in 1998 and replaced by life imprisonment.

Famous cases

Notable prosecutions include the Gunpowder Plot conspirators of 1605, Charles I (tried by a High Court of Justice in 1649), and William Joyce ("Lord Haw-Haw"), the last person executed for high treason in the United Kingdom, in 1946. Roger Casement was hanged for treason in 1916 for seeking German support for Irish independence.

Modern usage

Many states retain treason offences in their criminal codes, though prosecutions are now rare in peacetime democracies. The United States Constitution narrowly defines treason as levying war against the United States or adhering to its enemies, and requires two witnesses to the same overt act for a conviction.
