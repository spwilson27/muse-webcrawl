Sex and gender distinction

The distinction between sex and gender differentiates sex — a set of biological attributes associated with physical and physiological features such as chromosomes, gene expression, hormone levels, and reproductive anatomy — from gender, which refers to the socially constructed roles, behaviours, expressions, and identities of men, women, and gender-diverse people.

Terminology

The modern usage developed in the mid-20th century. Psychologist John Money introduced the term "gender role" in the 1950s in work on intersex people, and feminist scholars in the 1970s, such as Ann Oakley, adopted and popularised the sex/gender distinction to separate biological difference from culturally variable roles and expectations. The World Health Organization summarises: "sex" refers to the biological and physiological characteristics that define humans as female or male, while "gender" refers to the socially constructed characteristics of women, men, girls and boys.

Applications

The distinction is widely used in medicine, psychology, sociology, law, and public policy — for example, to design research that accounts for both biological sex and social gender, to analyse health disparities, and to frame anti-discrimination protections. It also underlies concepts such as gender identity, a person's internal sense of their own gender.

Criticism and debate

Scholars debate the adequacy of a strict binary distinction. Some philosophers and biologists argue that sex itself is partly interpreted through social frameworks; others defend the distinction as pragmatically useful while acknowledging that neither sex nor gender is strictly binary in all cases, pointing to intersex variation and cross-cultural gender categories. Usage also varies across disciplines and languages, and many style guides now give guidance on when to use each term.
