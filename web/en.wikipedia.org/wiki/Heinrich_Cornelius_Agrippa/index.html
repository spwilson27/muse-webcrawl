Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa von Nettesheim (14 September 1486 – 18 February 1535) was a German Renaissance polymath: physician, soldier, theologian, legal scholar and occult writer. He is best known for his De occulta philosophia libri tres (Three Books of Occult Philosophy, printed 1531–1533), the most comprehensive synthesis of Renaissance magic.

Life

Born in Nettesheim near Cologne, Agrippa led an itinerant career across Germany, France, Italy, England and the Low Countries, serving patrons including Margaret of Austria and Louise of Savoy, and lecturing on theology and medicine. His sceptical work De incertitudine et vanitate scientiarum (On the Uncertainty and Vanity of the Sciences and Arts, 1526) attacked the learning of his age, while a treatise on the nobility of women argued for female superiority.

Occult philosophy

The Three Books systematised the magical worldview of the Renaissance, drawing on Kabbalah, Neoplatonism, astrology and natural magic, and dividing reality into elemental, celestial and intellectual worlds. The work profoundly influenced later occultism; a spurious "fourth book" circulated under his name.

Legacy

Agrippa's reputation as a sorcerer fed legends linking him to the Faust tradition, though modern scholarship treats him as a serious, if controversial, humanist thinker.
