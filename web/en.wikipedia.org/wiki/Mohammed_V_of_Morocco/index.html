Mohammed V of Morocco

Mohammed V (10 August 1909 – 26 February 1961) was Sultan of Morocco from 1927 to 1953 and, after a period of exile imposed by the French protectorate, again from 1955 to 1957, becoming the first King of independent Morocco from 1957 until his death in 1961. A symbol of Moroccan nationalism, he is revered as the father of the nation's independence.

Sultan under the protectorate

The youngest son of Sultan Youssef, Mohammed was chosen by the French Resident General over his elder brothers and enthroned in 1927 at eighteen. Initially pliant, he gradually aligned himself with the nationalist movement: he refused to apply anti-Jewish Vichy decrees to Moroccan Jews during the Second World War, delivered the landmark Tangier speech of April 1947 calling for independence and Arab unity, and increasingly clashed with the protectorate authorities.

Exile and return

In August 1953 France deposed and exiled him — first to Corsica and then to Madagascar — installing Mohammed Ben Aarafa in his place. The move backfired: unrest, violence, and a national uprising (the Revolution of the King and the People) forced France to negotiate. Mohammed V returned in triumph in November 1955, and Morocco gained independence in 1956. He took the title of king in 1957 and laid the foundations of the modern Moroccan state before dying unexpectedly in 1961 after minor surgery. His mausoleum in Rabat, where he lies with his sons Hassan II and Prince Abdallah, is a masterpiece of Moroccan architecture.
