Deaf President Now (DPN) was a week-long protest in March 1988 at Gallaudet University in Washington, D.C., the world's only university designed for deaf and hard-of-hearing students. The protest began after the university's board of trustees selected Elisabeth Zinser, a hearing candidate, as Gallaudet's seventh president over two deaf finalists. Students, faculty and alumni demanded a deaf president, arguing that after 124 years the institution should be led by a member of the community it served. The protest is widely considered a landmark moment in the history of the Deaf community and of disability rights in the United States.

Background

Gallaudet was founded in 1864 and chartered by President Abraham Lincoln. Despite serving deaf students, it had never had a deaf president. When President Jerry C. Lee announced his resignation in 1987, the search for a successor drew strong hopes that a deaf candidate would finally be chosen. The two deaf finalists were Harvey Corson and I. King Jordan, dean of the College of Arts and Sciences. On 6 March 1988 the board announced the selection of Zinser, a hearing administrator from the University of North Carolina at Greensboro who did not know American Sign Language.

The protest

Students reacted with anger, marching to the Mayflower Hotel where the board was meeting and then blockading the campus gates. Classes were boycotted and the university was effectively shut down. The protesters issued four demands: Zinser's resignation and the appointment of a deaf president; the resignation of board chair Jane Bassett Spilman; an increase in deaf representation on the board to a 51% majority; and no reprisals against protesters. Student leaders including Bridgetta Bourne, Jerry Covell, Greg Hlibok and Tim Rarus became the public faces of the movement, which received extensive national media coverage and support from civil rights figures.

Outcome

After a week of mounting pressure, Zinser resigned on 10 March. Spilman also stepped down. On 13 March the board appointed I. King Jordan as Gallaudet's first deaf president, and all four demands were met. Jordan's remark that "Deaf people can do anything except hear" became an enduring slogan of deaf empowerment.

Legacy

Deaf President Now is credited with galvanising the American Deaf community, contributing momentum to the passage of the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990, and transforming expectations about deaf leadership in education and public life.
