E-Fuel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A power-to-liquid pilot plant producing electrofuel.

E-fuels, also known as electrofuels, are a class of synthetic fuels produced from captured carbon dioxide (or carbon monoxide) and hydrogen obtained from water electrolysis powered by low-carbon — usually renewable — electricity. The production route is commonly called power-to-liquid (for liquid fuels) or power-to-gas (for gaseous fuels such as e-methane). Common products include e-methanol, e-diesel, e-gasoline (e-petrol), e-kerosene for aviation, and e-methane.

Production

The basic process combines three steps. First, water is electrolysed to produce hydrogen using electricity. Second, carbon dioxide is obtained either from industrial point sources, from biogas, or directly from the air (direct air capture). Third, the hydrogen and carbon dioxide are combined in a synthesis step — for hydrocarbons, typically a reverse water–gas shift followed by Fischer–Tropsch synthesis; for methanol, direct catalytic hydrogenation of CO₂. Because the carbon released on combustion was previously captured, e-fuels can in principle be close to carbon-neutral, provided the electricity used is low-carbon.

Applications and policy

E-fuels are of particular interest for sectors that are difficult to electrify directly, above all aviation and shipping, because they are "drop-in" compatible with existing engines, aircraft, ships and fuel infrastructure. The European Union's ReFuelEU Aviation rules introduce a sub-mandate for synthetic aviation fuels, and e-fuels have been debated in the context of the EU's 2035 rules on new internal-combustion cars, with Germany pressing for an exemption pathway for vehicles running exclusively on e-fuels. Porsche and partners opened the Haru Oni pilot plant in Patagonia, Chile — a windy region chosen for cheap renewable power — to produce e-gasoline from 2022.

Criticism

The principal criticism of e-fuels is their low overall energy efficiency: converting electricity to hydrogen, synthesising fuel, and burning it in an engine typically delivers only about 10–20% of the original electricity as useful work, compared with roughly 70–80% for a battery-electric drivetrain. Production costs are correspondingly high, and critics argue scarce green hydrogen and renewable electricity are better used where direct electrification is possible. Supporters respond that e-fuels can decarbonise the existing vehicle fleet and long-haul transport, and can store surplus renewable energy.
