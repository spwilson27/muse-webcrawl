Buddhism in Japan

Buddhism in Japan has been practised for well over a millennium and has profoundly shaped Japanese culture, art, and thought. Buddhism was officially introduced in 538 or 552 CE, when the king of Baekje (Korea) sent a Buddha image and scriptures to the Yamato court. It spread under the patronage of Prince Shōtoku and, in the Nara period, became a state-supported religion centred on great temples such as Tōdai-ji, whose Great Buddha was consecrated in 752.

Major schools

In the Heian period, Saichō founded Tendai and Kūkai founded Shingon, both centred on the new capital's protective mountains. The Kamakura period produced the popular Pure Land movements of Hōnen (Jōdo-shū) and Shinran (Jōdo Shinshū), the militant exclusivism of Nichiren, and Zen — Eisai's Rinzai and Dōgen's Sōtō schools — patronised by the samurai class. Zen aesthetics in turn influenced tea ceremony, ink painting, gardens, and Noh theatre.

Shinto relations and suppression

Buddhism long coexisted and fused with Shinto (shinbutsu-shūgō), with kami worshipped as manifestations of buddhas. The Meiji government forcibly separated them in 1868 (shinbutsu bunri) and a wave of persecution (haibutsu kishaku) destroyed many temples and images.

Modern Japan

Today most Japanese funerals are Buddhist while weddings and festivals are often Shinto; large numbers of Japanese identify with both traditions. Major organisations include traditional denominations and new religious movements such as Soka Gakkai and Risshō Kōsei Kai. Zen and Japanese Buddhist thought have also exerted notable influence in the West.
