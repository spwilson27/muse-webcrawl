Rule 90

Rule 90 is an elementary cellular automaton notable for generating the Sierpiński triangle fractal from simple initial conditions. It belongs to Stephen Wolfram's classification of one-dimensional binary cellular automata, in which each cell's next state depends on its current state and those of its two neighbours.

Definition

In Rule 90, the new state of a cell is the XOR (exclusive or) of the states of its two neighbours: a cell becomes alive (1) if exactly one of its neighbours is alive, and dead (0) if both or neither are. The name "90" comes from the Wolfram code: writing the outputs for the eight possible neighbourhoods (111, 110, 101, 100, 011, 010, 001, 000) as a binary number gives 01011010, which is 90 in decimal.

The Sierpiński triangle

Starting from a single live cell on an otherwise empty row, Rule 90 produces a discrete Sierpiński triangle—a fractal of nested triangular holes with Hausdorff dimension log(3)/log(2) ≈ 1.585. The number of live cells after step n relates to the binary digit count of n; at steps that are powers of two, only two cells are alive. Because the update rule is addition modulo 2, the state of the automaton after n steps from a single cell reproduces the nth row of Pascal's triangle modulo 2, which is precisely what generates the fractal.

Properties

Rule 90 is additive and reversible in structure: the evolution is linear over the field GF(2), so the behaviour from multiple starting cells is the superposition (XOR) of the evolutions from each cell alone. Starting from random initial conditions, the pattern self-organises into interlocking triangular regions. The automaton has been used in designs for pseudorandom number generation and features frequently in discussions of how complexity emerges from simple rules.
