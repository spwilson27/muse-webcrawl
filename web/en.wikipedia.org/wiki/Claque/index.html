Claque

Audience members of a Parisian theatre in the 19th century, the heyday of the professional claque.

A claque is an organised group of professional applauders in French theatres and opera houses. Members of a claque are called claqueurs. Hiring a claque became a widespread practice in the 19th century, above all at the Paris Opéra, where the success or failure of a premiere could be influenced — and sometimes decided — by paid applause, laughter, weeping, or booing.

History

Paid applause dates to antiquity: the Roman emperor Nero maintained a corps of young men to applaud his own performances. In 16th-century France, the poet Jean Dorat is said to have hired applauders for performances of his works. The modern claque, however, was an institution of 19th-century Paris. Around 1820, a certain M. Sauton established an agency — an assurance de succès dramatique — that supplied theatre managers and performers with professional applauders for a fee.

The claque was led by a chef de claque, who met with performers and producers before a performance, decided where and how strongly applause should be applied, and collected payment. The chef distributed tickets to his team and sat in a conspicuous position — often in the middle of the parterre — so that his applause could serve as a cue.

Roles within the claque

The claque was a specialised workforce:

  • Commissaires — learned the work by heart and pointed out its merits to their neighbours during the intervals.
  • Rieurs — laughed loudly at the jokes in comedies.
  • Pleureuses — women hired to weep conspicuously at affecting scenes, sometimes dabbing their eyes with handkerchiefs.
  • Chatouilleurs ("ticklers") — kept the audience in good humour throughout the evening.
  • Bisseurs — clapped and shouted bis! ("again!") to demand encores.

The claque could also be deployed negatively: rival impresarios might hire claques to hiss and boo. Composers and singers were often obliged to pay the chef de claque as a kind of insurance; Richard Wagner and other visiting artists complained bitterly of the practice, and the extent of the payments demanded shaded into extortion.

Decline

The institution faded in the later 19th and early 20th centuries as theatrical taste, criticism, and recording changed the economics of reputation. The word survives metaphorically for any organised body of supporters who praise or applaud on cue — in politics, for example, a candidate's rent-a-crowd may be called a claque.
