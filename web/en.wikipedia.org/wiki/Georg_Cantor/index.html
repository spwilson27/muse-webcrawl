Georg Cantor

Georg Cantor

Born: 3 March 1845, Saint Petersburg, Russian Empire

Died: 6 January 1918, Halle, Germany

Known for: Set theory, transfinite numbers, diagonal argument

Georg Ferdinand Ludwig Philipp Cantor (3 March 1845 – 6 January 1918) was a German mathematician who created set theory, now a foundational theory of mathematics. He established the importance of one-to-one correspondence between the members of two sets, defined well-ordered sets, and proved that the real numbers are more numerous than the natural numbers — showing that there are infinitely many sizes of infinity.

Life

Cantor was born in Saint Petersburg to Danish-German parents and moved with his family to Germany in 1856. He studied at the University of Berlin under Weierstrass, Kummer and Kronecker, and spent his career at the University of Halle from 1869, becoming full professor in 1879. His later years were marked by recurring bouts of depression and poverty; he died in a sanatorium in Halle in 1918.

Work

In 1874 Cantor published the first of his revolutionary papers, proving the countability of the algebraic numbers and the uncountability of the reals. His 1891 diagonal argument gave a simpler, powerful proof that no set can be put into one-to-one correspondence with its power set (Cantor's theorem). He developed the theory of cardinal and ordinal numbers and the arithmetic of transfinite numbers, and posed the continuum hypothesis, which Hilbert placed first on his famous list of problems in 1900.

Reception

Cantor's ideas were fiercely opposed by Leopold Kronecker and initially by many contemporaries, but were championed by David Hilbert, who declared: "No one shall expel us from the paradise that Cantor has created."
