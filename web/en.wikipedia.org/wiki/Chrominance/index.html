Chrominance is the colour information of an image, conventionally separated from the luminance (brightness) information. Together they describe the full picture: luminance carries the detail and sharpness perceived by the eye, while chrominance carries hue and saturation. The separation made colour television backward-compatible with black-and-white receivers and remains the foundation of all modern image and video compression.

History

Colour television standards of the 1950s–1960s — NTSC, PAL and SECAM — encoded colour as small high-frequency chrominance subcarrier signals added to a compatible luminance signal; a monochrome set simply ignored them. In digital video the same split lives on as Y'CbCr (or YUV), where Y' is luma and Cb/Cr are the two chroma channels.

Relationship to compression

Because human vision resolves colour detail far more coarsely than brightness, chroma channels are routinely subsampled (see chroma subsampling) and quantised more aggressively than luma in JPEG, H.264, HEVC and successors, yielding large compression gains with little visible loss.
