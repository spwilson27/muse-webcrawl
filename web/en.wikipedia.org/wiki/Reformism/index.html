Reformism

Reformism is a political philosophy and a type of socialism that advocates the reform of an existing system or institution instead of its abolition and replacement via revolution. Within the socialist movement, reformism is the view that gradual changes through existing institutions can eventually lead to fundamental changes in a society's political and economic systems.

History

Reformist socialism was articulated by Eduard Bernstein in Evolutionary Socialism (1899), who argued, revising Marxism, that capitalism was becoming more flexible and that socialists should pursue change through democratic elections and trade unions. This sparked the revisionism controversy with orthodox Marxists such as Rosa Luxemburg, whose Reform or Revolution (1900) argued reforms without revolutionary transformation could not achieve socialism.

Modern usage

After the Second World War, many Western European social democratic parties adopted reformist programmes — accepting a mixed economy while expanding welfare states — exemplified by the German SPD's 1959 Bad Godesberg programme. The term is also used more broadly for gradualist approaches in other ideologies and movements, contrasted with revolutionary or reactionary change, and is related to incrementalism in policy-making.

Criticism

Critics from the revolutionary left argue reformism manages capitalism rather than transcending it and is vulnerable to rollback; defenders argue it delivers durable, democratic improvements in living standards without the costs of revolution.
