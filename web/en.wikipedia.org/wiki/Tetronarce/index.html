Tetronarce is a genus of electric rays in the family Torpedinidae, commonly known as torpedo rays. The genus was formerly subsumed within Torpedo, but molecular and morphological work in the 2010s led to its resurrection for the larger torpedo species. Species of Tetronarce are generally bigger and more robust than Torpedo species, with larger, more widely spaced spiracles and other distinguishing features.

Description

Like all torpedinids, Tetronarce species have a rounded, flabby pectoral disc, a short thick tail with two dorsal fins and a well-developed caudal fin, and a pair of large kidney-shaped electric organs at the base of the pectoral fins. These organs, made of modified muscle tissue, can generate powerful electric discharges — up to around 220 volts in the Atlantic torpedo — used to stun prey and deter predators. The skin is smooth and scaleless, and coloration is typically uniform dark brown, grey, or purplish above and pale below.

Species and distribution

Recognised species include the Atlantic torpedo (Tetronarce nobiliana) of the Atlantic Ocean and Mediterranean, the Pacific electric ray (T. californica) of the north-eastern Pacific, the Argentine torpedo (T. puelcha), the New Zealand torpedo (T. fairchildi), the Australian torpedo (T. macneilli), the Gulf torpedo (T. sinuspersici) of the Indo-Pacific, and others — about nine species in total. They are benthic, found on sandy or muddy bottoms from shallow coastal waters to depths of several hundred metres, and feed mainly on bony fishes and invertebrates. Reproduction is aplacental viviparous (ovoviviparous).

Relation to humans

The electric discharge is painful but rarely dangerous to humans. Some species are taken as bycatch in trawl fisheries; several are listed as Data Deficient or of conservation concern by the IUCN owing to limited information.
