Kalimba

The kalimba is a modern lamellaphone — a musical instrument in the idiophone family — consisting of metal tines mounted on a wooden soundboard or box resonator, played by plucking the tines with the thumbs. It is a twentieth-century development of the African mbira family of instruments.

History

Lamellaphones have existed in Africa for over a thousand years, first with bamboo tongues and later with forged metal keys; the mbira of the Shona people of Zimbabwe is the most famous traditional form. In the 1950s the English ethnomusicologist Hugh Tracey, who had recorded and studied African lamellaphones for decades, designed and marketed a diatonically tuned instrument under the name "Kalimba" (a Bantu word for a small lamellaphone), arranged like a piano keyboard to be accessible to Western musicians.

Playing

The player holds the instrument in both hands and plucks the tines downward with the thumbnails; alternating-thumbs patterns generate interlocking melodies and harmonies from the alternating left–right note layout. Kalimbas commonly have 7 to 17 tines. The instrument's bright, bell-like tone has made it popular in world music, film soundtracks, meditation music and viral internet performance.
