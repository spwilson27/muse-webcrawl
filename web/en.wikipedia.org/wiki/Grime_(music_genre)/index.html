Grime is a genre of electronic dance music and British rap that emerged in London in the early 2000s. It developed out of UK garage, jungle and dancehall on East London pirate radio stations, and is characterised by fast, syncopated breakbeats at around 140 BPM, sparse and angular synthesiser riffs — often cold, "eski" square-wave sounds — heavy sub-bass, and rapid-fire MCing, frequently in competitive "clash" formats between rival MCs.

History

Grime crystallised around 2002–2003 in the Bow area of East London. Key early figures include Wiley, credited as the genre's "godfather" and creator of the eski beat sound; Dizzee Rascal, whose 2003 debut Boy in da Corner won the Mercury Prize; and crews and collectives such as Roll Deep, Ruff Sqwad, Nasty Crew and later Boy Better Know (founded by Skepta and Jme). Distribution ran through pirate radio (Deja Vu 92.3, Rinse FM), DVDs such as Lord of the Mics, and independent record shops.

After a period of commercial crossover and then underground retrenchment in the late 2000s, grime enjoyed a major revival in the mid-2010s: Skepta's Konnichiwa (2016) won the Mercury Prize, and Stormzy's Gang Signs & Prayer (2017) became the first grime album to top the UK chart. Grime's rhythms and MC culture also fed into later British styles including UK drill, even as the genres remained distinct and sometimes rivalrous.

Characteristics

Production is typically built in software using minimal, futuristic synth leads, "sinogrime" pentatonic melodies or eski clicks and glides, with bars structured in 8- and 16-bar units for MCs to trade verses. Lyrically grime centres on London street life, territorial loyalty, braggadocio and social commentary, delivered in local accents and slang — a deliberate contrast to the Americanised delivery of earlier UK hip hop.
