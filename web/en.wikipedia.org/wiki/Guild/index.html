A guild is an association of artisans and merchants who oversee the practice of their craft/trade in a particular territory. The earliest types of guild formed as organizations of tradespeople belonging to a professional association. They sometimes depended on grants of letters patent from a monarch or other ruler to enforce the flow of trade to their self-employed members.

Guilds are sometimes said to have their origin in the Roman collegia, but they reached their height in the towns of medieval Europe from roughly the 11th to the 16th centuries.

Structure

Guild membership typically progressed through three grades: apprentice, journeyman, and master. An apprentice served a master for a term of years in exchange for training; a journeyman worked for wages by the day; a master, having produced a qualifying "masterpiece" and paid fees, could open his own workshop and take apprentices.

Functions

Guilds regulated prices, quality, hours of work, and entry into a trade. They also provided social welfare for members—supporting widows and orphans, funding funerals, maintaining chapels, and staging civic pageants. Merchant guilds controlled long-distance trade, while craft guilds regulated local manufacture.

Decline

From the 16th century onward, guilds came under pressure from growing national markets, state mercantilism, new manufacturing outside guild jurisdictions, and Enlightenment criticism of monopoly privilege. Many were abolished during the French Revolution era; in England they faded gradually during the Industrial Revolution. Modern survivals include the livery companies of the City of London.
