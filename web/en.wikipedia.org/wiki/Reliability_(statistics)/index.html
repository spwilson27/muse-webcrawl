Reliability (statistics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Consistency of repeated measurement

In statistics and psychometrics, reliability is the overall consistency of a measure. A measure is said to have a high reliability if it produces similar results under consistent conditions: "It is the characteristic of a set of test scores that relates to the amount of random error from the measurement process that might be embedded in the scores."

Reliability does not imply validity. That is, a reliable measure that is measuring something consistently is not necessarily measuring what it is supposed to be. For example, while there are many reliable tests of specific abilities, not all of them would be valid for predicting, say, job performance. However, reliability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for validity: a test cannot be valid if it is not reliable.

Types of reliability

Test-retest reliability is assessed by administering the same test twice at two different points in time and correlating the scores. Inter-rater reliability measures the degree of agreement between different observers assessing the same phenomenon. Parallel forms reliability compares two different versions of a measurement tool created with the same content. Internal consistency reliability examines whether the items within a single test measure the same construct, commonly estimated by Cronbach's alpha.

Classical test theory

In classical test theory, an observed score is the sum of a true score and an error score. Reliability is defined as the ratio of true score variance to observed score variance. Since true scores cannot be observed directly, reliability must be estimated indirectly from the observed data, for example by the methods listed above.

Estimation

Cronbach's alpha, developed by Lee Cronbach in 1951, measures reliability as a function of the number of test items and the average inter-item correlation. Other estimators include the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), split-half reliability corrected with the Spearman–Brown formula, and, in generalizability theory, the generalizability coefficient.
