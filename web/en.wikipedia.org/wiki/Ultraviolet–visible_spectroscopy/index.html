Ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy or ultraviolet–visible spectrophotometry (UV–Vis) refers to absorption spectroscopy or reflectance spectroscopy in part of the ultraviolet and the full, adjacent visible regions of the electromagnetic spectrum. Being relatively inexpensive and straightforward to implement, it is one of the most widely used analytical techniques in chemistry and materials science.

Principle

Molecules absorb ultraviolet or visible light when the photon energy matches the energy needed to promote an electron from an occupied molecular orbital to an unoccupied one — most commonly π → π*, n → π*, or charge-transfer transitions. The instrument records absorbance as a function of wavelength. Absorbance is related to concentration by the Beer–Lambert law, A = εlc, where ε is the molar absorptivity, l the path length and c the concentration, allowing quantitative analysis of solutions.

Instrumentation

A UV–Vis spectrophotometer consists of a light source (typically a deuterium lamp for ultraviolet and a tungsten–halogen lamp for visible light), a monochromator to select wavelengths, a sample holder for a cuvette, and a detector such as a photomultiplier tube or photodiode array. Instruments may be single-beam or double-beam, the latter comparing the sample against a reference simultaneously.

Applications

UV–Vis is used for quantitative determination of transition-metal ions, conjugated organic compounds and biological macromolecules; for measuring reaction kinetics; for determining the optical band gap of semiconductors; and as a detector in liquid chromatography. The colour of a chemical is directly related to the wavelengths it absorbs — a solution appears in the complementary colour of the light absorbed.
