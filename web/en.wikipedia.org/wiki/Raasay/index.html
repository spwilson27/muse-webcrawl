Raasay

Raasay (Scottish Gaelic: Ratharsair) is an island between the Isle of Skye and the mainland of Scotland. It is separated from Skye by the Sound of Raasay and from Scalpay by the Inner Sound. It is famous for being the birthplace of the Scottish Gaelic poet Sorley MacLean, an important figure in the Scottish Gaelic Renaissance.

Location: Inner Hebrides, Scotland
Area: 62.32 km² (24.06 sq mi)
Highest elevation: Dùn Caan, 443 m (1,453 ft)
Population: 161 (2011)
Main settlement: Inverarish

Geography

Raasay is about 14 miles (23 km) long and up to 3 miles (5 km) wide. The terrain is varied, with the flat-topped Dùn Caan being its highest point and most recognisable landmark, visible from much of Skye. The island's geology is complex and was the subject of a classic study by the geologist Ben Peach and others; it includes Torridonian sandstone, Jurassic sediments, and Tertiary igneous intrusions. The island of Rona lies just off its northern tip.

History

The island was long held by the MacLeods of Raasay, a branch of Clan MacLeod. Prince Charles Edward Stuart hid on Raasay briefly after the defeat at Culloden in 1746, and Dr Samuel Johnson visited in 1773, an occasion recorded by James Boswell. Raasay House, the former laird's seat, dates from this period. During the First World War, German prisoners worked the island's ironstone mine. The island was bought by the government in 1922. More recently, Raasay House has operated as an outdoor centre, and the Isle of Raasay Distillery opened in 2017, producing whisky and gin.

Culture

Raasay is strongly associated with the poet Sorley MacLean (Somhairle MacGill-Eain), who was born at Osgaig in 1911 and whose work, much of it rooted in the island's landscape and history, transformed modern Gaelic poetry. The island hosts an annual music event, and Gaelic remains an important part of its cultural identity.

Transport

A car ferry operated by Caledonian MacBrayne runs from Sconser on Skye to the new ferry terminal at Suisnish, near Inverarish, a crossing of about 25 minutes.
