Antonio de Mendoza

Antonio de Mendoza y Pacheco (c. 1490 – 21 July 1552) was a Spanish colonial administrator who served as the first viceroy of New Spain (1535–1550) and later as the second viceroy of Peru (1551–1552). Nicknamed "the Good Viceroy" (el buen virrey), he is considered one of the most capable administrators of the early Spanish Empire, overseeing the consolidation of Spanish rule in Mexico, sponsoring expeditions of exploration, and founding institutions that shaped colonial society.

Early life

Mendoza was born into the powerful House of Mendoza, one of Castile's great noble families; his father Íñigo López de Mendoza was Count of Tendilla and Captain-General of Granada. Antonio served at court and as ambassador to Hungary before Charles V appointed him in 1535 to govern New Spain, where royal authority needed to be asserted over the conquistadors, above all Hernán Cortés.

Viceroy of New Spain

Arriving in Mexico City in November 1535, Mendoza organised the viceregal administration, regulated the encomienda system, and attempted (with limited success) to curb abuses against indigenous peoples, in line with royal policy and the New Laws of 1542. He founded the first printing press in the Americas (1539), established the mint of Mexico City, encouraged mining at Zacatecas and elsewhere, and promoted missions and schools, including the Colegio de Santa Cruz de Tlatelolco for indigenous elites. The great illustrated ethnographic manuscript known as the Codex Mendoza was commissioned under his patronage.

Exploration

Mendoza sponsored several major expeditions: the journey of Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca's reports prompted Francisco Vázquez de Coronado's expedition to the north (1540–1542) in search of the Seven Cities of Cíbola; Hernán Cortés's and later fleets explored the Pacific coast; and in 1542 Juan Rodríguez Cabrillo sailed up the California coast while Ruy López de Villalobos crossed the Pacific to the Philippines. He also dispatched Fray Marcos de Niza and Estéban the Moor on reconnaissance northward.

Mixtón War

From 1540 to 1542 Mendoza personally took the field to suppress the Mixtón War, a major indigenous uprising in Nueva Galicia, defeating the Caxcan rebels after hard fighting. The campaign drew criticism for its severity but restored Spanish control in the northwest.

Viceroy of Peru and death

In 1550 Mendoza was transferred to Peru, despite age and ill health; he reached Lima in September 1551 but died less than a year later, on 21 July 1552, and was buried in Lima Cathedral. His son Francisco de Mendoza became a noted admiral and administrator.

Legacy

Mendoza County's namesakes include the Argentine city of Mendoza (founded by colonists from his viceroyalty's sphere) and numerous places in Mexico. Historians credit him with laying the institutional foundations of the viceroyalty that endured for three centuries.
