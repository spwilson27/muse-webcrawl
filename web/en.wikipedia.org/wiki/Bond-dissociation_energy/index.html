Bond-dissociation energy

Bond-dissociation energy
Symbol: BDE, D0, DH°
Type: Enthalpy change
Units: kJ/mol
Process: Homolytic bond cleavage A–B → A• + B•
Measured at: typically 298 K

The bond-dissociation energy (BDE, also called bond-dissociation enthalpy) is the standard enthalpy change when a chemical bond is cleaved by homolysis in the gas phase, that is, when the bond breaks so that each fragment retains one of the bonding electrons, producing two radicals. It is a measure of the strength of a specific bond in a specific molecule.

Definition and values

For a molecule A–B, the BDE is the enthalpy change of the reaction A–B → A• + B•. Typical values include about 436 kJ/mol for H–H, roughly 413 kJ/mol for a typical C–H bond in alkanes, and about 347 kJ/mol for a C–C single bond, while multiple bonds are stronger (C=C ~614 kJ/mol, C≡C ~839 kJ/mol). The bond-dissociation energy of a particular bond differs from the average bond enthalpy tabulated for a bond type because the successive bonds in a polyatomic molecule break with different energies; for example, removing the four hydrogens from methane requires four different step energies.

Uses

BDEs are used to estimate reaction enthalpies through Hess's-law cycles, to predict the stability of radicals (weaker C–H bonds correspond to more stable carbon radicals), and to rationalize reactivity in free-radical reactions, combustion, and atmospheric chemistry.
