Franck–Condon principle

FieldSpectroscopy, quantum mechanics
Named afterJames Franck, Edward Condon
Proposed1926

Vertical transitions between potential energy curves

The Franck–Condon principle describes the intensities of vibronic transitions — simultaneous changes in a molecule's electronic and vibrational energy levels — in absorption and fluorescence spectroscopy. Proposed by James Franck in 1925 and given quantum-mechanical form by Edward Condon in 1926, it is a cornerstone of molecular spectroscopy.

Statement

Electronic transitions occur so rapidly (in about a femtosecond) that the nuclei, being far heavier, do not appreciably change position or momentum during the transition. On a diagram of potential energy versus internuclear distance, transitions are therefore drawn as vertical lines. The most probable vibrational level of the excited state is the one whose geometry most resembles that of the ground state, and the transition intensity is proportional to the squared overlap of the vibrational wavefunctions — the Franck–Condon factor.

Consequences

The principle explains why absorption bands show progressions of vibrational peaks, why the strongest absorption often does not correspond to the pure electronic (0–0) transition, and the Stokes shift between absorption and emission spectra. It also underlies the analysis of photoelectron spectra and electron-transfer reactions.
