Ampère's force law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In magnetostatics, Ampère's force law — formulated by André-Marie Ampère in the 1820s — describes the force of attraction or repulsion between two current-carrying wires. It is the physical basis on which the SI unit of electric current, the ampere, was historically defined.

The special case of parallel wires

For two long, straight, parallel wires separated by a distance r and carrying currents Ia and Ib, the force per unit length on each wire is:

F/L = μ₀ Ia Ib / (2π r)

where μ₀ is the permeability of free space. Currents flowing in the same direction attract; currents in opposite directions repel. Until the 2019 redefinition of the SI base units, the ampere was defined as the current that, in two such wires one metre apart in vacuum, would produce a force of exactly 2 × 10−7 newtons per metre of length.

General form

Ampère's original formulation gives the force between two infinitesimal current elements in terms of their magnitudes, the distance between them, and the angles between the elements and the line joining them. Ampère derived his law from meticulous null-balance experiments in 1820–1825, within weeks of Ørsted's discovery that electric currents deflect compass needles. The general element-to-element law is mathematically equivalent, for closed circuits, to the modern formulation using the Biot–Savart law combined with the Lorentz force.

History and significance

Ampère's experiments founded the science he named "electrodynamics" and placed the interaction between currents at the heart of magnetism, supporting his hypothesis that magnetism itself arises from circulating electric currents within matter.
