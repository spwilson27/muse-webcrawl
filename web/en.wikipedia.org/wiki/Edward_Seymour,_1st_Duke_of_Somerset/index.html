Edward Seymour, 1st Duke of Somerset (c. 1500 – 22 January 1552), known as the Lord Protector, was an English nobleman who governed England as regent during the first years of the reign of his nephew, the young King Edward VI, from 1547 to 1549. He was the eldest surviving brother of Jane Seymour, Henry VIII's third queen, and rose to power through military service and court favour.

Rise to power

Seymour distinguished himself as a soldier in France and Scotland, leading the burning of Edinburgh in 1544 during the "Rough Wooing." When Henry VIII died in January 1547, Henry's will had named a council of regency for the nine-year-old Edward VI, but Seymour manoeuvred to be created Duke of Somerset and Lord Protector with near-royal powers. That September he won a crushing victory over the Scots at the Battle of Pinkie.

Protectorate

Somerset pushed the English Reformation decisively toward Protestantism: the repeal of heresy laws, the dissolution of the chantries, the first Book of Common Prayer (1549), and the Act of Uniformity. Socially minded by the standards of the day — he sympathised with complaints against enclosure and was remembered by commoners as "the Good Duke" — he nevertheless failed to prevent the twin rebellions of 1549, the Prayer Book Rebellion in the West and Kett's Rebellion in Norfolk, and his expensive wars against Scotland and France drained the treasury.

Fall and death

In October 1549 the Privy Council, led by John Dudley, Earl of Warwick, deposed Somerset; he was imprisoned in the Tower. Released and restored to the council in 1550, he was arrested again in 1551 on a constructed charge of treason and executed on Tower Hill on 22 January 1552, despite public sympathy. His London residence, Somerset House on the Strand, was completed only after his death.
