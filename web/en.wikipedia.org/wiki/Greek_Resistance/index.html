Greek Resistance

The Greek Resistance (Greek: Εθνική Αντίσταση) is the collective name for the armed and unarmed resistance movements that opposed the Axis occupation of Greece (1941–1944) during the Second World War. It was among the largest resistance movements in occupied Europe, fielding guerrilla armies that freed much of the mountainous interior and a civil infrastructure that governed liberated areas.

Occupation

After the German-led invasion of April 1941, Greece was partitioned among Germany, Italy, and Bulgaria. Occupation brought hyperinflation and the Great Famine of 1941–42, in which tens of thousands died in Athens and Piraeus, spurring mass resistance. One famous early act was the tearing down of the swastika from the Acropolis by Manolis Glezos and Apostolos Santas in May 1941.

Principal organisations

The largest movement was the communist-led National Liberation Front (EAM) and its army ELAS, which built the "Free Greece" of the mountains and in 1944 a rival government, the PEEA. The republican EDES under Napoleon Zervas operated mainly in Epirus, and the smaller EKKA in central Greece. British SOE missions liaised with the movements; in November 1942 a joint ELAS–EDES force with British commandos blew up the Gorgopotamos railway viaduct, cutting supply lines to Rommel's Afrika Korps. Struggles between the resistance groups, however, broke into open civil conflict from late 1943.

Liberation and aftermath

German forces withdrew in October 1944, but the return of the government-in-exile led within weeks to the Dekemvriana clashes in Athens and, after a period of persecution of the left, to the Greek Civil War of 1946–1949. Official recognition of the resistance was long politically contentious; the EAM resistance was formally recognised by the Greek state only in 1982.
