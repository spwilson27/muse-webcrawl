Africanfuturism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Africanfuturism is a cultural and literary aesthetic and a subcategory of science fiction centred on Africa and its cultures, history, mythology and points of view. The term is most closely associated with the Nigerian-American writer Nnedi Okorafor, who promoted it from 2019 to distinguish work rooted in African — rather than African-American or Western — experience from the broader label Afrofuturism.

Distinction from Afrofuturism

Afrofuturism, a term coined by critic Mark Dery in 1993, grew out of the African diaspora in the Americas and often engages with the legacies of slavery and alienation in the West. Okorafor argued that Africanfuturism is "specifically and more directly rooted in African culture, history, mythology and point-of-view" and does not privilege or centre the West; its imagined futures grow from African cosmologies, languages and landscapes, and it typically does not need to explain or translate them for an outside audience. Earlier African speculative writing — such as the works of Buchi Emecheta, Ben Okri and Kojo Laing — is often read retrospectively within the category.

Themes and works

Africanfuturist works blend advanced technology with indigenous spirituality, oral tradition and mythology: Okorafor's Binti trilogy (a Himba girl travelling to an interstellar university), her novels Who Fears Death and Lagoon, and Tade Thompson's Rosewater trilogy (set in a Nigeria transformed by alien contact) are prominent examples. The 2018 film Black Panther, with its hidden hyper-technological African kingdom of Wakanda, is frequently discussed in relation to both Afrofuturism and Africanfuturism. The genre's growth has been supported by African speculative-fiction magazines, anthologies and prizes such as the Nommo Awards of the African Speculative Fiction Society, founded in 2016.
