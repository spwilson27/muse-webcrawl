Hematology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hematology (also spelled haematology in British English) is the branch of medicine concerned with the study of the cause, prognosis, treatment, and prevention of diseases related to blood. It involves treating diseases that affect the production of blood and its components, such as blood cells, hemoglobin, blood proteins, bone marrow, platelets, blood vessels, the spleen, and the mechanism of coagulation.

Scope

Hematologists and hematopathologists treat conditions including:

  • Anemias — iron-deficiency anemia, hemolytic anemia, aplastic anemia, and sickle cell disease
  • Hemoglobinopathies — thalassemia and sickle cell disease
  • Bleeding and clotting disorders — hemophilia, von Willebrand disease, thrombocytopenia, and thrombotic disorders
  • Hematological malignancies — leukemias, lymphomas, multiple myeloma, and myelodysplastic syndromes
  • Transfusion medicine and stem cell transplantation

Diagnosis

Key laboratory methods include the complete blood count (CBC), blood film (peripheral smear) examination, coagulation studies (PT, aPTT), bone marrow aspiration and biopsy, flow cytometry, cytogenetics, and molecular testing. Hematology laboratories perform much of the routine diagnostic testing in hospital medicine.

Training

Hematology is a subspecialty of internal medicine or pediatrics. In the United States, physicians complete residency in internal medicine followed by a fellowship in hematology and/or medical oncology, which are frequently combined. In the United Kingdom, haematology is a standalone specialty combining clinical practice and laboratory pathology.

History

The field grew from early microscopical study of blood in the 17th–19th centuries, through the identification of blood cells and groups, and expanded rapidly with chemotherapy for leukemia, bone marrow transplantation, and targeted therapies such as imatinib for chronic myeloid leukemia.
