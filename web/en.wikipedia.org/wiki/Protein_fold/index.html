Protein fold

Classification: SCOP, CATH databases
Levels: Secondary structure arrangement and topology
Known folds: ~1,000–1,500 distinct folds estimated

A protein fold is a recurring three-dimensional arrangement of secondary structure elements — alpha helices and beta sheets — in a protein, defined by both the spatial arrangement of those elements and the topological connections between them. Proteins that share a fold may have quite different amino-acid sequences and functions, though shared folds often hint at common evolutionary ancestry.

Classification

Structural classification systems such as SCOP (Structural Classification of Proteins) and CATH (Class, Architecture, Topology, Homologous superfamily) organize domains hierarchically by fold. Common folds include the Rossmann fold of nucleotide-binding proteins, the immunoglobulin fold, the TIM barrel, and the four-helix bundle.

Significance

The number of distinct folds in nature appears limited — often estimated at only a few thousand — compared with the huge space of possible sequences. Fold recognition is a key step in protein structure prediction, and databases of folds underpin comparative modelling and drug-design work.
