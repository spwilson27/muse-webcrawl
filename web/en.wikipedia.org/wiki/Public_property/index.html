Public property

Public property is property owned by a government or community on behalf of the public, as distinct from private property owned by individuals or firms, and from common property held by a defined group. It includes public land, buildings such as schools, libraries and hospitals, infrastructure such as roads and water systems, and state enterprises.

Legal character

Legal systems treat public property under special rules. In civil-law countries, the public domain (domaine public) consists of assets devoted to public use that are inalienable and imprescriptible — they cannot be sold or acquired by adverse possession while so classified. In the United States, the federal government owns about 640 million acres, roughly 28% of the country's land area, managed chiefly by the Bureau of Land Management, Forest Service, National Park Service and Fish and Wildlife Service. The public trust doctrine holds that certain resources — navigable waters and submerged lands — are held by the state for public use and cannot be wholly alienated.

Use and access

Public property varies in openness: roads and parks are open to all; military bases and offices are restricted. In US constitutional law the public forum doctrine distinguishes traditional public forums such as streets and parks, where speech receives the strongest protection, from non-public forums. Management debates cover privatisation and asset sales, leasing of public assets, encroachments, and the "tragedy of the commons" — a problem of common-pool resources rather than of state property as such.

Distinctions

Public property is not the same as the public domain in intellectual property law (works free of copyright), nor as "public space", which can include privately owned spaces open to the public. State property in socialist economies historically extended to most productive assets; mixed economies place a narrower range — natural monopolies, defence, public goods — in public hands.
