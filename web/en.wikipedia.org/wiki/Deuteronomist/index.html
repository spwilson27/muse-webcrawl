Deuteronomist

The Deuteronomist, abbreviated Dtr, is one of the sources identified by the documentary hypothesis as having authored or edited portions of the Hebrew Bible, also collectively called the Deuteronomistic History. The term was coined by Martin Noth in his 1943 work Überlieferungsgeschichtliche Studien, in which he argued that a single author-editor working during the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE) composed a unified history comprising Deuteronomy, Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings, framing Israel's story with the theology of Deuteronomy.

The Deuteronomistic History

The History runs from Moses' farewell speeches in Deuteronomy through the conquest (Joshua), the period of the judges, the rise of the monarchy (Samuel), and its division and fall (Kings) ending with the release of Jehoiachin in Babylon. Its unifying theology is covenantal: Israel prospers when it worships Yahweh alone at the single chosen sanctuary (Jerusalem) and obeys the law, and is punished with disaster — culminating in exile — when it does not. Kings are judged almost entirely by this cultic standard.

Composition theories

Scholars debate how many hands produced the History. Frank Moore Cross and the "Harvard school" proposed a double redaction: a first edition (Dtr1) produced in the reign of Josiah (late 7th century BCE), celebrating his reforms as the climax of history, and an exilic revision (Dtr2) explaining the catastrophe. The "Göttingen school" (Rudolf Smend and followers) instead identified multiple exilic layers, including a nomistic (law-oriented) redactor. Some scholars further connect the Deuteronomist with the circle of the prophet Jeremiah, whose book bears strong Deuteronomistic editorial influence.

Style and ideology

Deuteronomistic writing is recognisable by a distinctive sermon-like prose and recurring formulas — injunctions to obey "with all your heart and all your soul", warnings against serving "other gods", and the insistence that sacrifice be offered only at "the place that the LORD will choose".
