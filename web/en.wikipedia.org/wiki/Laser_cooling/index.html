Laser cooling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Diagram of Doppler laser cooling: an atom absorbing a red-detuned photon from a laser beam opposing its motion]
Doppler cooling: atoms preferentially absorb photons opposing their motion.

Laser cooling includes several techniques by which atoms and molecules can be slowed and cooled to temperatures near absolute zero using laser light. In the basic Doppler method, photons slightly below an atomic resonance ('red-detuned') are preferentially absorbed by atoms moving toward the beam — because the Doppler effect shifts them into resonance — and each absorption slows the atom; re-emission in random directions averages to zero net push, so the ensemble cools.

Proposed in 1975 by Hänsch and Schawlow and by David Wineland and Dehmelt, Doppler cooling was demonstrated in the 1980s and extended by sub-Doppler (Sisyphus) mechanisms, reaching microkelvin temperatures. Together with evaporative cooling it enabled Bose–Einstein condensates (1995), optical atomic clocks, atom interferometers and trapped-ion Quantum computing. The 1997 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Steven Chu, Claude Cohen-Tannoudji and William Daniel Phillips 'for development of methods to cool and trap atoms with laser light'.

How cold

Doppler cooling limits sit near 100 μK for alkali atoms; polarisation-gradient (Sisyphus) cooling breaks through to a few μK; Raman sideband cooling (used by Wineland for ions) reaches the motional ground state. A magneto-optical trap (MOT, 1987) combines cooling with confinement and is the standard front end of cold-atom laboratories — a glowing ball of atoms at a millionth of a degree, colder than deep space.
