Clear-cell carcinoma

Clear-cell carcinoma is a type of carcinoma — a cancer arising from epithelial cells — in which the tumour cells have a characteristic clear appearance under the microscope. The clarity results from abundant cytoplasmic glycogen and lipid that are dissolved during routine tissue processing, leaving the cells looking empty or "clear". Clear-cell carcinomas can arise in several organs, most notably the kidney, ovary, endometrium, and vagina.

Types

Clear-cell renal cell carcinoma is the most common subtype of kidney cancer in adults, accounting for roughly 70–80% of renal cell carcinomas. It is strongly associated with loss or mutation of the VHL tumour suppressor gene on chromosome 3p, leading to stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors and a highly vascular tumour.

Clear-cell ovarian carcinoma is a subtype of epithelial ovarian cancer frequently associated with endometriosis; it often carries mutations in ARID1A and PIK3CA and tends to be relatively resistant to platinum-based chemotherapy. Clear-cell carcinoma of the endometrium is an uncommon, aggressive form of uterine cancer. Vaginal and cervical clear-cell adenocarcinoma became historically notable after its association in the 1970s with in-utero exposure to diethylstilbestrol (DES).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on histopathology, typically supplemented by immunohistochemistry (for example, CAIX and CD10 positivity in the renal type, and HNF-1β and napsin A in gynaecological types). Treatment depends on the organ of origin and stage, and may include surgery, anti-angiogenic targeted therapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors in renal disease, and surgery with chemotherapy in ovarian and endometrial disease.
