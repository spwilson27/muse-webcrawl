Thomas Edison

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Thomas Edison in his laboratory, an older man in a suit standing among electrical equipment, famous portrait photograph]
Edison in 1928.

Thomas Alva Edison (1847–1931) was an American inventor and businessman, described as America's greatest inventor. He developed many influential devices, including the phonograph, the motion picture camera and the first commercially practical electric light bulb, and created the first industrial research laboratory at Menlo Park, New Jersey. He held 1,093 US patents and founded companies that became General Electric.

2. Legacy

Career

Edison began as a telegraph operator, improving stock tickers and telegraph systems. His Menlo Park laboratory (1876) produced the phonograph (1877) and, after thousands of trials, a long-lasting incandescent lamp (1879), followed by whole systems for generating and distributing electric power. His rivalry with Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse over direct versus alternating current is known as the 'war of the currents'.

Legacy

Edison's model of organised, team-based invention — and his skill at commercialising technology — shaped modern R&D. Critics note he sometimes took credit for employees' work and fought aggressive patent battles, notably in the film industry.
