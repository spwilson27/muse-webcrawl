The MMR vaccine controversy began in 1998 with the publication of a fraudulent research paper in The Lancet by Andrew Wakefield and colleagues, which falsely claimed a link between the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine, a form of bowel disease, and autism. The paper received wide publicity and led to a sharp drop in vaccination rates in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, followed by increased incidence of measles and mumps.

Investigation and retraction

No subsequent reputable study reproduced the claims, and large epidemiological studies found no association between MMR and autism. Journalist Brian Deer's investigations, published in the Sunday Times and the BMJ, showed that Wakefield had undisclosed financial conflicts of interest, had manipulated evidence, and had conducted invasive procedures on children without ethical approval. The Lancet fully retracted the paper in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register by the General Medical Council.

Scientific consensus

There is overwhelming scientific consensus that the MMR vaccine is safe and effective and that no link exists between the vaccine and autism. Health authorities including the World Health Organization identify vaccine hesitancy fuelled by the controversy as a significant threat to public health.

Legacy

Despite the retraction, the claims continued to circulate and were amplified by anti-vaccination movements. The episode is widely cited as a case study in scientific fraud, media responsibility and the durability of health misinformation.
