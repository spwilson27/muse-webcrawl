Blue-plate special

[Hero image: a blue-plate special of meatloaf, mashed potatoes and peas served on a divided blue plate in an American diner]

In the United States, a blue-plate special is a discount-priced meal, usually changing daily, offered in inexpensive restaurants, especially diners. It typically consists of a meat and two or three vegetable side dishes, served on a single plate. The term became common during the Great Depression and remained a fixture of American diner culture into the mid-20th century.

Origins

Several explanations exist for the name. The most widely accepted is that the meals were served on a distinctive blue plate — often a divided plate, of a type made by several manufacturers, sometimes decorated with a willow pattern — that allowed the components of the meal to be served quickly and cheaply. Another account traces the term to the Fred Harvey railroad restaurants, which used blue-patterned china. The phrase is recorded in print from the 1920s.

Railroad dining cars and early diners adopted the blue plate to speed service and standardise portions; the "special" was a fixed menu of the day sold at a bargain price, allowing kitchens to use seasonal ingredients and leftovers efficiently.

Cultural references

The blue-plate special became a symbol of working-class American food. The retailer Kmart ran "Blue Light Special" promotions whose name played on the term. In popular usage "blue plate special" can also denote any standard, no-frills offering presented as a bargain.
