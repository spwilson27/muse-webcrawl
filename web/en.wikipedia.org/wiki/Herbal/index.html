Herbal

A herbal is a book containing the names and descriptions of plants, usually with information on their medicinal, culinary, toxic, hallucinogenic, aromatic, or magical properties, and in some cases the lore associated with them. Herbals were among the first literature produced in ancient Egypt, China, India, and Europe, and the first medical literature of these cultures. Many early herbals were also the foundation of botanical science.

History

The most influential herbal of antiquity was De materia medica by the Greek physician Dioscorides (1st century AD), which described some 600 plants and remained a standard reference for over 1,500 years. Medieval herbals, often lavishly illustrated manuscripts copied from classical sources, circulated in monasteries; the Old English Herbarium of Apuleius Platonicus is a notable example. Printing transformed the genre: the German "fathers of botany" — Otto Brunfels, Hieronymus Bock, and Leonhart Fuchs, whose De historia stirpium (1542) set new standards of accuracy in illustration — produced landmark printed herbals, and in England John Gerard's Herball, or Generall Historie of Plantes (1597) and Nicholas Culpeper's astrological Complete Herbal (1653) became enduringly popular.

Decline and legacy

As botany separated from medicine in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, the herbal gave way to the scientific flora and the pharmacopoeia. Herbals remain important sources for the history of medicine, plant nomenclature, and garden design, and interest in herbal medicine has kept classics such as Culpeper's in print to the present day.
