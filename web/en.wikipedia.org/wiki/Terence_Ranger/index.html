Terence Ranger

Terence Ranger
Born: 29 November 1929, South Norwood, London, England
Died: 3 January 2015, Oxford, England
Occupation: Historian
Known for: African history; the "invention of tradition"

Terence Osborn Ranger (29 November 1929 – 3 January 2015) was a British historian of Africa, particularly of Zimbabwe. He was a pioneering figure in the study of African resistance to colonialism and co-editor, with Eric Hobsbawm, of the influential volume The Invention of Tradition (1983).

Career

Ranger was educated at the University of Oxford, where he took his doctorate. In 1957 he joined the University College of Rhodesia and Nyasaland in Salisbury (now Harare), where his research into African religious and political resistance — including his book Revolt in Southern Rhodesia, 1896–97 (1967) — helped found a new African-centred historiography. His support for African nationalism led the Rhodesian government to restrict and effectively exile him in 1963.

He subsequently taught at the University of Dar es Salaam in Tanzania, in the United States at UCLA, and at the University of Manchester, before returning to Oxford as Rhodes Professor of Race Relations. Later works included Peasant Consciousness and Guerrilla War in Zimbabwe (1985), Voices from the Rocks (1999) on the Matobo Hills, and studies of Zimbabwe's post-independence politics, in which he criticised the ruling party's use of "patriotic history".

The invention of tradition

Ranger's 1983 essay "The Invention of Tradition in Colonial Africa", published alongside Hobsbawm's work on Europe, argued that many supposedly ancient customs — from tribal identities to ceremonies of colonial rule — were in fact recent constructions of the colonial encounter. The concept became one of the most widely cited ideas in modern historiography, and Ranger revisited and qualified it in later writing.
