Muhammad Ali Pasha (1769–1849) was an Ottoman Albanian commander who rose to become the wali (viceroy) of Egypt and Sudan and is widely regarded as the founder of modern Egypt. His reign, from 1805 to 1848, transformed Egypt from a neglected Ottoman province into a powerful regional state and founded the dynasty that ruled Egypt until 1952.

Rise to power

Born in Kavala in Ottoman Macedonia to an Albanian family, Muhammad Ali came to Egypt in 1801 as second-in-command of an Albanian contingent in the Ottoman expedition sent to expel the French. In the power vacuum after the French withdrawal he manoeuvred between Mamluk factions, Ottoman officials and the Cairo ulama, who in 1805 demanded his appointment as wali. Recognised by Sultan Selim III, he consolidated his rule by crushing the Mamluks, most famously in the massacre at the Cairo Citadel in March 1811.

Reforms and campaigns

Muhammad Ali abolished the old tax-farming system, made the state owner of agricultural land, and directed production toward export crops, especially long-staple cotton. He built factories, irrigation works, schools, and a modern conscript army and navy trained by European officers, dispatching campaigns against the Wahhabis in Arabia (1811–1818), into Sudan (1820–1824), and to Greece during the Greek War of Independence. His son Ibrahim Pasha's conquest of Syria in 1831–1840 brought Egypt into direct conflict with the Sultan and the European powers; the settlements of 1840–1841 forced his withdrawal but confirmed hereditary rule of Egypt and Sudan in his family.

Death and legacy

In 1848, with his health failing, Muhammad Ali yielded power to Ibrahim, who predeceased him; Muhammad Ali died in Alexandria on 2 August 1849 and was buried in the great mosque he had built in the Cairo Citadel. Historians credit him with founding the modern Egyptian state, its army, bureaucracy and education system, though his modernisation rested on heavy taxation, corvée labour and monopolies that burdened the peasantry.
