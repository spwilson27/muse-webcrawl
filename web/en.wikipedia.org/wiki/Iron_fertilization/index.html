Iron fertilization

Iron fertilization is the intentional introduction of iron, usually as ferrous sulphate, to the upper ocean to stimulate phytoplankton growth. It is proposed as a geoengineering technique to draw down atmospheric carbon dioxide, since blooms of phytoplankton fix carbon, some of which may sink to the deep ocean when the organisms die.

Background

Large regions of the ocean — the Southern Ocean, the equatorial Pacific and the subarctic Pacific — are "high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll" (HNLC) zones: rich in nitrate and phosphate but poor in phytoplankton. Oceanographer John Martin showed in the 1980s that the missing ingredient is iron, needed in trace amounts for photosynthesis, and famously quipped, "Give me a half tanker of iron, and I will give you an ice age." Iron arrives naturally in wind-blown dust, and iron supply has been linked to glacial–interglacial changes in atmospheric CO2.

Experiments

More than a dozen open-ocean experiments since IronEx I (1993), including SOIREE, EisenEx, SEEDS, SOFeX and LOHAFEX (2009), confirmed that adding iron triggers blooms. However, the fraction of carbon exported to depth proved variable and often modest, and blooms may be grazed by zooplankton or limited by other nutrients such as silicic acid.

Controversy

Critics cite uncertain sequestration, possible production of other greenhouse gases, shifts in marine ecosystems and the risk of commercial ventures outpacing regulation. A rogue 2012 dumping of iron sulphate off British Columbia by the Haida Salmon Restoration Corporation drew international condemnation. Ocean fertilization is restricted under the London Convention and Protocol and addressed by the Convention on Biological Diversity.
