Social epistemology is a branch of epistemology that studies the social dimensions of knowledge and justified belief. Traditional epistemology since Descartes focused on the individual knower in isolation; social epistemology examines how knowledge depends on other people and on institutions — through testimony, expert authority, peer review, education, and information technologies — and how groups, communities, and societies themselves may be said to know or to fail to know.

History

Although questions about testimony reach back to Hume and Thomas Reid, social epistemology emerged as a self-conscious field in the 1980s. Steve Fuller founded the journal Social Epistemology in 1987, and Alvin Goldman's Knowledge in a Social World (1999) gave the analytic approach a canonical statement, applying a "veritistic" framework — evaluating social practices by their tendency to produce true rather than false belief. The sociology of scientific knowledge associated with the Edinburgh Strong Programme and with Thomas Kuhn's account of scientific communities formed a parallel, more naturalistic influence.

Main topics

Testimony

Most of what any individual believes is acquired through the word of others. Philosophers debate whether testimony is a basic source of justification on a par with perception and memory (anti-reductionism, following Reid and C. A. J. Coady) or whether it must be reduced to more basic sources of evidence (reductionism, following Hume).

Peer disagreement

When epistemic peers — people equally competent and equally well informed — disagree, what should each believe? "Equal weight" or conciliationist views hold that each should move substantially toward the other's position; "steadfast" views hold that one may rationally retain one's belief.

Group knowledge and institutions

Social epistemologists ask whether organisations, juries, scientific communities, and markets can literally hold beliefs, and how institutions such as peer review, the press, libraries, and search engines should be designed to promote truth. Philip Kitcher's work on the division of cognitive labour applies economic modelling to the organisation of science.

Epistemic injustice

Miranda Fricker's Epistemic Injustice (2007) introduced the concepts of testimonial injustice — deflating a speaker's credibility because of prejudice — and hermeneutical injustice, where gaps in collective interpretive resources disadvantage a social group. Feminist epistemologists such as Helen Longino have argued that objectivity itself is a social achievement of diverse, critically interactive communities.

Relation to other disciplines

Social epistemology overlaps with the sociology of knowledge, science and technology studies, information science, and social network analysis, while retaining a normative focus on how knowledge-producing practices ought to be arranged.
