Robert David Putnam (born 9 January 1941) is an American political scientist and the Peter and Isabel Malkin Professor of Public Policy, emeritus, at the Harvard Kennedy School. He is among the most cited social scientists of his generation, known above all for popularising the concept of social capital and for Bowling Alone (2000), his best-selling diagnosis of the collapse of American community life since the 1960s.

Career

Putnam grew up in Port Clinton, Ohio — the setting of his later book Our Kids (2015) — graduated from Swarthmore College in 1963, studied at Balliol College, Oxford, and took his PhD at Yale in 1970. He taught at the University of Michigan before moving to Harvard in 1979, where he twice served as dean of the Kennedy School. His early work on political elites and on diplomacy produced the influential "two-level games" framework (1988), which models international negotiation as simultaneous bargaining at international and domestic tables.

Making Democracy Work and social capital

In Making Democracy Work: Civic Traditions in Modern Italy (1993, with Robert Leonardi and Raffaella Nanetti), Putnam traced the divergent performance of Italy's regional governments to centuries-old stocks of "social capital" — networks, norms of reciprocity, and trust arising from horizontal civic associations such as choirs, football clubs, and cooperatives. Regions rich in such civic community had effective institutions and prosperity; those dominated by vertical, clientelistic ties did not. The study made "social capital" a central — and contested — concept across the social sciences.

Bowling Alone and later work

Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community (2000), expanded from a 1995 essay, marshalled survey and archival data to show steep declines since the 1960s in club membership, volunteering, churchgoing, informal socialising — even league bowling, which Americans increasingly did alone. Putnam attributed the decline chiefly to generational replacement, television, suburbanisation, and time pressure, and warned of corrosive effects on health, education, and democracy. Critics questioned some measures and trends, but the book framed a generation's debate about civic decline. His later books include American Grace (2010, with David E. Campbell) on religion's polarising and cohesive roles, Our Kids (2015) on the growing opportunity gap, and The Upswing (2020, with Shaylyn Romney Garrett), arguing that America's individualistic turn since the 1960s parallels the Gilded Age and can be reversed as the Progressive Era reversed it. Putnam received the National Humanities Medal in 2012; the documentary Join or Die (2023) profiled his work.
