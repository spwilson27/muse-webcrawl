Nuclear magnetic resonance

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) is a physical phenomenon in which atomic nuclei in a strong constant magnetic field are perturbed by a weak oscillating magnetic field and respond by producing an electromagnetic signal at a characteristic frequency. The effect occurs in nuclei with non-zero spin, such as hydrogen-1 (1H) and carbon-13 (13C).

NMR is the basis of NMR spectroscopy, one of the principal techniques used by chemists and biochemists to determine the structure, dynamics and purity of molecules, and of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which is treated in the context of nuclear medicine and radiology.

Principle

A nucleus with spin behaves like a tiny magnet. In an external field B0 its spin states split in energy (the Zeeman effect), and it precesses at the Larmor frequency, proportional to the field and to the nucleus's gyromagnetic ratio. A radiofrequency pulse at that frequency tips the magnetisation; as the spins relax back (characterised by the relaxation times T1 and T2) they emit a signal — the free induction decay — whose Fourier transform gives the spectrum. The exact resonance frequency of a nucleus shifts slightly with its chemical environment (the chemical shift), and neighbouring spins split signals through spin–spin (J) coupling, allowing connectivity and molecular structure to be deduced.

History

NMR in bulk matter was first observed in 1946 by two groups — Felix Bloch's at Stanford and Edward Purcell's at Harvard — who shared the 1952 Nobel Prize in Physics. Commercial spectrometers followed in the 1950s. Key advances included Fourier-transform NMR (Richard Ernst, Nobel Prize 1991), two-dimensional methods, solid-state techniques, and the determination of protein structures in solution (Kurt Wüthrich, Nobel Prize 2002). NMR-based imaging, developed by Paul Lauterbur and Peter Mansfield (Nobel Prize 2003), became MRI.

Applications

In chemistry, 1H and 13C NMR are routine for identifying organic compounds; multidimensional experiments (COSY, HSQC, NOESY) map molecular structures, including those of proteins and nucleic acids. NMR is also used in materials science, petroleum logging (downhole NMR), food analysis, metabolomics, and quantum-information research. Industrial NMR spectrometers operate at fields of up to about 28 tesla (1.2 GHz for protons), using superconducting magnets cooled with liquid helium.
