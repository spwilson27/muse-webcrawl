The Distinguished Service Cross (DSC) is the United States Army's second highest military decoration, awarded for extreme gallantry and risk of life in actual combat with an armed enemy force. Actions that merit the Distinguished Service Cross must be of such a high degree that they are above those required for all other U.S. combat decorations, but do not meet the criteria for the Medal of Honor.

History

The decoration was established by President Woodrow Wilson on 2 January 1918 and confirmed by Congress on 9 July 1918, during the First World War. General John J. Pershing had requested a decoration comparable to European awards such as the British Military Cross to recognise valour by American soldiers. The first design was struck in 1918–1919; the medal is a bronze cross pattée bearing an eagle with outspread wings surrounded by a laurel wreath, suspended from a ribbon of red, white and blue.

Recipients

More than 13,000 awards have been made overall, the great majority during the two World Wars. Recipients include Alvin York (whose award was later upgraded to the Medal of Honor), Eddie Rickenbacker, and numerous soldiers of the Second World War, Korea, Vietnam and the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. Some awards have later been upgraded to the Medal of Honor on review; a small number have been awarded to foreign allied soldiers and to members of other U.S. services serving with the Army.

The Navy Cross (Department of the Navy) and the Air Force Cross are the equivalent decorations for the naval services and the Air Force and Space Force, and all three rank immediately below the Medal of Honor in their services' orders of precedence.
