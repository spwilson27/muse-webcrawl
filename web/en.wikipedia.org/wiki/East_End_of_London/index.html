The East End of London, often referred to within the capital simply as the East End, is the historic core of wider East London, lying east of the Roman and medieval walls of the City of London and north of the River Thames. It does not have universally accepted boundaries, but many regard the area as roughly coterminous with the modern London Borough of Tower Hamlets, together with southern parts of Hackney.

The East End grew as London expanded eastward along the Thames, absorbing riverside hamlets such as Wapping, Limehouse and Poplar that served the port, and inland villages including Bethnal Green, Stepney and Spitalfields. Its proximity to the Port of London made it a magnet both for industry and for successive waves of migrants — Huguenot silk weavers, Irish labourers, Ashkenazi Jews fleeing pogroms, and, after the Second World War, Sylheti Bangladeshis — each leaving a deep mark on its character.

History

Origins and growth

The East End originated in settlements outside the City walls, where land was cheaper and noxious trades excluded from the City could be practised. The building of St Katharine Docks (1828) and the West and East India Docks transformed the riverside districts, and the population exploded during the 19th century as dock work, warehousing, brewing, furniture-making and the "rag trade" drew workers into overcrowded terraces and lodging houses.

Poverty and reform

By the later 19th century the East End had become a byword for poverty, overcrowding and crime, documented by social investigators such as Henry Mayhew and Charles Booth, whose poverty maps coloured many East End streets black or dark blue — "lowest class; vicious, semi-criminal". Philanthropists responded with model housing, university settlements such as Toynbee Hall (1884), and missions; the Salvation Army was founded in Whitechapel in 1865. The unsolved Whitechapel murders of 1888, attributed to "Jack the Ripper", fixed the district's lurid reputation worldwide.

Twentieth century

East Enders played a celebrated part in resisting Oswald Mosley's British Union of Fascists at the Battle of Cable Street in 1936. The Blitz fell heavily on the docks and surrounding streets, and post-war clearance replaced many terraces with council estates. The closure of the upriver docks between the 1960s and 1980s brought severe unemployment, but from the 1980s the redevelopment of the Docklands — centred on Canary Wharf on the Isle of Dogs — brought finance and media jobs, while areas such as Shoreditch and Spitalfields gentrified rapidly.

Culture

The East End is the traditional home of the Cockney, London's working-class dialect and identity, whose speakers were said to be born within earshot of the bells of St Mary-le-Bow. Its culture is associated with the music hall, pie and mash shops selling jellied eels, and street markets including Petticoat Lane, Columbia Road flower market and Brick Lane, the centre of the Bangladeshi community and famous for its curry houses. The district's mosques, churches and former synagogues record its successive communities.

Districts

  • Whitechapel and Spitalfields — markets, the Royal London Hospital, and Huguenot and Jewish history.
  • Bethnal Green — the V&A Museum of Childhood and a strong boxing tradition.
  • Stepney, Mile End and Bow — the historic "Tower Hamlets" villages along the Roman road to Colchester.
  • Wapping, Limehouse and Poplar — the old dockside parishes.
  • The Isle of Dogs — dominated today by the Canary Wharf financial district.
