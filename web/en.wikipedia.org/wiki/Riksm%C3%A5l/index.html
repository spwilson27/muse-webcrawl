Riksmål

Riksmål (Norwegian for "language of the realm"; pronounced [ˈrɪksmoːl]) is an unofficial, conservative variety of the written Norwegian language Bokmål, closely based on the Danish-influenced written language used in Norway before the language reforms of the 20th century. The name was coined by the author Bjørnstjerne Bjørnson in 1899.

History

After Norway's separation from Denmark in 1814, the written language of administration and literature remained essentially Danish, gradually adapted to Norwegian usage. Official spelling reforms in 1907, 1917, and 1938 progressively "Norwegianized" the standard, bringing it closer to spoken Norwegian and, in some respects, to Landsmål/Nynorsk — a policy known as samnorsk (Common Norwegian).

Riksmål supporters, organized through Riksmålsforbundet (founded 1919) and backed by prominent writers and newspapers such as Aftenposten, resisted the more radical reforms and maintained the conservative norms in private publishing. The parent-led "language struggle" of the 1950s against samnorsk forms in school textbooks was a major episode of the conflict. When the government abandoned the samnorsk policy and liberalized Bokmål orthography in 1981 and 2005, many Riksmål forms became officially permitted again, narrowing the practical difference between moderate Bokmål and Riksmål.

Characteristics

Riksmål retains Danish-derived vocabulary and spellings, avoids many of the optional feminine-gender and diphthongized forms of official Bokmål, and preserves distinctions in vocabulary (for example, using efter-type or Danish-style forms traditionally) that reflect the older literary standard.
