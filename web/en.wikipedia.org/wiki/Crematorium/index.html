Crematorium

Image: The chapel of Golders Green Crematorium, London (placeholder)

A crematorium (also spelled crematory in American English) is a facility where the dead are cremated. Modern crematoria usually include one or more furnaces (cremators) and a chapel or ceremonial space, and are often associated with cemeteries or stand in landscaped grounds of their own.

History

Cremation is an ancient practice, but the modern crematorium dates from the 1870s, when improved furnace technology (notably designs by Friedrich Siemens and by Ludovico Brunetti, exhibited in Vienna in 1873) made rapid, smoke-free cremation possible. Milan opened an early crematorium in 1876; the Cremation Society of England, founded in 1874, built the Woking Crematorium in 1878, used for its first official cremation in 1885 after legal clarification. Cremation was legalised in Britain by the Cremation Act 1902, and Golders Green Crematorium (1902) became one of the most famous. In the United States the first modern crematory was built in 1876 at Washington, Pennsylvania, by Francis Julius LeMoyne.

Process

The body, in a combustible coffin or container, is placed in the cremator, a refractory-lined chamber heated to roughly 760–1,150 °C (1,400–2,100 °F), typically fired by natural gas or propane. The process takes about one to three hours; the remaining bone fragments are cooled and processed in a cremulator into the granular "ashes" (cremated remains) returned to the family. Modern facilities include emissions-control systems and facilities for viewing the charging of the coffin, and many provide chapels, music and webcasting for services, gardens of remembrance and columbaria for urns.

Prevalence

Cremation rates have risen steadily in many countries: Japan cremates nearly all its dead, and rates exceed 75% in the United Kingdom and Canada and have passed 60% in the United States, driven by cost, land scarcity and changing religious attitudes. The Catholic Church permitted cremation from 1963, though it retains a preference for burial and, since 2016, instructs that ashes be kept in sacred places rather than scattered or kept at home.
