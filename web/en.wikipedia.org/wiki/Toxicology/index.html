Toxicology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Toxicology is a scientific discipline, overlapping with biology, chemistry, pharmacology, and medicine, that involves the study of the adverse effects of chemical substances on living organisms and the practice of diagnosing and treating exposures to toxins and toxicants. The relationship between dose and its effects on the exposed organism is of high significance in toxicology.

History

The Swiss physician Paracelsus (1493–1541) is credited with the foundational principle of the field: "All things are poison, and nothing is without poison; the dosage alone makes it so a thing is not a poison" — often summarised as "the dose makes the poison". Mathieu Orfila, a nineteenth-century Spanish toxicologist, is often called the father of modern toxicology for his systematic treatise of 1813 and his work in forensic detection of poisons.

Dose–response

Effects of a toxicant depend on the dose, route and duration of exposure, and the susceptibility of the organism. A key metric is the LD50, the median lethal dose — the dose at which half of a test population dies. Dose–response curves describe how the magnitude of effect changes with dose, and concepts such as the no-observed-adverse-effect level (NOAEL) inform regulatory safety limits.

Branches

Toxicology includes medical toxicology, forensic toxicology, clinical toxicology, occupational toxicology, environmental toxicology and ecotoxicology, and regulatory toxicology, which supports the safety assessment of drugs, pesticides, food additives, and industrial chemicals. Modern methods increasingly use computational models (in silico) and cell-based (in vitro) assays to reduce animal testing.
