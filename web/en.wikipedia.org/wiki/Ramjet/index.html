Ramjet

A ramjet is a form of airbreathing jet engine that uses the engine's forward motion to compress incoming air without an axial compressor or a centrifugal compressor. Because ramjets cannot produce thrust at zero airspeed, they cannot move an aircraft from a standstill; a ramjet-powered vehicle must be boosted to an operating speed by another means, such as a rocket, or accelerated to high speed in a dive.

Ramjets work most efficiently at supersonic speeds around Mach 3 and can operate up to speeds of Mach 6. This type of engine operates on the same principles as the Brayton thermodynamic cycle as the turbojet, but since a ramjet has no compressor, it cannot operate at static or very low speeds.

Design and operation

A ramjet has three main sections: a converging intake that slows and compresses the incoming supersonic air, a combustor where fuel is injected and burned (at subsonic speeds within the engine), and a converging–diverging nozzle that accelerates the hot exhaust to produce thrust. The entire compression is achieved by "ramming" external air into the combustor with forward speed.

History

The concept was patented by French inventor René Lorin in 1913. In the 1930s, fellow Frenchman René Leduc experimented with ramjet-powered aircraft; his Leduc 010 flew powered tests beginning in 1949. During and after World War II, Germany, the United States, and the Soviet Union pursued ramjet research. The American Marquardt company built ramjet engines for the CIM-10 Bomarc surface-to-air missile and the D-21 reconnaissance drone. Operational weapons include the Soviet/Russian P-270 Moskit, the French ASMP, and the Franco-British Meteor beyond-visual-range air-to-air missile, which uses a throttleable ducted rocket — a solid-fuel variant of the ramjet principle.

Variants

  • Scramjet (supersonic-combustion ramjet) — combustion occurs at supersonic airflow, theoretically capable of Mach 12–24.
  • Solid-fuel ramjet / ducted rocket — uses a solid propellant gas generator feeding a ramjet combustor.
