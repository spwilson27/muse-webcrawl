Murder, Inc. — the name was a newspaper coinage, never used by the men themselves — was the enforcement arm of the American National Crime Syndicate in the 1930s. Based in Brooklyn, its killers, directed by Louis "Lepke" Buchalter and Albert Anastasia, carried out contract murders on behalf of Mafia and Jewish organised-crime bosses across the United States. Investigators credited the group with hundreds of killings (estimates range from 400 to 1,000 nationwide, many never solved).

Organisation

The group's core came from the Brownsville and Ocean Hill sections of Brooklyn — Jewish and Italian gunmen including Abe "Kid Twist" Reles, Martin "Buggsy" Goldstein, Harry "Pittsburgh Phil" Strauss (reputedly its most prolific killer) and Mendy Weiss. Their hangout was Rose Gold's candy store on Saratoga Avenue, open all night, where assignments from the syndicate's board were received by telephone. The system's point was insulation: the men who ordered a killing never met the men who performed it, and the killers usually had no connection to the victim. Fees reported in later testimony ranged from a few hundred dollars upward, with expenses.

Famous assignments included the 1935 murder of bootlegger Dutch Schultz (ordered to forestall his plan to assassinate prosecutor Thomas E. Dewey) and the 1936 killing of garment-industry figure Joseph Rosen.

Downfall

In 1940, facing a murder charge, Reles turned informant to Brooklyn district attorney William O'Dwyer's office, and his testimony over months of debriefing ("Canary, but he couldn't fly" became the tabloid line) implicated Buchalter, Anastasia's circle and dozens of killings. Goldstein, Strauss, Weiss and others were convicted of the 1936 murder of Joseph Rosen and executed at Sing Sing in 1941; Buchalter was executed in 1944 — the only major crime boss to receive the death penalty in the US. In November 1941 Reles, under 24-hour police guard on the sixth floor of Coney Island's Half Moon Hotel, fell to his death from a window, a mystery never resolved; with their star witness gone, several pending cases, including that against Anastasia, collapsed.

In popular culture

The story was dramatised in the 1960 film Murder, Inc. (with Peter Falk as Reles, an Oscar-nominated performance) and in Burton Turkus and Sid Feder's 1951 bestseller Murder, Inc.: The Story of the Syndicate, written by the prosecutor who tried the Rosen case.
