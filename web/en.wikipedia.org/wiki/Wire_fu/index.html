Wire fu

See also: Wuxia and Hong Kong action cinema.

Wire fu is an element of Hong Kong action cinema and the wuxia genre in which actors are suspended from thin wires and cables to perform gravity-defying martial-arts moves — flying leaps, gliding across water, fighting on bamboo tops and running up walls. The name blends "wire work" with "kung fu". Skilled riggers and choreographers move performers through the air while the wires are concealed by camera angles, lighting, costume or later digital removal.

Development

Wire techniques grew out of Peking opera acrobatic traditions and tricks used in Hong Kong cinema from the 1960s onward, including in the films of King Hu. Tsui Hark's productions of the 1980s and 1990s, such as Zu Warriors from the Magic Mountain and the Once Upon a Time in China series, pushed the style further. Action choreographer Yuen Woo-ping became its most famous exponent.

International influence

Yuen's choreography for The Matrix (1999) brought wire fu to mainstream Hollywood, and Ang Lee's Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon (2000) won a global audience for balletic wire-work swordplay. Zhang Yimou's Hero (2002) and House of Flying Daggers (2004) continued the style, and wire assistance is now a standard tool of stunt work worldwide, usually combined with digital wire removal and CGI. Critics sometimes use "wire fu" pejoratively for action that sacrifices physical plausibility, contrasting it with grounded fight choreography.
