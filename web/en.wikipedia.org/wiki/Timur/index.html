Timur

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: The Gur-e Amir mausoleum in Samarkand, Timur's tomb, with its ribbed blue dome]

Timur (1336 – 18 February 1405), known in the West as Tamerlane (from Timur-i Leng, 'Timur the Lame'), was a Turco-Mongol conqueror who built one of the largest land empires in history from his capital at Samarkand. Claiming the mantle of Genghis Khan, he conquered Persia, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus, much of Central Asia and the Delhi Sultanate's India, crushed the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara (1402), and died marching to invade Ming China. His campaigns were infamous for massacres and towers of skulls, yet he also made Samarkand a jewel of Islamic art and learning, and his descendants — the Timurids — produced a brilliant renaissance and, in India, the Mughal Empire.

Conquests

Rising from a minor clan near Kesh in Transoxiana, Timur mastered the Chagatai Khanate by 1370 and then campaigned almost without pause for thirty-five years: Persia (1386–87), the Golden Horde (Tokhtamysh defeated 1391 and 1395, with Moscow's fringe reached), India (Delhi sacked 1398, with immense slaughter and plunder), Syria (Aleppo and Damascus taken 1400–01; artists and craftsmen deported to Samarkand), and Anatolia, where at Ankara he captured Bayezid, inadvertently relieving Constantinople and throwing the Ottoman state into an interregnum.

Death and legacy

In the winter of 1404–05 Timur set out to conquer China with a huge army, but fell ill and died at Otrar in February 1405. His empire quickly fragmented among his sons and grandsons (Shah Rukh held the east), yet Timurid culture flourished — Ulugh Beg's astronomy, Herat's painting, and the lineage that led Babur to found the Mughal Empire in 1526. Timur is a national hero in Uzbekistan and a byword for destructive conquest elsewhere; his tomb in Samarkand, the Gur-e Amir, inspired later Mughal tombs including the Taj Mahal.
