Town and country planning in the United Kingdom

Town and country planning in the United Kingdom is the system of land-use regulation through which central government and local authorities control the development of land and buildings. The modern system was established by the Town and Country Planning Act 1947, which effectively nationalised the right to develop land: most development now requires planning permission from the local planning authority.

History

The roots of British planning lie in 19th-century public health legislation and the garden city movement of Ebenezer Howard, whose ideas produced Letchworth and Welwyn Garden City. The Housing, Town Planning, etc. Act 1909 first empowered local authorities to prepare planning schemes. The Town and Country Planning Act 1947, passed by the post-war Labour government, introduced the comprehensive modern system, alongside the creation of green belts and new towns.

The system today

Each local planning authority prepares a local plan setting out policies for development in its area. Individual proposals ("planning applications") are decided against the plan and national policy, including England's National Planning Policy Framework. Certain minor works are "permitted development" and do not need an application. Appeals against refusal are heard by the Planning Inspectorate. Planning is a devolved matter, so Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland operate their own policy frameworks.

Green belt

A distinctive feature of the English system is the green belt: designated land around cities where development is strictly restricted to prevent urban sprawl and keep land permanently open. Green belts cover roughly 13 per cent of England's land area.
