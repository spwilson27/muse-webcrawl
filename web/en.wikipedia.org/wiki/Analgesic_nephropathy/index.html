Analgesic nephropathy is kidney damage caused by the long-term, excessive use of analgesic (pain-relieving) medicines. It classically results from years of heavy consumption of combination analgesic preparations containing phenacetin, often mixed with aspirin, paracetamol (acetaminophen), codeine, and caffeine. The condition is characterised by renal papillary necrosis and chronic tubulointerstitial nephritis, progressing slowly to chronic kidney disease.

History and epidemiology

The syndrome was first recognised in Switzerland and Scandinavia in the 1950s and became strikingly common in Australia, where proprietary headache powders were habitually consumed, and in Belgium. In some regions, analgesic nephropathy once accounted for a substantial share of patients reaching end-stage kidney disease. Incidence fell markedly after phenacetin was withdrawn from the market in the 1970s and 1980s and combination powders were restricted.

Pathophysiology

Phenacetin and paracetamol metabolites concentrate in the renal papilla, where oxidative injury damages the papillary tissue and the vasa recta. Aspirin and other NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated blood flow to the medulla, compounding ischaemic injury. The result is necrosis of the renal papillae, which may slough into the urine, followed by scarring, calcification, and cortical atrophy overlying damaged papillae.

Signs and symptoms

Early disease is usually silent. Features include impaired urinary concentrating ability (often the first sign, causing nocturia and polyuria), sterile pyuria, mild proteinuria, hypertension, anaemia disproportionate to the degree of kidney failure, and episodes of flank pain or visible haematuria when necrotic papillae slough. Affected patients also have an increased risk of transitional cell (urothelial) carcinoma of the renal pelvis and bladder.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a history of substantial analgesic intake together with imaging findings — CT may show small, scarred kidneys with papillary calcifications, sometimes described as a "ring sign". Treatment is withdrawal of the offending analgesics, which can stabilise and sometimes improve kidney function, along with management of hypertension and complications of chronic kidney disease. Screening for urothelial malignancy is recommended.
