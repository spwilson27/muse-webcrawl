Cyberspace

Cyberspace is an interconnected digital environment. It is a type of virtual world popularised in science fiction, and now used to describe the whole range of information resources available through computer networks, above all the Internet. The word was coined by Danish artist-architect team Susanne Ussing and Carsten Hoff in the early 1980s, and popularised by Canadian-American science fiction writer William Gibson, first in his 1982 short story "Burning Chrome" and then in his 1984 novel Neuromancer, where he defined it as "a consensual hallucination experienced daily by billions of legitimate operators".

Meaning and usage

Unlike most computer terms, "cyberspace" does not have a fixed technical definition. In science and government usage it often denotes the global domain of electromagnetic information — networked computers, embedded systems, telecommunications — treated as a domain of operations alongside land, sea, air and space. The U.S. and other militaries maintain dedicated cyberspace commands. In cultural usage, the term evokes the immersive, disembodied quality of online life imagined by cyberpunk fiction, with its decks, ICE (Intrusion Countermeasures Electronics) and corporate data fortresses.

Culture

Gibson's description influenced the first generations of Internet designers and users; early virtual communities such as The WELL discussed cyberspace as a new social frontier, an idea codified in John Perry Barlow's 1996 "Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace", which asserted that governments had no sovereignty "where we gather". Legal scholars have debated how far existing law applies in cyberspace, with some early theorists arguing for cyberspace exceptionalism and others, such as Lawrence Lessig, arguing that code itself regulates behaviour.
