Dunedin

Dunedin (Māori: Ōtepoti) is the second-largest city in the South Island of New Zealand (after Christchurch), and the principal city of the Otago region. Its name comes from Dùn Èideann, the Scottish Gaelic name for Edinburgh, the capital of Scotland. Dunedin has a population of roughly 129,000 (city) and is known for its Scottish heritage, its student population, and its wildlife.

History

Māori settled the Otago coast from about 1300; the area around Otago Harbour was an important settlement and later a whaling station. In 1848, Scottish settlers of the Lay Association of the Free Church of Scotland founded Dunedin; the discovery of gold in 1861 (the Otago gold rush) made it briefly New Zealand's largest and wealthiest city, funding grand Victorian and Edwardian architecture — including the famously ornate Dunedin railway station (1906) — and the country's first university, the University of Otago (founded 1869).

Geography

The city occupies hills and valleys at the head of Otago Harbour, on the eroded remnants of an extinct volcano. Its central area is organized around the Octagon. Baldwin Street was long recognized as the world's steepest residential street. The Otago Peninsula is renowned for wildlife, including a mainland colony of northern royal albatrosses at Taiari Head and populations of yellow-eyed penguins and New Zealand sea lions.

Education and culture

The University of Otago and Otago Polytechnic give Dunedin a large student population ("Scarfies") and a prominent role in New Zealand's music scene — the "Dunedin sound" of 1980s Flying Nun bands such as The Clean, The Chills, and Straitjacket Fits. The city has a UNESCO City of Literature designation (2014).

Economy

Historically based on gold, farming, and manufacturing, Dunedin's economy now centres on education, health, research, tourism, and a growing technology sector. Port Chalmers serves as its deep-water port.
