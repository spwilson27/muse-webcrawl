Philippines v. China (PCA Case No. 2013–19), also known as the South China Sea Arbitration, was an arbitration case brought by the Republic of the Philippines against the People's Republic of China under Annex VII of the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), concerning certain issues in the South China Sea, including the legality of China's claimed "nine-dash line".

On 19 February 2013, China declared that it would not participate in the arbitration. On 29 October 2015, the arbitral tribunal ruled that it had jurisdiction over the case. On 12 July 2016, the tribunal ruled in favor of the Philippines on most of its submissions. It clarified that while it would not "rule on any question of sovereignty over land territory and would not delimit any maritime boundary", China's "nine-dash line" claim had no legal basis because it surpassed the entitlements China was allowed under UNCLOS.

Background

The arbitration was initiated following a series of disputes between the Philippines and China over maritime features in the South China Sea, including the 2012 standoff at Scarborough Shoal, after which China effectively gained control of the shoal. The Philippines argued that China's claims based on the nine-dash line were invalid under UNCLOS, that certain features claimed by China were rocks or low-tide elevations incapable of generating an exclusive economic zone, and that China had interfered with Philippine fishing and petroleum exploration and caused environmental damage through island-building.

Award

The tribunal concluded that China's claim to historic rights within the nine-dash line was contrary to UNCLOS and without lawful effect to the extent it exceeded China's maritime entitlements under the Convention. It found that none of the Spratly Islands features were capable of generating an exclusive economic zone, that China had violated the Philippines' sovereign rights in its exclusive economic zone, and that Chinese island-building had caused severe harm to the coral reef environment. China rejected the award, declaring it "null and void" with no binding force.

Reactions

The award was welcomed by the Philippines and several other states, which called for it to be respected as final and binding under UNCLOS. China maintained its position of non-acceptance and non-participation, and continued its activities in the South China Sea.
