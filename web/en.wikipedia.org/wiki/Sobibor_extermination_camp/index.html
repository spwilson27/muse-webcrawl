Sobibór extermination camp

Sobibór was a Nazi German extermination camp built and operated by the SS near the village of Sobibór in German-occupied Poland, as part of Operation Reinhard — the plan to murder the Jews of occupied Poland. It operated from May 1942 until the prisoner revolt of October 1943.

Construction and operation

Built in a remote forested area near the Bug river, Sobibór was designed exclusively for mass murder. Transports arrived by rail, primarily carrying Jews from Poland and the Netherlands, as well as smaller numbers from other occupied countries. Victims were directed to gas chambers disguised as showers and killed with engine exhaust. Only a few hundred prisoners were kept alive as forced laborers in sorting and maintenance details. Historians estimate that between 170,000 and 250,000 people were murdered at Sobibór.

The uprising of 14 October 1943

On 14 October 1943, prisoners led by Soviet-Jewish POW Alexander Pechersky and others carried out a planned revolt, killing eleven SS men and several Ukrainian guards. Roughly 300 prisoners escaped into the surrounding forests; about half were later recaptured or killed. The uprising remains one of the two successful armed revolts in a Nazi extermination camp, alongside the Treblinka revolt of August 1943.

Closure and legacy

Following the revolt, the SS dismantled the camp and planted trees over the site to conceal it. After the war, few perpetrators were brought to justice; commandant Franz Stangl was eventually extradited from Brazil and sentenced to life imprisonment. The site is now a museum and memorial maintained by Poland. Archaeological excavations in the 2010s located the remains of the gas chambers.
