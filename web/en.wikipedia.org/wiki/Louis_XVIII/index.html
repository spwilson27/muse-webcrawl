Louis XVIII

Louis XVIII (17 November 1755 – 16 September 1824), known as "Louis the Desired" (Louis le Désiré), was King of France from 1814 (in law from 1795) to 1824, with the interruption of the Hundred Days in 1815. Brother of the executed Louis XVI, he spent 23 years in exile during the French Revolution and Napoleonic Wars before the allies restored him. His reign — the Bourbon Restoration — tried to reconcile revolutionary France with monarchy under the Charter of 1814, a compromise his brother and successor Charles X would fatally abandon.

Exile and restoration

As Count of Provence, Louis fled France in June 1791 — succeeding where his brother's Flight to Varennes failed — and maintained a wandering émigré court at Koblenz, Verona, Mittau (under Russian protection) and finally Hartwell House in England. On the death of his nephew, the imprisoned Louis XVII (1795), royalists proclaimed him king. In 1814 Talleyrand and the Senate recalled him; he shrewdly granted the Charter, preserving the Code Napoléon, the revolutionary land settlement and a parliament — "to reign over two peoples" (royalists and revolutionaries), as he put it. He fled to Ghent during the Hundred Days and returned, in the bitter phrase, "in the baggage train of the allies".

Reign

The Second Restoration opened with the White Terror against Bonapartists and the ultra-royalist Chambre introuvable, which Louis dissolved in 1816 to govern from the centre. Ministries under Richelieu, Decazes and Villèle balanced reform and reaction: the press seesawed between freedom and censorship, the 1820 assassination of the Duc de Berry swung politics right, and French troops restored absolutism in Spain in 1823. Personally corpulent, witty and gout-ridden, Louis died in 1824 — the last French king to die in office while reigning.
