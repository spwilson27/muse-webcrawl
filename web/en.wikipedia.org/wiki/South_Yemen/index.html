South Yemen, officially the People's Democratic Republic of Yemen (PDRY) from 1970, was a state in the southern Arabian Peninsula from 1967 to 1990. It was the only avowedly Marxist state in the Arab world, and in 1990 it unified with the Yemen Arab Republic (North Yemen) to form the present Republic of Yemen.

Background and independence

The territory comprised the former British colony of Aden and the Aden Protectorates of the southern Arabian hinterland — the Federation of South Arabia. After an insurgency led by the National Liberation Front (NLF) against British rule, Britain withdrew and independence was proclaimed on 30 November 1967 as the People's Republic of South Yemen. The NLF's rivals in the FLOSY movement were defeated in street fighting in Aden.

The socialist state

The state adopted socialist policies: nationalisation of banks, insurance and much industry; land reform; and, unusually for the region, expanded women's rights and mass literacy campaigns. It aligned with the Soviet Union, which maintained naval facilities at Aden and on Socotra, and supported liberation movements including the Dhofar rebellion in Oman and, in the 1970s, the Eritrean and Palestinian movements. Relations with North Yemen oscillated between border wars (1972 and 1979) and unification talks.

Decline and unification

Internal factionalism within the ruling Yemeni Socialist Party repeatedly turned violent, most destructively in the January 1986 civil war, which killed thousands and drove President Ali Nasir Muhammad into exile. Weakened by that conflict and by the collapse of Soviet support, South Yemen agreed to unification with the North, proclaimed on 22 May 1990. Southern discontent with the union erupted in the 1994 civil war, in which southern secession was defeated; grievances later fed the Southern Movement (Hirak) from 2007 and the Southern Transitional Council during Yemen's civil war from 2015.
