Voltammetry

Voltammetry is a category of electroanalytical methods used in analytical chemistry and various industrial processes. In voltammetry, information about an analyte is obtained by measuring the current as the potential is varied. The analytical data for a voltammetric experiment comes in the form of a voltammogram, which plots the current produced by the analyte versus the potential of the working electrode.

Three-electrode system

Voltammetry experiments use a potentiostat to apply a controlled potential to a three-electrode cell consisting of a working electrode, a reference electrode (such as Ag/AgCl), and a counter (auxiliary) electrode. The current flows between the working and counter electrodes while the potential is measured against the reference.

History

Voltammetry developed from polarography, discovered by Jaroslav Heyrovský in 1922 (for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1959), which used a dropping mercury electrode. Modern solid electrodes, pulse techniques, and computer control have greatly expanded its sensitivity and range of applications.

Types

  • Cyclic voltammetry – the potential is swept linearly back and forth; widely used to study redox mechanisms.
  • Linear sweep voltammetry – a single linear potential ramp.
  • Differential pulse and squarewave voltammetry – pulse waveforms that improve sensitivity for trace analysis.
  • Anodic stripping voltammetry – preconcentration followed by oxidative stripping, used for trace metal determination.

Applications

Voltammetry is used for quantitative analysis of metal ions, organic compounds, and pharmaceuticals, as well as in sensors, corrosion studies, and fundamental investigations of electron-transfer kinetics.
