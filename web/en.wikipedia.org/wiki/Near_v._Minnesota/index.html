Near v. Minnesota

Near v. Minnesota

Court: Supreme Court of the United States

Decided: 1 June 1931

Citation: 283 U.S. 697

Holding: Prior restraints on publication are presumptively unconstitutional

Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697 (1931), was a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court recognizing freedom of the press against state prior restraint under the First Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Background

Jay Near published The Saturday Press in Minneapolis, a scandal sheet accusing local officials of corruption and gang ties — with content also marked by anti-Semitic and racist attacks. Minnesota's "gag law" allowed judges to declare "malicious, scandalous and defamatory" periodicals a public nuisance and suppress them permanently; a judge enjoined Near's paper.

Decision

In a 5–4 decision by Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes, the Court struck down the law as an unconstitutional prior restraint, protecting even abusive and scandalous publication against advance suppression (while leaving post-publication libel suits available). The case was the appeal of publisher Robert McCormick's support and is a foundational precedent cited in the Pentagon Papers case, New York Times Co. v. United States (1971).
