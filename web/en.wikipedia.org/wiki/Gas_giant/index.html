Gas giant

A gas giant is a giant planet composed mainly of hydrogen and helium. Jupiter and Saturn are the gas giants of the Solar System. The term was coined in 1952 by science fiction writer James Blish and was originally used to refer to all giant planets. Uranus and Neptune, composed largely of water, ammonia and methane ices, are often classified separately as ice giants.

Characteristics

Gas giants have no well-defined solid surface; their atmospheres gradually transition into hot, dense fluid interiors. Deep inside, hydrogen is compressed into a liquid metallic state that generates powerful magnetic fields. Jupiter's mass is about 318 times that of Earth, and its volume could contain more than 1,300 Earths. Both Jupiter and Saturn have extensive ring systems (Saturn's being spectacular) and large families of moons.

Formation

According to the core accretion model, a solid core of about 5–10 Earth masses forms first, then gravitationally attracts a massive envelope of hydrogen and helium from the protoplanetary disk before the disk disperses. An alternative, less favoured model is direct gravitational instability of the disk.

Exoplanets

Many exoplanets are gas giants, including "hot Jupiters" — giant planets orbiting very close to their stars, discovered in large numbers because of their strong effect on radial-velocity and transit surveys. The first exoplanet found around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b (1995), is a hot Jupiter.
