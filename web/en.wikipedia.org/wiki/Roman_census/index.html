Roman census

The census in ancient Rome was a register of citizens and their property, taken periodically for the purposes of taxation, military service and the determination of voting rights and social class. It was conducted by officials called censors, and concluded with a purification ceremony known as the lustrum, from which the five-year interval between censuses took its name.

The Republic

According to tradition, the census was instituted by King Servius Tullius in the 6th century BC. Under the Republic, two censors were elected roughly every five years. Each head of household had to declare his family, slaves and property under oath; the censors assigned citizens to centuries and tribes, enrolled them in one of five property classes, and could degrade those whose conduct they judged immoral. Refusal or failure to register carried severe penalties.

The Empire

Augustus revived the census of citizens and also ordered provincial censuses for taxation — the census of Quirinius in Judaea in AD 6 is mentioned in the Gospel of Luke in connection with the Nativity. Censuses in Egypt generated extensive surviving paperwork on papyrus. Censors ceased to be appointed under the emperors, who took over their functions, and the institution faded in late antiquity.
