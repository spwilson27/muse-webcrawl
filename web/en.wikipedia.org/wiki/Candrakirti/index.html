Candrakīrti (c. 600 – c. 650 CE) was a Buddhist scholar of Nalanda and a leading commentator on the works of Nagarjuna and Aryadeva. He is regarded as the foremost exponent of the Prāsaṅgika interpretation of Madhyamaka philosophy, which holds that emptiness is best demonstrated by drawing out the absurd consequences (prasaṅga) of opponents' views rather than by advancing independent theses.

Life

Little is known with certainty about Candrakirti's life. Tibetan sources place him at the great monastic university of Nalanda in northern India and credit him with debates against rival Hindu and Buddhist schools; later legend pictures him milking a painted cow to humble an arrogant abbot. His works were comparatively neglected in India but became foundational in Tibet.

Works

His most famous text is the Prasannapadā ("Clear Words"), a commentary on Nagarjuna's Mūlamadhyamakakārikā that defends Buddhapalita's commentarial approach against Bhavaviveka's criticisms. His Madhyamakāvatāra ("Introduction to the Middle Way") is an original verse treatise presenting the path of the bodhisattva according to the ten bhūmis (grounds), and remains a core textbook in Tibetan monastic education. He also wrote the Catuḥśatakaṭīkā, a commentary on Aryadeva's Four Hundred Verses.

Philosophy

Candrakirti defended a thoroughgoing account of emptiness: things lack intrinsic nature (svabhāva) at the ultimate level yet function conventionally, a position expressed through the doctrine of the two truths. He rejected the use of autonomous syllogisms in Madhyamaka argument, denied a foundational consciousness such as the Yogacara ālayavijñāna, and analysed causation through dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda).

Influence

Through Tibetan Buddhism, Candrakirti's interpretation became the dominant understanding of Madhyamaka; Tsongkhapa's reforms placed his works at the centre of the Gelug curriculum, and his texts are still memorised and debated in monasteries today.
