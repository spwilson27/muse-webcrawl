Supernova impostor

A supernova impostor is a stellar eruption that appears, for a time, to be a supernova but does not destroy the star. The events are powerful outbursts of massive stars — most often luminous blue variables (LBVs) — during which the star brightens enormously and ejects a substantial amount of material, yet survives and may erupt again. Supernova impostors are also classed as Type V supernovae in the historical classification scheme, and many are catalogued as LBV eruptions or "SN" designations until follow-up observations reveal the surviving star.

Characteristics

Impostor events typically reach absolute magnitudes of about −11 to −14, noticeably fainter than genuine supernovae, and their spectra show relatively slow expansion velocities, often a few hundred kilometres per second. The progenitor star remains visible after the eruption fades, which is the decisive evidence distinguishing an impostor from a terminal explosion.

Examples

  • Eta Carinae's Great Eruption (c. 1843) — the archetype; the star became the second-brightest in the sky and ejected the Homunculus Nebula, yet survives today.
  • P Cygni — erupted in the 17th century and remains a luminous blue variable.
  • SN 1961V — a famous debated case in NGC 1058, long argued over by astronomers as either a true supernova or an impostor.
  • SN 2009ip — underwent repeated impostor eruptions before a brighter event in 2012 whose nature remains debated.

Significance

Supernova impostors matter to surveys because they contaminate supernova samples and distance measurements, and because they reveal a channel by which massive stars shed large amounts of mass shortly before their final explosions, shaping the circumstellar environment that a real supernova shock will later encounter.
