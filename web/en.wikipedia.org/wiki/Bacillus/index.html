Bacillus is a genus of Gram-positive, rod-shaped bacteria, members of the phylum Bacillota, found in soil and the gut of animals. The genus is named from the Latin bacillus, "little stick", referring to the rod shape of its cells. Many species form dormant, highly resistant endospores that allow survival in harsh conditions for long periods.

Characteristics

Bacillus species are aerobic or facultatively anaerobic, catalase-positive, and typically motile by peritrichous flagella. Their ability to form endospores — among the most resilient biological structures known — makes them widespread in soil, water, and dust, and complicates sterilisation in food and medical settings.

Important species

  • Bacillus anthracis, the cause of anthrax, historically important as the first bacterium shown to cause disease (Koch, 1876).
  • Bacillus cereus, a cause of foodborne illness associated with reheated rice and other foods.
  • Bacillus subtilis, a model organism in laboratory studies of sporulation and a producer of enzymes and fermented foods such as nattō.
  • Bacillus thuringiensis, whose insecticidal crystal proteins are widely used as a biological pesticide (Bt) and in genetically modified crops.

Uses

Bacillus species are industrial workhorses: they produce proteases and amylases for detergents, antibiotics such as bacitracin, and are used as probiotics and in biotechnology. Their spore-forming habit means they are also model organisms for astrobiology and sterilisation research.
