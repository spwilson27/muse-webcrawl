Aperture masking

Aperture masking is a form of speckle interferometry in astronomy in which a mask with several small holes is placed in front of the primary mirror of a telescope. The mask converts the single aperture into an array of small sub-apertures that act together as an interferometer, allowing diffraction-limited imaging to be recovered from observations blurred by atmospheric turbulence.

Principle

Each pair of holes produces interference fringes whose phase would normally be corrupted by the atmosphere. By measuring closure phases — sums of fringe phases around triangles of holes, in which atmospheric errors cancel — astronomers can reconstruct images at angular resolutions approaching the diffraction limit of the full telescope diameter. The technique sacrifices light throughput for image sharpness and fidelity.

Development and use

Aperture masking was developed from speckle interferometry in the 1980s and 1990s, notably by groups working at the William Herschel Telescope and other large ground-based observatories. It has been used to image close binary stars, stellar surfaces, dusty shells around evolved stars, and the environments of young stars. The Near Infrared Imager and Slitless Spectrograph (NIRISS) aboard the James Webb Space Telescope includes a non-redundant aperture masking mode with a seven-hole mask, extending the technique to space-based observation of exoplanets and close companions.

Related techniques

Aperture masking is related to aperture synthesis in radio astronomy, to kernel-phase analysis, and to long-baseline optical interferometry carried out with separate telescopes such as the CHARA Array and the Very Large Telescope Interferometer.
