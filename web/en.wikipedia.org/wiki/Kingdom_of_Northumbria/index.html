The Kingdom of Northumbria (Old English: Norþanhymbra Rīce, "kingdom of the Northumbrians", i.e. the people north of the Humber) was an early medieval Anglo-Saxon kingdom in what is now northern England and south-east Scotland. It was formed by the union of the kingdoms of Bernicia (north) and Deira (south) under King Oswald and his successors, and at its height stretched from the Humber and Mersey to the Firth of Forth.

Golden age

Under kings such as Edwin (died 633), Oswald (died 642) and Oswiu (died 670), Northumbria was the dominant power in Britain. Edwin's conversion to Christianity through the Roman mission of Paulinus (627) and Oswald's patronage of the Irish monk Aidan, founder of the monastery of Lindisfarne, set up a creative tension between Roman and Celtic practice resolved at the Synod of Whitby (664) in Rome's favour. The 7th and 8th centuries are remembered as a Northumbrian golden age of learning: the Venerable Bede wrote his Ecclesiastical History at Monkwearmouth–Jarrow, whose twin monastery produced the great Codex Amiatinus; the Lindisfarne Gospels and the Ruthwell and Bewcastle crosses testify to its art and scholarship; and Alcuin of York became the leading scholar of Charlemagne's court.

Decline and Viking conquest

From the late 8th century, dynastic instability weakened the kingdom, and the Viking raid on Lindisfarne in 793 opened a new era. The Danish Great Heathen Army captured York in 867 and conquered Deira, which became the Scandinavian kingdom of Jórvík; Bernicia survived in reduced form under the earls of Bamburgh. The last Scandinavian king of York, Eric Bloodaxe, was expelled and killed in 954, whereupon Northumbria was absorbed into the rising kingdom of England under Eadred — though its distinct identity, law (the Danelaw in its south) and dialect long endured, and its northern lands were contested with the Kingdom of Alba.
