Metrical charms

The Anglo-Saxon metrical charms are a set of twelve Old English poems preserved in medieval manuscripts, mostly in the medical compilations known as the Lacnunga and Bald's Leechbook. They are "charms" in the sense of spoken or sung remedies and incantations, intended to heal illness, protect travellers, recover stolen cattle, ease childbirth, or bless fields. Composed in the alliterative verse typical of Old English poetry, they blend Christian elements — prayers, liturgical formulas, the names of saints — with older Germanic pagan material, including references to Woden (Odin).

The twelve charms

  • Æcerbot (the "Field Remedy") — a ritual for restoring barren land, the longest and most elaborate of the charms.
  • Nine Herbs Charm — praises nine sacred herbs and recalls Woden striking a serpent with nine twigs.
  • Wið færstice ("Against a sudden stitch") — against stabbing pains attributed to the projectiles of elves, gods, and witches; famous for the lines about "mighty women" riding over the burial mound.
  • A Journey Charm — protection for a traveller.
  • For Loss of Cattle and For Delayed Birth, among others addressing theft, childbirth, water-elf disease, and dwarf-caused illness.

Scholarship

The charms are a key source for the study of Anglo-Saxon medicine and folk belief, showing how learned, liturgical, and popular traditions interacted. J. R. R. Tolkien drew on the world of the charms and related texts for aspects of Middle-earth; the Nine Herbs Charm's imagery and the "elf-shot" concept have been widely discussed by historians of medicine and literature.
