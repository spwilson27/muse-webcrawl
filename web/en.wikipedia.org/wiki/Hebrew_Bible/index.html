The Hebrew Bible or Tanakh is the canonical collection of Hebrew scriptures, comprising the Torah (the five books of Moses), the Nevi'im (Prophets), and the Ketuvim (Writings). It is the textual source for most of the Christian Old Testament, although the order and contents of the books differ between traditions.

The Hebrew Bible was composed over a period of many centuries by various authors, drawing on oral traditions and earlier written sources. The Masoretic Text, vocalized and standardized by Jewish scribes between the 6th and 10th centuries CE, is the authoritative text of Rabbinic Judaism.

Contents

Torah

The Torah consists of Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy, containing narratives from creation through the Exodus and the giving of the Law to the death of Moses.

Nevi'im

The Prophets include the historical books (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) and the prophetic books (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve Minor Prophets).

Ketuvim

The Writings include poetry and wisdom literature such as Psalms, Proverbs, and Job, the Five Scrolls (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther), and Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, and Chronicles.

Textual history

The oldest surviving manuscripts include the Dead Sea Scrolls (3rd century BCE–1st century CE) and medieval codices such as the Aleppo Codex and the Leningrad Codex. Translations include the Greek Septuagint and the Aramaic Targums. Modern scholarship studies the Bible's composition through source criticism and the documentary hypothesis.
