Selective attention

Selective attention is the process of focusing on a particular object or stream of information in the environment while simultaneously ignoring other perceivable information. It is a central topic in cognitive psychology and neuroscience, because the capacity of the sensory and cognitive systems is limited while the amount of available stimulation is vast.

History of research

Modern research began in the 1950s with Colin Cherry's studies of the "cocktail party problem": how a listener can follow one conversation amid competing voices. Using dichotic listening tasks, in which different messages are presented to each ear, Cherry found that shadowed (attended) messages were remembered in detail, while unattended messages left only a trace of their physical characteristics.

Models

Donald Broadbent proposed an early filter model (1958), in which sensory information is selected at an early stage on the basis of physical features. Anne Treisman's attenuation model (1964) softened the filter into an attenuator that weakens rather than blocks unattended channels. Deutsch and Deutsch argued instead for late selection, in which all stimuli are processed for meaning and selection occurs at the stage of response. Later theories, such as perceptual load theory, reconcile early and late selection by noting that selection depends on how demanding the attended task is.

Neural mechanisms

Selective attention involves networks in the frontal and parietal cortex that bias processing in sensory areas. Attending to a location or feature enhances neural responses to stimuli there and suppresses responses elsewhere, a mechanism often described as a sensory "gain control".

Failures of selection

Phenomena such as inattentional blindness — failing to notice a clearly visible object when attention is engaged elsewhere — and change blindness demonstrate that unattended information may receive surprisingly little conscious processing.
