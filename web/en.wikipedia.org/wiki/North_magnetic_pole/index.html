North magnetic pole

The north magnetic pole is the point on the Earth's surface in the Northern Hemisphere at which the planet's magnetic field points vertically downward. It is the location towards which the north end of a compass needle points. Because the Earth's field is generated by a geodynamo in the liquid outer core rather than by a fixed bar magnet, the pole wanders continuously—it has moved hundreds of kilometres from the Canadian Arctic towards Siberia since its first measurement.

History

The pole was first located on 1 June 1831 by James Clark Ross on the Boothia Peninsula in what is now Nunavut, Canada. Subsequent expeditions—including Roald Amundsen's in 1903 and Canadian government surveys from 1948 onwards—found it progressively farther north and west. In 2007 surveys placed it at about 84°N in the Arctic Ocean. Its motion accelerated from roughly 10–15 km per year during much of the 20th century to as much as 50–60 km per year in the early 2000s, carrying it out of Canadian waters and toward Siberia, an acceleration attributed to changes in core flow, including a "tug of war" between magnetic flux patches beneath Canada and Siberia.

Magnetic versus geographic poles

The north magnetic pole is distinct from the geographic North Pole and from the north geomagnetic pole (the pole of the best-fitting dipole approximation). Strictly, because opposite poles attract, the "north magnetic pole" is physically a magnetic south pole: the north-seeking end of a compass is drawn to it. Magnetic declination—the angle between magnetic north and true north—depends on the observer's position relative to the pole and is charted in models such as the World Magnetic Model, updated regularly for navigation.
