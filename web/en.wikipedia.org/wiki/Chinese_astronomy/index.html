Chinese astronomy

Chinese astronomy is one of the oldest continuous astronomical traditions in the world, with records extending back more than three thousand years. Astronomy in imperial China was a state enterprise: court astronomers maintained the calendar, recorded celestial omens, and divided the sky into lunar mansions and asterisms distinct from the Western constellations.

History

Oracle bone inscriptions from the Shang dynasty already record eclipses and novae. Chinese astronomers compiled early star catalogues (associated with Shi Shen and Gan De, 4th century BCE) and kept remarkably complete records of comets — including Halley's Comet from 240 BCE — sunspots, and "guest stars" such as the supernova of 1054 that produced the Crab Nebula. The Dunhuang star map (c. 7th–8th century CE) is among the oldest surviving graphical star atlases.

System

The sky was organised into Three Enclosures around the northern celestial pole and Twenty-Eight Mansions along the ecliptic. Instruments included armillary spheres and water-powered astronomical clocks such as Su Song's clock tower of 1092. Calendar reform was a recurrent imperial project; the Shoushi calendar of Guo Shoujing (1281) calculated the tropical year at 365.2425 days. In the 17th century Jesuit astronomers such as Johann Adam Schall von Bell brought European methods into the Imperial Astronomical Bureau.
