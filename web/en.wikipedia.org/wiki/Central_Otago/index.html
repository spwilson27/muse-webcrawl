Central Otago is a region in the South Island of New Zealand, forming the inland part of the Otago region. It is characterised by a dry, semi-arid continental climate — among the most extreme in New Zealand — with hot summers, cold winters, and large daily temperature ranges. The landscape is dominated by block mountain ranges of schist, wide basins, and the upper reaches of the Clutha River (Mata-Au).

The region is the world's southernmost major wine-growing area and is internationally renowned for its pinot noir. It is also a major producer of summer stone fruit, particularly cherries, apricots, peaches and nectarines.

History

Māori traversed Central Otago for centuries on seasonal journeys, hunting moa and seeking pounamu (greenstone) from the West Coast. European settlement followed the discovery of gold near Lawrence in 1861 and at Cromwell and along the Kawarau River in 1862, sparking the Otago gold rush. Towns such as Clyde, Cromwell and Alexandra grew rapidly, and the dredging and sluicing landscapes of the goldfields remain prominent features. The Otago Central Railway, completed to Cromwell in 1921, linked the region with Dunedin; its route is now the Otago Central Rail Trail, a popular cycleway.

Geography

Central Otago lies in the rain shadow of the Southern Alps, receiving as little as 300–400 mm of rain annually in places, making it New Zealand's driest region. Major ranges include the Old Man, Dunstan, Raggedy and Pisa ranges. The Clutha River flows south-east through the region, dammed at Clyde for hydroelectric generation, forming Lake Dunstan.

Wine

Commercial viticulture began in the 1980s. The combination of high sunshine hours, cool nights and schist-derived soils produces highly regarded pinot noir and aromatic white wines around Gibbston, Bannockburn, Bendigo, Lowburn and Alexandra.
