Legitimacy (political)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In political science, legitimacy is the right and acceptance of an authority — usually a governing law or regime — by its subjects, such that the authority's commands are obeyed not merely from fear or advantage but from a sense of obligation. Max Weber's typology remains the starting point: traditional authority (sanctity of custom), charismatic authority (devotion to a leader's exceptional qualities) and rational-legal authority (belief in the legality of enacted rules and the right of office-holders to command).

Modern theory distinguishes inputs (procedures — elections, rule of law) from outputs (effective governance) as sources of legitimacy (Scharpf), and empirical legitimacy (a population's actual belief, measurable in surveys) from normative legitimacy (whether authority ought to be obeyed). Crises of legitimacy — the 1970s “legitimation crisis” literature (Habermas) applied to overloaded democracies; the collapse of communist regimes whose performance and belief both failed — show the concept's stakes: illegitimate rule must buy obedience with coercion or patronage, which are costlier and brittle. Legitimacy also operates internationally: recognition of governments, Security Council authorisation and contested claims (Kosovo, Taiwan) are legitimacy disputes by other means.
