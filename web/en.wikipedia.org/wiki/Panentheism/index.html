Panentheism

Panentheism (from Greek pân "all", en "in", and theós "God" — "all in God") is the belief that the divine intersects every part of the universe and also extends beyond space and time. The term was coined by German philosopher Karl Krause in 1828 to describe his own theological system, in an effort to reconcile traditional theism and pantheism.

Definition

Panentheism holds that God is both immanent in the world — the universe is contained within God — and transcendent, since God is greater than the universe. It differs from pantheism, which identifies God with the universe, and from classical theism, which typically emphasises God's separateness from creation. A common analogy is that of the universe as God's body, though panentheists vary widely in how literally they take this.

History

Ideas resembling panentheism appear in Hindu thought (for example in Vishishtadvaita and in passages of the Upanishads and Bhagavad Gita), in some strands of Neoplatonism, and in the mysticism of several traditions. Krause's coinage was taken up by German idealists and later by process theologians such as Charles Hartshorne and John B. Cobb Jr., for whom God is "dipolar" — both eternal and unchanging in essence, and temporal and affected by the world in experience. Panentheistic themes also appear in Orthodox Christian theology's distinction between God's essence and energies, and in the work of theologians such as Jürgen Moltmann and Philip Clayton.

Varieties and criticism

Panentheism ranges from views close to classical theism, in which the world exists "in God" by participation, to views close to pantheism. Critics argue the label is stretched across incompatible positions, while defenders see it as a middle way preserving both divine transcendence and intimate involvement with creation.
