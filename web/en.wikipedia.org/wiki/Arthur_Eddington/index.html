Sir Arthur Stanley Eddington (28 December 1882 – 22 November 1944) was an English astronomer, physicist and mathematician, one of the leading astrophysicists of the early twentieth century. He is best known for his work on the structure and evolution of stars, and for leading the 1919 solar-eclipse expeditions whose observations were announced as confirming Einstein's general theory of relativity.

Eddington was Plumian Professor of Astronomy at Cambridge and director of the Cambridge Observatory. He was also an influential populariser of science and a Quaker, whose religious convictions led him to be a conscientious objector during the First World War.

Stellar structure and relativity

In The Internal Constitution of the Stars (1926), Eddington developed the first physical models of stellar interiors, established the mass–luminosity relation for main-sequence stars, and identified the Eddington limit — the maximum luminosity of a body in hydrostatic equilibrium, above which radiation pressure blows material away.

He was among the first English-speaking scientists to learn of Einstein's general relativity, and co-authored the 1918 report that introduced it to the English-speaking world. His 1919 eclipse measurements from Príncipe island, announced jointly with a parallel expedition to Sobral, Brazil, made Einstein world-famous.

Later work and controversies

Eddington championed Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar's early work, then controversially rejected Chandrasekhar's limit for white-dwarf mass in 1935 — a dispute now judged to have delayed recognition of black-hole physics. His later 'fundamental theory', an attempt to derive physics from pure reasoning and numerology, was not accepted by the scientific community.

Despite this, his astrophysical work — stellar structure, radiative equilibrium, the physics of the interstellar medium — remains foundational, and his books, including The Nature of the Physical World (1928), are classics of science writing.
