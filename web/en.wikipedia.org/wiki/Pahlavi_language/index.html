Pahlavi, also known as Middle Persian, is an extinct Middle Iranian language that was the literary and administrative language of the Sasanian Empire (224–651 CE) and remained in use by Zoroastrian communities for several centuries after the Muslim conquest of Persia. It developed from Old Persian, the language of the Achaemenid inscriptions, and is a direct ancestor — in vocabulary and structure — of modern Persian (Farsi), though New Persian came to be written in the Arabic script.

History

The term "Pahlavi" derives from Parthava, "Parthia," reflecting the language's origins in the Parthian era. Middle Persian became the official language of the Sasanian state and the vehicle of Zoroastrian religious literature. After the 7th-century conquest, it gradually gave way to New Persian as a spoken language, but Zoroastrian priests continued to copy and compose Pahlavi texts into the 9th and 10th centuries.

Script

Pahlavi was written in scripts derived from Imperial Aramaic, written right to left. Several variants existed: Inscriptional Pahlavi (used on coins and royal inscriptions), Psalter Pahlavi, and the highly cursive Book Pahlavi used in manuscripts. The scripts are notorious for ambiguity — Book Pahlavi reduced the letter inventory so far that many characters represent several sounds — and for the use of Aramaic heterograms (huzwāresh), words written in Aramaic form but read as their Persian equivalents.

Literature

The surviving Pahlavi literature is predominantly religious: translations and commentaries (zand) on the Avesta, and 9th–10th century compilations such as the Bundahishn (a cosmogony), the Denkard ("Acts of Religion"), and the Arda Viraf Namag (the Book of Arda Viraf). Secular and wisdom literature includes the Karnamag i Ardashir i Babagan, a romanticized biography of the Sasanian founder, and the romance of Khusrau and his page.

Linguistic features

Compared with Old Persian, Middle Persian shows a greatly simplified inflectional system: the old case system and grammatical gender disappeared, and constructions such as the ezafe and split ergativity in past-tense transitive verbs emerged — features carried into New Persian.
