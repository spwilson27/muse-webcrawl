Halwa

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Image placeholder: Halwa

Halwa (also spelled halva, halwah and several other ways) is a family of dense, sweet confections originating in Persia and widely spread across the Middle East, Central and South Asia, the Balkans and parts of Europe and North Africa. The name derives from the Arabic ḥalwā (حلوى), meaning "sweet". Two broad families predominate: flour-based halwa, made by frying flour or semolina in oil or ghee and sweetening it with syrup, and nut-butter-based halva, most famously made from tahini (sesame paste) combined with hot sugar syrup.

Varieties

  • Semolina halwa (Indian sooji ka halwa, Greek halvas) — semolina toasted in fat with syrup, nuts and raisins; common in religious offerings and festivals.
  • Tahini halva — crumbly sesame confection sold in blocks, often marbled with chocolate or studded with pistachios; popular in Turkey, the Levant, Israel and the Balkans.
  • Sunflower halva — made with sunflower seed paste in Eastern Europe and Russia.
  • Gajar ka halwa — an Indian dessert of grated carrots simmered in milk, sugar and ghee.
  • Pashmak-like floss halvas and regional specialities such as Turkish pişmaniye and Iranian and Central Asian sohan-type sweets.

History

Halwa is described in medieval Arabic cookbooks and spread with Persian and Ottoman culinary influence. It remains associated with celebrations, fasting periods and hospitality across many cultures, including Ramadan iftars, Lent in Greek Orthodox tradition, and Diwali and prasad in India.
