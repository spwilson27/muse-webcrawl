Normal pressure hydrocephalus


Labelled placeholder: NPH brain imaging

Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), also called malresorptive hydrocephalus, is a form of communicating hydrocephalus in which excess cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) accumulates in the ventricles of the brain, causing them to enlarge, while intracranial pressure remains within normal limits. The condition classically presents with a triad of symptoms: gait disturbance, dementia (cognitive impairment), and urinary incontinence — sometimes remembered as "wobbly, wacky, and wet." NPH mainly affects older adults and is notable because it is one of the few potentially treatable causes of dementia.

Signs and symptoms

Gait disturbance usually appears first: a broad-based, shuffling "magnetic" gait in which the feet appear stuck to the floor. Cognitive symptoms include slowed thinking, apathy, forgetfulness and impaired executive function. Urinary urgency and incontinence tend to develop later. Symptoms typically progress gradually over at least three to six months.

Causes and mechanism

In idiopathic NPH the mechanism is thought to involve impaired absorption of CSF at the arachnoid granulations, possibly with altered CSF dynamics and reduced compliance of the brain, despite normal opening pressure on lumbar puncture. Secondary NPH can follow subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, head injury, or brain surgery.

Diagnosis

Neuroimaging (MRI or CT) shows enlarged ventricles (Evans index > 0.3) out of proportion to cortical sulcal enlargement, often with disproportionately enlarged subarachnoid-space hydrocephalus (DESH). Diagnostic tests include the lumbar tap test, external lumbar drainage, and CSF infusion studies to predict shunt responsiveness.

Treatment

Treatment is surgical placement of a cerebrospinal fluid shunt — most commonly a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, often with a programmable valve — draining excess CSF to the peritoneal cavity. Gait improves most reliably; cognitive and bladder symptoms may improve less. Complications include shunt malfunction, infection, and subdural hematoma from overdrainage.

History

NPH was described by the Colombian neurosurgeon Salomón Hakim in 1957, with the syndrome published in 1965 together with Raymond Adams; it is sometimes called Hakim's syndrome or the Hakim–Adams syndrome.
