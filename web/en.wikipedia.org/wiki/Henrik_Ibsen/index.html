Henrik Ibsen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: Henrik Ibsen in old age: the white-maned playwright with mutton-chop whiskers and a stern, heavy-lidded gaze]

Henrik Johan Ibsen (20 March 1828 – 23 May 1906) was a Norwegian playwright, theatre director and poet, the founder of modern realist drama and one of the most performed dramatists in the world. In a sequence of prose plays from The Pillars of Society (1877) to When We Dead Awaken (1899) he put the bourgeois drawing room under moral X-ray — marriage, money, heredity, respectability — and changed what theatre could be about.

His major plays include the verse dramas Brand (1866) and Peer Gynt (1867, with Grieg's music), A Doll's House (1879), Ghosts (1881), An Enemy of the People (1882), The Wild Duck (1884), Rosmersholm (1886), Hedda Gabler (1890) and The Master Builder (1892).

Exile and the prose cycle

Born in the timber port of Skien to a merchant family ruined when he was eight, Ibsen apprenticed as an apothecary, failed into the theatre at Bergen (where he staged plays and married Suzannah Thoresen) and Christiania, and in 1864 left Norway in frustration for 27 years of exile in Rome, Dresden and Munich. Distance sharpened his aim: the dozen prose plays of his maturity dissected Norwegian provincial life with a technique of retrospection — the past unpacked scene by scene — that became modern drama's grammar.

Fame and controversy

A Doll's House's final door-slam was heard round Europe; Ghosts, with its syphilis and its ghosts of dead ideas, was called an open drain in London. Chekhov, Shaw (whose Quintessence of Ibsenism, 1891, made him England's Ibsenite-in-chief), Joyce and Arthur Miller are among his heirs; he returned to Christiania (Oslo) in 1891 as a national monument, dying there in 1906.
