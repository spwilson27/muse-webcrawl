Waffen-SS

The Waffen-SS (German: [ˈvafn̩ʔɛsˌʔɛs]; "Armed SS") was the armed wing of the Nazi Party's Schutzstaffel (SS). It grew from three regiments in 1939 into an organisation of more than 38 divisions by 1945, fighting alongside — but never formally part of — the Wehrmacht. Its units included Germans and, increasingly as the war went on, foreign volunteers and conscripts from occupied and allied countries. The Waffen-SS was responsible for extensive war crimes, and at Nuremberg the SS, including its armed formations (with exceptions mainly for conscripts), was declared a criminal organisation.

Origins

The Waffen-SS developed from the SS-Verfügungstruppe, Hitler's personal guard (the Leibstandarte SS Adolf Hitler) and the Death's Head (Totenkopf) units that ran concentration camps. Under Heinrich Himmler it expanded into a parallel army ideologically committed to the Nazi regime, competing with the Wehrmacht for men and equipment.

Second World War

Elite panzer divisions such as Leibstandarte, Das Reich, Totenkopf and Wiking fought on the Eastern Front and in Normandy and the Ardennes, gaining a reputation for both battlefield effectiveness and fanaticism. Waffen-SS units committed numerous atrocities, including the Malmedy massacre of American prisoners (1944), the Oradour-sur-Glane massacre in France (1944), and widespread massacres of civilians and Jews in the East. As manpower shortages grew, the organisation recruited or conscripted hundreds of thousands of non-Germans — Balts, Ukrainians, Bosnian Muslims, French, Scandinavians and others — many of them unwilling.

Post-war

The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg declared the SS a criminal organisation. Veterans' lobbying groups such as HIAG later sought to portray the Waffen-SS as "soldiers like any others", a claim rejected by historians, and public commemoration of Waffen-SS units remains controversial, especially in the Baltic states.
