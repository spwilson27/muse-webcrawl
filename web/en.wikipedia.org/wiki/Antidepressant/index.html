Antidepressant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Antidepressants are a class of medications used to treat major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, chronic pain and some other conditions. Their use has increased markedly since the late 1980s with the introduction of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs).

Classes

The main classes are: SSRIs (e.g. fluoxetine, sertraline, escitalopram), which block serotonin reuptake; SNRIs (e.g. venlafaxine, duloxetine), which also block norepinephrine reuptake; tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline, imipramine), an older class with more side effects and toxicity in overdose; monoamine oxidase inhibitors (e.g. phenelzine), restricted by dietary interactions; and atypical agents such as bupropion, mirtazapine and the rapid-acting NMDA antagonist esketamine, approved for treatment-resistant depression.

Effectiveness and side effects

Meta-analyses find antidepressants modestly more effective than placebo on average, with larger benefits in severe depression; individual response varies and several trials are often needed. Common side effects include nausea, sexual dysfunction, weight change and sleep disturbance. Abrupt discontinuation can cause a discontinuation syndrome, and regulators carry warnings about a possible increase in suicidal thinking in adolescents and young adults early in treatment.

History

The first antidepressants were discovered by serendipity in the 1950s: iproniazid, a tuberculosis drug and MAO inhibitor, and imipramine, the first tricyclic. These discoveries gave rise to the monoamine hypothesis of depression. Fluoxetine (Prozac), launched in 1987, made antidepressants a cultural phenomenon in the 1990s.
