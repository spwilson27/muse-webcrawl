Molybdenum

Molybdenum is a chemical element with the symbol Mo and atomic number 42. It is a silvery-grey transition metal with an extremely high melting point (2,623 °C), among the highest of all pure elements. Molybdenite, its principal ore, was long confused with lead ores and graphite; the element was distinguished by Carl Wilhelm Scheele in 1778 and first isolated by Peter Jacob Hjelm in 1781. Its name comes from the Greek molybdos, meaning lead.

Applications

About 86% of molybdenum produced is used in metallurgy, chiefly in steels and superalloys, where it greatly improves strength, hardness, corrosion resistance, and performance at high temperature. It is used in aircraft parts, industrial motors, filaments, and as a catalyst in petroleum refining. Molybdenum compounds such as molybdenum disulfide serve as dry lubricants.

Biological role

Molybdenum is an essential trace element: it sits at the active site of enzymes such as nitrogenase (used by bacteria to fix nitrogen), sulfite oxidase, and xanthine oxidase, in the form of the molybdenum cofactor. See also: Periodic table, Transition metal.
