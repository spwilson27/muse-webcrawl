Progressivism in the United States

Progressivism in the United States is a political philosophy and reform movement that reached its height during the Progressive Era of the 1890s to the 1920s. Progressives sought to address problems caused by industrialization, urbanization, immigration, and political corruption through government action, expertise, and social reform.

Progressive Era reforms

Reformers achieved antitrust legislation, regulation of railroads and food safety, the federal income tax (16th Amendment), direct election of senators (17th Amendment), prohibition (18th Amendment), and women's suffrage (19th Amendment). Presidents Theodore Roosevelt, William Howard Taft, and Woodrow Wilson each advanced progressive agendas. Muckraking journalists such as Upton Sinclair and Ida Tarbell exposed abuses that fueled reform.

Later development

Modern American progressivism, revived as a label in the 21st century, advocates policies such as universal healthcare, action on climate change, higher minimum wages, reduced income inequality, and regulation of large corporations. It is a prominent tendency within the Democratic Party, though the term's meaning differs from its Progressive Era usage.
