De Excidio et Conquestu Britanniae (Latin for "On the Ruin and Conquest of Britain") is a work written in Latin by the 6th-century AD British cleric Gildas. It is a sermon, in three parts, in which Gildas condemns the acts of his contemporaries, both secular and religious, in post-Roman Britain, and is an important—though problematic—source for the history of Britain in the 5th and 6th centuries.

Summary

The first part consists of an explanation of Gildas's work and a brief narrative of Roman and post-Roman Britain, from its conquest under Rome to Gildas's own time. It includes references to the Roman withdrawal, the appeal to Aetius (the "Groans of the Britons"), the arrival of the Saxons, and the victory of Ambrosius Aurelianus at the Battle of Badon. It is one of the few contemporary sources for these events.

The second part is a condemnation of five British kings—Constantine, Aurelius Conanus, Vortipor, Cuneglas, and Maglocunus (Maelgwn)—whom Gildas denounces for their sins in the manner of an Old Testament prophet. The third part is a similar attack on the clergy of the age, whom he accuses of corruption and incompetence.

Dating and significance

The date of composition is disputed, with proposals ranging from the late 5th century to the 570s; many scholars place it around 540. Gildas's account was used extensively by Bede in his Ecclesiastical History of the English People, and the text survives in manuscripts dating from the 11th century onward, the earliest (British Library Cotton Vitellius A. VI) having been badly damaged by fire in 1731.

Historians treat the work cautiously: it was written as a moral sermon rather than a history, and its chronology is vague. Nevertheless, it remains the only substantial written source produced in Britain during the period it describes.
