Entropy compression

In mathematics and theoretical computer science, entropy compression is an information-theoretic method for proving that a random process terminates, originally used by Robin Moser to prove an algorithmic version of the Lovász local lemma. The method is related to the incompressibility method and the analysis of algorithms by encoding their execution history.

The method

The entropy compression argument works as follows. Suppose a randomized algorithm runs for many steps while consuming random bits, and suppose its entire execution can be logged in such a way that the algorithm (or an external observer) could be perfectly reconstructed from the log plus the random bits it has not yet used. If one can show that running the algorithm for a very long time would allow the random bits consumed to be compressed — encoded in fewer bits than the number actually used — this contradicts the fact that most random strings are incompressible. Hence the algorithm must terminate with high probability, and quickly.

The name comes from this accounting: the "entropy" (randomness) consumed by the algorithm is compressed by exploiting the structure of the history log.

Applications

Moser introduced the technique in 2009 to give the first constructive, algorithmic proof of the Lovász local lemma, showing that under the lemma's conditions a satisfying assignment can be found efficiently by a simple resampling algorithm. The paper of Moser and Gábor Tardos (2010) developed the general resampling framework. Entropy compression has since been used to obtain improved bounds in combinatorics, including results on non-repetitive colourings, acyclic edge colouring of graphs, and other problems previously handled only by probabilistic counting arguments.
