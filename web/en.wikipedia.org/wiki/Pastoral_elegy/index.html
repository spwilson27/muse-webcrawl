Pastoral elegy

A pastoral elegy is a poem about both death and idyllic rural life, usually featuring shepherds who mourn a fellow shepherd or poet. It is a subgenre of the pastoral and the elegy.

Pastoral elegy
Form: Elegiac poem
Precursor: Theocritus, Idyll I
Famous examples: Lycidas, Adonais, Thyrsis

Conventions

Typical conventions include invocation of the Muse, the mourning of all nature, processions of mourners, attacks on corrupt clergy or fate, description of flowers strewn on the bier, and a concluding consolation or apotheosis in which the dead shepherd is resurrected or taken into heaven.

History

The form begins with Theocritus's lament for Daphnis, Bion's Lament for Adonis, and the Lament for Bion attributed to Moschus. Virgil's fifth Eclogue mourns Daphnis. Renaissance examples include Edmund Spenser's Astrophel for Philip Sidney.

Major English examples

John Milton's Lycidas (1637), for Edward King, is often considered the greatest pastoral elegy in English. Percy Bysshe Shelley's Adonais (1821) mourns John Keats, and Matthew Arnold's Thyrsis (1865) mourns Arthur Hugh Clough. Later poets both used and subverted the artificiality of the form.
