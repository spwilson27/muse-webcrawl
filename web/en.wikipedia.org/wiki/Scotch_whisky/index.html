Scotch whisky (Scottish Gaelic: uisge-beatha, "water of life"), often simply called Scotch, is malt whisky or grain whisky (or a blend of the two) made in Scotland. By law, Scotch must be produced at a distillery in Scotland from water and malted barley (to which only whole grains of other cereals may be added), fermented only by yeast, distilled at less than 94.8% alcohol by volume, and matured for at least three years in oak casks in Scotland, before being bottled at a minimum of 40% ABV.

Categories

The Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009 define five categories: single malt (from one distillery, malted barley only), single grain, blended malt, blended grain, and blended Scotch whisky (a blend of malt and grain whiskies). Blended Scotch accounts for the great majority of sales worldwide, while single malts command premium prices.

Regions

Traditionally Scotch is grouped by producing region: the Highlands (including the islands), Speyside, the Lowlands, Islay — famous for heavily peated, smoky malts — and Campbeltown. Distilleries traditionally smoked some malt over peat fires, giving regional flavour variation.

History

The earliest written record of distilling in Scotland dates to 1494, in an Exchequer Roll entry for Friar John Cor to make aqua vitae. Illicit stills flourished until the Excise Act of 1823 licensed legal distilling. The invention of the Coffey (continuous) still made grain whisky possible, phylloxera's devastation of cognac vineyards in the 1880s opened the English market, and Prohibition-era exports and post-war global marketing made Scotch one of the world's great spirits and one of the UK's largest exports.
