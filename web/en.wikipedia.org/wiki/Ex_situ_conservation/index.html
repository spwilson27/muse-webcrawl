Ex situ conservation

Ex situ conservation (Latin for "off-site conservation") is the process of protecting an endangered species of plant or animal outside its natural habitat — for example, by removing part of the population from a threatened habitat and placing it in a new location, such as a zoo, botanical garden, seed bank, or captive breeding programme. It is complementary to in situ conservation, which protects species in their natural surroundings.

Techniques

Facilities used for ex situ conservation include seed banks, field gene banks, cryopreservation stores, zoos, aquaria, and botanical gardens. Seeds of many crops are dried and frozen at low temperature; the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway, opened in 2008, stores duplicate seed samples from gene banks around the world in permafrost-cooled vaults. The Millennium Seed Bank Partnership, coordinated by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew, conserves seeds from tens of thousands of wild plant species.

For animals, captive breeding programmes coordinated through studbooks — such as the European Endangered Species Programme and the Species Survival Plan — manage genetic diversity with the goal of eventual reintroduction, as achieved with the Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, and the California condor.

Advantages and limitations

Ex situ methods provide insurance against extinction in the wild, material for research and education, and stock for restoring populations. Limitations include adaptation to captivity, loss of genetic diversity, high cost, and the fact that species may survive only while their habitats remain destroyed. Conservation biologists therefore treat ex situ work as a backup to habitat protection rather than a substitute for it.
