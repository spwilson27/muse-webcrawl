Diastole

Diastole (/daɪˈæstəli/ dy-AST-ə-lee) is the relaxed phase of the cardiac cycle when the chambers of the heart re-fill with blood. The contrasting phase is systole, when the chambers contract and eject blood. The term comes from the Greek διάστολη (diastolē), meaning dilation or expansion.

Physiology

During ventricular diastole, pressure in the ventricles drops below that in the atria, the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves open, and blood flows passively from the atria and great veins into the ventricles; atrial contraction in late diastole tops up ventricular filling. Meanwhile the semilunar valves are closed, preventing backflow from the aorta and pulmonary artery, and the coronary arteries fill preferentially during this phase.

Diastole is conventionally divided into early diastole (including isovolumetric relaxation and rapid filling), diastasis (slow passive filling), and late diastole (atrial systole). At rest, diastole occupies roughly two-thirds of the cardiac cycle; as heart rate rises, diastole shortens disproportionately.

Clinical relevance

The lower number in a blood pressure reading is the diastolic arterial pressure maintained during diastole. Impaired ventricular relaxation or increased stiffness causes diastolic dysfunction, which can lead to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).
