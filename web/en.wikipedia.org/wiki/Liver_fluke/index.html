Liver fluke

Fasciola hepatica, the common sheep liver fluke.

Liver fluke is a collective name for parasitic flatworms (trematodes) that live in the liver, bile ducts, or gallbladder of their vertebrate hosts. The most important species are Fasciola hepatica (the common liver fluke of sheep and cattle), Fasciola gigantica, and the "Asian liver flukes" Clonorchis sinensis and Opisthorchis species, which infect humans who eat raw freshwater fish. Collectively these parasites infect tens of millions of people and cause serious losses in livestock production.

Life cycle

Liver flukes have complex life cycles involving a freshwater snail as intermediate host. Eggs shed in the host's faeces hatch into miracidia that infect snails; after asexual multiplication, cercariae emerge and encyst as metacercariae — on vegetation in the case of Fasciola, or within fish in the case of Clonorchis and Opisthorchis. The definitive host is infected by eating the encysted stage. The young flukes migrate through the liver to the bile ducts, where they mature and may live for many years.

Disease

Infection with Fasciola (fasciolosis) causes an acute phase of fever and abdominal pain during migration, then chronic bile-duct inflammation. The fish-borne flukes cause clonorchiasis and opisthorchiasis; chronic infection is strongly associated with cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer), and Opisthorchis viverrini and Clonorchis sinensis are classified as Group 1 carcinogens. In livestock, fluke burdens cause anaemia, weight loss ("liver rot"), condemned livers, and death.

Treatment and control

Triclabendazole is the drug of choice for fasciolosis, being effective against both immature and adult flukes; praziquantel is used for the fish-borne species. Control combines snail management, strategic deworming of livestock, safe disposal of sewage, and health education to discourage consumption of raw water plants and raw fish.
