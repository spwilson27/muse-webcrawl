Leukoplakia

Leukoplakia
Specialty: Dentistry, oral medicine
Appearance: White patch on oral mucosa that cannot be scraped off

Leukoplakia is a firmly attached white patch on a mucous membrane that is associated with an increased risk of cancer. It most commonly affects the oral cavity — the tongue, gums, floor of the mouth, and inner cheeks — though it can also occur elsewhere in the gastrointestinal tract, urinary tract, and larynx.

Causes and risk factors

The precise cause is often unknown, but chronic irritation is a major factor. Tobacco use, both smoked and smokeless, is the most common association; alcohol, friction from rough teeth or dentures, and human papillomavirus infection have also been implicated. Homogeneous leukoplakia consists of a uniform white plaque, whereas non-homogeneous forms — speckled, nodular, or verrucous — carry a higher risk of malignant transformation.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical, defined by exclusion: a white patch that cannot be characterised as any other disease and cannot be scraped off (unlike oral candidiasis). Biopsy is commonly performed to check for dysplasia or carcinoma. Management involves removing irritants, stopping tobacco use, and long-term monitoring; some lesions are surgically excised.

Prognosis

A proportion of leukoplakias undergo malignant transformation into squamous cell carcinoma, with reported rates varying widely by population and lesion type, which is why the condition is classed among the oral potentially malignant disorders.
