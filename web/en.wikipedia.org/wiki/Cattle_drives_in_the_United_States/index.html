Cattle drives in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cattle drives were the overland movement of herds of cattle from ranching regions, especially Texas, to railheads and markets in the northern United States. The classic era of the great drives lasted roughly from 1866 to the mid-1880s, during which an estimated five to six million cattle were trailed north, creating the enduring image of the American cowboy.

Origins

During the American Civil War, Texas herds multiplied untended while northern demand for beef soared; after the war a steer worth a few dollars in Texas could fetch ten times that at eastern markets. In 1867 Joseph McCoy established Abilene, Kansas, as a railhead on the Kansas Pacific Railway and promoted the Chisholm Trail from Texas, opening the great trailing era.

Famous trails

The Chisholm Trail ran from south Texas through Indian Territory to Kansas rail towns such as Abilene, Ellsworth, Wichita and Dodge City. The Western Trail later served Dodge City and continued north to Ogallala, Nebraska. The Goodnight–Loving Trail, blazed in 1866 by Charles Goodnight and Oliver Loving, ran west to New Mexico and Colorado. A typical drive of 2,000–3,000 longhorns employed about a dozen cowboys under a trail boss, with a chuck wagon, cook and a remuda of spare horses, covering 15–25 km per day over journeys of two to four months.

Decline

The drives declined as railroads reached Texas, barbed wire fenced the open range, quarantine laws restricted Texas cattle carrying tick fever, and the harsh winters of 1886–87 destroyed herds on the northern plains. Ranchers shifted to raising cattle nearer to rail transport.

Legacy

The cattle-drive era shaped the mythology of the American West in dime novels, Wild West shows, film and television, and its trails remain marked heritage routes in Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas.
