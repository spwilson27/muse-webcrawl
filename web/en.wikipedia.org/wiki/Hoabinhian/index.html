Hoabinhian

The Hoabinhian is a lithic industry and archaeological technocomplex of Southeast Asia, dating from roughly 32,000 to 2,000 years ago, first defined by French archaeologists from assemblages excavated in Hòa Bình Province, northern Vietnam, in the 1920s. It is characterised by unifacially flaked pebble tools, most famously the "Sumatralith".

Definition and distribution

The term was adopted at the First Congress of Prehistorians of the Far East in Hanoi in 1932. Hoabinhian assemblages are found across mainland Southeast Asia — Vietnam, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Myanmar, and Malaysia — and into southern China and Sumatra. Tool assemblages typically include cobble tools (choppers and picks), flake tools, and, in later phases, edge-ground axes heralding the Neolithic.

Subsistence and burials

Hoabinhian people were hunter-gatherers exploiting a wide range of forest and riverine resources, evidenced by faunal remains of deer, wild cattle, shellfish, and fish. Some burials, often flexed and accompanied by ochre and cobbles, have been excavated at sites such as Gua Cha in Malaysia and numerous Vietnamese cave sites. Debate continues over whether late Hoabinhian groups practised incipient plant management, including the use of betel nut and other domesticates.
