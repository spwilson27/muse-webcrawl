Canon law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Canon law (from Greek kanon, a rule or measuring rod) is a set of ordinances and regulations made by ecclesiastical authority for the government of a Christian organisation and its members. The Catholic Church's canon law is the oldest continuously functioning legal system in the West, codified most recently in the 1983 Code of Canon Law for the Latin Church (replacing the 1917 Code) and the 1990 Code of Canons of the Eastern Churches; Anglican, Orthodox and other churches maintain their own canons.

History and content

Medieval canon law — systematised by Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) and papal decretals — covered clergy discipline, sacraments, marriage (whose rules shaped European family law for centuries), church property, crimes and procedure, and interacted with revived Roman law to form the ius commune. Its courts and concepts (due process elements, corporate personality, just-war and natural-law theorising) influenced secular legal development, including common-law equity and international law's founders. Today Catholic canon law governs internal matters: sacraments, annulments of marriage, clergy discipline and penalties (such as excommunication), church governance and temporal goods — with enforcement and reform (notably on sexual-abuse cases) matters of wide public attention.
