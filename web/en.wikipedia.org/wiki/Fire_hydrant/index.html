Fire hydrant

A fire hydrant, fireplug, firecock (archaic), hydrant, or Johnny pump (New York City slang) is a connection point by which firefighters can tap into a water supply. It is a component of active fire protection. Underground fire hydrants have been used in Europe and Asia since at least the 18th century, while above-ground pillar-type hydrants are a 19th-century invention.

Design

The user attaches a hose to the fire hydrant, then opens a valve on the hydrant to provide a powerful flow of water, on the order of 350 kPa (50 psi) or more, depending on the municipal water supply. The user can attach this hose to a fire engine, which can use a powerful pump to boost the water pressure and possibly split it into multiple streams. Care must be taken not to open or close a fire hydrant too quickly, as this can cause a water hammer.

In areas subject to freezing, only part of the hydrant is above ground. These dry-barrel hydrants keep the main valve below the frost line and drain after use, so no water remains in the barrel to freeze. In warm climates, wet-barrel hydrants keep water up to the outlets at all times, with a separate valve for each outlet. Most dry-barrel hydrants are designed with a flanged breakaway joint at ground level so that a vehicle strike breaks the flange rather than the valve, limiting water loss.

Appearance and colour coding

Hydrants are painted in bright colours for visibility. In the United States, NFPA 291 recommends colour-coding the top and caps to indicate available flow: red for less than 500 US gallons per minute, orange for 500–999, green for 1,000–1,499, and light blue for 1,500 or more. Bodies are often silver, chrome yellow or red. In the United Kingdom and many other countries hydrants are underground, marked by a yellow "H" sign.

History

Before piped water supplies, firefighters relied on bucket brigades and hand-pumped engines filled from cisterns, wells and "fire plugs" — wooden pipes that could be bored into for water. Cast-iron mains and standardised hydrants spread in the 1800s; the modern dry-barrel design is associated with inventions such as the 1801 patent of Frederick Graff Sr. of the Philadelphia Water Works and later improvements by Birdsill Holly.

Other uses and misuse

Unauthorised use of hydrants to cool off in summer — often with a spray cap to limit flow — is a long-standing practice in some cities but is restricted because of water waste and pressure loss. Hydrants are also used for flushing water mains and, with backflow prevention, for filling street sweepers and tankers.
