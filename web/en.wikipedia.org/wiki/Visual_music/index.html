Visual music, sometimes called colour music, is an art form in which visual elements — colour, shape, and movement — are organised in time in ways analogous to musical composition. It encompasses colour organs and light instruments, the abstract films of pioneers such as Oskar Fischinger, computer-generated animation, and contemporary live audiovisual performance. The term also describes films and videos that translate musical structures into imagery, as well as works by composers that take visual art as their model.

History

The idea of colour music dates back at least to the 18th century, when Louis-Bertrand Castel proposed his ocular harpsichord. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, inventors built colour organs such as Alexander Rimington's colour organ and Thomas Wilfred's Clavilux, which performed "lumia" compositions of pure light. Painters including Wassily Kandinsky theorised correspondences between sound and colour.

Film and animation

In the 1920s and 1930s, artists such as Walther Ruttmann, Hans Richter, and Oskar Fischinger created abstract animated films synchronised to music. Mary Ellen Bute, John and James Whitney, Jordan Belson, and Norman McLaren extended the field with optical printing, pendulum machines, and early computer graphics. John Whitney's pioneering use of computers in the 1960s laid groundwork for today's real-time visual performance and music visualizers.

Visual music today

Digital tools have made visual music widespread: VJs perform live visuals at concerts, software generates imagery from audio, and artists explore synaesthetic correspondences between sound and image. The Center for Visual Music preserves and promotes historical works in the field.
