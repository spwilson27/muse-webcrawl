Fu-Go (Japanese: 風船爆弾, fūsen bakudan, lit. "balloon bomb") was an incendiary balloon weapon deployed by Japan against the United States during World War II. A hydrogen balloon with a load varying from a 12-kilogram (26 lb) incendiary bomb to one 15 kg (33 lb) antipersonnel bomb and four 5 kg (11 lb) incendiary devices attached, the Fu-Go was designed as a cheap weapon intended to make use of the jet stream over the Pacific Ocean to reach the continental United States and cause forest fires and panic.

Operation

Between November 1944 and April 1945, Japan launched more than 9,000 balloons from sites on Honshu. The balloons, made of paper or rubberised silk, carried a barometric altitude-control device that vented hydrogen when they rose too high and dropped sandbag ballast when they sank, keeping them in the jet stream at about 9,000 metres for the roughly three-day crossing. At the end of the flight the mechanism released the bombs and an explosive charge destroyed the balloon.

About 300 balloons were found or observed across North America, from Alaska and Canada to Mexico, but the damage inflicted was minimal. The only deaths attributed to the weapon occurred on 5 May 1945 near Bly, Oregon, where a woman and five children were killed when they disturbed a bomb on the ground — the only known fatalities on the continental United States from enemy action in the war. One balloon also briefly shorted power lines feeding the Hanford Site in Washington, interrupting the plutonium production plant's electricity supply for a time.

News blackout and end of the campaign

American authorities imposed a voluntary press blackout on reports of balloon landings, reasoning that denying Japan any knowledge of results would discourage the attacks. Deprived of evidence of success — and having lost its hydrogen plants to bombing — Japan abandoned the campaign in April 1945. The Fu-Go remains history's first intercontinental weapon delivery system, and the longest-range attacks ever conducted at that time.
