The Second Council of Nicaea is recognized as the last of the first seven ecumenical councils by the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Catholic Church. It met in 787 AD in Nicaea (now İznik, Turkey), the city where the First Council of Nicaea had been held in 325. The council was convened by the Byzantine empress Irene, acting as regent for her son Constantine VI, and presided over by Patriarch Tarasios of Constantinople.

Background

Since 726, when Emperor Leo III began removing religious images, the Byzantine Empire had been divided by Iconoclasm, the official policy forbidding the making and veneration of icons of Christ, the Virgin Mary, and the saints. The iconoclast Council of Hieria (754) had condemned icons as idolatrous. Irene, an iconophile, sought to reverse the policy and restore unity with the papacy and the West; papal legates from Pope Adrian I attended the council.

Proceedings and decree

An initial attempt to meet in Constantinople in 786 was broken up by iconoclast soldiers, so the council reconvened at Nicaea in September 787. Over 300 bishops attended across eight sessions. The council annulled the decrees of Hieria and issued a decree (the Horos) permitting the veneration of icons. It drew the classic distinction between latreia — the worship due to God alone — and proskynesis, the relative honour or veneration (dulia) given to icons, which passes to the prototype represented, following the teaching of St Basil the Great and the theology of John of Damascus. The council also issued 22 disciplinary canons.

Aftermath

The council's decrees were initially mistranslated for Charlemagne's court, leading to the Frankish Libri Carolini and the Council of Frankfurt (794) rejecting them, but Rome upheld Nicaea II. Iconoclasm was briefly renewed in the empire from 815 until the final restoration of icons in 843, an event celebrated in Orthodoxy as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. Nicaea II remains the authoritative settlement of the icon question in both the Catholic and Orthodox churches, though many Protestant traditions rejected its decrees during the Reformation.
