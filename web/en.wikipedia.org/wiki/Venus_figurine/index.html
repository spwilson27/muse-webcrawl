Venus figurine

Venus figurines are prehistoric statuettes of women, mostly dating from the Gravettian period of the Upper Palaeolithic (roughly 30,000–22,000 years ago), found across Europe and as far east as Siberia. Typically carved from stone, ivory, bone or clay and between about 3 and 40 cm tall, they often emphasise breasts, hips and abdomen, with small or absent faces, arms and feet.

Discovery and naming

The name, coined in the 19th century after the Roman goddess Venus, reflects early assumptions that they were erotic or fertility idols. The ceramic Venus of Dolní Věstonice is among the oldest known fired-clay objects; the ivory Venus of Hohle Fels (c. 40,000 BP) is among the earliest figurative art.

Interpretation

Proposed meanings include fertility symbols, mother-goddess religion, self-portraits by women, obstetric or health amulets, erotica, and clan or exchange tokens. Many figurines were coated with red ochre. No single explanation commands consensus, and modern scholars caution against projecting later goddess religions onto Palaeolithic finds.

Distribution

Over 200 figurines are known, concentrated in France, Italy, Central Europe, Ukraine and Russia, with notable gaps and regional styles in dress, hair and ornament details.
