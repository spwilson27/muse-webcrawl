Pictogram

A pictogram (also called a pictograph or pictogramme) is a graphic symbol that conveys its meaning through its pictorial resemblance to a physical object. Pictograms are frequently used in writing and graphic systems in which the characters are to a considerable extent pictorial in appearance. Early written symbols were based on pictographs (pictures which resemble what they signify) and ideograms (symbols which represent ideas).

Modern pictograms for public information, designed to be understood without words

History

Pictography was a form of proto-writing used in Neolithic cultures worldwide; notable examples include the Jiahu symbols of China (c. 6600 BC) and later rock art traditions. Pictographs formed the basis of early writing systems such as Sumerian cuneiform and Egyptian hieroglyphs, in which stylised pictures gradually acquired phonetic values. Chinese characters likewise descend in part from pictographs.

Modern use

Because pictograms can be understood across language barriers, they are widely used for wayfinding and safety communication: road signs, airport signage, Olympic sports symbols (introduced prominently at the 1964 Tokyo Games), hazard pictograms under the Globally Harmonized System of classification and labelling of chemicals, and the standard symbols developed by the US Department of Transportation and AIGA in the 1970s. The Austrian philosopher Otto Neurath pioneered the systematic use of pictograms for statistics with his Isotype system in the 1920s–30s.
