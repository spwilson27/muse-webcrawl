Wicklow Mountains

Wicklow Mountains
Irish: Sléibhte Chill Mhantáin
Highest peak: Lugnaquilla (925 m)
Location: County Wicklow, Ireland
Geology: Granite with mica-schist

The Wicklow Mountains (Irish: Sléibhte Chill Mhantáin) form the largest continuous upland region in Ireland. They occupy the whole centre of County Wicklow and extend into County Dublin, County Carlow, and County Wexford. The highest peak is Lugnaquilla at 925 metres (3,035 ft). The range is part of the Wicklow Mountains National Park, one of Ireland's national parks, established in 1991.

Geology and landscape

The mountains consist mainly of a granite core surrounded by mica-schist, formed during the Caledonian orogeny about 400 million years ago and sculpted by glaciers during the last ice age into rounded summits, deep corrie lakes, and U-shaped valleys such as Glenmalure, Glendalough, and Glenmacnass. The Military Road, built by the British Army after the 1798 rebellion to gain access to the hills, traverses the range and passes the bleak Sally Gap and Wicklow Gap.

History and recreation

The mountains sheltered rebels after the 1798 rising, including Michael Dwyer. Today the area is popular for hillwalking; the Wicklow Way, Ireland's first waymarked trail (opened 1980), runs about 127 km through the range. Glendalough, with its early medieval monastic site founded by St Kevin, is a major visitor attraction. Reservoirs in the range, including those at Poulaphouca (Blessington Lakes) and Vartry, supply Dublin with drinking water, and peat bogs on the summits are an important habitat and carbon store.
