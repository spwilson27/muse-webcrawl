Goguryeo–Sui War

The Goguryeo–Sui War were a series of campaigns launched by the Sui dynasty of China against Goguryeo, one of the Three Kingdoms of Korea, between 598 and 614. The Sui's costly and ultimately unsuccessful invasions — above all the catastrophic campaign of 612 — exhausted the dynasty's resources, provoked widespread revolt, and contributed directly to the Sui's collapse in 618. The wars are celebrated in Korean history for the resistance of Goguryeo, in particular the victory of the general Eulji Mundeok at the Battle of Salsu River.

Background

After reunifying China in 589, the Sui dynasty demanded submission from Goguryeo, which controlled the Liaodong Peninsula and northern Korea and maintained relations with the Eastern Turks. In 597 Goguryeo forces raided Sui territory, giving Emperor Wen a pretext for war.

The campaign of 598

In 598 Emperor Wen launched an invasion by land and sea with some 300,000 troops. Storms wrecked the Sui fleet, disease and Goguryeo resistance stalled the land advance, and the expedition withdrew with heavy losses. A negotiated settlement followed in which King Yeongyang nominally apologised.

The campaign of 612

Emperor Yang, who succeeded in 604, prepared for years: he mobilised an army that Chinese sources put at over a million men, dug the Grand Canal extension toward the northeast, and concentrated supplies near the border. The 612 invasion besieged the fortress of Liaodong (Yodong) without success, while a detached force of some 305,000 men advanced on the Goguryeo capital, Pyongyang. Feigning surrender negotiations, Eulji Mundeok lured the Sui army deep into Goguryeo territory, then ambushed it as it retreated across the Salsu River (the modern Chongchon). Sui sources claimed only about 2,700 of the detached force survived.

Campaigns of 613 and 614

Emperor Yang invaded again in 613, but the rebellion of his official Yang Xuangan forced a withdrawal. A fourth campaign in 614 ended with a nominal Goguryeo submission and the surrender of a Sui defector, after which the exhausted emperor abandoned further attempts.

Aftermath

The wars' cost in men, treasure and corvée labour ignited rebellions across China; the Sui dynasty fell in 618 and was replaced by the Tang dynasty, which would resume the struggle against Goguryeo in the Goguryeo–Tang War. In Korea the wars, and Salsu in particular, became enduring symbols of national resistance.
