Myotome

A myotome is a group of muscles that a single spinal nerve innervates. The term is also used in embryology for the part of a somite that develops into skeletal muscle. Testing myotomes is a standard part of the neurological examination, because weakness in a particular myotome can localize damage to a specific spinal cord level or nerve root.

Embryology

During development, each somite divides into a sclerotome (forming vertebrae and ribs), a dermatome (forming dermis), and a myotome. The myotome splits into an epaxial division, giving rise to the deep muscles of the back, and a hypaxial division, forming the muscles of the limbs and body wall. Muscle precursor cells from each somite migrate while retaining the innervation of their segment of origin, which is why adult muscles keep a segmental nerve supply even when they move far from their embryonic position.

Clinical significance

Clinicians test representative movements for each spinal level. Commonly assessed myotomes include:

  • C5 — shoulder abduction and elbow flexion (deltoid, biceps)
  • C6 — wrist extension
  • C7 — elbow extension (triceps)
  • C8 — finger flexion
  • L2–L3 — hip flexion
  • L3–L4 — knee extension (quadriceps)
  • L5 — great toe extension and hip abduction
  • S1 — ankle plantar flexion (calf muscles)

A herniated intervertebral disc compressing a single nerve root typically produces weakness in the corresponding myotome, together with sensory loss in the matching dermatome and a diminished reflex. By contrast, diseases of muscle itself (myopathies) tend to cause symmetrical, proximal weakness that does not respect myotomal boundaries.
