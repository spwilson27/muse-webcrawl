Undular bore

An undular bore is a wave phenomenon in which a moving front of disturbed fluid is followed by a train of smooth, oscillating waves called whelps, rather than by the turbulent, breaking front of a typical bore. Undular bores occur when the change in depth across the front is relatively small, so that the flow remains smooth and the oscillations disperse behind the leading wave.

Occurrence

In rivers and estuaries, weak tidal bores — such as those on the River Severn in England or the Qiantang River in China at moderate tides — take undular form. In the atmosphere, undular bores form on stable layers near the surface, often generated by thunderstorms or cold fronts; the morning glory cloud of Australia's Gulf of Carpentaria is a famous example, appearing as a long roll cloud at the bore's leading edge, sometimes used by glider pilots for soaring.

Physics

Undular bores are described approximately by the Korteweg–de Vries equation, in which nonlinearity steepening the front is balanced by dispersion, so that the front resolves into a rank-ordered train of solitary waves (solitons) with the largest wave first. They contrast with turbulent bores, which occur when the Froude number of the incoming flow exceeds roughly 1.3–1.5.
