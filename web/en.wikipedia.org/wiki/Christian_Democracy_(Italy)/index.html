Christian Democracy (Italy)


Placeholder image: the Christian Democracy shield and cross emblem

Christian Democracy (Italian: Democrazia Cristiana, DC) was a Christian democratic political party in Italy, founded in 1943 and dissolved in 1994. It was the dominant party of Italian politics for most of the postwar period: it participated in every government from 1944 to 1994 and supplied nearly all of Italy's prime ministers during those decades, including Alcide De Gasperi, Aldo Moro, Amintore Fanfani, Giulio Andreotti and Romano Prodi (as an independent associated with the DC's successor politics). Its symbol was a shield bearing a crusader cross and the motto Libertas.

History

The DC was founded clandestinely in 1942–43 by former members of Luigi Sturzo's Italian People's Party (1919–1926) together with younger Catholic activists of FUCI and Catholic Action. De Gasperi, its first leader, became prime minister in December 1945 and led Italy through the founding of the Republic, the 1946 referendum, the 1948 constitution, and the pivotal 1948 general election, in which the DC won an absolute majority of seats with strong American support against the communist-socialist Popular Front.

For four decades the DC governed in coalition with smaller centrist parties, excluding the Italian Communist Party (PCI) from national power during the Cold War. In the 1960s Aldo Moro engineered the "opening to the left", bringing the Socialists into government; in the late 1970s the DC governed with PCI parliamentary support during the "historic compromise" period, ended by Moro's kidnapping and murder by the Red Brigades in 1978.

Dissolution

The party collapsed in 1992–94 under the weight of the Tangentopoli corruption investigations (Mani pulite) and the end of the Cold War, which removed its anti-communist rationale. In January 1994, under secretary Mino Martinazzoli, the DC was dissolved and refounded as the Italian People's Party (PPI); its factions scattered across the new centre-right and centre-left coalitions of the "Second Republic".

Ideology

The DC was a catch-all party of the Catholic centre, combining social-market economics, Atlanticism and European integration with a dense network of influence over state institutions, public enterprises and Catholic associations — a system critics dubbed partitocrazia (partyocracy).
