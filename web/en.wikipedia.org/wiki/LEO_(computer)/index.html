LEO (computer)

LEO (Lyons Electronic Office) was a series of computers built between 1951 and the mid-1960s by J. Lyons and Co., the British catering and food-manufacturing company famous for its tea shops. LEO I, which ran its first regular business application — a bakery valuations job — in November 1951, is recognized as the first computer used for commercial business data processing in the world.

Origins

Lyons was a company unusually devoted to clerical efficiency and scientific management. After a 1947 study trip to the United States, where its managers saw early computers such as ENIAC, Lyons decided to build its own machine, drawing on the design of Cambridge University's EDSAC, whose team under Maurice Wilkes it partly funded. Cambridge mathematician John Pinkerton led the engineering. LEO I used some 6,000 thermionic valves, mercury delay-line memory, and paper-tape input and output, occupying a large room in Lyons's Cadby Hall headquarters in Hammersmith, London.

Business use and successor machines

After valuing bakery output, LEO took over payroll — computing the wages of Lyons's thousands of employees — teashop ordering, stock control and other clerical work. The success led Lyons to form LEO Computers Ltd in 1954 to sell machines externally; LEO II models went to customers including Ford UK, Kodak and the Ministry of Pensions. The transistorized LEO III, first installed in 1962, supported time-sharing-like multiprogramming and a high-level language (CLEO). LEO Computers merged into English Electric LEO and then Marconi, and ultimately into ICL in 1968, by which time the American giants dominated the market.

Legacy

LEO's significance lies in demonstrating that computers were practical business tools, not merely scientific instruments, and in pioneering business programming, systems analysis and even the idea of a corporate computing service — Lyons ran jobs for outside firms as a bureau. The LEO Computers Society and the Centre for Computing History preserve its machines' documentation, recordings and oral histories.
