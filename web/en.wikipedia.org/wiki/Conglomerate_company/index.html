Type: Multi-industry corporation

Conglomerate (company)

A conglomerate is a multi-industry company — that is, a combination of multiple business entities operating in entirely different industries under one corporate group, usually involving a parent company and many subsidiaries. Conglomerates are often large and multinational.

History

The conglomerate form became popular in the United States during the 1960s, when companies such as ITT, Textron, and Ling-Temco-Vought grew rapidly by acquiring unrelated businesses, partly aided by antitrust rules that limited mergers within the same industry. Many conglomerates later underperformed and were broken up in the 1980s, as investors applied a "conglomerate discount" to their shares.

Examples

Well-known conglomerates include Berkshire Hathaway, which owns businesses ranging from insurance to railroads and confectionery; General Electric in its classic form; Tata Group and Reliance Industries in India; and the Japanese keiretsu and Korean chaebol groups such as Samsung.

Advantages and disadvantages

Diversification can reduce overall business risk and allow internal allocation of capital. Critics argue that conglomerates are harder for investors to value, that cross-subsidization can prop up weak divisions, and that focused companies generally perform better.
