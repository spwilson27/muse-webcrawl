Case grammar

Case grammar is a theory of grammatical analysis developed by the American linguist Charles J. Fillmore in the late 1960s, most famously in his 1968 paper "The Case for Case". It proposes that the syntactic structure of a sentence is determined by a set of universal semantic roles, or "deep cases", that noun phrases bear to the verb.

Deep cases

Fillmore's original inventory included the Agentive (the animate instigator of the action), Instrumental (an inanimate force or object involved), Dative (the animate being affected), Factitive (the thing created by the action), Locative (the place or spatial orientation), and Objective (the semantically most neutral case, representing what moves or changes). Later versions revised the inventory, and the roles came to be known as thematic or semantic roles.

Influence

Case grammar grew out of dissatisfaction with the treatment of subjects and objects in generative grammar: verbs like "open" appear with different subjects ("John opened the door", "The key opened the door", "The door opened") yet the underlying relations remain constant. The theory strongly influenced frame semantics, Lexical Functional Grammar, and computational linguistics, notably through case frames used in early natural-language understanding systems and in knowledge representation.
