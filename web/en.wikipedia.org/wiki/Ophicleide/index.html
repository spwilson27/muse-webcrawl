Ophicleide

Ophicleide
Classification: Aerophone, keyed brass
Invented: 1821, Paris
Inventor: Jean Hilaire Asté (Halary)
Related: Serpent, tuba, saxophone family (by keywork analogy)

The ophicleide is a family of conical-bore keyed brass instruments invented in early 19th-century France. The name comes from the Greek ophis ("serpent") and kleis ("key"), meaning "keyed serpent": it was developed as an improved, keyed successor to the wooden serpent, which it resembled in serving as the bass voice of wind ensembles.

Construction

The instrument consists of a long tube of brass bent into a U shape, with a cup-shaped mouthpiece similar to that of a modern trombone or tuba. Rather than finger holes covered directly, it uses a system of large padded keys operated by levers — typically nine to eleven keys — adapting the keywork technology newly developed for woodwinds.

History and use

Patented in 1821 by the Parisian maker Jean Hilaire Asté, known as Halary, the ophicleide became a standard bass instrument in French and British orchestras and military bands for much of the nineteenth century. Berlioz scored for it prominently — most famously in the Symphonie fantastique (1830), where ophicleides intone the Dies irae — and Mendelssohn and Wagner also wrote for it.

From the later nineteenth century it was progressively displaced by the tuba, whose valves gave a more even and reliable sound. The ophicleide survives today mainly in historically informed performances, where its distinctive, reedy bass timbre restores the sonority composers originally intended.
