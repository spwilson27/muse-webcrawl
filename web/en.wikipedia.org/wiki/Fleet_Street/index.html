Fleet Street is a street in Central London, England. It runs west to east from Temple Bar at the boundary with the City of Westminster to Ludgate Circus at the site of the London Wall, and takes its name from the River Fleet, London's largest subterranean river.

The press

Fleet Street became synonymous with the British national press. Printing began in the street around 1500, when Wynkyn de Worde set up a press near St Bride's Church, still known as the journalists' church. By the 18th century daily newspapers were published there, and in the 20th century nearly all national titles had offices on or near the street, including The Times, the Daily Telegraph, the Daily Express and News of the World. The phrase "Fleet Street" remains a metonym for the British press even though the newspapers departed.

Decline of the press era

In the 1980s Rupert Murdoch's News International moved production to Wapping, breaking the print unions' power in the controversial "Wapping dispute" of 1986. Other publishers followed, relocating to Docklands and elsewhere for cheaper, computerised premises. Reuters, the last major news organisation on the street, left in 2005. The street is now dominated by law firms and investment banks, reflecting its position between the City and the Royal Courts of Justice.

Landmarks

Landmarks include St Bride's Church, with its tiered "wedding cake" spire; the art deco former Daily Express Building; Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese, a pub rebuilt in 1667 and frequented by Dr Johnson; and the demon barber Sweeney Todd's legendary address in folklore.
