Thief-taker

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Thief-takers operated in the criminal world of 17th- and 18th-century England

In 17th- and 18th-century England, a thief-taker was a private individual hired to capture criminals and recover stolen property. Before the creation of professional police forces, law enforcement depended heavily on victims prosecuting offenders themselves; thief-takers acted for hire, receiving rewards ("blood money") offered by statute or by victims for the conviction of felons.

Corruption

The reward system invited abuse. Thief-takers might negotiate with thieves, returning stolen goods for a fee and no questions asked, or act as fences and informers. The most notorious, Jonathan Wild, styled himself "Thief-Taker General of Great Britain and Ireland" while secretly running a vast criminal network; he was hanged in 1725. Others, such as the thief-takers attached to the Bow Street magistrates' office, formed the nucleus of the Bow Street Runners, often considered the first professional police force in London.

Decline

Parliamentary reforms and the creation of the Metropolitan Police in 1829 gradually removed the need for private thief-takers, and the reward-based system was dismantled during the nineteenth century.
