Operational art

In the field of military theory, the operational art (also called operational warfare or operational level of war) represents the intermediate level of command that connects strategic aims with the tactical employment of forces. It involves the planning and conduct of campaigns and major operations to accomplish strategic and operational objectives within a theatre of war.

History

The concept was developed in the 1920s and 1930s primarily by theorists of the Soviet Red Army, including Alexander Svechin, Mikhail Tukhachevsky, Vladimir Triandafillov and Georgii Isserson. They argued that modern war, conducted by mass armies across enormous fronts, could not be won in a single battle; instead, a series of successive operations, conducted in depth, was required to destroy the enemy's forces and will to resist. German practice in the Second World War, and later NATO and US doctrine — including AirLand Battle and the formal adoption of the "operational level of war" in the 1980s — drew on similar ideas.

Characteristics

Operational art considers factors such as the arrangement of major operations in time, space and purpose; the sequencing of battles and engagements; logistics and sustainment across a theatre; and the identification of the enemy's centre of gravity and culminating point. Its characteristic formation is the army group or front, and its characteristic product is the campaign plan.
