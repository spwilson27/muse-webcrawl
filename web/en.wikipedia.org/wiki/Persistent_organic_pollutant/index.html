Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) are organic compounds that are resistant to degradation through chemical, biological, and photolytic processes. Because of their persistence, POPs bioaccumulate with potential adverse impacts on human health and the environment. They are transported by wind and water over long distances — the "grasshopper effect" of repeated evaporation and deposition carries them toward polar regions — so they are found even in the Arctic, far from any source.

The Stockholm Convention

The Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants, adopted in 2001 and in force from 2004, commits parties to eliminate or restrict listed POPs. The initial twelve substances, nicknamed the "dirty dozen", included the pesticides aldrin, chlordane, DDT, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, hexachlorobenzene, mirex, and toxaphene; the industrial chemicals PCBs; and the unintentional by-products dioxins and furans. The list has since expanded to include substances such as PFOS, endosulfan, lindane and, more recently, PFOA and other PFAS-related compounds.

Properties and effects

Most POPs are halogenated, typically chlorinated, compounds of low water solubility and high lipid solubility, which causes them to accumulate in fatty tissue and biomagnify up food chains. Exposure is associated with cancers, endocrine disruption, reproductive and developmental disorders, and immune effects. DDT, though banned in many countries, remains permitted under the Convention for disease-vector control against malaria.

Sources

POPs originate from pesticides, industrial processes (including production of PVC and chlorinated solvents), and unintentional formation in combustion and waste incineration. Legacy contamination persists in soils, sediments and stockpiles long after production ceases.
