Reflecting telescope

Reflecting telescope (reflector)
Objective: Curved mirror
First built: Isaac Newton, 1668
Key designs: Newtonian, Cassegrain, Ritchey–Chrétien, Dobsonian

A reflecting telescope (also called a reflector) is a telescope that uses a single curved mirror or a combination of mirrors to form an image. Isaac Newton built the first practical reflecting telescope in 1668 to avoid the chromatic aberration that plagued refractors of his day — mirrors reflect all colours to the same focus, so a reflector is free of that defect.

Designs

Newtonian

A paraboloidal primary mirror at the base of the tube reflects light back up to a small flat diagonal mirror, which diverts the beam to an eyepiece at the side of the tube near the top. Mounted on a simple altazimuth base, this is the Dobsonian, the most popular amateur design.

Cassegrain family

A convex secondary mirror reflects the converging beam back through a hole in the primary to a focus behind it, folding a long focal length into a short tube. The classical Cassegrain, the Ritchey–Chrétien (used by the Hubble Space Telescope and most professional instruments), and catadioptric Schmidt–Cassegrain and Maksutov variants all follow this pattern.

Advantages and limitations

Mirrors can be made very large and supported from behind, so nearly all large research telescopes — from the 5 m Hale to the segmented 10 m Keck telescopes and the James Webb Space Telescope — are reflectors. Drawbacks include diffraction from the secondary mirror's supports, coma in fast Newtonian systems, and the need for periodic realuminising and collimation.
