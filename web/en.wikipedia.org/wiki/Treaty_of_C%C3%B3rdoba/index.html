Treaty of Córdoba

The Treaty of Córdoba was signed on 24 August 1821 in the city of Córdoba, Veracruz, by Agustín de Iturbide, commander of the Army of the Three Guarantees, and Juan O'Donojú, the Spanish chief political officer (jefe político superior) of New Spain. The treaty formally established Mexican independence from Spain and ratified the principles of the Plan of Iguala.

Background

By 1821 the Mexican War of Independence, begun in 1810 by Miguel Hidalgo, had reached its decisive phase. Iturbide's Plan of Iguala united royalists and insurgents behind independence, religion, and union, and the Army of the Three Guarantees swept toward Mexico City largely unopposed.

Terms

The treaty recognized Mexico as an independent empire. It provided that the Mexican crown would be offered first to Ferdinand VII of Spain or another member of the Spanish royal family; if none accepted, Mexico could choose its own monarch. Spanish troops were to withdraw from the capital, and the property and persons of Spaniards in Mexico were guaranteed protection.

Aftermath

O'Donojú, who lacked clear authority from Madrid to recognize independence, helped arrange the peaceful entry of Iturbide's army into Mexico City on 27 September 1821. The Spanish Cortes later repudiated the treaty, but Spain's effective rule in mainland Mexico had ended. Mexico formally declared independence on 28 September 1821, and Iturbide was crowned Emperor Agustín I in 1822.
