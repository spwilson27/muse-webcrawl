Circuit breaker

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A circuit breaker is an electrical safety device designed to protect an electrical circuit from damage caused by overcurrent — overload or short circuit. Its basic function is to interrupt current flow to protect equipment and prevent fire. Unlike a fuse, which operates once and must be replaced, a circuit breaker can be reset (manually or automatically) to resume normal operation.

Operation

Breakers detect faults by several mechanisms: a bimetallic strip that bends when heated by sustained overload (thermal trip), an electromagnet that acts almost instantly on short-circuit current (magnetic trip), or electronic sensing in larger units. When tripped, the mechanism forcibly opens the contacts — often with an arc-quenching chamber, air blast, vacuum or SF₆ insulation at high voltages — so that the contacts cannot be held closed against a fault.

Types

Miniature circuit breakers (MCBs) protect final circuits in homes and light commercial premises, typically up to 125 A, with standardised B, C and D trip curves. Moulded-case circuit breakers (MCCBs) serve larger distribution loads, while air and vacuum circuit breakers protect medium- and high-voltage networks. Related protective devices include residual-current devices (RCD/GFCI), which trip on earth-leakage current to protect people, and surge protectors against transient overvoltages.

History

An early form was described by Edison in an 1879 patent application, and the modern thermal-magnetic miniature breaker was commercialised in the 1920s, gradually displacing fuses in new installations.
