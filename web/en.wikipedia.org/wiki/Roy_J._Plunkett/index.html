Roy Joseph Plunkett (June 26, 1910 – May 12, 1994) was an American chemist who accidentally discovered polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), the polymer later marketed by DuPont as Teflon, in 1938.

Discovery of Teflon

Working at DuPont's Jackson Laboratory in Deepwater, New Jersey, on new chlorofluorocarbon refrigerants, Plunkett prepared tetrafluoroethylene (TFE) gas and stored it in small cylinders on dry ice. On April 6, 1938, a cylinder that still weighed as if full produced no gas; Plunkett and his assistant Jack Rebok sawed it open and found a white, waxy powder — the TFE had polymerised into PTFE. The new material proved extraordinarily inert, heat-resistant and slippery, with the lowest coefficient of friction of any known solid.

Career and legacy

Plunkett became director of research for DuPont's tetraethyl lead and Freon operations and retired in 1975. PTFE found early use in the Manhattan Project (valve seals resistant to uranium hexafluoride) and post-war fame as a non-stick coating, as well as in aerospace, electrical insulation and medical devices. Plunkett was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 1985. His discovery is often cited as a classic case of serendipity in industrial research.
