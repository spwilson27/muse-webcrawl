Audio crossover

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An audio crossover is a class of electronic filter circuit that splits an audio signal into two or more frequency ranges, so that the signals can be routed to loudspeaker drivers designed to operate within those ranges. A typical two-way speaker uses a crossover to send low frequencies to a woofer and high frequencies to a tweeter; three- and four-way systems add midrange and subwoofer bands.

Passive and active crossovers

Passive crossovers, built from capacitors, inductors, and resistors, sit between a single power amplifier and the drivers inside the speaker cabinet. They are inexpensive and simple but waste some power, interact with driver impedance, and offer limited adjustability. Active (electronic) crossovers split the signal at line level before amplification, giving each band its own amplifier ("bi-amping" and "tri-amping"); they allow precise control of crossover frequency, slope, and level, and are common in studio monitors and sound reinforcement. Digital crossovers implemented in DSP offer the greatest flexibility, including time alignment and equalization.

Filter characteristics

Crossover filters are described by their order (slope): first-order filters roll off at 6 dB per octave, second-order at 12 dB/octave, and so on. Classic alignments include Butterworth, Linkwitz–Riley, and Bessel responses, which trade steepness against phase behavior and flatness of the summed response. The crossover frequency and slopes are chosen around the drivers' usable ranges, and polarity, level matching, and acoustic offsets must be handled so the outputs sum coherently.

Applications

Besides multi-way loudspeakers, crossovers are used to feed subwoofers in home theater bass management, in car audio, and in large PA systems where separate amplifier racks drive low, mid, and high-frequency speaker arrays.
