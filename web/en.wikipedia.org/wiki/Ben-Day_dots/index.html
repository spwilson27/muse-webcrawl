Ben-Day dots

Ben-Day dots are a printing technique, named after illustrator and printer Benjamin Henry Day Jr., in which areas of colour or shading are created from patterns of small, evenly spaced dots. Developed in 1879, the process allowed inexpensive reproduction of tonal images in letterpress printing: dots of a single colour in regular patterns, varying in size or overlap, produce the optical impression of additional colours and shades when viewed at a distance.

Technique

In the original process, a transparent overlay sheet pre-printed with a dot pattern was cut and applied to artwork, then photographed to produce the printing plate. By combining dot patterns in the process colours (cyan, magenta, yellow and black) at different screen angles, printers could generate a wide gamut of hues at very low cost. The method is a relative of halftone screening, but uses dots of deliberately visible, uniform spacing — the hallmark of cheap colour printing in pulp magazines, newspaper comics and mid-20th-century comic books, where four-colour dots on newsprint gave the era's comics their distinctive look.

In popular culture and art

The pop artist Roy Lichtenstein made Ben-Day dots his signature device, enlarging the dot patterns of comic strips and advertisements into monumental paintings such as Whaam! (1963) and Drowning Girl (1963), thereby turning a commercial printing texture into a symbol of Pop art. The dots have since become shorthand for vintage comics in graphic design, and appear prominently in the visual style of the animated film Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse (2018).
