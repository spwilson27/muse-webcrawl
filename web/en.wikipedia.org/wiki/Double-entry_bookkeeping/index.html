Double-entry bookkeeping

FieldAccounting
Key principleEvery entry has an equal and opposite entry; total debits = total credits
Early codifierLuca Pacioli, Summa de arithmetica (1494)
Core equationAssets = Liabilities + Equity

Double-entry bookkeeping is the standard system of accounting in which every financial transaction is recorded in at least two accounts — as a debit in one and an equal credit in another — so that the books always balance and errors are revealed by disagreement. It underlies virtually all modern accounting and financial reporting.

Principles

Accounts are classified as assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses. By convention, increases in assets and expenses are debits, while increases in liabilities, equity and income are credits. The accounting equation, Assets = Liabilities + Equity, holds after every entry, and a trial balance — the sum of all debit balances against all credit balances — checks arithmetic accuracy. A sale on credit, for example, debits accounts receivable and credits revenue; the later payment debits cash and credits receivables.

History

The method developed among merchants of medieval Italy: examples appear in Florentine and Genoese records from the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, possibly influenced by earlier practices in the Islamic world, India and Korea. It was first described in print by the Franciscan friar Luca Pacioli in his Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita (1494), which codified the "Venetian method" of journals, ledgers and closing entries — Pacioli is accordingly remembered as the "father of accounting", though he presented the system as existing practice rather than his own invention. The method spread through Europe with translated manuals and became universal with the rise of joint-stock companies, whose investors and auditors required systematic, checkable accounts.

Significance

By producing a coherent picture of capital, profit and obligations, double entry made possible the balance sheet and income statement, credit assessment, and much of the administrative machinery of capitalism — a connection famously (if debatably) argued by Werner Sombart and anticipated by Goethe, who called it "among the finest inventions of the human mind". Modern accounting software preserves the debit-credit structure beneath user-friendly interfaces.
