Ulfberht swords

Ulfberht swords are a group of about 170 medieval swords found in Northern Europe, dated to the 9th to 11th centuries, having blades inlaid with the inscription +VLFBERH+T or variants of it. The word is a Frankish personal name, presumably that of a smith or workshop, and became a kind of trademark for high-quality blades. The swords coincide with the Viking Age and were prized across Scandinavia, the Baltic and the Frankish world.

Steel and quality

Metallurgical studies have shown that many genuine Ulfberht blades were made of unusually pure crucible steel with high carbon content (around 1.2%), giving them exceptional strength and flexibility for the period — centuries before comparable steel was routinely produced in Europe. Where the crucible steel originated is debated; hypotheses include Central Asian or Middle Eastern ingots traded north via the Volga route. Later or inferior copies, identified by garbled inscriptions (such as +VLFBERHT+ with different letter placement or spelling) and lower-quality metal, appear to be contemporary imitations of the prestigious brand.

Distribution and finds

Finds cluster in Norway, Finland, the Baltic states, Germany and Russia, frequently from rivers and graves. The concentration in Scandinavia, rather than Francia where the blades were likely forged, suggests export along trade routes. Research popularised by a 2012 documentary and subsequent scholarship frames Ulfberht as an early example of brand counterfeiting: buyers paid premiums for the name, and imitators supplied the demand.
