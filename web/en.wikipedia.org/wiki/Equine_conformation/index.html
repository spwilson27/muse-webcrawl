Equine conformation

Equine conformation evaluates a horse's bone structure, musculature, and the proportions of its body parts in relation to each other. It is a key factor in judging a horse's likely athletic ability, soundness, and suitability for a particular discipline. No horse has perfect conformation; the ideal varies with breed and intended use.

Evaluation

Conformation is typically assessed with the horse standing squarely on level ground, viewed from the front, side, and rear, and then at the walk and trot to evaluate movement. Judges consider balance (often described by dividing the horse into thirds), structural correctness of the legs, the shape and set of the neck, the slope of the shoulder and pasterns, and the muscling of the hindquarters.

Common faults

Frequently noted conformation faults include sickle hocks, cow hocks, base-narrow or base-wide stances, toeing in ("pigeon-toed") or toeing out ("splay-footed"), upright ("straight") pasterns, a long back, an ewe neck, and club feet. Some faults merely affect appearance, while others predispose the horse to lameness or limit performance.

Relation to performance

Different disciplines reward different conformations: a racehorse benefits from a long, sloping shoulder and powerful hindquarters, while a draft horse is heavier-boned with shorter, more upright pasterns suited to pulling. Breed standards codify the desired conformation for registered animals.
