Islamic calligraphy

Islamic calligraphy is the artistic practice of handwriting and calligraphy based on the Arabic alphabet, developed in the Islamic world. It is known in Arabic as khatt Arabi ("Arabic design"). Because Islamic tradition generally avoids depictions of sentient beings in religious contexts, calligraphy became a principal form of artistic expression in Islamic culture, used to beautify the written word — particularly verses from the Qur'an.

Development

The earliest Arabic script used for Qur'ans was the angular Kufic script. The 10th-century vizier Ibn Muqla is credited with systematizing cursive scripts by a geometric system of dots and circles. His successor Ibn al-Bawwab refined the cursive hands, and Yaqut al-Musta'simi later perfected them by cutting the reed pen (qalam) obliquely.

Principal styles

  • Kufic — angular, monumental script of early Qur'ans and inscriptions.
  • Naskh — a clear cursive hand that became the standard for copying books and the basis of modern Arabic type.
  • Thuluth — a large, elegant script with tall verticals, used for mosque decoration and headings.
  • Nastaʿlīq — a flowing script developed in Persia, dominant in Persian, Urdu, and Ottoman Turkish poetry.
  • Diwani — an ornate Ottoman chancery script.
  • Maghrebi — scripts of North and West Africa.
  • Bihari, Muhaqqaq, Tawqi — other historical cursive hands.

Instruments and masters

Calligraphers write with a reed pen (qalam), soot-based ink, and specially prepared paper. The Ottoman Empire made calligraphy (hat sanatı) a court art; masters such as Şeyh Hamdullah and Hâfız Osman set standards copied for centuries. Calligraphy adorns mosques (for example the inscriptions of the Dome of the Rock and Ottoman imperial mosques), coins, tiles, textiles, and manuscripts, and calligrams shape words into images of animals or objects.
