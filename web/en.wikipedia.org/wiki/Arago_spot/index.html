Arago spot

The Arago spot, also called the Poisson spot or Fresnel spot, is a bright point that appears at the centre of a circular object's shadow when it is illuminated by a point source of light. It arises from Fresnel diffraction: waves diffracted around the rim of the obstacle all travel equal distances to the centre of the shadow and therefore interfere constructively there. The spot plays a counterintuitive role in the history of optics, because it was predicted as an apparent reductio ad absurdum of the wave theory and then observed.

History

In 1818, during the French Academy of Sciences prize competition on diffraction, Siméon Denis Poisson argued against Fresnel's wave-theoretic memoir by pointing out that it absurdly implied a bright spot in the middle of a round shadow. François Arago, head of the judging committee, performed the experiment and found the spot exactly as Fresnel's theory predicted — a dramatic confirmation of the wave nature of light. (Jacques Maraldi had in fact observed the effect as early as 1723, unnoticed.)

Conditions

The spot requires a sufficiently small, coherent (or point-like) source, a smooth circular obstacle, and appropriate geometry so that the shadow falls in the Fresnel near-field regime; everyday shadows from extended sources do not show it.
