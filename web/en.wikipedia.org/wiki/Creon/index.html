Creon

Image: Creon in a modern stage production of Antigone (placeholder)

Creon (Greek: Κρέων, "ruler") is a figure of Greek mythology best known as the king of Thebes in the tragedies of Sophocles. He is the brother of Jocasta and thus the uncle — and, after their deaths, successor — of the doomed house of Oedipus. His name is borne by several mythological figures, including a king of Corinth who appears in the story of Medea, but the Theban Creon is by far the most famous.

In the Theban cycle

Creon serves as regent or adviser under Laius, Oedipus and Oedipus's sons. In Sophocles' Oedipus Rex he is accused by Oedipus of conspiring for the throne, protesting that he has power without its burdens. In Oedipus at Colonus he attempts to force the exiled Oedipus back to Thebes.

Antigone

His defining role is in Antigone. After Eteocles and Polynices kill each other in the war of the Seven against Thebes, Creon takes the throne and decrees that Eteocles be buried with honour while Polynices, as a traitor, be left unburied on pain of death. Antigone defies the edict and buries her brother; Creon, inflexible in defence of civic order and his own authority, condemns her to be entombed alive. Warned too late by the blind prophet Tiresias that the gods reject his decree, he relents, but Antigone has already hanged herself; her death triggers the suicides of Creon's son Haemon, her betrothed, and of Creon's wife Eurydice, leaving Creon utterly broken. The play is traditionally read as a conflict between divine/familial law and the law of the state, and between conscience and tyranny.

Other Creons

Creon of Corinth, in Euripides' Medea, arranges the marriage of his daughter Glauce (Creusa) to Jason and is killed, with her, by Medea's poisoned gifts. Another Creon, son of Heracles and Megara's father in some traditions, belongs to the Theban Heracles cycle.
