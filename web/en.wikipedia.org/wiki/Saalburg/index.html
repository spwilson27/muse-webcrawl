The Saalburg is a Roman fort located on the crest of the Taunus ridge northwest of Bad Homburg in Hesse, Germany. It formed part of the Upper Germanic Limes, the frontier line of the Roman Empire, and is the most completely reconstructed Roman fort in Germany, functioning today as an open-air museum and research institute.

History

A first timber fort was established around 90 CE to guard a pass over the Taunus on the border with the Germanic Chatti. It was rebuilt in stone around 135 CE and enlarged to house a part-mounted cohort of roughly 600 men, the Cohors II Raetorum equitata, together with a civilian settlement (vicus) of traders, craftsmen and families outside the walls. The fort was evacuated around 260 CE as the Romans abandoned the territory east of the Rhine in the face of Alamannic pressure.

Reconstruction

Excavations began in the mid-19th century under Louis Jacobi and his son. Between 1897 and 1907, at the instigation of Kaiser Wilhelm II, the fort was rebuilt on its original foundations to a degree unusual for the period — walls, gates, headquarters building (principia) with its standards shrine, granary and commander's house were reconstructed, making the Saalburg a landmark of early experimental archaeology and heritage presentation. Its museum displays finds from the Limes, and in 2005 the site was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List as part of the Frontiers of the Roman Empire.
