Thomas J. Sargent, Nobel laureate in economics.

Thomas John Sargent (born 19 July 1943) is an American economist and the W. R. Berkley Professor of Economics and Business at New York University. He is one of the leaders of the "rational expectations revolution" in macroeconomics and a founder of modern dynamic macroeconomic modelling. In 2011 he shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Christopher A. Sims "for their empirical research on cause and effect in the macroeconomy".

Education and career

Sargent earned his B.A. from the University of California, Berkeley in 1964 — where he was named the university's most distinguished scholar — and his PhD from Harvard University in 1968. After service in the U.S. Army, he taught at the University of Pennsylvania, the University of Minnesota (1975–1987), the University of Chicago, and Stanford University, where he was a senior fellow at the Hoover Institution. He joined NYU in 2002. He has been a fellow of the Econometric Society (its president in 2005) and president of the American Economic Association (2007).

Contributions

Working especially with Neil Wallace, Sargent developed models in which expectations are "rational" in the sense of John Muth and Robert Lucas Jr. The Sargent–Wallace policy-ineffectiveness proposition (1975) argued that systematic monetary policy cannot stabilise output when expectations adjust rationally. His celebrated study "The Ends of Four Big Inflations" (1982) showed that the abrupt, credible ends of the 1920s hyperinflations supported the view that inflation depends on expectations about fiscal and monetary regimes — a theme extended in The Conquest of American Inflation (1999) and, with François Velde, The Big Problem of Small Change (2002).

With Lars Peter Hansen, Sargent developed methods for estimating and testing dynamic models under rational expectations and, later, "robustness" methods for decision-making under model uncertainty (Robustness, 2008). His textbooks — Dynamic Macroeconomic Theory (1987), and with Lars Ljungqvist Recursive Macroeconomic Theory — have trained generations of macroeconomists in recursive methods. His econometric work on vector autoregressions and structural interpretation paralleled that of his Nobel co-laureate Sims.
