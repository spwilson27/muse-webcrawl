Taos Pueblo

Taos Pueblo (Northern Tiwa: Tə̂otho or Tə̂obo) is an ancient pueblo belonging to a Tiwa-speaking Native American tribe of Puebloan people. It lies about 1 mile (1.6 km) north of the modern city of Taos, New Mexico, and is renowned for its multi-storey adobe buildings, continuously inhabited for roughly a thousand years.

The buildings

The main structures — Hlauuma (north house) and Hlaukwima (south house) — are terraced adobe complexes of many individual homes, built of earth, straw and timber, traditionally accessed by ladders through roof hatchways. The Rio Pueblo de Taos flows between the two clusters. The San Geronimo chapel, rebuilt in the 19th century after the original mission church was destroyed in the Mexican–American War, stands in the plaza.

History

Taos Pueblo was a centre of trade between Plains and Pueblo peoples for centuries before Spanish colonisation. Its people joined the Pueblo Revolt of 1680, led regionally by Popé, which expelled the Spanish for twelve years. In 1970 the United States returned 48,000 acres of ancestral land, including the sacred Blue Lake, to the pueblo — a landmark in Native American land-rights history celebrated after a 60-year campaign.

Recognition

Taos Pueblo is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 1992) and a US National Historic Landmark. Around 150 people live in the historic buildings full-time without running water or electricity, with the wider tribal community of about 4,500 living in the surrounding area.
