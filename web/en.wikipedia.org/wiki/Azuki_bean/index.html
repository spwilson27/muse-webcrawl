Azuki bean

Azuki bean
Scientific nameVigna angularis
FamilyFabaceae (legume)
OriginEast Asia
Other namesAdzuki bean, red mung bean
Main producersChina, Japan, Korea, Taiwan

The azuki bean (Vigna angularis), also called adzuki bean or red mung bean, is an annual vine widely grown throughout East Asia for its small red seeds, about 5 mm long. It is one of the most important legume crops in Japan, China and Korea, and is best known internationally in the form of sweet red bean paste used in East and Southeast Asian confectionery.

Cultivation and history

Genetic evidence suggests the azuki bean was domesticated in East Asia several thousand years ago, with wild populations of Vigna angularis var. nipponensis ranging from Japan and Korea through China to the Himalayas. The cultivated plant is a bushy annual with trifoliate leaves and yellow flowers, producing clusters of slender pods each containing 6–10 smooth seeds. The beans are usually a uniform deep red, though cultivars exist in white, black, grey and mottled colours. Major growing regions include Hokkaido in Japan — where the bean is a signature crop — and northeast China.

Culinary uses

In Japanese cuisine, azuki is most famously prepared as sekihan (rice cooked with the beans, served on festive occasions) and as anko, the sweetened paste that fills mochi, dorayaki, taiyaki and many other wagashi sweets. In China the paste fills mooncakes, tangyuan and zongzi, and the whole beans are used in soups and in the porridge eaten at the Laba festival; in Korea, pat is used in patbingsu (shaved ice dessert), porridge (patjuk) and rice cakes. Mashed and sweetened azuki is also eaten in Vietnam and parts of India.

Nutrition

Azuki beans are a good source of protein, complex carbohydrates, dietary fibre, folate, iron, potassium and zinc, and are low in fat. Like other legumes they are used in vegetarian diets as a staple protein source, and in traditional East Asian medicine they have been ascribed diuretic and detoxifying properties.
