Easterlin paradox

The Easterlin paradox is a finding in happiness economics formulated by economist Richard Easterlin in his 1974 paper "Does Economic Growth Improve the Human Lot? Some Empirical Evidence". It states that at a point in time, happiness varies directly with income both among and within countries, but over time, happiness does not trend upward as income continues to grow — at least once a basic level of subsistence has been reached.


Average happiness shows little long-term trend despite rising income

The findings

Easterlin compared survey data on reported happiness across countries at different income levels and over time within the United States. Cross-sectionally, richer people and richer countries reported higher happiness. Longitudinally, however, US happiness showed no clear upward trend between the 1940s and 1970s despite substantial growth in real income per capita. Later versions of the argument drew on Japan's rapid postwar growth and, subsequently, on China's economic boom, in which life satisfaction did not rise commensurately.

Explanations and debate

Proposed explanations include relative income and social comparison (people judge their material circumstances against others), hedonic adaptation (aspirations rise with income), and the distinction between absolute needs and status goods. The paradox has been challenged by economists such as Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers, whose analyses of broader datasets find that life satisfaction rises with the logarithm of income both within and across countries and over time, with no obvious satiation point; Easterlin and collaborators have replied by distinguishing short-run from long-run relationships and questioning some datasets. The debate helped spur the growth of happiness economics and policy interest in measures of well-being beyond GDP.
