The rock-wallabies are the wallabies of the genus Petrogale. They are marsupials found in rocky, rugged terrain across Australia. There are 17 species, including the allied rock-wallaby, the yellow-footed rock-wallaby, and the brush-tailed rock-wallaby. Like other macropods, they are herbivorous.

Description

Rock-wallabies are small to medium-sized wallabies, typically weighing between 1 and 12 kilograms depending on the species. They are supremely adapted to life among rocks: their hind feet have rough, padded soles that provide grip on rock surfaces, and their long, bushy tails act as counterbalances when moving across uneven terrain. Their fur is often thick and may be brightly patterned, particularly in the yellow-footed rock-wallaby.

Behaviour and habitat

Rock-wallabies live in colonies in rocky outcrops, boulder piles, cliffs, and gorges, where they shelter during the day in caves and crevices and emerge at night to feed on grasses, leaves, fruits, and bark. Their complex social structure involves hierarchies maintained within colonies. Many species have suffered severe declines due to predation by introduced red foxes and competition with feral goats, and several are listed as endangered or vulnerable.
