Analytical mechanics

BranchesLagrangian mechanics; Hamiltonian mechanics
Key figuresJoseph-Louis Lagrange; William Rowan Hamilton; Carl Jacobi

Analytical mechanics is a reformulation of classical mechanics that describes the motion of systems using scalar quantities — energy, work and generalised coordinates — rather than the vector forces of Newtonian mechanics. Its two main formulations are Lagrangian mechanics, introduced by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1788, and Hamiltonian mechanics, developed by William Rowan Hamilton in the 1830s.

Lagrangian mechanics

Lagrangian mechanics describes a system by its Lagrangian, the difference between kinetic and potential energy expressed in generalised coordinates. The equations of motion follow from the principle of stationary action, making the approach especially powerful for constrained systems, where constraint forces need not be considered explicitly.

Hamiltonian mechanics

Hamiltonian mechanics uses generalised coordinates and their conjugate momenta, with dynamics governed by the Hamiltonian, typically the total energy. Hamilton's equations are symmetric in coordinates and momenta, and the framework underlies statistical mechanics, perturbation theory and the path toward quantum mechanics through canonical quantisation.

Applications

Analytical methods are used throughout physics and engineering, from orbital dynamics and rigid-body motion to field theory, where the Lagrangian density of a field generates its equations of motion.
