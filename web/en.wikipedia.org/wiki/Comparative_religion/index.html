Comparative religion

Comparative religion is the branch of the study of religions concerned with the comparison of the doctrines and practices of the world's religions. It seeks both to identify patterns shared across traditions — such as myths of creation, rituals of initiation, concepts of an afterlife, and codes of ethics — and to understand each tradition in its own historical and cultural context.

History

Comparisons of religions were made in antiquity — Herodotus compared Greek and Egyptian gods — and by medieval scholars such as the Persian al-Biruni, whose study of Indian religion is regarded as a pioneering work of the field. The modern academic discipline arose in the nineteenth century with the growth of philology and the translation of Asian scriptures into European languages. Max Müller, who edited the fifty-volume Sacred Books of the East, is often called the founder of comparative religion; his dictum was that "he who knows one religion knows none."

Approaches

Scholars distinguish several approaches: the historical approach traces how religions influenced one another; the phenomenological approach, associated with Gerardus van der Leeuw and Mircea Eliade, classifies religious phenomena such as sacrifice, prayer, and sacred space; and sociological and anthropological approaches, following Émile Durkheim and others, study religion's functions in society. A persistent debate concerns whether comparison illuminates or distorts: critics warn against flattening different traditions into a single mold, a concern sometimes called the problem of perennialism.

Classification of religions

Comparative studies commonly group religions into families: the Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and others), the Dharmic or Indian religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism), and the East Asian traditions (Taoism, Confucianism, Shinto), alongside indigenous and folk religions. Such schemes are heuristic and contested, but they organize curricula in most world-religions courses.
