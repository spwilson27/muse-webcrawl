Warp and weft

WarpLengthwise (longitudinal) yarns held stationary in tension on a loom
WeftYarns drawn through the warp by means of a shuttle or other device, forming the crosswise threads
Also calledWarp: ends. Weft: picks, filling
ResultWoven fabric (cloth)

Warp and weft are the two basic components used in weaving to turn thread or yarn into fabric. The lengthwise or longitudinal warp yarns are held stationary in tension on a frame or loom while the transverse weft (sometimes woof) is drawn through and inserted over and under the warp. A single thread of the weft crossing the warp is called a pick. The weft is an essential part of making fabric, and the yarn used as the weft is sometimes more pliable than that used as the warp.

Terminology

The word weft derives from the Old English word wefan, to weave. Warp comes from the Old English wearp, from the verb weorpan, to throw. The individual warp threads are called ends, and the total number of ends in a fabric determines its width and fineness. In North American usage the weft is also called the fill or filling.

Weaving process

The warp threads are wound onto the loom under tension and passed through heddles mounted on shafts (harnesses). Raising and lowering selected shafts separates the warp threads into a V-shaped opening called the shed. The weft is then passed through the shed — historically carried by a shuttle holding a bobbin of weft yarn, and in modern power looms by rapier, projectile, air-jet or water-jet systems — after which a reed beats the pick into place against the already woven cloth. The sequence of interlacing determines the weave structure: the simplest is plain weave, in which the weft passes alternately over and under successive warp threads; other basic structures include twill and satin.

Properties

Because the warp is held under sustained tension during weaving, warp yarns must generally be stronger than weft yarns; they are often given additional twist and may be coated with size to reduce abrasion. Fabric usually stretches less along the warp (lengthwise) direction than across it, which is why garments are conventionally cut with their principal seams following the warp.

History

Warp-and-weft weaving is among the oldest textile technologies, practised since the Neolithic period. Early looms included the warp-weighted loom, on which the warp hung vertically from a beam and was tensioned by clay or stone weights, and ground looms pegged out horizontally. Weaving of cotton, linen, wool and silk developed independently in many cultures, and the basic warp–weft principle remains unchanged in industrial textile production today.
