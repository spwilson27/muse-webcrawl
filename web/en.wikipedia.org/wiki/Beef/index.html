Beef is the culinary name for meat from cattle (Bos taurus). In prehistoric times, humankind hunted wild aurochs and later domesticated cattle. Since that time, numerous breeds have been specially bred for the quality or quantity of their meat. Today, beef is the third most widely consumed meat in the world, after pork and poultry. As of 2018, the United States, Brazil, and China were the largest producers of beef.

History

Cattle were domesticated from the aurochs around 10,500 years ago in the Near East. Beef from steers and heifers is generally higher in quality; meat from bulls is usually tougher, and meat from calves is sold as veal. The Norman Conquest introduced the French word bœuf into English, an example of the linguistic divide in which the animals kept the Anglo-Saxon name (cow) while the meat took the Norman French term (beef).

Cuts and preparation

A carcass is first divided into primal cuts — such as chuck, rib, loin and round in the American system — then into retail cuts including steaks (ribeye, sirloin, T-bone, fillet), roasts, brisket and ground (minced) beef. Beef may be cooked by grilling, roasting, frying, braising or stewing, and preserved by curing, smoking or drying (biltong, jerky). Famous preparations include the hamburger, steak tartare, beef Wellington, and Japanese wagyu beef from breeds such as Kobe and Matsusaka, prized for intense marbling.

Nutrition and health

Beef is a complete protein and a rich source of vitamin B12, iron (in the readily absorbed haem form), zinc and creatine. Health authorities have associated high consumption of red and processed meat with increased risks of colorectal cancer and cardiovascular disease; the IARC classifies processed meat as a Group 1 carcinogen and red meat as Group 2A.

Production and environment

The largest beef-producing countries are the United States, Brazil, China, Argentina, Australia and India (including buffalo meat). Cattle raising has a large environmental footprint: beef production is a leading driver of deforestation in the Amazon and produces substantial greenhouse-gas emissions per kilogram compared with other meats, which has driven interest in feed additives, improved grazing management and meat alternatives.

Religious and cultural aspects

Beef is forbidden in Hinduism, where the cow is revered, and cattle slaughter is banned or restricted in most Indian states; it is avoided by many Jains and some Buddhists. Beef is permitted in Judaism and Islam only when slaughtered according to kosher or halal rules respectively.
