Fiqh

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fiqh is Islamic jurisprudence: the human effort to understand and articulate Sharia — God's law — from its sources, principally the Quran and the Sunna, supplemented by consensus (ijma) and analogical reasoning (qiyas) and, in Shia jurisprudence, reason ('aql). Its product is the detailed law applied by jurists (fuqaha) and muftis: rules of worship, transactions, marriage and divorce, inheritance, crime and procedure, each act classified on a fivefold scale from obligatory (fard/wajib) through recommended and neutral to reprehensible and forbidden (haram).

From the eighth and ninth centuries, schools of law (madhhabs) crystallised around eponymous masters — Abu Hanifa, Malik ibn Anas, al-Shafi'i (whose Risala founded the theory of sources, usul al-fiqh) and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, with the Ja'fari school in Twelver Shi'ism. In the Ottoman Empire and modern states, fiqh was partly codified (the Ottoman Mecelle, 1869–76, an early codification of Hanafi civil law) and partly displaced by European-style codes, surviving most fully in family law across the Muslim world.
