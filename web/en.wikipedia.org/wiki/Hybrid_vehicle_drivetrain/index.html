Hybrid vehicle drivetrain

The drivetrain of a hybrid vehicle transmits power from the engine or motor to the driven wheels, and in a hybrid electric vehicle it combines at least two different power sources, typically an internal combustion engine and one or more electric motors. Hybrid drivetrains are commonly classified by their architecture as parallel, series, or power-split (series-parallel), and by their degree of hybridization, from mild hybrids to full hybrids and plug-in hybrids.

Hybrid drivetrain layouts
ParallelEngine and motor both drive the wheels
SeriesEngine drives a generator; motor alone drives the wheels
Power-splitPlanetary gearset combines both paths

Parallel hybrid

In a parallel hybrid, both the engine and the electric motor are connected to the transmission and can drive the wheels simultaneously or independently. The motor doubles as a generator during braking and cruising to recharge the battery. Honda's Integrated Motor Assist and many 48-volt mild-hybrid systems use this layout.

Series hybrid

In a series hybrid, the combustion engine drives a generator, and an electric motor alone propels the vehicle; the engine has no mechanical connection to the wheels. This arrangement allows the engine to run near its optimal speed but incurs conversion losses. Nissan's e-Power system and range-extended electric vehicles such as the BMW i3 REx work this way.

Power-split hybrid

The power-split, or series-parallel, design uses a planetary gearset to divide engine power between the wheels and a generator, allowing both mechanical and electrical paths. Toyota's Hybrid Synergy Drive, used in the Prius, is the best-known example, and similar e-CVT systems are used by Ford and Hyundai.

Components

Key components include the traction battery, inverter, DC-DC converter, electric machines and a control unit that decides the power split. Regenerative braking recovers kinetic energy that would otherwise be lost as heat.
