Strong acid

Common strong acids: hydrochloric, sulfuric and nitric acid

A strong acid is an acid that dissociates essentially completely in aqueous solution, releasing hydronium ions; its acid dissociation constant Ka is very large (pKa < −1.74, i.e. stronger than the hydronium ion itself). Because dissociation is effectively total, the concentration of hydronium in a solution of a strong acid is approximately equal to the analytical concentration of the acid (for the first proton, in the case of diprotic acids).

Common strong acids

The strong acids most often encountered are the hydrohalic acids hydrochloric acid (HCl), hydrobromic acid (HBr) and hydroiodic acid (HI); the oxoacids nitric acid (HNO3), perchloric acid (HClO4) and sulfuric acid (H2SO4, strong for its first proton); and chloric acid (HClO3). In water, acids stronger than hydronium are levelled to the strength of H3O+ (the levelling effect), so ranking them requires measurements in other solvents or gas-phase data.

Superacids

Acids stronger than 100% sulfuric acid are called superacids; examples include triflic acid, fluorosulfuric acid, and "magic acid", a mixture of fluorosulfuric acid and antimony pentafluoride. Their acidity is expressed with the Hammett acidity function H0 rather than pH.

Properties and hazards

Strength is distinct from concentration and from corrosiveness: hydrofluoric acid, for example, is a weak acid by dissociation but is exceptionally hazardous because fluoride penetrates tissue and sequesters calcium. Strong acids are generally very corrosive, react with most metals (liberating hydrogen with many), and must be handled with appropriate protective equipment.
