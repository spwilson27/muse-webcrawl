Tao Te Ching

The Tao Te Ching (Chinese: 道德經; pinyin: Dàodéjīng) is a Chinese classic text and foundational work of Taoism. Traditionally credited to the sage Laozi, it is usually dated to the late 4th or 3rd century BCE, during the Warring States period.

Content

The text consists of 81 short chapters, divided into two parts: the Tao Ching (chapters 1–37), concerning the Tao (the Way), and the Te Ching (chapters 38–81), concerning Te (virtue or power). Composed in a terse, poetic and often paradoxical style, it expounds concepts such as wu wei (effortless action or non-action), naturalness, simplicity and spontaneity, and counsels rulers to govern with minimal interference. Its famous opening line declares that "the Tao that can be told is not the eternal Tao".

Textual history

The oldest surviving manuscripts are the Guodian Chu Slips, discovered in a tomb in 1993 and dated to around 300 BCE, and the silk manuscripts found at Mawangdui in 1973. The received text was standardised through the commentary of Wang Bi in the 3rd century CE. Together with the Zhuangzi, the Tao Te Ching forms the foundation of philosophical Taoism.

Influence

The Tao Te Ching is among the most translated works in world literature and has influenced Chinese philosophy, religion, calligraphy and martial arts, as well as many Western thinkers and artists.
