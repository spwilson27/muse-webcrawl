Bottom trawling

Bottom trawling is an industrial fishing method in which a large net is dragged along the seafloor behind one or two trawlers. Weights, chains, and heavy "doors" keep the net open and in contact with the bottom, sweeping up demersal fish and shellfish such as cod, haddock, shrimp, and scallops.

Bottom trawling
Type: Fishing method
Target species: Demersal fish, shrimp, scallops
Concerns: Habitat destruction, bycatch

Method

An otter trawl uses two steel doors (otter boards) to spread the net horizontally; beam trawls hold the net open with a rigid beam. Rockhopper gear, fitted with large rubber discs, lets modern trawls work over rough ground that once protected fish refuges.

Environmental impact

Bottom trawling is widely criticised for damaging seabed habitats. The gear crushes and buries corals, sponges, and seagrass, resuspends sediment, and releases carbon stored in the seabed. It also produces high levels of bycatch — unwanted species thrown back, often dead. Studies estimate trawlers sweep an area of seabed comparable to a large fraction of the world's continental shelves each year.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions restrict or ban bottom trawling in sensitive areas; the European Union has banned it below 800 metres in the North-East Atlantic, and marine protected areas increasingly prohibit it. Critics of the practice compare it to clear-cutting a forest to catch the animals in it.
