The Five Barbarians (Chinese: 五胡, Wǔ Hú) is a Chinese historical term for five non-Han peoples who settled in northern China during the Han dynasty and later, during the chaos of the early 4th century, overthrew the Western Jin dynasty and founded competing kingdoms. The five groups are conventionally listed as the Xiongnu, Xianbei, Jie, Qiang, and Di.

The five peoples

  • Xiongnu — a confederation of steppe nomads, long-standing rivals of Han China; their southern branch settled in Shanxi.
  • Xianbei — Mongolic-speaking peoples from the eastern steppe who later founded the Northern Wei dynasty and reunified northern China.
  • Jie — a group possibly of Central Asian origin, based in Hebei; they founded the Later Zhao state.
  • Qiang — peoples of the western highlands (Tibetan-Burman speaking), in modern Gansu/Qinghai.
  • Di — inhabitants of the Qinling and western Sichuan borderlands who founded the Former Qin state.

Background

Successive Chinese dynasties had settled surrendered steppe and western peoples inside the frontier as soldiers, herders, and labourers, often under harsh conditions and official discrimination. After the civil wars of the Eight Princes (291–306) devastated the Jin court, famine, taxation, and corvée demands drove these communities to revolt.

The uprising

In 304 the Xiongnu leader Liu Yuan declared himself King of Han, founding the state of Han-Zhao. His forces sacked Luoyang in 311 (the Disaster of Yongjia), capturing Emperor Huai, and took Chang'an in 316, ending the Western Jin. The Jin court fled south of the Yangtze, founding the Eastern Jin at Jiankang (Nanjing), ushering in centuries of north–south division.

Sixteen Kingdoms

The century after 304 is known as the Sixteen Kingdoms period (304–439): a kaleidoscope of short-lived states founded by the five peoples and by Han Chinese warlords. The Di-led Former Qin briefly unified the north in 376 before its catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Fei River (383). Northern reunification was finally achieved by the Xianbei Northern Wei in 439.

Historiography

The term "Five Barbarians" and the traditional narrative of "disorder caused by the barbarians" (五胡亂華) reflect the viewpoint of Han Chinese historians; modern scholarship stresses instead the long integration of these peoples within China, the structural collapse of Western Jin, and the substantial sinicisation that followed under states such as Northern Wei.
