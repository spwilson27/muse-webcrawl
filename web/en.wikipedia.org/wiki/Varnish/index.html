Varnish is a clear transparent hard protective coating or film. It is not a stain, and usually has a yellowish shade that develops over time due to its primary components. A varnish normally consists of three components: a drying oil, a resin, and a thinner or carrier solvent. It is primarily used as a wood finish where the natural wood grain is meant to be visible; it is applied over wood stains as a final step to achieve a film for gloss and protection.

History

The earliest known varnishes were composed of resin — for example, pine resin — and a solvent, used in ancient Egypt. Varnishes utilising drying oils were developed later; the oil varnishes used on Renaissance and Baroque string instruments, such as those of Amati and Stradivari, have been the subject of centuries of research and speculation.

Types

Oil varnishes combine a drying oil (such as linseed or tung oil) cooked with resin. Spirit varnishes dissolve resin in alcohol — shellac is the most common example. Modern formulations include polyurethane, lacquer, conversion varnish, and catalysed coatings, offering faster drying and greater durability, especially marine spar varnishes formulated with UV absorbers for exterior use.
