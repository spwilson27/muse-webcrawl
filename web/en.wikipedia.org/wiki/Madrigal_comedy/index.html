Madrigal comedy

Madrigal comedy is a term for a cycle of secular madrigals that together tell a comic story, popular in Italy in the late sixteenth century. Often regarded as a precursor of opera, a madrigal comedy strings together a series of madrigals — sometimes interspersed with lighter villanelle — whose texts advance a single humorous plot, usually drawn from the situations of commedia dell'arte.

Examples

The most famous example is Orazio Vecchi's L'Amfiparnaso (1597), subtitled a "commedia harmonica," which dramatizes the loves and quarrels of stock characters such as Pantalone and the innamorati. Alessandro Striggio's Il cicalamento delle donne al bucato (1567), a cycle about women gossiping at the wash, is an earlier example, and Adriano Banchieri composed several madrigal comedies in the early seventeenth century.

Performance

Unlike opera, madrigal comedy was sung throughout by an ensemble of singers rather than acted by costumed soloists, though modern performances are sometimes staged. Vecchi insisted that L'Amfiparnaso was meant to be heard rather than seen, describing it as a spectacle for the ear.
