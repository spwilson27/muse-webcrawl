A semi-trailer truck, also known as a tractor-trailer, semi, or in British usage an articulated lorry, is the combination of a tractor unit and one or more semi-trailers to carry freight. A semi-trailer attaches to the tractor with a type of hitch called a fifth wheel, so that a substantial part of the trailer's weight is borne by the tractor. The result is a vehicle in which both the tractor and trailer have axles close to each end, and which can be separated so the tractor can haul many different trailers.

Configuration

The tractor unit carries the engine, cab and drive axles, together with the fifth-wheel coupling plate on which the trailer's front end rests. The trailer's kingpin engages a locking jaw in the fifth wheel, allowing the combination to pivot in corners. Because the semi-trailer has no front axle, landing gear is lowered to support it when uncoupled. Common trailer types include dry vans, refrigerated ("reefer") trailers, flatbeds, tankers, and container chassis carrying intermodal containers.

History

The semi-trailer concept dates to the 1910s: Alexander Winton is credited with an early design in 1898 for hauling his automobiles, and the Fruehauf corporation commercialised semi-trailers from 1914. The fifth-wheel coupling was refined in the following decades, and the design became dominant for long-distance freight in the mid-twentieth century as paved highway networks, including the US Interstate System, expanded. In Europe, shorter overall-length regulations favoured cab-over-engine ("cab over") tractor designs, whereas North American conventionals kept a long bonnet.

Operation and regulation

Driving a semi-trailer truck requires specialised licensing in most jurisdictions, and the vehicles are subject to limits on weight, dimensions, driver hours and emissions. Braking is by compressed-air systems with fail-safe spring brakes, and electrical and pneumatic connections between tractor and trailer are made through coiled lines ("suzies" in British usage). At loading docks, a forklift loads pallets through the rear doors; the detachable trailer also enables "drop and hook" operations that keep expensive tractors moving.

Advantages

Compared with a rigid truck, the semi-trailer combination offers a large, uninterrupted cargo volume, a payload distribution that can be adjusted by sliding the trailer tandem axles, and the flexibility of swapping trailers. The format underpins most long-haul road freight in North America, Europe and Australia, where extended "road trains" link several trailers behind one tractor.
