Cable car (railway)

A cable car (in British English usually a cable tram) is a type of cable railway used for mass transit in which rail cars are hauled by a continuously moving cable running at a constant speed. Individual cars stop and start by releasing and gripping this cable as required. Cable cars are distinct from funiculars, where the cars are permanently attached to the cable and the cable stops and starts with them.

How it works

The cable runs in a conduit beneath the street between the rails, powered by large winding wheels in a central powerhouse. Each car has a grip — a vice-like device operated by the gripman — that reaches through a narrow slot in the conduit to clamp onto the cable; releasing the grip and applying brakes stops the car. Because all cars share the cable, the system's speed is fixed, and operation depends heavily on crew skill, especially on hills and at junctions, where the grip must be released and the car coasts ("let go") across crossing cables.

History

The first successful cable street railway was San Francisco's Clay Street Hill Railroad, which began service on 1 August 1873, promoted by Andrew Smith Hallidie, whose family business made wire rope. Cable traction offered a way to climb San Francisco's steep hills that horse-drawn cars could not manage. The technology spread rapidly in the 1880s and 1890s to Chicago (which built the largest system), New York, London, Melbourne and many other cities, but the arrival of the cheaper and more flexible electric streetcar after 1888 led to wholesale abandonment by the early 20th century.

Surviving systems

The San Francisco cable car system is the world's last manually operated cable car system and a National Historic Landmark; three lines survive (Powell–Hyde, Powell–Mason, and California Street), overhauled in a major 1982–84 rebuild. Other passenger cable railways survive in Wellington, New Zealand, and in vestigial forms such as street-running shuttles elsewhere.
