Export of cryptography from the United States

The "munitions" T-shirt: export-controlled RSA code worn as a protest.

The export of cryptography from the United States to other countries has experienced various levels of restrictions over time. During the Cold War, the United States treated cryptographic devices and software as munitions, listing them on the United States Munitions List and regulating their export under the International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR). Export of strong encryption required a licence, and products sold abroad were often limited to weak keys of 40 bits.

History

In the early 1990s, as the internet commercialised, these rules collided with the software industry and civil-liberties advocates in what became known as the Crypto Wars. Incidents included the investigation of Phil Zimmermann, author of PGP, after his software spread abroad; the Clipper chip proposal for key-escrowed encryption (1993); and Daniel J. Bernstein's lawsuit challenging the restrictions on publishing cryptographic source code, in which courts recognised source code as speech protected by the First Amendment.

Liberalisation

In 1996, executive orders moved commercial cryptography from the Munitions List to the Commerce Control List under the Export Administration Regulations. By 1999–2000, most restrictions on mass-market and open-source encryption software were removed, though exports to a small set of embargoed states remained controlled. Cryptography continues to be a controlled dual-use technology, coordinated internationally through the Wassenaar Arrangement.
