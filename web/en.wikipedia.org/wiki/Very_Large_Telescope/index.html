The Very Large Telescope (VLT) is an astronomical facility operated by the European Southern Observatory on Cerro Paranal in the Atacama Desert of northern Chile, one of the driest and clearest observing sites on Earth. It consists of four Unit Telescopes, each with an 8.2-metre primary mirror, and four movable 1.8-metre Auxiliary Telescopes. The Unit Telescopes have Mapuche names: Antu (Sun), Kueyen (Moon), Melipal (Southern Cross) and Yepun (Venus).

Capabilities

The telescopes can work singly or be combined in the VLT Interferometer (VLTI), which links the beams to achieve the angular resolution of a telescope as large as the separation between them — up to about 200 metres — reaching milliarcsecond resolution with instruments such as GRAVITY. Advanced adaptive optics correct for atmospheric turbulence, yielding images sharper than those of many space telescopes in the near-infrared. Instruments have included FORS, UVES, NACO, SINFONI, MUSE, X-shooter, ESPRESSO (for exoplanet radial-velocity work) and SPHERE (for direct imaging of exoplanets).

Discoveries

The VLT has tracked stars orbiting the Milky Way's central supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* (work recognized by the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics), taken the first direct image of an exoplanet (2M1207b, in 2004), measured the most distant gamma-ray bursts, determined distances to gamma-ray burst host galaxies, and characterized the atmospheres of exoplanets. It remains one of the most productive ground-based observatories in the world, and a pathfinder for ESO's Extremely Large Telescope under construction on nearby Cerro Armazones.
