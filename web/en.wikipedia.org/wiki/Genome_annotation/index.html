Genome annotation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genome annotation is the process of identifying the locations of genes and other functional elements in a genome sequence and attaching biological information to them. A genome sequence by itself is a long string of nucleotides; annotation converts it into a usable map of protein-coding genes, RNA genes, regulatory regions, repeats, and other features.

Structural annotation

Structural annotation identifies the physical features of the genome. Gene prediction combines ab initio algorithms, which find genes from sequence signals such as start and stop codons, splice sites, and codon usage patterns, with evidence-based methods that align expressed sequence tags, RNA sequencing data, and protein sequences from related organisms. Popular pipelines include MAKER, BRAKER, and the NCBI Eukaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline. Annotation also marks repetitive elements, pseudogenes, and non-coding RNA genes such as tRNAs and rRNAs.

Functional annotation

Functional annotation assigns putative functions to the identified features, typically by similarity to characterized proteins in databases such as UniProt and by classification into families and domains using resources such as Pfam and InterPro. Gene Ontology terms are commonly used to describe molecular function, biological process, and cellular component in a standardized vocabulary.

Challenges

Annotation is error-prone: gene models may be fragmented or fused, alternative splicing complicates transcript prediction, and functional assignments based on similarity can propagate errors between databases. Because gene prediction relies on the completeness of the underlying assembly, annotation quality improves with each improved genome release, as seen in successive GENCODE and RefSeq annotations of the human genome.
