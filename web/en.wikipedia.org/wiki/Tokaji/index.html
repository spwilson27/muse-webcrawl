Tokaji (Hungarian: Tokaji) is the name of the wines from the Tokaj wine region in north-eastern Hungary (also the Tokaj wine region in Slovakia). The name derives from the Hungarian town of Tokaj. Tokaji is most celebrated for its Aszú, a sweet white wine made from grapes affected by noble rot (Botrytis cinerea), which is among the oldest botrytised wines in the world and was famously praised by Louis XIV of France as the "wine of kings, king of wines".

History

Viticulture in the region dates back centuries; the Tokaj-Hegyalja wine region was among the first in the world to receive an official vineyard classification, in the 18th century. According to tradition, the first aszú wine was made around 1630 by Laczkó Máté Szepsi. The region's vineyard area was delimited by royal decree in 1737. Under communism quality declined, but production revived after 1990 with foreign investment. The historic wine region is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Making Aszú

Aszú berries — individual grapes shrivelled by botrytis — are picked by hand and collected in baskets called puttony. A paste of these berries is added to a base wine or fermenting must; historically, the number of puttonyos (3 to 6) indicated the sweetness level. The extremely sweet, free-run juice of aszú berries is called Eszencia (essence), one of the rarest and most expensive wines in the world. The principal grape varieties are Furmint and Hárslevelű, with smaller amounts of Sárgamuskotály (Muscat).

Styles

Besides Aszú and Eszencia, the region produces dry and off-dry Furmint wines, the blended sweet wine Szamorodni, and late-harvest styles. Tokaji was a protected name within the European Union, and after legal disputes its use was restricted to the Hungarian (and adjacent Slovak) region, ending its use by producers in Alsace and elsewhere.
