Operation Gladio

Type: Clandestine stay-behind operation

Period: 1950s–1990

Region: Western Europe

Publicly revealed: 24 October 1990, by Giulio Andreotti

Operation Gladio was the codename for clandestine "stay-behind" operations of armed resistance organized by NATO and the CIA in Western European countries during the Cold War. The name originates from the Italian network, after the gladius, a short sword used by Roman legionaries. The operations were designed to prepare for a possible Warsaw Pact invasion, with secret arms caches, guerrilla training, and resistance networks that would operate behind enemy lines.

History

Stay-behind networks were first established in the late 1940s and early 1950s, drawing on wartime resistance experience. Coordination was provided through NATO-linked bodies such as the Clandestine Planning Committee and the Allied Clandestine Committee, with the participation of Western intelligence services and the secret services of the host countries. Networks existed in Italy, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, West Germany, Greece, Turkey, and other countries, each with its own codename — for example, SDRA8 in Belgium and the "Organisation Armée Secrète"-adjacent structures in France.

Exposure

The existence of Gladio became public in 1990 when Italian Prime Minister Giulio Andreotti confirmed the network to parliament, following investigations by magistrate Felice Casson into the 1972 Peteano bombing. Revelations triggered parliamentary inquiries in Italy, Belgium, and Switzerland, and a European Parliament resolution condemning the networks. In Italy, Gladio had been managed by the military intelligence service SISMI; documents indicated the structure was formally dissolved in 1990.

Controversies

In Italy, the exposure of Gladio became entangled with investigations into the "strategy of tension" — the period of bombings and violence during the Years of Lead — and into the secretive Propaganda Due (P2) masonic lodge, whose membership list included Gladio figures. Some researchers have alleged that elements of stay-behind networks were involved in, or exploited by, far-right violence intended to discredit the left; the full extent of such involvement remains disputed among historians, and several specific allegations have not been substantiated. The scale of secrecy — many elected governments were unaware of the networks on their territory — raised enduring questions about democratic oversight of intelligence services.
