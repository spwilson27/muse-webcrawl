Karl Guthe Jansky (October 22, 1905 – February 14, 1950) was an American physicist and radio engineer who, in August 1931, first discovered radio waves emanating from the Milky Way. He is considered one of the founding figures of radio astronomy.

Early life and education

Jansky was born in what was then the Territory of Oklahoma, where his father, Cyril M. Jansky, was Dean of the College of Engineering at the University of Oklahoma. His mother, Nellie Moreau, was of French and English descent. Karl's brother, Cmore Jansky, was an electrical engineer who helped build some of the earliest radio transmitters in Wisconsin. Karl earned a Bachelor of Science in physics from the University of Wisconsin in 1927.

Career at Bell Labs

In 1928 Jansky joined the Bell Telephone Laboratories in New Jersey. Bell Labs wanted to investigate atmospheric and ionospheric properties using short wavelengths (around 10–20 meters) with the goal of using them for transatlantic radio telephone service. As a radio engineer, Jansky was assigned the job of investigating sources of static that might interfere with radio voice transmissions.

The discovery

At Bell Labs in Holmdel, New Jersey, Jansky built a directional antenna designed to receive radio waves at 20.5 MHz (about 14.6 meters wavelength). The antenna was mounted on a turntable that allowed it to rotate, earning it the nickname "Jansky's merry-go-round." By recording signals on a pen-and-paper chart recorder, Jansky identified three types of static: nearby thunderstorms, distant thunderstorms, and a faint steady hiss of unknown origin. He spent over a year investigating the third type and determined that the radiation peaked once every 23 hours and 56 minutes — the length of a sidereal day — indicating an astronomical rather than terrestrial source. By 1932 he concluded the source lay in the direction of Sagittarius, toward the center of the Milky Way. He published his findings in 1933.

Legacy

Although Jansky wished to investigate further, Bell Labs reassigned him to other projects, and he did no more work in astronomy. His discovery nonetheless founded a new science. The unit of radio flux density, the jansky (Jy), is named in his honor. A full-scale replica of his antenna stands at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory in Green Bank, West Virginia, and the Karl G. Jansky Very Large Array in New Mexico also bears his name. Grote Reber followed up on Jansky's work and built the first parabolic radio telescope.
