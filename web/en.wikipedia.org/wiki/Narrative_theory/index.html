Narrative theory

Narrative theory, or narratology, is the study of narrative structure and the ways that narratives affect perception, meaning, and experience. Drawing on literary theory, linguistics, and semiotics, it seeks to describe the grammar common to stories across media — novels, film, oral tale, history, games — and to analyse how elements such as plot, narrator, character, time, and point of view produce narrative effects. The term narratologie was proposed by Tzvetan Todorov in 1969.

Narratology analyses the relation between story (what is told) and discourse (how it is told).

Origins

The roots of narrative theory lie in Aristotle's Poetics, with its account of plot (mythos) as an imitation of action structured by beginning, middle, and end. The modern discipline began with the Russian Formalists: Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) identified 31 narrative functions and seven character roles across fairy tales, while Viktor Shklovsky distinguished fabula (the events in their causal order) from syuzhet (their artistic arrangement) — the pair later restated as "story" versus "discourse".

Structuralist narratology

In the 1960s and 1970s, French structuralists generalised these insights. Claude Bremond modelled narrative as branching sequences of possibilities; A. J. Greimas proposed actantial roles and the semiotic square; Roland Barthes's "Introduction to the Structural Analysis of Narratives" (1966) and S/Z (1970) layered codes of meaning over the text. Gérard Genette's Narrative Discourse (1972) provided the field's most influential systematic vocabulary — order, duration, and frequency (tense); focalization and distance (mood); and narrator levels and person (voice) — refined by Mieke Bal and Shlomith Rimmon-Kenan. Seymour Chatman's Story and Discourse (1978) synthesised the Anglo-American and French traditions.

Postclassical narratology

From the 1990s, scholars such as David Herman broadened the field into "postclassical" narratologies: cognitive narratology studies how readers build mental models of storyworlds; feminist narratology (Susan Lanser, Robyn Warhol) examines voice and gender; rhetorical narratology (James Phelan, Wayne Booth) treats narrative as purposeful communication between authors and audiences; and transmedial narratology extends analysis to film, comics, and digital media. Monika Fludernik's "natural" narratology grounds narrativity in readers' experience of embodied consciousness.

Key concepts

  • Narrativity — the quality that makes a text or artefact a narrative; often linked to event sequencing, experientiality, and tellability.
  • Focalization — the perceptual or cognitive perspective through which events are presented, distinct from the narrator who speaks.
  • Story and discourse — the distinction between the content of a narrative and its expression.
  • Unreliable narration — narration whose account diverges from the norms of the work, as theorised by Booth.
  • Possible worlds and storyworlds — the mentally constructed worlds readers infer from narrative cues.

Applications

Narrative theory informs literary criticism, film studies, historiography (Hayden White's analysis of historical writing as narrative emplotment), law, medicine (narrative medicine), psychology, and game studies, where the narratology–ludology debate questioned how far interactive media fit classical models of story.
