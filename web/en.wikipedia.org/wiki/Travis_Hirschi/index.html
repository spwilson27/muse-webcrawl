Travis Warner Hirschi (April 15, 1935 – January 2, 2017) was an American criminologist known for his social control theories of crime and delinquency. He was among the most cited criminologists of the twentieth century and served as president of the American Society of Criminology.

Social bond theory

In Causes of Delinquency (1969), based on a large self-report study of boys in Richmond, California, Hirschi inverted the usual criminological question: instead of asking why people offend, he asked why most people conform. His answer was the social bond: people refrain from crime when they are bonded to conventional society through four elements — attachment to parents, teachers, and peers; commitment to conventional goals such as education and career; involvement in conventional activities; and belief in the legitimacy of rules. Weak bonds free individuals to respond to the ever-present temptations of delinquency.

Self-control theory

With Michael R. Gottfredson, Hirschi later proposed in A General Theory of Crime (1990) that low self-control, established in childhood primarily through parental socialisation, explains all crime — and analogous imprudent behaviours such as smoking, accidents, and unstable employment — across the life course. People with low self-control are impulsive, insensitive, physical, risk-taking, short-sighted, and nonverbal, and they gravitate toward the immediate gratifications crime offers when opportunity arises. The theory became one of the most tested (and debated) in criminology; Hirschi later revised his view of self-control toward a set of social controls measured by bonds, notably in work integrating the two theories.

Career

Hirschi earned his PhD in sociology from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1968, and taught at the University of California, Davis, the University at Albany (SUNY), and the University of Arizona, from which he retired. His textbook and articles with Gottfredson and others reshaped debates about the age–crime curve, the stability of criminal propensity, and the relative merits of control versus learning and strain theories.
