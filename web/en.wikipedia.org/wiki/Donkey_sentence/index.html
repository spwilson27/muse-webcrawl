Donkey sentence

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A donkey sentence is a sentence in which a pronoun appears to act as a bound variable although it is not in the scope of the expression it depends on, posing a classic problem for theories of anaphora in formal semantics. The stock example, discussed by Peter Geach in 1962, is:

Every farmer who owns a donkey beats it.

The problem

The pronoun it intuitively means "the donkey he owns", as if bound by the indefinite a donkey. But standard predicate logic cannot represent this directly: the indefinite inside the relative clause should be confined to that clause, while it lies outside it; nor is a donkey naturally read as existential here, since the sentence requires that each farmer beat every donkey he owns. Related examples include Geach's "Just one monk who had abstained..." cases and pronouns following conditionals, as in "If a farmer owns a donkey, he beats it."

Solutions

Donkey sentences motivated major developments in semantic theory, notably Hans Kamp's discourse representation theory and Irene Heim's file change semantics, in which indefinites introduce discourse referents accessible to later pronouns and themselves carry no fixed quantificational force. Alternative accounts include E-type pronoun analyses, in which it functions as a disguised definite description.
