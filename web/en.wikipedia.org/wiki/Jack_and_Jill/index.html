Jack and Jill

"Jack and Jill" (sometimes "Jack and Gill") is a traditional English nursery rhyme. The Roud Folk Song Index gives it the number 10266. The earliest surviving printing dates from about 1765, in Mother Goose's Melody, with the first verse and the lines about plastering Jack's head with vinegar and brown paper.

Lyrics

Jack and Jill went up the hill
To fetch a pail of water;
Jack fell down and broke his crown,
And Jill came tumbling after.

Up Jack got, and home did trot,
As fast as he could caper;
And went to bed to mend his head
With vinegar and brown paper.

Later versions add verses in which Jill is scolded by her mother for laughing at Jack's misfortune, and other continuations. Nineteenth-century editions often added moralizing stanzas.

Origins and interpretations

The rhyme's origins are uncertain. One long-standing theory connects "Jack and Jill" to the Norse myth, recorded by Snorri Sturluson, of the children Bil and Hjúki, taken by the moon-god Máni as they carried a bucket of water — said to be visible as markings on the moon. "Jack" and "Jill" were also generic names for a young man and woman in early modern English, and a "jack" and a "gill" were small liquid measures (the gill being a quarter of a pint), which has suggested a lost alehouse or measures rhyme. A local tradition in Kilmersdon, Somerset, claims a real couple met their fate on a hill there in 1697.

In popular culture

The rhyme has inspired countless parodies, pantomimes, and works including Louisa May Alcott's novel Jack and Jill (1880). Its names remain the archetypal English pairing, and "jack and jill" survives as a term for paired fixtures and events, from bathroom designs to pre-wedding fundraising parties in parts of Canada and the United States.
