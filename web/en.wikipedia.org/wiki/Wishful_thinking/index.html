Wishful thinking

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Wishful thinking
TypeCognitive bias; informal fallacy
RelatedOptimism bias, confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, appeal to consequences

Wishful thinking is the formation of beliefs based on what might be pleasing to imagine, rather than on evidence, rationality, or reality. It is a product of resolving conflicts between belief and desire, and is related to the concepts of self-deception and motivated reasoning. Studies have consistently found that, holding other factors equal, subjects predict positive outcomes to be more likely than negative outcomes (optimism bias), and that desires distort the perception of ambiguous stimuli—people literally tend to see what they want to see.

As a fallacy

In argumentation, wishful thinking functions as a variety of the appeal to consequences: "X must be true because I wish it were," or its mirror image, fear-driven belief sometimes called "dreadful thinking". Philosophers note that the desirability of a proposition has no bearing on its truth, though deciding how much evidence to demand before acting may legitimately depend on the stakes.

Psychological findings

Experimental research shows wishful thinking in probability judgments: fans overestimate their team's chances of winning, patients may overestimate recovery odds, and investors overestimate returns. Visual-perception experiments (for example, work by Balcetis and Dunning) found that desirable objects are perceived as closer and ambiguous figures are interpreted as the interpretation that earns a reward. Not all such effects reflect distortion of perception rather than of judgment or reporting, and the size and generality of the effect remain subjects of debate.

Counterpoints

Some degree of positive illusion has been associated with mental health, persistence, and achievement—the "depressive realism" literature suggests mildly depressed people can be more accurate in some judgments. In politics and planning, wishful thinking is implicated in the planning fallacy, in which projects are systematically underestimated in cost and duration. Organisations attempt to counter it with reference-class forecasting, pre-mortems, and devil's advocacy.
