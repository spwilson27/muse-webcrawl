Category theory

[Image: a chalkboard commutative diagram — boxes labelled with letters, connected by labelled arrows forming a square]

Category theory is a general theory of mathematical structures and their relations. A category consists of objects and of morphisms (arrows) between them, composed associatively with identity arrows; examples include the category of sets with functions, of groups with homomorphisms, and of topological spaces with continuous maps.

Introduced by Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane in a 1945 paper on algebraic topology, category theory was created to make precise ideas such as 'natural' constructions, and has since become a unifying language across algebra, topology, logic, theoretical computer science and mathematical physics.

Core ideas

Functors map one category to another, preserving structure; natural transformations compare functors. Universal properties define objects by their relationships — products, limits, and adjoint functors recur throughout mathematics, leading to the joke that category theory is 'abstract nonsense', a label its practitioners adopted with pride.

Yoneda's lemma — that an object is determined by the morphisms into (or out of) it — is among the deepest elementary results, expressing the idea that things are known by their relationships.

Applications

Category theory underlies modern algebraic geometry and homological algebra; in computer science it informs functional programming, type theory and database theory (monads in programming are a categorical concept); in logic, topos theory generalises set theory itself.

Its abstraction is contested as well as celebrated: critics find it excessively general, while supporters credit it with revealing connections invisible from inside any single field.
