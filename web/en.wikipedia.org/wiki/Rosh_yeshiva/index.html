Rosh yeshiva

A rosh yeshiva (Hebrew: ראש ישיבה, literally "head of the yeshiva"; plural roshei yeshiva) is the title given to the dean of a yeshiva, a Jewish educational institution devoted principally to the study of the Talmud and rabbinic literature. The rosh yeshiva is generally recognised as the institution's senior scholar and its ultimate authority on matters of Torah study and religious practice within the yeshiva.

Role

The central function of the rosh yeshiva is to deliver the principal shiur (lecture), often the advanced daily or weekly lecture (shiur klali) attended by the whole student body, in which a Talmudic topic is analysed in depth. He typically sets the curriculum and the standard of scholarship of the institution, examines and admits senior students, and serves as the decisive voice in questions of Jewish law put to the yeshiva.

In many yeshivas the rosh yeshiva shares leadership with the mashgiach ruchani, the spiritual supervisor responsible for students' religious and moral development, and with lecturers known as ramim (from rav maggid shiur, "teacher who delivers a lecture"). Some large yeshivas have several roshei yeshiva, frequently members of the founding family, and succession is often hereditary or passes to a son-in-law or a leading disciple.

History

The institution in its modern form derives from the great Lithuanian yeshivas of the nineteenth century, above all the Volozhin Yeshiva (founded 1802), whose rosh yeshiva — notably Rabbi Naftali Zvi Yehuda Berlin, the Netziv — became the model for the office. From Eastern Europe the title and institution spread to Israel and the United States, where yeshivas such as Lakewood's Beth Medrash Govoha and the Mir Yeshiva grew around charismatic roshei yeshiva. In the Hasidic world the equivalent authority is usually the rebbe, while in the Sephardic tradition the head of a yeshiva may be styled rosh yeshiva as well.

Because of their learning, roshei yeshiva are frequently counted among the gedolim ("great ones"), the leading rabbinic authorities of their generation, and their opinions carry weight far beyond their own institutions.
