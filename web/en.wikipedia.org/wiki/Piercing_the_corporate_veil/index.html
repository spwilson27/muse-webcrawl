Piercing the corporate veil

Piercing the corporate veil
Legal areaCorporate law
EffectShareholders held personally liable for the company's obligations
Foundation caseSalomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] (contrast)

Piercing the corporate veil is a legal decision by a court to disregard the separate legal personality of a corporation and hold its shareholders or directors personally liable for the company's debts or wrongdoing. It is an exception to the general rule, established in English law by Salomon v A Salomon & Co Ltd [1897] AC 22, that a company is a legal person distinct from its owners, who benefit from limited liability.

United States

US courts most often pierce the veil of closely held corporations when shareholders have ignored corporate formalities, commingled funds, grossly undercapitalised the company, or used it to perpetrate fraud or injustice. Tests vary by state; many require (1) complete domination of the corporation by its owners and (2) that recognising the corporate form would sanction a wrong or produce an inequitable result. Courts are generally more reluctant to pierce in contract cases than in tort cases, since contract creditors could have investigated the company in advance.

United Kingdom

English courts take a much narrower approach. In Adams v Cape Industries plc [1990] the Court of Appeal refused to pierce the veil merely because a corporate group had structured itself to avoid future liabilities. In Prest v Petrodel Resources Ltd [2013], the Supreme Court restated the doctrine: piercing is available only in limited circumstances, chiefly the "evasion principle", where a person under an existing obligation deliberately uses a company to evade it, and it should be a last resort.

Factors considered

Common factors include inadequate capitalisation, failure to observe formalities (minutes, separate accounts), commingling of assets, non-functioning officers, siphoning of funds, and use of the company as a mere façade or alter ego. No single factor is usually decisive.
