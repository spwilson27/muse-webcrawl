An Ebola outbreak is an occurrence of cases of Ebola virus disease (EVD), a severe viral haemorrhagic fever caused by ebolaviruses. Since the disease was first identified in 1976 in two simultaneous outbreaks — in Nzara (South Sudan) and in Yambuku, near the Ebola River in Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo) — more than thirty outbreaks have been recorded, mostly in Central and West Africa. Case fatality rates have varied from about 25% to 90%, averaging around 50%.

Major outbreaks

  • 1976, Zaire and Sudan — the first recognised outbreaks (318 and 284 cases respectively) identified the new virus, named after the Ebola River.
  • 1995, Kikwit, DRC — 315 cases; amplified in a hospital setting.
  • 2000–2001, Uganda — the largest outbreak to that date (425 cases of Sudan ebolavirus).
  • 2013–2016, West Africa — beginning in Guéckédou, Guinea, the epidemic spread through Liberia and Sierra Leone, becoming by far the largest on record, with roughly 28,600 cases and 11,300 deaths, exported cases in several other countries, and a WHO declaration of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (August 2014). It ended in 2016.
  • 2018–2020, Kivu, DRC — the second-largest outbreak (about 3,470 cases and 2,280 deaths), fought amid armed conflict; the first in which the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine and monoclonal antibody treatments were used at scale.
  • 2022, Uganda — a Sudan ebolavirus outbreak of 164 cases, declared over in January 2023.

Transmission and control

Ebola spreads through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of a symptomatic person or someone who has died of the disease, and via contaminated objects; it is not considered airborne, and patients are not infectious before symptoms appear. Fruit bats are believed to be the natural reservoir, with spillover to humans directly or through hunted or scavenged animals such as apes and duikers. Outbreak control rests on rapid case identification and isolation, contact tracing and 21-day monitoring, safe and dignified burials, infection control in health facilities, and community engagement. Ring vaccination with rVSV-ZEBOV (Ervebo, licensed 2019) and treatments including the monoclonal antibodies Inmazeb and Ebanga have improved outcomes in recent outbreaks.

Course of disease

After an incubation period of 2–21 days, illness begins abruptly with fever, fatigue, muscle pain and headache, followed by vomiting, diarrhoea, rash and, in some patients, internal and external bleeding and multi-organ failure. Survivors may suffer long-term sequelae ("post-Ebola syndrome") and the virus can persist in immune-privileged sites such as the eye and semen, occasionally causing late flare-ups.
