The Capitoline Museums occupy palaces around Michelangelo's Piazza del Campidoglio.

The Capitoline Museums (Italian: Musei Capitolini) are a group of art and archaeological museums on top of the Capitoline Hill in Rome, Italy, arranged around the Piazza del Campidoglio designed by Michelangelo. They are considered the world's oldest public museums: their origin traces to 1471, when Pope Sixtus IV donated a group of ancient bronze statues to the people of Rome, and they were opened to the public in 1734 under Clement XII.

Buildings

The museums occupy the Palazzo dei Conservatori and the Palazzo Nuovo, which face each other across the square, and the Palazzo Senatorio. An underground gallery, the Galleria Lapidaria, links the palaces and passes through the Tabularium, the ancient Roman archive, which offers a celebrated view over the Roman Forum.

Collections

Highlights include the original bronze equestrian statue of Marcus Aurelius (a copy stands in the piazza), the Capitoline Wolf — the bronze she-wolf suckling Romulus and Remus, symbol of Rome — the marble Capitoline Venus, the Dying Gaul, the Spinario (boy pulling a thorn from his foot), and the remains of the colossal statue of Constantine. The Pinacoteca (picture gallery) holds works by Titian, Tintoretto, Rubens, Guido Reni and Caravaggio, including the latter's John the Baptist and The Fortune Teller.
