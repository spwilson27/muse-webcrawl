The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, commonly known as the McCarran–Walter Act, reorganised United States immigration and naturalisation law into a single statute. Passed by Congress over President Harry S. Truman's veto on 27 June 1952, it remains — as amended — the basic framework of US immigration law (codified at Title 8 of the US Code).

Provisions

The Act retained the national-origins quota system of the Immigration Act of 1924, but removed racial barriers to immigration and citizenship: it ended the exclusion of Asian immigrants, allotting small quotas (the "Asia-Pacific Triangle") and allowing Japanese, Korean and other Asian aliens to naturalise for the first time. It introduced preference categories for skilled workers and relatives of US citizens, and broadened the grounds for exclusion and deportation, particularly targeting alleged communists and subversives in the climate of McCarthyism.

Opposition and aftermath

Truman vetoed the bill as discriminatory, and his commission later recommended abolishing the quota system; liberal critics called the act a Cold War measure that preserved racial hierarchy while claiming reform. Its discriminatory features were progressively dismantled, above all by the Hart–Celler Act of 1965, which abolished national-origins quotas, but the 1952 Act's structure of admissibility, deportation and naturalisation still underlies US law.
