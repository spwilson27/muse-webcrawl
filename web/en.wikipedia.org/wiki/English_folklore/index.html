English folklore

English folklore comprises the myths, legends, customs, dances, songs and superstitions traditionally found in England. It draws on Anglo-Saxon, Celtic, Norse and Norman sources and has been transmitted largely by oral tradition, later supplemented by antiquarian collections from the seventeenth century onwards.

Legendary figures

The best-known figures include Robin Hood, the outlaw of Sherwood Forest; King Arthur and his knights (whose legends have strong Welsh and Cornish associations but became central to English identity); Lady Godiva of Coventry; Herne the Hunter of Windsor Forest; and Dick Whittington, the poor boy who became Lord Mayor of London. Local legends attach to standing stones, hills and wells across the country.

Customs and calendar

Seasonal customs include May Day celebrations with maypoles and morris dancing, Plough Monday, Shrove Tuesday pancake races, Harvest festivals, Mummers' plays at Christmas, and bonfires on Guy Fawkes Night (5 November), commemorating the Gunpowder Plot of 1605. Cheese-rolling at Cooper's Hill in Gloucestershire and bottle-kicking in Leicestershire are famous surviving local contests.

Creatures and superstitions

Traditional beliefs feature fairies, boggarts, black dogs such as Black Shuck, dragons (for example the Lambton Worm), giants, and witches. Superstitions include touching wood for luck, fear of Friday the thirteenth, and avoiding walking under ladders. Many customs were recorded by antiquarians such as John Aubrey and, in the nineteenth century, by collectors of the Folk-Lore Society, founded in 1878.
