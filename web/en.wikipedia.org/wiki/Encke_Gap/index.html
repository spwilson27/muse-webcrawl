Encke Gap

The Encke Gap in Saturn's A ring, imaged by Cassini

The Encke Gap is a 325-kilometre-wide gap in Saturn's A ring, centred at a radial distance of about 133,590 kilometres from the centre of Saturn. It is named after the German astronomer Johann Franz Encke, who was long credited with its discovery, although the gap was probably first observed by other astronomers, including Father Francesco de Vico in 1837 and possibly William Henry Dawes; Encke himself observed a low-contrast feature in the A ring in 1837 but did not identify the modern gap.

Characteristics

The Encke Gap is the second most prominent gap in Saturn's main ring system, after the Cassini Division. Unlike the largely empty Cassini Division, the Encke Gap contains several faint, narrow ringlets, including one near its centre that coincides with the orbit of the small moon Pan.

Pan and the gap's edges

The gap is kept open by the gravitational action of Pan, a walnut-shaped moon roughly 28 kilometres across that orbits within the gap. Pan's gravity sweeps up ring particles and produces scalloped waves along the gap's edges. Images from the Cassini spacecraft revealed these wavy edges and thin dusty ringlets in detail. Small ring particles and wakes generated by Pan create spiral patterns in the surrounding material.
