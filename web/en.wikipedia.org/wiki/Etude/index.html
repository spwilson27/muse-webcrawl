Étude

An étude (French for "study") is an instrumental musical composition, usually short, designed to provide practice material for perfecting a particular musical skill. The tradition of writing études emerged in the early nineteenth century with the rapidly growing popularity of the piano as a home instrument, and teachers needed exercises of increasing difficulty. Although many études are purely didactic, the most famous — by Chopin and Liszt — transcend the practice room and are performed as concert works.

History

Precursors include the exercises of Muzio Clementi (Gradus ad Parnassum) and the studies of Carl Czerny, whose hundreds of études remain staples of piano pedagogy. Frédéric Chopin's two sets of Études, Opp. 10 and 25 (1829–1836), revolutionised the genre: each isolates a technical problem — chromatic runs, wide left-hand stretches, octaves, thirds — yet is a fully realised piece of music. Franz Liszt's Transcendental Études (final version 1852) pushed virtuosity further still.

Later developments

Claude Debussy's twelve Études (1915) explore pianistic colour; Alexander Scriabin and Sergei Rachmaninoff wrote concert études (études-tableaux); György Ligeti's three books of Études (1985–2001) subject rhythm and pattern to intense study. Études exist for other instruments too, including Rodolphe Kreutzer's 42 studies for violin and études for flute, guitar and voice (vocalises).
