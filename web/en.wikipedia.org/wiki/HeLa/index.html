HeLa is an immortalised human cell line used in scientific research. It is the oldest and most commonly used human cell line, derived from cervical cancer cells taken on February 8, 1951, from Henrietta Lacks, a 31-year-old African-American patient at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, Maryland. The line proved remarkably durable and prolific, dividing indefinitely in culture — unlike normal cells, which are subject to the Hayflick limit.

History

Researcher George Otto Gey found that Lacks's tumour cells, cultured by Mary Kubicek, grew more vigorously than any human cells previously kept alive in vitro. HeLa cells were soon distributed worldwide and mass-produced; they enabled Jonas Salk's testing of the polio vaccine and have since been used in research on cancer, viruses, radiation, toxicology, genetics, and space biology, contributing to tens of thousands of studies and several Nobel Prize-winning discoveries.

Biology

HeLa cells are aneuploid, with a highly rearranged genome containing human papillomavirus (HPV-18) sequences; the viral E6 and E7 proteins disable the p53 and Rb tumour suppressors, and telomerase activity maintains their telomeres, conferring immortality. Because the cells are hardy and fast-growing, they have contaminated many other cell cultures — a problem publicised by Walter Nelson-Rees in the 1970s.

Ethics and the Lacks family

The cells were taken without Lacks's knowledge or consent, standard practice at the time but central to later debates on informed consent and commercialisation of human tissue. Her identity became public in the 1970s, and Rebecca Skloot's 2010 book The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks brought the story wide attention. In 2013, after the HeLa genome was published without family consultation, the US National Institutes of Health reached an agreement giving the Lacks family a role in governing access to the genome data; Johns Hopkins and other institutions have since formally recognised her contribution.
