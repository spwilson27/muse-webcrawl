Cleaner fish

DietEctoparasites, dead tissue, mucus

Cleaner fish are fish that feed on the dead skin, ectoparasites, and infected tissue of other animals ("clients") in a cleaning symbiosis, one of the best-known examples of mutualism in marine biology. The cleaner gains food while the client benefits from parasite removal and improved health. The behavior has evolved independently in several lineages, most famously among the cleaner wrasses of the Indo-Pacific.

Cleaning stations

Cleaners typically occupy a fixed location on a reef called a cleaning station, where client fish — including large predators such as groupers and moray eels — visit and adopt distinctive postures inviting inspection. Cleaners advertise themselves with conspicuous coloration (often a bold longitudinal stripe) and a characteristic bobbing "dance." Clients suppress their normal predatory responses while being serviced, and cleaners may even clean inside clients' mouths and gill cavities.

Cheating

The mutualism is not perfectly honest: cleaners prefer to eat client mucus over parasites, and may "cheat" by biting mucus or scales, causing clients to jolt. Clients respond with punishment (chasing the cleaner) or by leaving, and cleaners behave more cooperatively when bystander clients are watching — a reputation effect studied extensively in the bluestreak cleaner wrasse. The false cleanerfish (Aspidontus taeniatus), a blenny that mimics the cleaner wrasse, exploits the system by biting chunks of fin from unsuspecting clients.

Other cleaners

Cleaner behavior also occurs among gobies in the Atlantic (e.g., Elacatinus species), juvenile angelfish, cichlids, catfish, and some cleaner shrimps performing analogous roles. In aquaculture, lumpfish and ballan wrasse are deployed in salmon cages to control sea lice.
