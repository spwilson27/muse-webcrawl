Proton nuclear magnetic resonance

A 1H NMR spectrum of an organic compound.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (proton NMR, 1H NMR, or H-1 NMR) is the application of nuclear magnetic resonance in NMR spectroscopy with respect to hydrogen-1 nuclei within the molecules of a substance, in order to determine the structure of its molecules.

Principles

Hydrogen-1 is the most NMR-sensitive stable isotope at natural abundance. In a strong magnetic field, proton spins absorb radio-frequency radiation at a resonance frequency influenced by their chemical environment. The resulting chemical shift, reported in parts per million (ppm) relative to a reference such as tetramethylsilane (TMS), together with signal integration (proportional to the number of protons) and spin–spin coupling patterns (splitting into doublets, triplets, multiplets), reveals how many distinct proton environments a molecule has and how they are connected.

Typical chemical shifts

Alkyl protons appear near 0.9–1.5 ppm; protons next to electronegative atoms near 3–4 ppm; alkene protons near 4.5–6 ppm; aromatic protons near 7–8 ppm; aldehyde protons near 9–10 ppm; and carboxylic acid protons near 11–12 ppm. Symmetrical molecules give fewer signals — benzene shows a single peak for all six protons.

Applications

1H NMR is the workhorse of organic structure determination and reaction monitoring, used in chemistry, pharmaceuticals, and materials science. Samples are usually dissolved in deuterated solvents such as CDCl3 or DMSO-d6 so the solvent's own protons do not overwhelm the spectrum.
