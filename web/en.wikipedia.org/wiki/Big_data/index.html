Big data

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
[Image: A visualisation of vast streams of data flowing between servers]

Big data refers to data sets so large, fast-growing, or complex that traditional data-processing methods are inadequate. It is commonly characterised by the 'three Vs' — volume, velocity, and variety (later extended with veracity and value).

The 2000s saw search engines and web companies (Google's MapReduce paper, 2004; the open-source Hadoop, from 2006) pioneer distributed storage and processing across commodity clusters. Later systems (Spark, streaming platforms, cloud data warehouses and lakes) made big-data analytics routine infrastructure.

Technologies and uses

Data is stored in distributed file systems and NoSQL databases, processed in batch or streams, and analysed with statistics and machine learning. Applications include recommendation systems, fraud detection, genomics, urban planning, and large-scale science (particle physics, astronomy surveys).

Big data raises concerns: privacy and surveillance, algorithmic bias, energy consumption of data centres, and the 'data exhaust' of everyday life feeding profiling.
