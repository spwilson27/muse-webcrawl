Iodine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Iodine
Symbol: I
Atomic number: 53
Group: 17 (halogens)
Type: Wikipedia article (recreated from memory)

Iodine is the chemical element with the symbol I and atomic number 53. It is a dark, lustrous purple-black solid that sublimes into a striking violet vapour; it is the heaviest halogen that is stable and essential to life.

Iodine is required by the thyroid gland to make hormones that regulate metabolism; deficiency causes goitre and developmental harm, which is why table salt is iodised in many countries. Tincture of iodine is a classic antiseptic, and iodine gives a deep blue-black colour with starch — a standard laboratory test.

[Image: Iodine crystals subliming into violet vapour in a flask]

History

Bernard Courtois discovered iodine in 1811 in Paris while extracting salts from seaweed ash for saltpetre; Joseph Gay-Lussac named it from the Greek iodes, violet-coloured. Humphry Davy independently characterised it soon after.

Chemistry

Iodine is only slightly soluble in water but dissolves readily in potassium iodide solution and in alcohol. It is the least reactive of the common halogens.
