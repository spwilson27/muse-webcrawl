The Kerry Bog Pony is a small mountain-and-moorland pony breed native to County Kerry in south-western Ireland. Traditionally used by small farmers to carry peat (turf) from the bogs and for light draught and farm work, the ponies are noted for their hardiness, sure-footedness on soft ground, easy-keeping qualities and gentle temperament, which makes them suitable children's ponies.

History

Ponies of this type lived semi-ferally on the peatlands of Kerry for centuries, adapted to the poor grazing and harsh weather of the bog. Written references to small "hobbies" in the region date back hundreds of years, and the breed is thought to be closely related to the ancient Irish Hobby. Mechanisation in the 20th century sharply reduced the working population, and by the 1990s the breed was close to extinction. John Mulvihill of County Kerry led efforts to identify and preserve the remaining stock; DNA studies supported the breed's distinctness, and in 2005 the Kerry Bog Pony was officially recognised by the Irish Department of Agriculture and the European Commission as a distinct rare breed, with a breed society and studbook established to manage its recovery.

Characteristics

Kerry Bog Ponies stand about 10 to 11.2 hands high. Stallions and mares are compact and strong, with a thick winter coat, low-set tail and hard hooves. Chestnut, often with a flaxen mane and tail, is characteristic, but bay, black, brown, palomino and dun also occur. Numbers remain small, and the breed is listed as endangered.
