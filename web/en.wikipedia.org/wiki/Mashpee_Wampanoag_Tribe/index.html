Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe

The Mashpee Wampanoag Tribe is a federally recognised tribe of Wampanoag people based in Mashpee, Massachusetts, on Cape Cod. The tribe is one of two federally recognised Wampanoag tribes in Massachusetts, the other being the Wampanoag Tribe of Gay Head (Aquinnah) on Martha's Vineyard. The Mashpee Wampanoag are descendants of the Indigenous peoples who inhabited the region for thousands of years and who famously interacted with the Pilgrims of Plymouth Colony in 1620–21 under the leadership of the Massasoit Ousamequin.

History

Mashpee was established in the 17th century as a "praying town", and in 1660 the Wampanoag reserved a tract of land for their own use. Through the 18th and 19th centuries the community retained a distinct identity despite pressure on its land base; Mashpee was incorporated as a town in 1870, ending formal tribal guardianship, but community members continued to organise as a tribe.

In 1976 the tribe sued the town of Mashpee for the return of tribal lands in Mashpee Tribe v. New Seabury Corp. A jury found that the Mashpee were a tribe but had ceased to be one at crucial dates, and the claim failed — a landmark and controversial case in federal Indian law.

Federal recognition and land

After a long petition process, the Mashpee Wampanoag gained federal recognition in February 2007. In 2015 the U.S. Department of the Interior approved taking roughly 321 acres in Mashpee and Taunton into trust for the tribe. That decision was challenged in court, and the tribe's land status became the subject of the high-profile dispute following the Supreme Court's Carcieri v. Salazar (2009) ruling, which limited trust acquisitions to tribes "under federal jurisdiction" in 1934. In December 2021 the Interior Department affirmed the tribe's eligibility, confirming its reservation land.

The tribe has pursued development of the First Light Resort & Casino in Taunton. It also operates a government centre and health services in Mashpee, and supports the Wôpanâak Language Reclamation Project, which has revived the Wampanoag language — dormant for generations — now taught to tribal children.
