Monk seals are earless seals of the tribe Monachini, comprising two living species: the Mediterranean monk seal (Monachus monachus) and the Hawaiian monk seal (Neomonachus schauinslandi). A third species, the Caribbean monk seal (Neomonachus tropicalis), was hunted to extinction and formally declared extinct in 2008, the only seal species known to have been driven extinct by humans in modern times. Monk seals are the only earless seals adapted to warm, tropical and subtropical waters, and both surviving species are among the most endangered marine mammals in the world.

Description

Monk seals take their name from their solitary habits, the folds of skin around the neck said to resemble a monk's cowl, and their smooth, robe-like coat. Adults measure roughly 2.3–2.8 m and weigh 230–610 kg, with females slightly larger than males in the Mediterranean species. They feed on fish, squid, octopus, lobsters and eels, foraging on the seabed, and haul out on sandy beaches and, in the Mediterranean, in secluded sea caves where they give birth.

Conservation

The Mediterranean monk seal numbers around 700–900 individuals scattered around the eastern Mediterranean, the Aegean and the Atlantic coast of northwest Africa (notably the Cabo Blanco peninsula in Mauritania, site of the largest colony), having been reduced by hunting, entanglement in fishing gear, habitat disturbance and deliberate killing by fishers. The Hawaiian monk seal numbers about 1,600 animals, mostly in the Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument, and is the subject of an intensive recovery programme by NOAA involving rehabilitation, vaccination, translocation and protection from sharks, marine debris and disease. Conservation measures have slowly increased both populations in recent decades, and the Mediterranean species was reclassified from Critically Endangered to Endangered and later Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List as numbers recovered.

The extinct Caribbean monk seal was once distributed throughout the Caribbean Sea and Gulf of Mexico; recorded by Columbus's crews in 1494, it was hunted for oil and skins and last reliably sighted in 1952 at Serranilla Bank.
