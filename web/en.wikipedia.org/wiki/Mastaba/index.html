Mastaba

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mastaba (from the Arabic word for a bench of mud) is a type of ancient Egyptian tomb consisting of a flat-roofed, rectangular structure with inward-sloping sides, built of mudbrick or stone, with a flat top and a subterranean burial chamber. Mastabas were the standard form of tomb for royalty and high officials during the Early Dynastic Period and the Old Kingdom of ancient Egypt, before and alongside the development of pyramids.

Structure

The above-ground superstructure of a mastaba was typically a rectangular block with slightly battered (sloping) walls, resembling the benches outside Egyptian houses, from which its modern Arabic name derives. Beneath it, a vertical shaft descended to the burial chamber, which was sealed after interment. The superstructure contained a chapel where relatives and priests could make offerings to the deceased. A characteristic feature was the false door, a carved stone representation of a doorway through which the spirit (ka) of the deceased was believed to pass to receive offerings. Many mastabas also contained a serdab, a sealed chamber housing a statue of the deceased, with small slits allowing the statue to observe the offering chapel.

Development

Early mastabas of the First Dynasty were built of mudbrick and imitated palace architecture with elaborate niched facades. Over time they were increasingly built of stone and decorated with painted or carved relief scenes of daily life, offering lists and the titles of the tomb owner. The royal mastaba evolved into the Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara — essentially a series of mastabas stacked one atop another — from which the true pyramid developed. Non-royal officials continued to be buried in mastabas, grouped in great necropolises at Saqqara and Giza, throughout the Old Kingdom and later periods.
