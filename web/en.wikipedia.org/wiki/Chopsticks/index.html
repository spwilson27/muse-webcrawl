Chopsticks

Chopsticks are shaped pairs of equal-length sticks that have been used as kitchen and eating utensils in most of East Asia and parts of Southeast Asia for over three millennia. They are held in the dominant hand, secured by the thumb and fingers, and used as extensions of the hand to pick up food.

History

The earliest chopsticks, made of bronze, were found at Anyang in China and date to around 1200 BCE during the Shang dynasty; originally they were likely cooking tools for reaching into hot pots before becoming eating utensils by the Han dynasty. Their use spread with Chinese cultural influence to Korea, Japan, Vietnam and elsewhere.

Regional variations

  • China — kuaizi: typically long (about 27 cm), made of bamboo, wood, plastic or metal, used with woks and communal dishes.
  • Japan — hashi: shorter (about 20–23 cm), often lacquered wood or bamboo, suited to deboned fish dishes served individually.
  • Korea — jeotgarak: medium length, characteristically made of flat stainless steel and paired with a spoon, used with hot metal bowls and slippery noodles.
  • Vietnam — đũa: long, like Chinese chopsticks, usually bamboo or wood.

Etiquette

Customs vary, but common taboos include sticking chopsticks vertically into rice (which resembles funeral incense offerings), spearing food, passing food directly from chopsticks to chopsticks, and pointing with them. Resting chopsticks across the bowl or on a chopstick rest (hashioki) is preferred when not eating.

Materials and environment

Disposable wooden and bamboo chopsticks are produced in enormous quantities — tens of billions of pairs annually — prompting environmental criticism and taxes or bans in some places; reusable chopsticks of metal, melamine or fibreglass are common in homes and restaurants.
