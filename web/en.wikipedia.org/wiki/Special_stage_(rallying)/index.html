Special stage (rallying)

A special stage (SS) is a section of closed public or private road used for timed competition in a rally. Together, special stages are the competitive heart of the event: crews drive them as fast as possible one car at a time, and the sum of stage times (plus any penalties) determines the result. Between special stages, crews travel on open roads in normal traffic during "road sections" or liaison, observing all traffic laws.

A World Rally Car on a gravel special stage.

Format

Stages are typically 5–50 km long, started at intervals (usually one to three minutes) by a start marshal with a countdown. Timing is to the tenth of a second (historically to the second in some national events). Co-drivers read pacenotes describing corners and hazards. Some rallies end with a "Power Stage" offering bonus championship points, and spectator-friendly "super special stages" run two cars side by side on parallel tracks in stadiums or city centres.

Safety

Because speeds are high and roads are closed only for the event, safety planning covers spectator placement, marshal points, radio networks, and medical cover. Notorious stages include the Ouninpohja stage of Rally Finland, famous for its jumps and average speeds, and the Col de Turini of the Monte Carlo Rally, often run at night in icy conditions. The 1986 accidents involving Group B cars — including the fatal crash of Henri Toivonen and Sergio Cresto in Corsica — led to the abolition of Group B and sweeping changes to stage safety.

Notable stages

  • Ouninpohja (Finland) — fast gravel, big jumps.
  • Col de Turini (Monte Carlo) — Alpine pass, mixed ice and asphalt.
  • El Condor and Mina Clavero (Argentina) — rocky mountain stages.
  • Arganil (Portugal) — classic stage of the 1970s–80s.
  • Whaanga Coast (New Zealand) — flowing coastal gravel.
