Sign language

[Image: Two people conversing in American Sign Language, hands raised mid-sign with expressive facial grammar.]

A sign language is a language that uses manual communication — hand configurations, movements and locations, together with facial expressions and body posture — instead of sound to convey meaning. Sign languages are complete natural languages with their own grammar, not gestures or codes for spoken languages: American Sign Language (ASL), British Sign Language (BSL) and French Sign Language (LSF) are mutually unintelligible, and ASL descends largely from LSF rather than from English.

History and recognition

Deaf communities have signed for as long as records exist, but systematic education began with Abbé Charles-Michel de l'Épée's Paris school in the 18th century; his successors spread sign-based teaching, including to the United States, where Thomas Gallaudet and Laurent Clerc founded the first American school for the deaf in 1817. The 1880 Milan Congress pushed oral-only education and suppressed signing in schools for nearly a century. William Stokoe's 1960 linguistic analysis of ASL demonstrated that signs have sub-lexical structure analogous to phonemes, founding sign linguistics and helping restore sign language's status; many countries now legally recognise their national sign languages.

Structure

Signs are formed from parameters — handshape, orientation, location, movement and non-manual markers — that play the role of phonology in spoken languages. Sign languages exploit space grammatically, placing referents at locations and directing verbs between them, and can express simultaneity impossible in speech. There are hundreds of sign languages worldwide, including village sign languages shared by deaf and hearing villagers alike.
