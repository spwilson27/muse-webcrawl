Motivational interviewing (MI) is a counselling approach designed to help people resolve ambivalence about behaviour change by eliciting and strengthening their own motivations. It was developed by psychologists William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick, initially for work with problem drinkers, and is now used across healthcare, addiction services, social work and corrections.

Principles

MI is practised in a guiding, non-confrontational style described by its founders as a "spirit" of partnership, acceptance, compassion and evocation. Rather than persuading or warning the client — approaches thought to provoke resistance — the practitioner listens reflectively and draws out the client's own "change talk": statements of desire, ability, reasons and need for change.

The core skills are commonly summarised by the acronym OARS: Open questions, Affirmations, Reflective listening and Summaries. Four overlapping processes structure the work: engaging, focusing, evoking and planning.

Evidence

Meta-analyses find small-to-moderate effects of MI on alcohol and substance use and on various health behaviours such as smoking cessation, diet and medication adherence, often comparable to longer treatments despite MI's brevity. It is frequently combined with other interventions, for example in motivational enhancement therapy within large trials such as Project MATCH.

History

Miller first described the approach in a 1983 paper arising from his work in Norway, and with Rollnick published Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior in 1991. Subsequent editions (2002, 2013 and 2023) broadened the method beyond addiction. Training is coordinated internationally through the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers (MINT), founded in 1997.
