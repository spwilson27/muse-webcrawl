Plutonium-239 (239Pu or Pu-239) is an isotope of plutonium. Plutonium-239 is the primary fissile isotope used for the production of nuclear weapons, although uranium-235 is also used for that purpose. Plutonium-239 is also one of the three main isotopes demonstrated usable as fuel in thermal spectrum nuclear reactors, along with uranium-235 and uranium-233. Plutonium-239 has a half-life of 24,110 years.

Production

Plutonium-239 is formed in nuclear reactors when a neutron is absorbed by a uranium-238 nucleus, forming uranium-239, which quickly undergoes two beta decays to neptunium-239 and then to plutonium-239. It was first produced and isolated in 1940–41 by Glenn Seaborg and colleagues at Berkeley by deuteron bombardment of uranium, and its fissile properties — established in secret wartime research — made it the focus of the Manhattan Project's production reactors at Hanford.

Nuclear properties

239Pu decays by alpha emission to uranium-235 with a half-life of about 24,110 years. It is fissile: absorption of a thermal neutron usually leads to fission, releasing about 200 MeV of energy and on average nearly three neutrons, allowing a self-sustaining chain reaction. The bare-sphere critical mass is about 10 kg. Even-numbered plutonium isotopes, especially plutonium-240, undergo spontaneous fission at relatively high rates, which is why weapons-grade plutonium is defined as having a low Pu-240 content (typically under 7%).

Uses

Plutonium-239 was used in the Trinity test device and in the Fat Man bomb dropped on Nagasaki in August 1945, and remains the principal material in modern nuclear warheads. Mixed with uranium oxide as MOX fuel, reactor-grade plutonium is also used as fuel in some nuclear power reactors.

Handling and hazards

Plutonium-239 is an alpha emitter; its principal hazard is internal exposure, since inhaled or ingested particles can irradiate tissues and increase cancer risk. The metal is pyrophoric and is stored as the oxide for stability. Its long half-life makes plutonium-bearing waste a long-term stewardship problem.
