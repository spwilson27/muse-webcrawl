Chữ quốc ngữ (𡨸國語, "script of the national language") is the Latin-script writing system used for the Vietnamese language. It is the official script of Vietnam and one of the few Latin-based systems used for a language of East and Southeast Asia.

Structure

The script uses the Latin alphabet with additional letters formed by diacritics (ă, â, đ, ê, ô, ơ, ư) and a system of tone marks — grave, acute, hook above, tilde and dot below — that allow one syllable to be written with several stacked diacritics, as in ệ. Digraphs and a trigraph (ngh) represent single sounds, reflecting 17th-century Portuguese-influenced orthographic conventions.

History

The system was developed by Portuguese and French Jesuit missionaries, notably Francisco de Pina and Gaspar do Amaral, and was published in systematic form by Alexandre de Rhodes in his 1651 Dictionarium Annamiticum Lusitanum et Latinum. Long used mainly by the Catholic Church, it spread in the late 19th and early 20th centuries through colonial schools and the Vietnamese-language press, and became the vehicle of mass literacy campaigns after 1945.

Use

Because it represents Vietnamese phonemically, chữ quốc ngữ can be learned far more quickly than the character-based scripts it displaced; its adoption is credited with Vietnam's high literacy rate. Older scripts survive in ceremonial, scholarly and decorative contexts.
