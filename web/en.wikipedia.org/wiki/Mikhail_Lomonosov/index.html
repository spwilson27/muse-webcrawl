Mikhail Vasilyevich Lomonosov (1711–1765) was a Russian polymath — scientist, writer, poet and educator — sometimes called the founder of Russian science and of the modern Russian literary language. Rising from a peasant family in the far north of Russia, he walked to Moscow to study, later trained in Germany, and returned to transform the St. Petersburg Academy of Sciences; in 1755 he co-founded the university that now bears his name, Moscow State University.

Scientific work

Lomonosov developed a corpuscular-kinetic theory of heat, rejecting the caloric theory, and in a 1748 letter to Leonhard Euler stated a principle of the conservation of matter and motion, anticipating the law of conservation of mass; his experiments sealing and weighing metals in retorts (1756) supported this conclusion. Observing the transit of Venus in 1761, he correctly inferred that Venus has an atmosphere. He also worked on the nature of electricity, the physics of freezing, mineralogy, and the origin of coal and icebergs, and revived the art of mosaic and glassmaking in Russia.

Language and literature

His Russian Grammar (1755) codified the language, and his theory of the three styles of literary Russian shaped Russian writing for generations. His odes, notably the "Ode on the Seizure of Khotyn" (1739) and the meditations on natural phenomena, mark the beginning of modern Russian poetry.

Legacy

Moscow State University has borne his name since 1940. The lunar crater Lomonosov, a Martian crater, and a ridge in the Arctic Ocean are named after him, and he remains a central figure of the Russian Enlightenment.
