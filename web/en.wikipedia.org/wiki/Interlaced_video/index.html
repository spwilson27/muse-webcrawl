Interlaced video

Interlaced video (also known as interlaced scan) is a technique for doubling the perceived frame rate of a video display without consuming extra bandwidth. The interlaced signal contains two fields of a video frame captured consecutively. This enhances motion perception to the viewer and reduces flicker by taking advantage of the persistence of vision.

How it works

Each frame is split into two fields: the first field contains all the odd-numbered lines of the image, and the second field contains all the even-numbered lines. The fields are displayed in rapid succession, so a 60-fields-per-second interlaced signal (as in NTSC) delivers 30 complete frames per second while appearing to flicker far less than a 30 Hz progressive signal.

History

Interlacing was developed in the 1930s, notably by RCA engineer Randall Ballard, to allow early television broadcasts to achieve acceptable picture quality within limited channel bandwidth. It was built into the NTSC, PAL, and SECAM analogue television standards.

Problems and deinterlacing

Interlacing causes artifacts such as "combing" when the two fields of a moving image are combined, interline twitter on fine detail, and difficulties in scaling and compression. Modern displays are progressive, so interlaced broadcasts must be deinterlaced. Digital formats such as 1080i retain interlacing, while most newer formats favor progressive scan.
