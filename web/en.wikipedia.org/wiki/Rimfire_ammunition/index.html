Rimfire ammunition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rimfire ammunition is a type of metallic cartridge used in firearms where the primer is located within a hollow circumferential rim of the casing. During firing, a hammer or firing pin strikes and crushes the rim to ignite the primer, which ignites the propellant. Invented in 1845 by Louis-Nicolas Flobert, it contrasts with the more common centerfire ammunition, in which the primer is a separate component seated in the centre of the case head.

Characteristics

Because the case head must be crushable to ignite the primer, rimfire cases are thin and pressures are limited compared to centerfire designs. Rimfire cartridges are generally not reloadable, since the priming compound is spun into the rim during manufacture and the rim is deformed on firing. They are, however, inexpensive to manufacture and produce mild recoil and noise, making them dominant in small-calibre sporting, training and plinking roles.

Popular rimfire cartridges

  • .22 Long Rifle (.22 LR) — by far the most common; introduced in 1887, used worldwide for target shooting, small-game hunting and training.
  • .22 Winchester Magnum Rimfire (.22 WMR) — a more powerful magnum rimfire introduced in 1959.
  • .17 Hornady Magnum Rimfire (.17 HMR) — a high-velocity necked-down rimfire introduced in 2002.
  • .22 Short and .22 Long — 19th-century cartridges still made for specialized uses.

History

Flobert's 1845 BB cap, a tiny cartridge propelled by primer alone, was the first rimfire. Smith & Wesson built its early revolvers around rimfire cartridges such as the .22 Short (1857) and .32 Rimfire; larger rimfires such as the .44 Henry powered Civil War-era Henry and Winchester lever-action rifles. As centerfire designs matured, larger rimfires became obsolete, leaving the small-calibre niche in which rimfire thrives today.
