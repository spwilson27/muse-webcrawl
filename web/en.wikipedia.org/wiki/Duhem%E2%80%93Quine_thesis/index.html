Duhem–Quine thesis

The Duhem–Quine thesis is a thesis in the philosophy of science holding that scientific hypotheses cannot be tested in isolation: an empirical prediction follows only from a hypothesis together with a body of auxiliary assumptions, background theories, and statements about initial conditions, so a failed prediction does not by itself show which member of the set is false. It is named after Pierre Duhem and W. V. O. Quine.

Content

Duhem argued in 1906 that a physicist can never subject an isolated hypothesis to experimental test, since deriving any prediction requires a theoretical scaffolding of further hypotheses; when the prediction fails, logic alone does not identify the culprit. Quine radicalised the point into confirmational holism: "our statements about the external world face the tribunal of sense experience not individually but only as a corporate body", and in principle any statement can be retained if enough adjustments are made elsewhere in the web of belief.

Implications and responses

The thesis is a standard challenge to naive falsificationism and a motivation for analyses of scientific change that invoke conventions, values, and auxiliary hypotheses. Critics such as Adolf Grünbaum argued that in many concrete cases the blame for a failed prediction can be localised, and philosophers debate whether Duhem's moderate thesis for physics supports Quine's stronger general claim.
