Yogachara

Yogachara (Sanskrit: योगाचार, "yoga practice"; also spelled Yogācāra) is an influential tradition of Buddhist philosophy and psychology that emerged in India between the 4th and 5th centuries CE. Along with Madhyamaka, it is one of the two principal schools of Indian Mahayana philosophy. Its central doctrine is vijñapti-mātra, usually translated "representation-only" or "consciousness-only": what we experience as an external world is a construction of consciousness.

Key doctrines

Eight consciousnesses. Yogachara analysis extends the traditional six consciousnesses with two more: the afflicted mentality (kliṣṭa-manas), which mistakes the deeper consciousness for a self, and the storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna), which accumulates karmic "seeds" (bīja) and perfuming impressions (vāsanā) that ripen into future experience.

Three natures (trisvabhāva). Experience is analysed as the imagined nature (parikalpita) — the false subject–object duality projected onto experience; the dependent nature (paratantra) — the causal flow of experience itself; and the perfected nature (pariniṣpanna) — the absence of that imagined duality within the dependent flow.

Transformation of the basis (āśraya-parāvṛtti). Awakening is described as a fundamental overturning of the storehouse consciousness, ending the seed-based construction of a deluded world.

Founders and texts

The school is traditionally founded on the half-brothers Asanga and Vasubandhu (4th–5th century), with earlier roots in the Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra and the works of Maitreyanātha. Key treatises include the Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra, Asanga's Mahāyānasaṃgraha, and Vasubandhu's Viṃśikā (Twenty Verses) and Triṃśikā (Thirty Verses). Later systematizers such as Dharmapāla and the logician Dignāga and Dharmakīrti developed the tradition further.

Influence

Transmitted to China by Xuanzang in the 7th century, Yogachara became the Faxiang school; in modified forms (through the Awakening of Faith and tathāgatagarbha thought) it deeply shaped East Asian Buddhism, including Zen, and Tibetan Buddhism. Modern scholars have compared its analysis of experience with phenomenology and cognitive science.
