Pterosaur

Pterosaurs (from Greek ptero, "wing" and sauros, "lizard") are an extinct group of flying reptiles in the order Pterosauria. They existed during most of the Mesozoic: from the Late Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous (228 to 66 million years ago). Pterosaurs are the earliest vertebrates known to have evolved powered flight, preceding birds and bats by tens of millions of years. Their wings were formed by a membrane of skin, muscle and other tissues stretching from an enormously lengthened fourth finger to the ankles.

Anatomy

The pterosaur wing was supported by the arm and a hyper-elongated fourth finger; the first three fingers remained small and clawed. A unique bone, the pteroid, supported a forward membrane (propatagium). Bones were hollow and air-filled, connected to an air-sac respiratory system. Many pterosaurs bore elaborate head crests used in display. Their bodies were covered in hair-like filaments called pycnofibres, suggesting insulation and warm-bloodedness.

Groups

Early, long-tailed forms are informally grouped as "rhamphorhynchoids" (a paraphyletic grade including Dimorphodon and Rhamphorhynchus), while the advanced Pterodactyloidea had short tails, fused jaw bones and great diversity: fish-eating pteranodontids with huge crests, the toothed ornithocheirids, tapejarids, and the giant terrestrial-stalking azhdarchids, including Quetzalcoatlus northropi — among the largest flying animals of all time, with a wingspan estimated at 10–11 metres.

Palaeobiology

Pterosaurs occupied varied niches: aerial piscivores, insectivores, filter-feeders such as Pterodaustro, and terrestrial foragers. Trackways show they walked quadrupedally on land, folding their wings. Eggs were soft-shelled and buried; evidence suggests the young (flaplings) could fly soon after hatching but may have received parental care. Pterosaurs became extinct in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, leaving no descendants.

History of discovery

The first pterosaur fossil was described by Cosimo Collini in 1784 and initially interpreted as an aquatic animal; Georges Cuvier identified it as a flying reptile in 1809 and named it "Ptéro-Dactyle". Richard Owen and others popularised finds such as Mary Anning's Dimorphodon. Pterosaur research flourished from the 1970s onward, transforming their image from clumsy gliders to sophisticated, active fliers.
