Delicatessen

A delicatessen, or deli, is a shop selling cured meats, cheeses, prepared salads and other ready-to-eat foods, often also serving made-to-order sandwiches and light meals. The word comes from the German Delikatessen, "delicacies" or fine foods, itself from the French délicatesse. Delicatessens range from traditional European fine-food shops to the Jewish and Italian delis of North American cities, and to small corner shops selling sandwiches and groceries in New York, where "deli" commonly denotes a bodega-like store.

History

German immigrants brought the delicatessen to the United States in the mid-nineteenth century, and the format was enriched by Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe, whose kosher delicatessens — serving pastrami, corned beef, knishes, matzo ball soup and pickles — became institutions in New York and other cities; famous examples include Katz's Delicatessen, founded in 1888, and the late Carnegie Deli. Italian delicatessens, featuring cured pork products, cheeses and antipasti, developed in parallel. In Germany and central Europe, a Delikatessen shop remains primarily a purveyor of luxury and speciality foods.

Foods

Typical deli fare includes cold cuts and cured meats (pastrami, salami, ham, corned beef), smoked fish, cheeses, olives, pickles, prepared salads such as coleslaw and potato salad, soups and large sandwiches — including the Reuben — often accompanied by a pickle spear. Many delis slice meats to order and offer catering platters.
