The Zulu Kingdom (Zulu: amaZulu), sometimes called the Zulu Empire, was a monarchy in Southern Africa that extended along the coast of the Indian Ocean from the Tugela River in the south to Pongola River in the north. Formed from a small Nguni clan, the kingdom grew under Shaka (r. 1816–1828) into the dominant power of the region, in the upheavals known as the Mfecane. It was defeated by the British Empire in the Anglo-Zulu War of 1879 and finally absorbed into the Colony of Natal in 1897.

Shaka and the military system

Shaka, son of the chief Senzangakhona, rose through service in Dingiswayo's Mthethwa army and succeeded to the Zulu chieftaincy in 1816. He reorganised his forces into age-grade regiments (amabutho) quartered in military kraals, replaced long throwing spears with the short stabbing iklwa, introduced large cowhide shields and close-order tactics — including the famous "bull horn" formation — and incorporated conquered peoples into a centralised kingdom. By his assassination in 1828 by his half-brothers Dingane and Mhlangana, the kingdom dominated much of present-day KwaZulu-Natal and raided far beyond it.

Later kings and the Anglo-Zulu War

Dingane (r. 1828–1840) clashed with the Boer Voortrekkers, massacring Piet Retief's party but suffering defeat at the Battle of Blood River (1838); he was overthrown by his brother Mpande (r. 1840–1872). Under Cetshwayo (r. 1873–1884) the kingdom faced a British ultimatum designed to be rejected. In the Anglo-Zulu War of 1879 the Zulu army inflicted one of the worst defeats ever suffered by a British column at Isandlwana, but lost the war at Ulundi. Zululand was divided among thirteen chiefdoms, descended into civil war, and was annexed by Britain in 1887 and incorporated into Natal in 1897.

Legacy

The kingdom's heritage remains central to Zulu identity in South Africa, and its monarch — today Misuzulu kaZwelithini — continues as a traditional leader within the Republic of South Africa.
