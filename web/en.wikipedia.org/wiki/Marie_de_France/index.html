Marie de France (fl. 1160–1215) was a poet, possibly born in France, who lived in England during the late twelfth century and wrote in Anglo-Norman French at the court of Henry II. She is the earliest known French woman poet and is best known for the Lais of Marie de France, a collection of twelve short narrative poems about love and adventure, drawn from Breton and Celtic sources. Nothing certain is known of her life beyond what she reveals in her works; "Marie de France" is the signature she gives herself in her Fables: "Marie ai num, si sui de France" ("My name is Marie, and I am from France").

Works

The Lais are short romances, mostly between 100 and 1,000 lines, written in octosyllabic couplets and dedicated to a "noble king", probably Henry II. They include Guigemar, Equitan, Le Fresne, Bisclavret (about a werewolf), Lanval (an Arthurian tale of a knight and a fairy mistress), Les Deux Amanz, Yonec, Laüstic (the nightingale), Milun, Chaitivel, Chevrefoil (a brief episode of Tristan and Iseult), and Eliduc. The lais are notable for their sympathetic treatment of women, their exploration of love outside marriage, and their blend of courtly and magical elements.

Marie also translated 102 of Aesop's Fables into Anglo-Norman verse, claiming to translate from an English version attributed to King Alfred, and wrote the L'Espurgatoire seint Patriz (Saint Patrick's Purgatory), an account of a knight's vision of the afterlife. A saint's life, La Vie Seinte Audree (on Saint Audrey of Ely), has also been attributed to her.

Identity

Various candidates have been proposed for Marie, including Mary, Abbess of Shaftesbury (a half-sister of Henry II), Marie of Boulogne, and Marie de Champagne — but none has been proven. She was evidently highly educated, knowing Latin, French, and English, and possibly Breton and Welsh.
