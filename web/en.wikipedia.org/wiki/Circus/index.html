Circus

A circus is a travelling company of performers who present a variety of entertainment acts: acrobatics, juggling, clowning, aerial performance, human feats of strength and balance, and, historically, animal acts. The modern circus takes its name from the circular performance space (the "ring") and was established by Philip Astley in London in the late 18th century.

History

Astley's Amphitheatre (1768) combined trick horse-riding with other acts in a 42-foot-diameter ring — still the standard ring size. The travelling big-top tent was popularised in the United States in the 19th century by showmen such as P. T. Barnum. Circus acts traditionally include flying trapeze (invented by Jules Léotard, 1859), tightrope walking, contortion, unicycle, and the ringmaster's presentation.

Modern developments

Contemporary "new circus" (nouvelle cirque), exemplified by Canada's Cirque du Soleil (founded 1984), emphasises theatrical storytelling and acrobatics without animals. Many countries now restrict or ban wild-animal acts, and the largest traditional circus, Ringling Bros. and Barnum & Bailey, closed its animal-free-criticised touring format in 2017 before relaunching without animals in 2023.
