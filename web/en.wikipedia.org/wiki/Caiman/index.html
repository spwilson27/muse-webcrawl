A caiman (also spelled cayman) is an alligatorid belonging to the subfamily Caimaninae, one of two primary lineages within the family Alligatoridae, the other being alligators. Caimans are native to Central and South America and inhabit marshes, swamps, lakes and slow-moving rivers.

Species

Six living species are generally recognised. The spectacled (common) caiman (Caiman crocodilus) is the most widespread; the broad-snouted caiman (C. latirostris) and yacare caiman (C. yacare) occupy southern waters. The black caiman (Melanosuchus niger) is the largest predator in the Amazon basin, reaching up to five metres. The two dwarf caimans of the genus Paleosuchus — Cuvier's dwarf caiman and the smooth-fronted caiman — are the smallest crocodilians in the Americas, at around 1.2–1.5 metres.

Biology and ecology

Caimans are nocturnal predators feeding on fish, amphibians, birds and small mammals, with larger black caimans taking deer and capybaras. Females build mound nests of vegetation and guard their hatchlings in crèches. Caimans are themselves prey: jaguars famously hunt them, and their eggs and young are taken by birds, fish and larger reptiles. Several species were heavily hunted for their skins in the mid-20th century; farming, ranching and protected status have since allowed most populations to recover, though habitat loss remains a concern.
