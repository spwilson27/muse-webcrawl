Racovian Catechism

The Racovian Catechism is a nontrinitarian statement of faith from the 16th century. The catechism was published by the Polish Brethren of the Minor Reformed Church of Poland in 1605, at Raków, Kielce Voivodeship, a centre of Socinian thought and home to the famous Racovian Academy.

Contents

Composed in Polish and soon translated into Latin and other languages, the catechism set out the Brethren's beliefs in question-and-answer form: the unity of God the Father; the humanity of Jesus Christ, whose divinity they understood in terms of his exalted office rather than a co-eternal nature; the denial of the Trinity, original sin, and predestination; believer's baptism; and a strong emphasis on ethical living and pacifism. Fausto Sozzini (Faustus Socinus) had died in 1604, the year before publication, and the catechism represented the mature statement of the theology he had systematised.

Reception and suppression

The catechism became notorious among orthodox Protestants and Catholics alike. A Latin edition dedicated to James I of England scandalised him, and in 1614 copies were publicly burned in London by the common hangman after Parliament denounced the work. In Poland, the Racovian Academy and printing press were suppressed in 1638, and the Polish Brethren were expelled from the Commonwealth in 1658. Despite suppression, the catechism deeply influenced early Unitarianism in Transylvania and England, and anticipated later rationalist theology.
