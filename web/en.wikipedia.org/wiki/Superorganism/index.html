Superorganism

A superorganism is an organised society of organisms that functions as a single functional unit — conventionally, a colony of eusocial insects such as ants, termites or honey bees, in which individuals specialise into castes analogous to the cells and tissues of a body, and in which reproduction is concentrated in one or a few members (queens), much as germ cells carry reproduction in a multicellular organism.

An ant colony: the classic example of a superorganism

History of the concept

The term was coined in 1911 by the American entomologist William Morton Wheeler, who studied ant colonies as integrated units. The idea was developed further by writers such as Herbert Spencer (who applied it to human society) and, in the later twentieth century, by Edward O. Wilson and Bert Hölldobler in works including The Superorganism (2008), which argued that selection can act at the level of the colony.

Characteristics

Features commonly cited include a reproductive division of labour, cooperative care of the young, overlapping generations within the colony, collective homeostasis (for example, temperature regulation in beehives and termite mounds), and colony-level behaviour such as foraging trails and swarm decision-making that no single individual directs.

Broader applications

The concept has been extended, sometimes controversially, to coral colonies, siphonophores, microbial biofilms, the Gaia hypothesis of the Earth as a self-regulating system, and human organisations. Critics caution that the analogy can obscure important differences between colonies and true organisms, particularly the absence of a single genome and of a segregated germ line in most colonies.
