Watermelon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A watermelon sliced open showing scarlet flesh studded with black seeds]

The watermelon (Citrullus lanatus) is a large trailing vine of the gourd family and its fruit — a smooth, hard-rinded berry that is about 92% water, with sweet flesh ranging from deep red through pink, orange and yellow, flecked with black or white seeds. It began in northeastern Africa, where wild melons still grow, was cultivated in Egypt by 2000 BCE, and reached China by the 10th century and the Americas with colonists and enslaved Africans. China now grows well over half the world's crop. Seedless watermelons, a mid-20th-century Japanese innovation, dominate rich-country markets.

History

Archaeologists have found watermelon seeds in Libyan sites around 5,000 years old and in Egyptian tombs — seeds were reportedly left in Tutankhamun's tomb for the journey to the afterlife. Wild ancestors are bitter and were valued first as a portable water source in dry country (the Kalahari's tsamma melon sustained peoples and explorers alike). Breeding turned bitterness to sweetness; by the 1800s American seed catalogues listed dozens of varieties, and the icebox melons of the South became a summer institution — and, shamefully, a racist caricature in post-slavery imagery, a stereotype historians now document carefully.

Seedless fruit and modern production

Seedless watermelons are triploids: a normal diploid pollinator is crossed with a tetraploid parent, and the sterile offspring make fruit with only soft white seed traces, provided diploid "polleniser" rows are interplanted to trigger fruit set. The technique, pioneered by Hitoshi Kihara in Japan in the 1930s–40s, reached market in the 1950s and took decades to perfect. Japanese growers also made headlines with cube watermelons grown in glass boxes — ornamental, expensive, and usually unripe. The red flesh owes its colour to lycopene, the same antioxidant pigment as in tomatoes.
