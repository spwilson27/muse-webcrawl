The Geographica (Ancient Greek: Γεωγραφικά, Geōgraphiká), also called the Geography, is an encyclopaedia of geographical knowledge in 17 books written in Greek by Strabo, a Greek geographer from Amasia in Pontus, and completed around AD 23. It is the most extensive surviving work of ancient geography and a principal source for the topography, peoples and history of the world of the early Roman Empire.

Contents

After two introductory books defending geography as a branch of philosophy and reviewing predecessors such as Eratosthenes, Hipparchus and Posidonius, Strabo describes the known world region by region: Iberia and Gaul (Books 3–4), Italy and Rome (5–6), northern and eastern Europe and the Balkans (7), Greece (8–10), Asia Minor (11–14), and finally India, Persia, Arabia, Egypt and North Africa (15–17). The work interweaves physical geography with myth, history and ethnography, and drew on Strabo's own extensive travels from Armenia to Italy and from the Black Sea to Ethiopia.

Transmission and influence

The Geographica is preserved practically complete (only part of Book 7, on Macedonia and Thrace, is lost) through Byzantine manuscripts. It was comparatively little cited in antiquity but became foundational after its translation into Latin in the 15th century, shaping Renaissance cartography; modern scholarship mines it for lost authors and for data on ancient place names, roads, and natural phenomena.
