Elohim

Elohim (Hebrew: אֱלֹהִים, romanized: ʾĔlōhīm) is a Hebrew word meaning "gods" or "godhood". Despite its plural form, in the Hebrew Bible it most often refers to the single God of Israel and takes singular verbs — as in the opening of Genesis: "In the beginning, Elohim created the heavens and the earth." It is one of the most common names of God in the Bible, occurring more than 2,500 times.

Grammar and usage

The word is the plural of eloah, related to el ("god"), a word shared across Semitic languages and the name of the chief god El in the Canaanite pantheon at Ugarit. When Elohim denotes the God of Israel, its plural morphology is usually explained as a "plural of majesty" or an intensive plural. Elsewhere the same word, with plural agreement, denotes the gods of other nations, angels or divine beings, and in a few passages even human judges or rulers (as in Psalm 82).

In the Hebrew Bible

Elohim is the characteristic divine name of certain strands of the biblical text: the first creation account (Genesis 1) uses Elohim exclusively, and the Psalms of the "Elohistic Psalter" (Psalms 42–83) prefer it to the name YHWH. In the documentary hypothesis, the source that uses Elohim before the revelation of the divine name in Exodus is called the Elohist (E) source.

Later interpretations

Jewish tradition treats Elohim as God's name in his attribute of justice and as Creator, contrasted with the mercy connoted by YHWH; the two names are combined in much of Genesis 2–3. In the Book of Mormon and Latter-day Saint theology, Elohim is the proper name of God the Father. The word also gained popular notoriety in the 20th century through "ancient astronaut" literature, which misread its plurality as evidence of multiple beings — an interpretation rejected by scholars of Hebrew.
