Public diplomacy

In international relations, public diplomacy broadly speaking is any of the various government-sponsored efforts aimed at communicating with and influencing foreign publics to establish a dialogue designed to inform and influence. These efforts range from international broadcasting and cultural exchanges to educational programmes and digital engagement.

History

The phrase "public diplomacy" is commonly attributed to Edmund Gullion, dean of the Fletcher School of Law and Diplomacy at Tufts University, who used it in 1965 to describe the influence of public attitudes on foreign policy. During the Cold War, the United States Information Agency (USIA) was the principal organ of American public diplomacy until its functions were absorbed into the Department of State in 1999.

Methods

Common tools include international broadcasting services such as the BBC World Service and Voice of America, academic and professional exchange programmes such as the Fulbright Program, cultural institutes, and state-backed cultural diplomacy initiatives. In the twenty-first century, social media and digital platforms have become central channels, sometimes termed "digital diplomacy".

Relation to soft power

Public diplomacy is one of the principal instruments through which states exercise soft power — the ability to attract and persuade rather than coerce — as described by Joseph Nye. Critics sometimes distinguish public diplomacy from propaganda on the basis of two-way communication and credibility, though the boundary is contested.
