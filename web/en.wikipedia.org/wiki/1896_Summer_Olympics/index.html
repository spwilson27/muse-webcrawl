1896 Summer Olympics

The 1896 Summer Olympics, officially known as the Games of the I Olympiad, were the first international Olympic Games held in modern history. Organised by the International Olympic Committee (IOC), founded by French aristocrat Pierre de Coubertin, the Games were held in Athens, Greece, from 6 to 15 April 1896. Fourteen nations (according to the IOC) and 241 athletes (all male) took part in 43 events in 9 sports, centred on the restored Panathenaic Stadium.

Revival of the Games

At a congress in Paris in 1894, Coubertin's proposal to revive the ancient Olympic Games was approved and the IOC created, with Greek Demetrios Vikelas as its first president. Athens was chosen to host. The Greek government and public were enthusiastic, and the marble Panathenaic Stadium — site of the ancient games — was lavishly restored with funding from the businessman George Averoff.

Highlights

The United States dominated athletics, winning nine of twelve track and field events on the opening days, with James Connolly becoming the first Olympic champion of the modern era by winning the triple jump. The marathon, invented for the Games and run from Marathon to Athens, was won by the Greek water-carrier Spyridon Louis, who became a national hero. Other sports included gymnastics, weightlifting, wrestling, fencing, shooting, tennis, swimming and cycling. Winners received a silver medal and an olive branch; the modern gold-silver-bronze convention came later.

Legacy

Though modest by later standards, the Athens Games proved the revival viable and fixed the four-year Olympic cycle. The success of 1896 is commemorated as the birth of the modern Olympic movement, and Athens again hosted the Summer Games in 2004.
