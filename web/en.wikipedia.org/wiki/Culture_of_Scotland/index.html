Culture of Scotland

Placeholder image: Culture of Scotland

The culture of Scotland encompasses the traditions, languages, arts, and institutions of Scotland, a country within the United Kingdom. Distinctive elements include the clan system and Highland traditions, the tartan and kilt, bagpipe music, Gaelic and Scots languages, and contributions to philosophy, literature, and science during the Scottish Enlightenment.

Languages

English is the most widely spoken language. Scottish Gaelic, a Celtic language, is spoken mainly in the Highlands and the Hebrides, while Scots is spoken across the Lowlands. All three receive varying degrees of official recognition; the Gaelic Language (Scotland) Act 2005 established Gaelic as an official language of Scotland.

National symbols

Scotland's symbols include the thistle (national flower), the unicorn (national animal), and the Saltire — the blue-and-white cross of St Andrew, Scotland's patron saint, whose feast day on November 30 is a national holiday. The Royal Standard of Scotland features the Lion Rampant. "Flower of Scotland" is commonly sung as an unofficial anthem at sporting events.

Music and dance

The Great Highland bagpipe is Scotland's most famous instrument, and pipe bands perform worldwide. Traditional music also features the fiddle, accordion, and clàrsach (Celtic harp), and Gaelic song traditions including waulking songs and puirt à beul. Highland dancing and Scottish country dancing, including ceilidhs, are popular social traditions.

Clans, tartan, and Highland dress

The clan system historically organized Highland society around kinship groups led by chiefs. Tartan patterns became associated with particular clans, and the kilt — once proscribed after the Jacobite rising of 1745 under the Dress Act — was revived in the 19th century and remains formal and ceremonial dress, worn with accessories such as the sporran and sgian-dubh.

Literature and philosophy

Scotland's literary heritage includes medieval makars, Robert Burns (the national poet, celebrated at Burns suppers on January 25), Sir Walter Scott, Robert Louis Stevenson, and modern writers such as Muriel Spark and Irvine Welsh. The Scottish Enlightenment of the 18th century produced thinkers including David Hume and Adam Smith, and Scotland's universities gained international renown.

Festivals and events

The Edinburgh Festival Fringe, founded in 1947, is the world's largest performing arts festival, held each August alongside the Edinburgh International Festival. Highland games — featuring caber tossing, hammer throw, and tug of war — are held across Scotland and the diaspora. Hogmanay (New Year) celebrations are traditionally more prominent than Christmas, which was discouraged for centuries after the Reformation.

Cuisine

Scottish food includes haggis (the national dish), Cullen skink, porridge, shortbread, tablet, and oatcakes, along with renowned produce such as Aberdeen Angus beef, Arbroath smokies, and Scotch whisky, one of Scotland's most famous exports.
