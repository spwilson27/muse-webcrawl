Soyuz programme

The Soyuz programme (Russian: Союз, "Union") is a human spaceflight programme initiated by the Soviet Union in the early 1960s. The Soyuz spacecraft was originally part of a Moon landing project intended to put a Soviet cosmonaut on the Moon. It is the third Soviet human spaceflight programme, after the Vostok and Voskhod programmes. Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the programme continued under the Russian space agency, Roscosmos, and Soyuz remains in service today.

History

Designed under Sergei Korolev's OKB-1 bureau, the Soyuz 7K-OK spacecraft first flew uncrewed in 1966. Its first crewed flight, Soyuz 1 in April 1967, ended in tragedy when Vladimir Komarov was killed during re-entry. After redesign, Soyuz became the workhorse ferry for the Salyut and Mir space stations, flew the Apollo–Soyuz Test Project with the United States in 1975, and from 2011 to 2020 — between the retirement of the Space Shuttle and the advent of Crew Dragon — was the only vehicle carrying crews to the International Space Station.

The spacecraft

Soyuz consists of three modules: a spherical orbital module, a bell-shaped descent module in which the crew of up to three rides during launch and landing, and a cylindrical service module carrying propulsion, power and life-support systems. It is launched on the Soyuz rocket from Baikonur Cosmodrome in Kazakhstan (and, for uncrewed versions, other sites). Successive versions include Soyuz-T, Soyuz-TM, Soyuz-TMA and the current Soyuz MS, first flown in 2016. The uncrewed Progress cargo spacecraft is derived from the Soyuz design.

Legacy

With well over 140 crewed flights, Soyuz is the longest-serving and one of the most reliable crewed spacecraft designs in history, and its basic architecture has remained recognisable for nearly six decades.
