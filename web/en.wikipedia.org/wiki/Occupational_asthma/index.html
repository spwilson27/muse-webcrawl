Occupational asthma

Occupational asthma is new-onset or recurrent asthma caused by exposure to agents in the workplace. It is one of the most common occupational lung diseases in developed countries, estimated to account for roughly 10–25% of adult-onset asthma cases. Two main forms are recognised: sensitiser-induced occupational asthma, which develops after a latent period of sensitisation to a workplace agent, and irritant-induced asthma, which follows high-level exposure to an irritant, the extreme form being reactive airways dysfunction syndrome (RADS).

Causes

More than 400 workplace agents have been reported to cause occupational asthma. High-molecular-weight agents, typically proteins, include flour and grain dusts (a major cause in bakers), laboratory animal allergens, latex, and enzymes used in detergents. Low-molecular-weight chemicals include diisocyanates (used in paints and foams), acid anhydrides, plicatic acid in western red cedar, persulphate salts in hairdressing, and various metal salts. Jobs at particular risk include baking, spray painting, animal handling, healthcare, hairdressing, and woodworking.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Symptoms — wheeze, chest tightness, cough, and breathlessness — characteristically worsen during or after work and improve on days away from work, although this pattern may fade with chronic exposure. Diagnosis combines a clinical history, objective confirmation of asthma (spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility or peak flow monitoring), and evidence linking asthma to the workplace, such as serial peak expiratory flow records at and away from work, specific IgE testing for some agents, or specific inhalation challenge testing in specialist centres.

Management and prognosis

Early diagnosis and complete avoidance of further exposure to the causative agent offer the best chance of recovery; continued exposure is associated with worsening and potentially irreversible airflow obstruction. Pharmacological treatment follows standard asthma guidelines, but avoidance remains the cornerstone. Occupational asthma is a prescribed/reportable disease in many jurisdictions, and affected workers may be entitled to compensation. Workplace surveillance and exposure controls aim to prevent sensitisation in the wider workforce.
