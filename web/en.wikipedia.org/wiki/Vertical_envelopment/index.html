Vertical envelopment

Vertical envelopment is a military tactic in which troops are delivered by air — by parachute, glider or helicopter — onto or behind an enemy position, seizing key terrain and cutting lines of communication while a ground force attacks from the front. It is the airborne and air-assault form of the classic envelopment or pincer movement, extended into the third dimension.

History

The concept was pioneered in the Second World War with large airborne operations such as the German capture of Crete (1941) and the Allied landings in Normandy and Operation Market Garden (1944). The advent of the helicopter transformed the tactic: in the Korean and especially the Vietnam War, "airmobile" forces used helicopters to land directly on objectives, exemplified by the Battle of Ia Drang (1965), and to be supplied and extracted by air.

Characteristics

Vertical envelopment offers surprise, speed and the ability to bypass obstacles and fortifications, but air-delivered troops are lightly equipped, vulnerable during landing, and dependent on air supply and timely link-up with ground forces. Air superiority and suitable weather are usually prerequisites.
