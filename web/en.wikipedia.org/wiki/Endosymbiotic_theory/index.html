Endosymbiotic theory

Endosymbiotic theory, or symbiogenesis, is the leading evolutionary theory of the origin of eukaryotic cells from prokaryotic organisms. It holds that mitochondria and plastids (such as chloroplasts) in eukaryotic cells evolved from free-living bacteria that were engulfed by an ancestral cell and formed an endosymbiotic relationship.

History

The idea was suggested by Andreas Schimper (1883), developed by Konstantin Mereschkowsky (1905–1910), and proposed for mitochondria by Ivan Wallin in the 1920s, but was widely accepted only after Lynn Margulis's 1967 paper "On the Origin of Mitosing Cells" and her 1970 book Origin of Eukaryotic Cells. The discovery that mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular genomes, similar to bacterial DNA, provided decisive support.

Evidence

Evidence includes: organelles reproduce by binary fission; they have double membranes consistent with engulfment; their ribosomes resemble bacterial (70S) ribosomes; their genes and gene order resemble those of alphaproteobacteria (for mitochondria) and cyanobacteria (for chloroplasts); and many ancestral organelle genes have transferred to the nucleus. Secondary endosymbiosis — engulfment of a eukaryotic alga — explains plastids with three or four membranes in groups such as euglenids and apicomplexans.

Significance

Endosymbiosis is now regarded as a major driver of evolutionary innovation, responsible for the energy metabolism of nearly all complex life.
