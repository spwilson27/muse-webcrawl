The Autochrome Lumière was an early colour photography process, patented in 1903 and marketed by the Lumière brothers of France from 1907. It was the first commercially successful colour process and remained the principal colour medium until the advent of subtractive colour film in the 1930s.

Process

An Autochrome plate was coated with millions of microscopic grains of potato starch dyed orange-red, green and blue-violet, acting as colour filters, and filled with lampblack. Over this mosaic lay a panchromatic black-and-white emulsion. Exposure through the filter mosaic and reversal processing produced a positive transparency that reconstructed colour when viewed against light. Plates required long exposures — typically many times those of contemporary black-and-white material.

Use

Autochrome was used by pictorialist photographers and for major documentary projects. Albert Kahn's Archives of the Planet sent photographers worldwide from 1909 to 1931 to record human cultures in Autochrome colour. The process was also used extensively by National Geographic in the 1920s and 1930s.

Decline

Film-based versions — Lumicolor (1931) and Alticolor — prolonged the process, but Kodachrome (1935) and Agfacolor offered finer grain and greater speed, ending Autochrome's commercial life.
