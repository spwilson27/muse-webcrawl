Corinth Canal

Corinth Canal
Location: Isthmus of Corinth, Greece
Length: 6.4 km (4.0 mi)
Width: 21.4 m (70 ft) at base
Completed: 1893

The Corinth Canal (Greek: Διώρυγα της Κορίνθου) connects the Gulf of Corinth with the Saronic Gulf in the Aegean Sea. It cuts through the narrow Isthmus of Corinth and separates the Peloponnese from the rest of the Greek mainland. It was built between 1881 and 1893.

History

Several ancient rulers dreamed of cutting the isthmus: Periander of Corinth instead built the diolkos, a paved trackway over which ships were hauled on rollers in the 6th century BC. Demetrius, Julius Caesar, Caligula and Nero all considered or attempted a canal; Nero ceremonially broke ground with a golden spade in AD 67, but the project was abandoned after his death. The modern canal was begun by a French company under concession from the Greek government, which went bankrupt; a Greek company completed it, and it opened in 1893 — notably for the first modern Olympic era.

Characteristics

The canal is 6.4 km (4 mi) long and only 21.4 m (70 ft) wide at sea level — too narrow for most modern ocean-going ships, so it is used mainly by tourist and small vessels. Its steep limestone walls rise up to 90 m above the water. Submersible bridges at both ends lower below the water to let ships pass, and the canal saves the 185-nautical-mile journey around the Peloponnese for small craft. Landslides from the steep walls have periodically closed the canal, including significant closures in 2021.
