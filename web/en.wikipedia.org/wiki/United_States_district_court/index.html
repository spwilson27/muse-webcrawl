The United States district courts are the trial courts of the U.S. federal judiciary. There is one district court for each of the 94 federal judicial districts, including at least one district in each state, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico; three territorial courts serve the U.S. Virgin Islands, Guam, and the Northern Mariana Islands. Nearly all civil and criminal cases in the federal system begin in a district court.

Jurisdiction

District courts have original jurisdiction over federal criminal prosecutions, civil cases arising under federal law ("federal question" jurisdiction), civil suits between citizens of different states where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000 ("diversity" jurisdiction), admiralty and maritime cases, and cases to which the United States is a party. They also include specialized units: bankruptcy judges of the district's bankruptcy court (a unit of the district court) and United States magistrate judges who assist district judges with preliminary proceedings, misdemeanors, and, by consent, full civil trials.

Judges

District judges are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, and hold office during good behaviour under Article III of the Constitution — effectively lifetime appointments. After meeting age and service requirements, judges may take senior status, continuing to hear a reduced caseload and creating a vacancy for a new appointee. Each district also has a United States Attorney, the chief federal prosecutor, and a federal public defender in most districts.

Appeals

Appeals from district courts go to the United States court of appeals for the regional circuit in which the district sits (or, in patent and certain other cases, to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit). District court decisions are published selectively in the Federal Supplement reporter.
