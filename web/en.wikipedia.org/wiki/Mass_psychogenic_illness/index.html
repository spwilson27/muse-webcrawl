Mass psychogenic illness (MPI), also called mass hysteria, epidemic hysteria or mass sociogenic illness, is the rapid spread of symptoms of illness through a cohesive group, where the symptoms have no corresponding organic cause. Episodes are generally understood as a form of conversion disorder amplified by social modelling, anxiety and the "line of sight" transmission of symptoms from person to person.

Characteristics

Outbreaks typically occur in enclosed, stressed communities — most often schools and workplaces — and spread rapidly among people who can see or hear affected individuals. Common features identified in the literature include symptoms without plausible organic basis, predominance among adolescents and females in many reported outbreaks, hyperventilation-related complaints, rapid onset and recovery, and resolution when the group is dispersed. Some researchers distinguish a "mass anxiety hysteria" type, driven by a sudden perceived threat (such as a strange smell), from a "mass motor hysteria" type, involving dissociative or motor behaviour developing in settings of chronic stress.

Historical examples

  • Dancing manias of medieval and early modern Europe, in which groups danced uncontrollably for days.
  • Salem witch trials (1692–93), in which the accusers' fits have retrospectively been attributed in part to mass psychogenic phenomena, though this remains debated.
  • The Halifax Slasher panic (1938) and the Mad Gasser of Mattoon (1944), in which reported attacks produced symptoms without an identified assailant.
  • The June bug epidemic (1962) in a US textile mill, where dizziness and nausea were attributed to insects but no cause was found.
  • Tanganyika laughter epidemic (1962), in which uncontrollable laughter and related symptoms spread through schools.
  • Havana syndrome (from 2016) has been debated as possibly involving a psychogenic component, alongside other proposed explanations; assessments differ among agencies and researchers.

Diagnosis and management

MPI is a diagnosis of exclusion: investigators must first rule out environmental toxins, infections and deliberate exposure. Management emphasises calm, authoritative communication, separating symptomatic individuals, avoiding reinforcement through attention, and allowing rest; the symptoms are real to those experiencing them and are not considered feigned.
