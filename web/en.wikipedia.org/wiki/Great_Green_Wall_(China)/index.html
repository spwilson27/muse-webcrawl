The Great Green Wall, officially known in China as the Three-North Shelter Forest Program, is a series of human-planted windbreaking forest strips (shelterbelts) in China, designed to hold back the expansion of the Gobi Desert. Begun in 1978, it is the largest ecological engineering project in the world, planned to continue until 2050 in eight phases. The name is an analogy to the Great Wall of China.

Project

The shelterbelt runs roughly 4,500 kilometres from Heilongjiang in the north-east to Xinjiang in the west, across 13 provinces and regions, in an area covering over 4 million square kilometres — about 42% of China's land area. Alongside fast-growing poplars and other trees, plantings include drought-resistant shrubs such as saxaul and sea-buckthorn. Methods include aerial seeding, fencing off areas for natural regeneration, and "straw checkerboard" grids that stabilise dunes, most famously at the Tengger Desert near Shapotou. The government announced in late 2024 that the belt encircling the Taklamakan Desert had been completed, though the broader Three-North program continues to 2050.

Effects and criticism

China credits the project with raising forest cover in the north, reducing dust storms reaching Beijing, and expanding national forest cover from about 12% in 1981 to over 23% by 2020. Critics note that many monoculture poplar plantations have died or stunted, that planting in arid lands can deplete groundwater, and that desertification continued in places despite the program. In response, policy since the 2000s has shifted towards native shrubs, grazing bans ("Grain for Green" conversion of farmland), and protecting natural vegetation rather than indiscriminate tree planting.
