White stork

White stork
Scientific name: Ciconia ciconia
Family: Ciconiidae
Conservation status: Least Concern

The white stork (Ciconia ciconia) is a large bird in the stork family Ciconiidae. Its plumage is mainly white, with black on the wings, and the adults have long red legs and long pointed red beaks. It measures on average 100–115 cm (39–45 in) from beak tip to end of tail, with a wingspan of 155–215 cm (61–85 in). It is a long-distance migrant, breeding in Europe, northwestern Africa, and southwestern Asia, and wintering mainly in Africa and the Indian subcontinent.

Behaviour and ecology

White storks are carnivores, taking a wide range of animal prey including insects, amphibians, reptiles, small mammals, and small birds. They are monogamous breeders that build large stick nests, often on rooftops, chimneys, and specially erected platforms in human settlements, and the same nest may be reused for many years. Largely mute, they communicate by clattering their bills. Females typically lay four eggs, which hatch asynchronously after about 33–34 days of incubation.

Relationship with humans

The white stork is deeply embedded in European folklore, most famously in the legend that storks deliver babies to families. Its close association with human habitation has made it a symbol of fertility and good luck in many cultures; it is the national bird of Lithuania and Belarus, and it features prominently in the folklore of Poland, where a large share of the world population breeds.

Conservation

Populations declined during the 20th century due to habitat change, wetland drainage, and collisions with power lines, but conservation measures and reintroduction programmes have aided recovery, and the species is now listed as Least Concern by the IUCN.
