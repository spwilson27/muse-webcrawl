Bond valuation

Bond valuation
FieldFinance, fixed income
Key conceptPresent value of future cash flows
Related topicsYield curve, duration, coupon

Bond valuation is the process by which an investor arrives at an estimate of the theoretical fair value (or intrinsic worth) of a particular bond. As with any security or capital investment, the theoretical fair value of a bond is the present value of the stream of cash flows it is expected to generate. Hence, the value of a bond is obtained by discounting the bond's expected cash flows to the present using an appropriate discount rate.

Theoretical basis

As above, the fair price of a "straight bond" (a bond with no embedded options) is usually determined by discounting its expected cash flows at the appropriate discount rate. The formula commonly applied is as follows. For discussion on the selection of the appropriate discount rate, see below.

P = Σ C/(1+i)t + M/(1+i)n

where P is the price, C the coupon payment, i the discount rate (yield to maturity), M the value at maturity (par value), n the number of coupon payments, and t the time in years from now.

Bond price

If the bond is priced correctly, its price is exactly equal to the present value of its cash flows. Bonds are quoted in the market at prices that respond to changes in interest rates: when market rates rise, existing bond prices fall (because their fixed coupons are less attractive), and when rates fall, prices rise. A bond trading above its par value is said to trade at a premium; one trading below par trades at a discount.

Yield to maturity

The yield to maturity (YTM) is the discount rate at which the sum of all future cash flows from the bond equals its current price. It is the internal rate of return of the bond's cash flows and is the most commonly quoted measure of a bond's return, assuming the bond is held to maturity and all payments are made as scheduled.

Accrued interest

Between coupon payment dates, the buyer of a bond must compensate the seller for interest earned since the last coupon date. The quoted ("clean") price excludes this accrued interest; the settlement ("dirty") price includes it.

Sensitivity measures

Because bond prices are sensitive to interest-rate changes, investors use duration and convexity to measure and manage that risk. Macaulay duration is the weighted average time to receipt of cash flows; modified duration approximates the percentage price change for a one-percentage-point change in yield.
