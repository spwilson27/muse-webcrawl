Burgh

A burgh (pronounced roughly "burra") is an autonomous municipal corporation in Scotland and Northern England, usually a city, town, or toun in modern Scots, having been granted a royal charter. The word is cognate with borough and with German Burg, and burghs were the units of local government in Scotland from the 12th century until their abolition in 1975.

History

The first burghs were created by King David I of Scotland in the 12th century, beginning with places such as Berwick-upon-Tweed, Roxburgh, Edinburgh, and Stirling. Burghs were granted privileges including the right to hold markets, elect magistrates (a provost and baillies), and administer their own courts. Their inhabitants, the burgesses, held special trading rights, and merchants of a burgh typically enjoyed a monopoly of foreign trade within its bounds.

Types of burgh

  • Royal burghs — held directly from the Crown, with the greatest privileges; they sent representatives to the Parliament of Scotland.
  • Burghs of barony — held from a feudal superior (a baron or abbot), with more limited rights.
  • Burghs of regality — held from a lord of regality.
  • Police burghs — created under 19th-century legislation to handle policing, lighting, and cleansing in growing towns regardless of older status.

Abolition

Burghs were abolished as organs of local government by the Local Government (Scotland) Act 1973, effective 16 May 1975, and replaced by district councils and later by single-tier council areas. Many former burghs retain ceremonial provosts and community councils, and "royal burgh" survives as an honorary title.
