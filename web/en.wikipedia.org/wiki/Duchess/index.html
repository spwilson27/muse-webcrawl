Duchess

A duchess is a member of nobility who ranks directly below the monarch (or a prince or princess). Usually, the wife (spouse) of the duke holds this title, or it can be held suo jure by a woman who inherited the title in her own right. In European history, the term has been used for the female ruler of a duchy, as well as for the consort of a duke.

Usage

In the British peerage, the wife of a duke is styled "Her Grace, the Duchess of X", and a duchess in her own right — a rare occurrence, since most dukedoms descend in the male line — is likewise addressed as "Your Grace". A widow of a duke is known as a dowager duchess, or by her forename as "[Name], Duchess of X", to distinguish her from the wife of the current duke. Royal duchesses, such as the Duchess of Cambridge or the Duchess of Sussex, hold their titles through marriage to royal dukes and are styled "Her Royal Highness".

History

The title derives, like duke, from the Latin dux, a military leader. In the Frankish and later medieval worlds, duchesses could exercise considerable power as regents and rulers of duchies in their own right; notable examples include Matilda of Tuscany and Eleanor of Aquitaine, Duchess of Aquitaine in her own right before her marriages to the kings of France and England.

In other countries

Equivalent titles exist throughout European nobility: Herzogin in German, duquesa in Spanish and Portuguese, duchessa in Italian, hertuginde in Dutch, and hertiginna in Swedish. In modern republics the title survives as a courtesy or historical title without legal privilege.
