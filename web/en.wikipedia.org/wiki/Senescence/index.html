Senescence

Senescence (from Latin senescere, "to grow old") is the gradual deterioration of functional characteristics in living organisms. The word applies to cellular senescence — the loss of a cell's power of division and growth — or to the senescence of the whole organism, i.e. biological ageing. Organismal senescence involves an increase in mortality and/or a decrease in fecundity with increasing age, at least in the latter part of an organism's life cycle.

Cellular senescence

Leonard Hayflick demonstrated in the 1960s that normal human cells divide only a limited number of times in culture (the Hayflick limit), a limit now known to result from telomere shortening. Cells also enter senescence in response to DNA damage, oncogene activation, and oxidative stress. Senescent cells remain metabolically active and secrete inflammatory factors (the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP), which can promote wound healing and suppress tumours in the short term but drive tissue ageing and disease when such cells accumulate.

Evolutionary theories

Major explanations of ageing include mutation accumulation (Medawar), antagonistic pleiotropy (Williams, 1957) — genes beneficial early in life may be harmful late — and Kirkwood's disposable soma theory, which frames ageing as the result of limited investment in somatic maintenance in favour of reproduction.

Negligible senescence

Some organisms, such as hydra, certain tortoises, and the naked mole-rat, show negligible senescence, with mortality rates that do not measurably increase with age. Plants undergo forms of senescence too, including seasonal leaf senescence.
