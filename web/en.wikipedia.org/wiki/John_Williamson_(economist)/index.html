John Williamson (7 June 1937 – 11 April 2021) was a British-born economist who spent much of his career in Washington, D.C., at the Institute for International Economics (now the Peterson Institute). He is best known for coining the term "Washington Consensus" in 1989 and for his work on exchange-rate regimes, including the "crawling peg" and target-zone proposals.

Education and career

Williamson studied at the London School of Economics and received his PhD from Princeton University in 1963. He taught at the University of York and the University of Warwick, advised the UK Treasury and the International Monetary Fund, and worked in Brazil at the Pontifical Catholic University of Rio de Janeiro. In 1981 he joined the newly founded Institute for International Economics in Washington, where he remained a senior fellow for four decades.

The Washington Consensus

In a 1989 paper, "What Washington Means by Policy Reform," Williamson listed ten policy prescriptions — fiscal discipline, public spending priorities, tax reform, interest-rate and exchange-rate liberalisation, trade and foreign-investment liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, and secure property rights — that he believed commanded broad agreement in Washington for Latin American reform. The phrase "Washington Consensus" escaped his control, becoming a slogan for market-oriented "neoliberal" reform that Williamson himself often criticised as a distortion of his original, narrower list.

Later work

Williamson wrote extensively on exchange-rate arrangements for developing countries, high-income-country monetary coordination, and the economics of crises such as the 1997 Asian financial crisis. He died on 11 April 2021, aged 83.
