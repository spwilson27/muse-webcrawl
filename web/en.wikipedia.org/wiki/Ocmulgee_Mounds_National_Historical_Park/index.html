Ocmulgee Mounds National Historical Park (formerly Ocmulgee National Monument) in Macon, Georgia, United States preserves traces of over ten millennia of Southeastern Native American culture, including major earthworks built before 1000 CE by the South Appalachian Mississippian culture. These include the Great Temple and other ceremonial mounds, a burial mound, and defensive trenches.

History

People first settled the Macon Plateau around the end of the last ice age. The Mississippian culture built a chiefdom centre here around 900–1150 CE, constructing large platform mounds and a circular earth lodge whose original clay floor survives. Later, the Lamar culture occupied a village site a few miles downstream. The Muscogee (Creek) Nation, whose ancestors built the mounds, maintain a close association with the park; the Muscogee regard Ocmulgee as a sacred ancestral homeland.

Designations

The site was authorised as a national monument in 1934 and established in 1936. Large-scale excavations in the 1930s, employing hundreds of workers, were among the biggest archaeological projects in the United States. The park was redesignated a national historical park in 2019, and its area was expanded. The Ocmulgee Indigenous Celebration is held at the park each September.
