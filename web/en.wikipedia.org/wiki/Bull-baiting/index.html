Bull-baiting

Bull-baiting is a blood sport involving the setting of dogs upon a tethered bull. Popular in England from the medieval period until the 19th century, it was staged in pits and market places, with spectators betting on the outcome. The bull was typically tied by a rope to an iron stake, giving it a circle of movement of roughly 30 feet, while specially bred dogs — the ancestors of the Bulldog — were released one or two at a time to seize the bull by the nose and hold on.

History

Baiting was widespread at fairs and wakes, and in some towns was a customary obligation: in Stamford, the "bull running" was held annually for centuries. Butchers in several jurisdictions were required to have bulls baited before slaughter, based on the belief that baiting made the meat tender and wholesome; fines were imposed for selling the flesh of an unbaited bull.

The baiting dogs were bred low to the ground with undershot jaws and great tenacity, so they could grip the bull's nose and avoid being gored or tossed. A dog that held on was said to "pin" the bull, winning the stake for its owner.

Abolition

Opposition grew through the 18th and early 19th centuries. A bill to ban baiting was debated in Parliament in 1800 and narrowly defeated; William Windham famously defended the sports of the people against what he saw as puritanical interference. Reformers such as Richard Martin eventually succeeded with the Cruelty to Animals Act 1835, which prohibited bull-baiting, bear-baiting and cockfighting in England and Wales, effectively ending the practice. Variants persisted elsewhere, including in parts of South Asia and Latin America.

Legacy

The sport shaped several dog breeds, including the Bulldog, the Bull Terrier and the Staffordshire Bull Terrier, and left expressions in the language, such as the "bull ring" markets of towns like Birmingham and Banbury where baits were held.
