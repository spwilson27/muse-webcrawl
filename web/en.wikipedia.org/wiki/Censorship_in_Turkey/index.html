Censorship in Turkey

Access-blocking notices are a common sight for Turkish internet users.

Key lawsPress Law; Penal Code Articles 299, 301; Internet Law No. 5651; Anti-Terror Law
RegulatorsBTK (ICTA), RTÜK, Press Advertisement Institution
Notable blocksYouTube (2007–2010, 2014), Twitter (2014), Wikipedia (2017–2020)

Censorship in Turkey is regulated by domestic and international legislation, the latter in theory guaranteeing freedom of expression, but has historically been a significant constraint on press freedom. Journalists and international monitors describe an environment in which legal provisions against insulting the president, defaming state institutions, and disseminating "terrorist propaganda" are used against critical reporting, and in which most major media are owned by conglomerates close to the government.

Historical background

Press control in Turkey descends from late Ottoman censorship and continued through the early Republic. Military interventions in 1960, 1971, and 1980 brought direct media controls, and Article 301 of the Penal Code — criminalising insults to "Turkishness" and state institutions — was used against writers including Orhan Pamuk and Hrant Dink. Although EU accession reforms softened some provisions, restrictive laws remained.

After 2013 and the 2016 coup attempt

Coverage restrictions during the 2013 Gezi Park protests drew attention to self-censorship in mainstream media. Following the failed coup attempt of July 2016, a state of emergency saw over a hundred media outlets closed by decree and many journalists arrested; Turkey became the world's leading jailer of journalists in several annual counts by the Committee to Protect Journalists. The sale of the Doğan media group to a pro-government holding in 2018 consolidated ownership further. A 2022 "disinformation law" criminalised the spread of information deemed false concerning security and public order, drawing criticism from press-freedom groups.

Internet censorship

Under Law No. 5651, courts and the Information and Communication Technologies Authority (BTK) have blocked tens of thousands of websites. YouTube was blocked for extended periods beginning in 2007; Twitter and YouTube were blocked during the 2014 local elections; and Wikipedia was blocked from April 2017 until January 2020, when the Constitutional Court ruled the ban a violation of freedom of expression. A 2020 law required social media companies to appoint local representatives and remove content on demand.
