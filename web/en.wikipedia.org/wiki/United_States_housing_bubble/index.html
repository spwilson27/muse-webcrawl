United States housing bubble

The United States housing bubble was a real estate bubble affecting over half of the U.S. states. It peaked in early 2006, began to decline in 2006 and 2007, and precipitated the subprime mortgage crisis and the Great Recession.

Background

After the dot-com bubble burst around 2000, the Federal Reserve cut interest rates sharply, with the federal funds rate reaching 1% in 2003. Cheap credit, combined with lax lending standards, government housing policies, and speculation, drove a rapid increase in homeownership and house prices. The Case-Shiller home price index rose about 124% between 1997 and 2006.

Risky lending and securitization

Lenders issued large volumes of subprime and Alt-A mortgages, including interest-only, negative-amortization, and low-documentation loans. These mortgages were bundled into mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations and sold to investors worldwide; credit rating agencies assigned many of them AAA ratings. Financial institutions took on heavy leverage against these assets.

Collapse

As prices peaked and adjustable rates reset higher, delinquencies surged. By 2007 major subprime lenders failed; in 2008 the collapse of Lehman Brothers triggered a global financial crisis. U.S. home prices ultimately fell roughly 27% from their peak, millions of homes entered foreclosure, and the unemployment rate reached 10% in October 2009.

Aftermath

The crisis led to the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP), the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, and the creation of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.
