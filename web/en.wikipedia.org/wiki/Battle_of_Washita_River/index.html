The Battle of Washita River took place on 27 November 1868, when Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer led the U.S. 7th Cavalry in a dawn attack on the Southern Cheyenne village of Chief Black Kettle on the Washita River in present-day Oklahoma. It was the opening engagement of General Philip Sheridan's winter campaign of 1868–69 during the American Indian Wars, and remains controversial, often described as a massacre.

Background

Black Kettle was a leading peace chief who had survived the Sand Creek massacre of 1864 and had signed the Medicine Lodge Treaty. After raids on Kansas settlements in 1868 — carried out by other bands — Sheridan ordered a winter campaign against the Southern Plains tribes. Custer's scouts, including Osage trackers, followed a war party's trail through the snow to Black Kettle's village.

The attack

At dawn on 27 November the 7th Cavalry charged the sleeping village from four directions. Black Kettle and his wife were killed as they tried to escape across the river. Custer reported 103 Cheyenne warriors killed; Cheyenne accounts and later historians put total deaths lower but include a significant number of women and children. The soldiers captured 53 women and children and destroyed the village's lodges, supplies and pony herd — some 875 horses were shot.

Withdrawal and aftermath

Learning that further villages lay downstream and that a detachment under Major Joel Elliott had been cut off and killed, Custer withdrew after dark. The attack intimidated the Southern Cheyenne and other tribes into moving onto reservations, a key objective of the campaign. The site is now preserved as the Washita Battlefield National Historic Site.
