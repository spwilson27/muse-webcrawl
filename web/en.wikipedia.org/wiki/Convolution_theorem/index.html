Convolution theorem

In mathematics, the convolution theorem states that under suitable conditions the Fourier transform of a convolution of two functions is the pointwise product of their Fourier transforms. In other words, convolution in one domain equals pointwise multiplication in the other domain.

Statement

If f and g are functions with Fourier transforms F and G, then the Fourier transform of their convolution (f ∗ g) is the product F · G. Conversely, the Fourier transform of the product f · g is the convolution of F and G (up to scaling factors depending on the convention used). Analogous theorems hold for the Laplace transform, the discrete Fourier transform (where the convolution is circular), and other integral transforms.

Applications

The theorem is of great practical importance. In signal processing, the output of a linear time-invariant system is the convolution of the input with the system's impulse response; the theorem lets engineers instead multiply in the frequency domain. Computationally, large convolutions are evaluated efficiently by transforming with the fast Fourier transform, multiplying, and transforming back — the basis of fast multiplication algorithms for large integers and polynomials as well.
