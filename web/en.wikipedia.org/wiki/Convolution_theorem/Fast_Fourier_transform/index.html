Fast Fourier transform

A fast Fourier transform (FFT) is an algorithm that computes the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) of a sequence, or its inverse, far more efficiently than the naive definition. Direct evaluation of an n-point DFT costs O(n2) operations, whereas an FFT achieves O(n log n).

The Cooley–Tukey algorithm

The best-known FFT, published by James Cooley and John Tukey in 1965 (with antecedents going back to Gauss around 1805), recursively splits a DFT of composite size n = n1n2 into smaller DFTs, recombining them with "twiddle factors". The radix-2 case divides the sequence into its even- and odd-indexed parts — the famous butterfly pattern. Variants include prime-factor, split-radix and real-input algorithms, and libraries such as FFTW adaptively choose among them.

Applications

FFTs are among the most important algorithms in applied computing. Combined with the convolution theorem, they enable fast convolution, large-integer multiplication, and polynomial multiplication. They are fundamental to spectral analysis, digital signal processing, audio and image compression (JPEG, MP3), solving partial differential equations, and OFDM communications used in Wi-Fi and LTE.
