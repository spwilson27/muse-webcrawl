Fourier transform

The Fourier transform is a mathematical transform that decomposes a function of time (or space) into its constituent frequencies. Named after Jean-Baptiste Joseph Fourier, it generalizes the Fourier series of periodic functions to non-periodic signals, producing a continuous spectrum:

f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x) e−2πixξ dx.

Properties

The transform is invertible (under mild conditions), linear, and converts differentiation into multiplication by the frequency variable. Its central structural result is the convolution theorem: the Fourier transform of a convolution is the product of the individual transforms, and vice versa. Plancherel's theorem shows it preserves energy, and the uncertainty principle bounds how concentrated a function and its transform can simultaneously be.

Variants

Related transforms include the discrete Fourier transform for sampled data — computed by fast Fourier transform algorithms — the Laplace transform, and the short-time Fourier transform used for time-varying spectra.

Applications

Fourier analysis is foundational in signal processing, audio and image compression, solving differential equations, X-ray crystallography, spectroscopy, quantum mechanics (position–momentum duality), and probability theory via characteristic functions.
