Bilinear interpolation

In mathematics, bilinear interpolation is a method of multivariable interpolation on a regular rectilinear grid in two dimensions. It generalizes linear interpolation by performing it first in one direction and then in the other. Despite its name, the resulting interpolant is not linear in the coordinates but is a product of linear functions — a quadratic polynomial of the form a + bx + cy + dxy — and it reproduces the known values exactly at the four grid corners.

Bilinear interpolation on the unit square: the value at the red point is computed from the four corner values.

Method

Suppose the function values f are known at the four corners of a rectangle: Q11 = (x1, y1), Q12 = (x1, y2), Q21 = (x2, y1), and Q22 = (x2, y2). To estimate f at a point (x, y) inside the rectangle, one first linearly interpolates along x at the lower and upper edges, then linearly interpolates between those two results along y. The order of the two steps does not matter.

On the unit square the formula simplifies to a weighted average of the four corner values, with weights (1−x)(1−y), x(1−y), (1−x)y, and xy, which are the areas of the sub-rectangles opposite each corner.

Properties

The interpolated surface is continuous across cell boundaries, but its derivative is generally not: gradients can jump at grid lines. Bilinear interpolation preserves monotonicity roughly but can blur sharp features. Because it is cheap and simple, it is widely used in image scaling, texture mapping in computer graphics, digital terrain models, and resampling of gridded data such as weather fields.

Extensions

The same construction in three dimensions is trilinear interpolation, and the approach generalizes to n-linear interpolation on hyperrectangles. Smoother alternatives include bicubic interpolation, which uses derivatives or a larger stencil to achieve continuous gradients, and bilinear interpolation is also the basis of the bilinear transform used in digital filter design.
