Recusancy

Image placeholder: recusant households worshipped in private chapels.

Recusancy (from the Latin recusare, "to refuse") was the state of those who refused to attend Anglican services during the history of England, Wales and Scotland after the Protestant Reformation. The term was specifically applied to Roman Catholics, who were subject to fines, property restrictions and other penalties for refusing to conform to the established Church of England.

Legislation

The Act of Uniformity 1558 required attendance at Anglican services, and the Recusancy Act 1559 imposed a fine of twelve pence for each absence. Under Elizabeth I the penalties escalated: the 1581 act raised the fine to £20 per month, a ruinous sum, and later statutes restricted recusants' movement and property. Similar legislation applied in Ireland.

Recusant communities

Despite persecution, Catholic communities survived, particularly in Lancashire, Yorkshire and parts of the Midlands, often protected by landed gentry families. Priests trained abroad, at seminaries such as Douai, returned as missionaries and were sheltered in priest holes in recusant houses. Many recusant families maintained their faith for generations until the gradual relief acts of the late eighteenth century and Catholic Emancipation in 1829 removed most disabilities.
