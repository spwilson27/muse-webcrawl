District heating (also known as heat networks or teleheating) is a system for distributing heat generated in a centralized location through a system of insulated pipes for residential and commercial heating requirements such as space heating and water heating. The heat is often obtained from a cogeneration plant burning fossil fuels or biomass, but heat-only boiler stations, geothermal heating, heat pumps, and central solar heating are also used, as well as heat waste from factories and electrochemical processes.

District heating plants provide efficiency and better pollution control than localized boilers. According to some research, district heating with combined heat and power plants is the cheapest method of cutting carbon emissions and has one of the lowest carbon footprints of all fossil fuel generation plants.

History

Hot water heating systems circulated in the Roman baths and hypocausts, and Chaudes-Aigues in France operated a geothermal district heating system from the 14th century. The first commercial steam district heating system in the United States was launched in Lockport, New York, in 1877 by Birdsill Holly, regarded as the founder of modern district heating. Systems spread widely in northern and eastern Europe and the Soviet Union in the twentieth century.

Generations of technology

Systems are commonly classified into generations: first-generation steam systems; second-generation pressurised hot water above 100 °C; third-generation pre-insulated pipes with water below 100 °C, dominant in Scandinavia from the 1970s; and fourth-generation low-temperature systems (roughly 30–70 °C) designed to integrate large shares of renewable energy, surplus heat, and large heat pumps with low network losses.

Advantages and disadvantages

Central plants can exploit waste heat, use diverse fuels, and achieve economies of scale in emission control, while building operators avoid individual boilers. Against this stand the high capital cost of pipe networks, distribution heat losses, and the requirement of sufficient heat density, which generally confines economic operation to urban areas.
