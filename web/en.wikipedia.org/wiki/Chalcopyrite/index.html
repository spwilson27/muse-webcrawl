Chalcopyrite

Chalcopyrite (KAL-kə-PY-ryte) is a copper–iron sulfide mineral with the chemical formula CuFeS2. It is the most important ore of copper, accounting for the great majority of the world's copper production. Chalcopyrite has a brassy to golden-yellow colour and a metallic lustre, and it is often confused with gold and pyrite, though it is softer and more brittle than pyrite and develops an iridescent tarnish of blues, purples, and greens on exposure to air.

Occurrence

Chalcopyrite crystallises in the tetragonal system and typically occurs as massive aggregates or striated tetrahedral-like (sphenoidal) crystals. It forms in a wide range of geological settings: porphyry copper deposits (the world's largest source of copper), hydrothermal veins, contact metamorphic deposits, and as a primary mineral disseminated in igneous rocks. It is the primary copper mineral in the giant porphyry deposits of Chile, Peru, the southwestern United States, and Indonesia, and occurs with pyrite, bornite, chalcocite, quartz, and calcite.

The name derives from the Greek chalkos ("copper") and pyrite, reflecting its appearance as a copper-bearing "pyrites".

Copper extraction

Despite containing about 34.5% copper by weight in pure form, chalcopyrite is typically mined from low-grade ores (often under 1% Cu) in huge porphyry systems. The ore is crushed, ground, and concentrated by froth flotation, then smelted and refined. Because chalcopyrite is refractory, much research addresses hydrometallurgical leaching of chalcopyrite concentrates, whose surfaces tend to passivate during leaching.

Weathering

On exposure to air and water, chalcopyrite oxidises and can produce acid mine drainage and secondary copper minerals such as malachite, azurite, covellite, and chalcocite; the enrichment of copper below the water table in weathered deposits (supergene enrichment) historically yielded many rich orebodies.
