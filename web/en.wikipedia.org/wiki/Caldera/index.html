Caldera

A caldera is a large cauldron-like hollow that forms shortly after the emptying of a magma chamber in a volcanic eruption. When large volumes of magma are erupted over a short time, structural support for the rock above the magma chamber is gone.

TypeVolcanic landform

Formation

The ground surface then collapses downward, creating a depression. Although sometimes described as a crater, the feature is actually a type of sinkhole, as it is formed through subsidence and collapse rather than an explosion or impact.

Examples

Famous calderas include Yellowstone Caldera in Wyoming, Lake Toba in Sumatra — site of a supervolcanic eruption about 74,000 years ago — Santorini in Greece, and Crater Lake in Oregon, formed by the collapse of Mount Mazama. See also Volcano and Magma.

Caldera lakes and resurgence

Many calderas fill with water to form lakes. Magma may rise again, lifting the caldera floor in a resurgent dome. Some, like Yellowstone, remain volcanically active with geysers and hot springs.
