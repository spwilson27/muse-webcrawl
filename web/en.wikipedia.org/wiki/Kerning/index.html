Kerning

In typography, kerning is the process of adjusting the spacing between individual letter pairs (or other characters) to achieve a visually pleasing result. It reduces the space between certain pairs, such as "A" and "V," whose shapes create excessive openings, and may increase space between others. Kerning is distinct from tracking (letter-spacing), which adjusts spacing uniformly across a range of characters, and from leading, the spacing between lines.

History

The term derives from the French carne, "projecting part of a letter," via the metal-typesetting practice of cutting away part of a type's body so that one character could overlap the shoulder of its neighbour—the kern. In digital typography, kerning values are stored in the font itself as pairs of characters with adjustment values, usually in thousandths of an em, and are applied automatically by layout software using the font's kern table or the OpenType GPOS table. Modern fonts may contain hundreds or thousands of kern pairs.

Automatic and manual kerning

Most applications apply metric kerning, which uses the pairs built into the font by its designer, or optical kerning, which computes spacing from the shapes of the glyphs regardless of font data—useful when mixing fonts. Display type such as headlines, logos, and signage is often kerned manually ("visual kerning") because at large sizes the built-in pairs may look uneven. Poor kerning, sometimes jokingly called "keming," can produce awkward gaps or unintended readings, and deliberate fine kerning is a hallmark of professional typesetting.

Subtleties

Kerning interacts with punctuation, numerals, and case: pairs such as "F." or "W," typically need strong negative kerns, while combinations of vertical-stemmed capitals need none. Software implements kerning through the CSS properties font-kerning and letter-spacing, the TeX engine's font metrics, and similar mechanisms.
