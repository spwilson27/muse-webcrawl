Occipital bone

The occipital bone is a cranial bone at the back and base of the human skull. Trapezoidal in shape and curved on itself, it encloses the posterior part of the cranial cavity and contains the foramen magnum, the large oval opening through which the medulla oblongata passes to become the spinal cord.

Structure

The bone is conventionally divided into four parts arranged around the foramen magnum: the squamous part behind and above, forming the curved back of the skull; the basilar part in front, which joins the sphenoid bone; and the two lateral (condylar) parts on either side. On either side of the foramen magnum are the occipital condyles, smooth kidney-shaped surfaces that articulate with the atlas (the first cervical vertebra) to form the joint that nods the head.

On the external surface, the external occipital protuberance (inion) and the superior and inferior nuchal lines give attachment to neck muscles and ligaments, including the trapezius. Internally, the bone is hollowed by grooves for the dural venous sinuses — the transverse and sigmoid sinuses and the occipital sinus — and a cruciform eminence divides the internal surface into four fossae lodging the occipital lobes of the cerebrum above and the cerebellum below.

Development and articulation

The occipital bone ossifies from several centers, partly in membrane and partly in cartilage, and in infancy the four parts are separate bones united by cartilage; they fuse into one bone around the fourth to sixth year of life. Occasionally the squamous part remains separate as an interparietal (Inca) bone. The occipital bone articulates with the parietal bones at the lambdoid suture, with the temporal bones, the sphenoid, and the atlas.

Clinical notes

Fractures of the occipital bone may result from direct blows to the back of the head, and basal fractures can damage the lower cranial nerves passing nearby. The foramen magnum region is clinically important in Chiari malformations, in which cerebellar tissue descends through the opening.
