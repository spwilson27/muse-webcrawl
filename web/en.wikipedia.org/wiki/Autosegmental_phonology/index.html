Autosegmental phonology


Image placeholder: autosegmental representation

Autosegmental phonology is a framework of phonological analysis proposed by John Goldsmith in his 1976 MIT dissertation, Autosegmental Phonology. It departs from the strictly linear segment sequence of classical generative phonology by representing features such as tone on autonomous tiers, linked to segments by association lines.

Motivation

Goldsmith developed the theory to account for the behaviour of tone in African languages such as Igbo and Mende, where tones can remain stable when vowels are deleted, spread across syllables, or shift independently of the segments carrying them — phenomena difficult to express if tone is merely a feature of individual segments. The Well-formedness Condition governs how association lines may be drawn (for example, they may not cross).

Development

The framework was extended to vowel harmony, nasalisation and other features that spread across segment sequences, and to non-concatenative morphology, notably John McCarthy's 1979 analysis of Arabic root-and-pattern morphology. It prepared the ground for feature geometry and other multi-tiered representations, and remains influential in the analysis of tonal phenomena.
