Mudéjar art

RegionIberian Peninsula (Spain)
Period12th–17th centuries
MediaArchitecture, tilework, carpentry, plasterwork
UNESCOMudéjar Architecture of Aragon inscribed 1986

Mudéjar art is a style of ornamentation and architecture produced in the Christian kingdoms of medieval Iberia that incorporated Islamic artistic techniques, materials and decorative motifs. It takes its name from the Mudéjares, Muslims who remained in territories reconquered by Christian rulers. The style flourished from the 12th to the 17th century and is characterised by the use of brick, glazed ceramic tile, carved plaster and elaborate wooden ceilings worked by Muslim or Muslim-trained craftsmen for Christian patrons.

Characteristics

Mudéjar buildings blend Romanesque, Gothic or Renaissance structures with Islamic decoration: horseshoe and polylobed arches, sebka diaper patterns in brick, muqarnas vaulting, geometric tile mosaics (alicatado) and artesonado wooden coffered ceilings. Brick, cheap and versatile, became an expressive structural and decorative material, especially in Aragon and Castile.

The style is not a unified school but a family of regional practices united by the transfer of Islamic craft traditions into Christian service.

Examples

Outstanding examples include the Alcázar of Seville, rebuilt for Pedro I of Castile in the 1360s by craftsmen from Granada and Toledo; the synagogues of Toledo, Santa María la Blanca and El Tránsito; and the brick church towers of Teruel and Zaragoza in Aragon, whose Mudéjar architecture was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1986 and extended in 2001.

Mudéjar techniques later crossed the Atlantic, influencing colonial architecture in Latin America.

Historiography

The term Mudéjar as an art-historical category was coined by José Amador de los Ríos in 1859. Scholars debate the extent to which the style should be read as a symbol of interfaith coexistence (convivencia) or primarily as the product of Christian patronage employing subordinate Muslim labour.
