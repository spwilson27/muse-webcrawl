Nondisjunction

Nondisjunction is the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during cell division. The result is daughter cells with an abnormal number of chromosomes — aneuploidy — with one cell gaining a chromosome and another losing it. Nondisjunction can occur in meiosis I, meiosis II, or mitosis.

Mechanism

In meiosis I nondisjunction, homologous chromosomes fail to separate, so both homologs go to one pole; in meiosis II nondisjunction, sister chromatids fail to separate. Causes include defective spindle assembly checkpoint signalling, abnormal cohesion between sister chromatids (which deteriorates with maternal age in oocytes), and recombination errors. Mitotic nondisjunction after fertilisation produces a mosaic individual with two or more cell lines of different chromosome number.

Consequences

Most autosomal monosomies and many trisomies are lethal in early development, but some are viable. The best-known result of nondisjunction is Down syndrome, caused by trisomy 21. Other viable aneuploidies include trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome), trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome), and sex-chromosome conditions such as Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), Turner syndrome (45,X), trisomy X and 47,XYY. The risk of meiotic nondisjunction rises sharply with maternal age, which is the strongest known risk factor for Down syndrome.
