Elision

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Elision is the omission of one or more sounds — typically a vowel, consonant, or an entire syllable — from a word or phrase, producing a form that is easier or faster to pronounce. Elided forms are common in connected speech in many languages and are often marked in writing with an apostrophe, as in English I'm, don't, or the poetic o'er (over) and e'er (ever). The phenomenon is also known as contraction in its most familiar written form.

In phonetics

In fluent speech, speakers regularly drop unstressed sounds. English speakers commonly elide the schwa in words like family (pronounced "fam'ly") or history ("hist'ry"), and the /t/ in phrases like "next day". Such reductions are systematic rather than careless: they follow the phonotactic rules of the language and aid rhythmic regularity.

In writing and poetry

Poets elide syllables to fit the metre of a line; in classical Latin and Greek verse, elision of a final vowel before an initial vowel of the following word was a regular rule of scansion (in Latin, elisio or synaloepha). In French, elision is obligatory in writing: the final vowel of words such as le, je, and de is dropped before a word beginning with a vowel, producing l'homme, j'aime, and d'accord. Italian and Catalan use similar written elisions.

Types

  • Aphaeresis — loss of a sound at the beginning of a word ('cause for because).
  • Syncope — loss of a sound in the middle of a word (ev'ry for every).
  • Apocope — loss of a sound at the end of a word.

Related processes

Elision differs from assimilation, in which a sound changes to resemble a neighbouring one, and from liaison, in which a normally silent final consonant is pronounced before a following vowel. Together these sandhi processes shape the rhythm and flow of spoken language.
