The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), also known by its original name Rijndael, is a specification for the encryption of electronic data established by the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in 2001. It is a symmetric block cipher that replaced the older Data Encryption Standard (DES) and is now used worldwide to protect classified and commercial information, including in TLS, Wi-Fi (WPA2/WPA3), disk encryption and VPNs.

Selection process

In 1997 NIST announced an open competition to develop a successor to DES. Fifteen candidate algorithms were submitted; in October 2000 NIST selected Rijndael, designed by Belgian cryptographers Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen, praising its security, performance in both hardware and software, and flexibility. AES was published as FIPS PUB 197 in November 2001 and approved for protecting U.S. classified information up to Top Secret (with 192- or 256-bit keys) in 2003.

Description

AES operates on a fixed 128-bit block arranged as a 4×4 array of bytes called the state. Depending on key length it performs 10, 12 or 14 rounds, each consisting of four steps: SubBytes (a nonlinear byte substitution using an S-box derived from the multiplicative inverse in the finite field GF(28)), ShiftRows, MixColumns and AddRoundKey, where round keys are derived from the main key by a key schedule. The final round omits MixColumns.

Security

No practical attack against full AES is known; the best public results are marginal theoretical improvements over brute force (such as biclique attacks) that do not threaten real-world use. Most real compromises involve side channels or misuse of modes of operation rather than the cipher itself. Many processors (Intel AES-NI, ARM Cryptography Extensions) include dedicated AES instructions, making encryption very fast.
