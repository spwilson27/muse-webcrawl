Voyageurs

Voyageurs (French for "travellers") were French Canadians who engaged in the transporting of furs by canoe during the fur trade years in North America, primarily in the 17th, 18th and early 19th centuries. Employed by companies such as the North West Company and the Hudson's Bay Company, they paddled birchbark canoes along the lake and river routes between Montreal, Grand Portage and the Athabasca country.

Voyageurs in a canot du maître, the large freight canoe of the fur trade.

Life and work

Voyageurs were contracted as engagés, typically for one to three years. Their workday ran from before dawn to after dark, paddling at up to 55 strokes a minute and carrying two 90-pound packs across portages. The mangeurs de lard ("pork-eaters") worked the Montreal–Grand Portage run, while the hivernants ("winterers") travelled deep into the interior and wintered at trading posts.

Culture

Voyageur culture blended French and Indigenous practices; their songs — such as "Alouette" and "En roulant ma boule" — became staples of French-Canadian folk music. Distinctive dress included the woven ceinture fléchée sash, moccasins and a tuque.
