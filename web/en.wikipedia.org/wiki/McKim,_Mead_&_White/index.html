McKim, Mead & White

McKim, Mead & White
Industry: Architecture
Founded: 1879, New York City
Founders: Charles Follen McKim, William Rutherford Mead, Stanford White
Style: Beaux-Arts, American Renaissance

McKim, Mead & White was an American architectural firm based in New York City, the pre-eminent practice of the American Renaissance and Beaux-Arts era. It was founded in 1879 by Charles Follen McKim, William Rutherford Mead and Stanford White, all of whom had worked in the office of H. H. Richardson.

Work

The firm shaped the civic face of the United States around the turn of the twentieth century. Its major works include the Boston Public Library (1895), a "palace for the people" fronted by an Italian Renaissance palazzo facade; the campus plan and principal buildings of Columbia University, including Low Memorial Library; the original Pennsylvania Station in New York (1910), a vast Beaux-Arts terminus modelled on the Baths of Caracalla, whose demolition in 1963 galvanised the modern preservation movement; the Morgan Library; Rhode Island State House; and the Villard Houses in New York, which established the firm's reputation for shingle-style and Renaissance Revival domestic work.

The partners' roles were complementary: McKim was the firm's design conscience and classicist, White its brilliant, flamboyant decorator-designer — his 1906 murder by Harry Thaw over Evelyn Nesbit became one of the era's great scandals — and Mead the organiser who ran the office, then the largest architectural practice in the world.

Legacy

Though its neoclassical idiom fell from favour with the rise of modernism, the firm continued under successor partners into the mid-twentieth century. Its buildings remain touchstones of American civic architecture, and the loss of Pennsylvania Station is still cited as the catalyst for New York's Landmarks Preservation Law of 1965.
