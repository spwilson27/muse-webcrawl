Ubiquitin is a small (8.6 kDa) regulatory protein found in most tissues of eukaryotic organisms. It was discovered in 1975 by Gideon Goldstein and further characterised throughout the 1970s and 1980s. Ubiquitin is one of the most conserved proteins known: the human and yeast sequences differ by only three amino acids.

The addition of ubiquitin to a substrate protein is called ubiquitination (or ubiquitylation). It can affect proteins in many ways: it can mark them for destruction via the proteasome, alter their cellular location, affect their activity, and promote or prevent protein interactions.

Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination is carried out by a cascade of three enzymes: ubiquitin-activating enzymes (E1s), ubiquitin-conjugating enzymes (E2s), and ubiquitin ligases (E3s), of which humans have several hundred. Ubiquitin's C-terminal glycine forms an isopeptide bond with a lysine on the substrate. Further ubiquitins can be added to one of ubiquitin's own seven lysines, forming chains; chains linked through lysine 48 typically target the protein to the 26S proteasome for degradation, while lysine 63-linked chains serve signalling roles in DNA repair, endocytosis and inflammation.

Discovery and the Nobel Prize

The role of ubiquitin in targeted protein degradation was worked out by Aaron Ciechanover, Avram Hershko and Irwin Rose in the late 1970s and early 1980s; the three shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for the discovery of ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation".

Disease relevance

Defects in the ubiquitin–proteasome system are implicated in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases (in which ubiquitin-positive inclusions are a hallmark), and immune disorders. Proteasome inhibitors such as bortezomib are used in cancer therapy, and an entire class of drugs — targeted protein degraders and PROTACs — exploits the ubiquitin machinery to destroy disease-causing proteins.
