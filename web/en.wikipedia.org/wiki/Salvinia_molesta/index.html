Salvinia molesta

KingdomPlantae
OrderSalviniales
FamilySalviniaceae
GenusSalvinia
SpeciesS. molesta

Salvinia molesta, commonly known as giant salvinia or as kariba weed after it infested a large portion of Lake Kariba, is an aquatic fern native to south-eastern Brazil. It is a free-floating plant that reproduces vegetatively and can form dense mats on still water, doubling in biomass in a matter of days under favorable conditions. It is considered one of the world's worst invasive aquatic weeds and is included on the IUCN list of the 100 worst invasive alien species.

A mature mat of giant salvinia; the leaves bear water-repellent hairs tipped with eggbeater-like structures.

Description

The plant consists of horizontal stems bearing pairs of floating leaves and a submerged, root-like frond. The floating leaves change shape as the plant crowds: primary-stage plants have small, flat, oval leaves, while in dense mats the leaves fold and become tightly packed and keel-shaped. The upper leaf surface carries stiff hairs, each splitting into four branches joined at the tip like an eggbeater — a structure that creates the superhydrophobic "salvinia effect" studied for biomimetic applications. The species is a pentaploid hybrid that produces no viable spores and spreads entirely by fragmentation.

Invasion and impacts

Spread through the aquarium and water-garden trades, giant salvinia has invaded waterways in Africa, Asia, Australia, and the Americas. Its mats block sunlight, deplete oxygen, obstruct irrigation and transport, interfere with hydroelectric generation, and degrade habitat for fish and waterbirds. The infestation of Lake Kariba on the Zambezi in the late 1950s — which at its peak covered some 1,000 km² — gave the plant its common name "kariba weed."

Control

The most successful control has come from the biological control agent Cyrtobagous salviniae, a small weevil whose larvae feed on the plant's rhizomes and buds. Releases of the weevil dramatically cleared infestations at Lake Kariba, Lake Moondarra in Australia, and many other sites, and it is regarded as one of the classic successes of weed biological control. Mechanical harvesting and herbicides are used for smaller infestations but are costly and often impractical at large scales.
