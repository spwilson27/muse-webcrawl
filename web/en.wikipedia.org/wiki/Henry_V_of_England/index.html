Henry V (16 September 1386 – 31 August 1422), also called Henry of Monmouth, was King of England from 1413 until his death in 1422. Despite his relatively short reign, Henry's outstanding military successes in the Hundred Years' War against France made England one of the strongest military powers in Europe. Henry is known and celebrated as one of the greatest warrior-kings of medieval England, and is famously immortalized in Shakespeare's Henriad plays.

Early life

Henry was born at Monmouth Castle, the son of the future Henry IV and Mary de Bohun. He gained military experience young, fighting against Owain Glyndŵr's Welsh revolt and at the Battle of Shrewsbury in 1403, where he was struck in the face by an arrow and famously saved by a delicate extraction of the arrowhead. As Prince of Wales he clashed politically with his ailing father, but on his accession in March 1413 he moved quickly to reconcile former enemies, suppress the Lollard heresy, and unite the country behind a renewed war for the English claim to the French crown.

War in France

In 1415 Henry invaded France, captured Harfleur, and, with his army depleted by dysentery, marched for Calais. Intercepted near the village of Azincourt on 25 October 1415, his outnumbered force of English and Welsh longbowmen and dismounted men-at-arms won the stunning Battle of Agincourt, destroying much of the French nobility. Returning in 1417, Henry systematically conquered Normandy. The Treaty of Troyes (1420) recognised him as heir and regent of France; he married Catherine of Valois, daughter of Charles VI. At his death — probably of dysentery, at Vincennes in August 1422, two months before Charles VI — he left an infant son, Henry VI, heir to both kingdoms in the short-lived dual monarchy.

Legacy

Henry promoted the use of written English in government, and his reign strengthened royal administration and naval power. Shakespeare's portrayal — the wild Prince Hal reformed into the heroic king of the St Crispin's Day speech — fixed his image for posterity, though modern historians also note the ruthlessness of episodes such as the killing of French prisoners at Agincourt and the starvation of Rouen's expelled civilians.
