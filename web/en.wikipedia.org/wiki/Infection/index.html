Infection

An infection is the invasion of tissues by pathogens, their multiplication, and the reaction of host tissues to the infectious agent and the toxins they produce. An infectious disease, also known as a transmissible or communicable disease, is an illness resulting from an infection. Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites and prions.

Transmission and course

Infections may be transmitted directly between people (by droplets, contact or bodily fluids), through contaminated food, water or surfaces (fomites), or by vectors such as mosquitoes and ticks. After an incubation period, infection may cause symptoms — fever, inflammation, pain — or remain subclinical. The immune system's response determines whether the infection is cleared, becomes latent or chronic, or overwhelms the host; severe systemic infection can lead to sepsis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis relies on clinical signs, laboratory culture, microscopy, antigen and antibody tests, and molecular methods such as PCR. Treatment depends on the pathogen: antibiotics for bacteria, antivirals for some viruses, antifungals and antiparasitics for other agents, along with supportive care. Prevention includes vaccination, sanitation, hand hygiene, safe food handling and infection control in hospitals, where healthcare-associated infections remain a major problem. The rise of antimicrobial resistance is a growing global threat.

History

Infectious diseases were humanity's leading cause of death for most of history. The germ theory of disease, developed in the 19th century by figures such as Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch, revolutionised medicine; antibiotics and vaccines in the 20th century dramatically reduced infectious mortality, shifting attention in wealthy countries to chronic disease — though pandemics such as COVID-19 show the continuing danger.
