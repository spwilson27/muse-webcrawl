Medicalization

Medicalization is the process by which human conditions and problems come to be defined and treated as medical conditions, becoming the subject of medical study, diagnosis, prevention, or treatment. Examples historically discussed include alcoholism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, childbirth, menopause, sadness, and aging. The concept was developed by sociologists such as Irving Zola, Ivan Illich, and Peter Conrad, who analyzed how the expansion of medical jurisdiction ("medical imperialism") can recast social, moral, or political problems as individual pathologies.

Drivers

Conrad identified three key drivers of medicalization: the medical profession itself, social movements and patients' groups that seek legitimization of their conditions, and commercial interests — notably pharmaceutical companies marketing treatments for newly defined conditions (sometimes called "disease mongering"). Biotechnology, managed care, and consumer demand have shifted the engines of medicalization over time.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue medicalization can pathologize normal variation, encourage overtreatment, and depoliticize social problems. Conversely, medical recognition can reduce stigma and unlock research, care, and insurance coverage; demedicalization (as with homosexuality's removal from the DSM in 1973) can also occur.
