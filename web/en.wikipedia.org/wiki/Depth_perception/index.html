Depth perception

Depth perception is the ability to perceive the world in three dimensions (3D) and the distance of an object. The brain achieves depth perception mainly through binocular vision — combining the slightly different images from the two eyes — but also through a variety of monocular cues available to a single eye.

Binocular disparity: each eye sees a slightly different view, and the brain fuses them into a single three-dimensional percept.

Binocular cues

Retinal disparity

Because the eyes are separated by roughly 6 cm, each retina receives a slightly different image. The visual system computes the difference (disparity) between corresponding points to derive depth; this is the basis of stereopsis and of stereoscopic displays and 3D cinema.

Convergence

The eyes rotate inward to fixate near objects; the brain uses the muscular signals of convergence as a depth cue at close range.

Monocular cues

  • Relative size and texture gradient: identical objects appear smaller and textures finer with distance.
  • Occlusion (interposition): an object that blocks another is perceived as nearer.
  • Linear perspective: parallel lines appear to converge with distance.
  • Aerial perspective: distant objects look paler and bluer due to atmospheric scattering.
  • Motion parallax: near objects move faster across the visual field than far ones as the observer moves.
  • Accommodation: the lens changes shape to focus at different distances.

Development and disorders

Stereopsis emerges in infancy at around three to five months. Conditions such as strabismus (misaligned eyes) or amblyopia can impair depth perception, and a small percentage of people are effectively stereoblind, relying entirely on monocular cues.
