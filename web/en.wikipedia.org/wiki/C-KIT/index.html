C-KIT

C-KIT, also known as KIT, CD117, and proto-oncogene c-KIT, is a receptor tyrosine kinase protein that in humans is encoded by the KIT gene. It is the cell-surface receptor for stem cell factor (SCF, also called KIT ligand or steel factor). Multiple transcript variants encoding different isoforms have been found for this gene. KIT was first described by the German biochemist Axel Ullrich in 1987 as the cellular homolog of the feline sarcoma viral oncogene v-kit.

Function

KIT signaling is essential for the development and function of several cell lineages, including hematopoietic stem cells, mast cells, melanocytes, germ cells, and the interstitial cells of Cajal in the gastrointestinal tract. Binding of stem cell factor dimerizes the receptor, activating its intrinsic tyrosine kinase activity and downstream pathways such as PI3K/AKT, Ras/MAPK, and JAK/STAT, which regulate cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation.

Clinical significance

Activating mutations in KIT drive several cancers. Gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) commonly carry mutations in exon 11 or exon 9 of KIT, and the tyrosine kinase inhibitor imatinib (Gleevec), which blocks KIT, revolutionized their treatment. Mutations are also found in a subset of acute myeloid leukemias, systemic mastocytosis (where the D816V mutation is characteristic), seminomas, and some melanomas. Loss-of-function mutations in humans cause piebaldism, an autosomal dominant disorder of pigmentation.

Diagnostic use

Immunohistochemical staining for CD117 is a standard diagnostic marker for gastrointestinal stromal tumors and mast cell disease, helping to distinguish GIST from other spindle-cell tumors of the gut.
