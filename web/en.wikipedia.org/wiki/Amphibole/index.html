Amphibole

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hornblende, the most familiar amphibole, in a metamorphic rock.

Amphibole is a group of inosilicate (chain silicate) minerals, typically dark in color, that form prismatic or needle-like crystals. Amphiboles are double-chain silicates with the general formula based on (Si,Al)8O22 units, incorporating varying amounts of calcium, sodium, magnesium, iron, and hydroxyl. They are important constituents of both igneous and metamorphic rocks.

Structure and properties

The amphibole structure consists of two parallel silicate chains cross-linked into an "I-beam" arrangement, giving crystals two cleavages intersecting at about 56° and 124° — the key feature distinguishing them from the similar pyroxenes, whose cleavages meet near 90°. Most amphiboles are monoclinic; a few are orthorhombic. Hardness is about 5–6 on the Mohs scale.

Principal members

The most common amphibole is hornblende, abundant in diorite, andesite, and amphibolite. Others include the calcium amphiboles tremolite and actinolite, the sodic amphiboles glaucophane and riebeckite, anthophyllite, and cummingtonite–grunerite. Tremolite, actinolite, anthophyllite, grunerite (amosite), and riebeckite (crocidolite) can occur in fibrous form as varieties of asbestos.

Occurrence and significance

Amphiboles crystallize in intermediate igneous rocks and are characteristic of medium-grade metamorphism; the rock amphibolite is named for them, and the amphibolite facies is a standard metamorphic grade. Glaucophane signals high-pressure, low-temperature (blueschist) metamorphism in subduction zones.
