Delta-sigma modulation

Delta-sigma (ΔΣ) modulation is a method for encoding analogue signals into digital signals, used in analogue-to-digital and digital-to-analogue converters. It oversamples the signal at many times the Nyquist rate and uses noise shaping to push quantisation noise out of the band of interest, achieving high resolution with a low-resolution (often 1-bit) quantiser.

Principles

A delta-sigma modulator integrates the difference (delta) between the input and a feedback signal, then quantises the accumulated sum (sigma). The feedback loop shapes the spectrum of the quantisation error so that noise is small at low frequencies and large at high frequencies, where a digital low-pass filter can remove it. Decimation then reduces the high-rate bitstream to a lower sample rate at higher bit depth.

Applications

Delta-sigma ADCs dominate precision measurement, audio recording and instrumentation because of their excellent linearity and resolution, which can exceed 24 bits at audio bandwidths. Delta-sigma DACs are standard in CD players, audio codecs and the Super Audio CD format, whose Direct Stream Digital encoding is essentially a 1-bit delta-sigma bitstream.

History

The technique derives from delta modulation, a method for transmitting voice, and was developed in the early 1960s, with foundational work by Inose, Yasuda and Murakami at the University of Tokyo.
