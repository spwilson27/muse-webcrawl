Glycerides, more correctly known as acylglycerols, are esters formed from glycerol and fatty acids. Glycerol has three hydroxyl functional groups, which can be esterified with one, two, or three fatty acids to form mono-, di-, and triglycerides respectively.

Types

Monoglycerides (monoacylglycerols) have a single fatty acid chain and are widely used as emulsifiers in foods (for example glycerol monostearate, E471). Diglycerides (diacylglycerols) have two chains; 1,2-diacylglycerol is an important second messenger in cell signalling, activating protein kinase C. Triglycerides (triacylglycerols) have all three positions esterified and are the main constituents of body fat in humans and other animals, and of vegetable oils; they serve as the principal long-term energy store. Partial glycerides are produced industrially by glycerolysis of fats or by esterification of glycerol with fatty acids.

Metabolism

Dietary triglycerides are hydrolysed by pancreatic lipase to monoglycerides and free fatty acids for absorption, resynthesised in enterocytes, and transported in chylomicrons; in adipose tissue they are stored and later mobilised by lipolysis. Elevated blood triglyceride levels (hypertriglyceridemia) are a cardiovascular risk factor.
