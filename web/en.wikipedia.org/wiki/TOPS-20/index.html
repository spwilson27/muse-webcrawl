TOPS-20 was a proprietary operating system from Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC) for its DECSYSTEM-20 mainframe computers, which used the 36-bit PDP-10 architecture. Released in 1976, TOPS-20 was derived from the TENEX system developed at Bolt, Beranek and Newman (BBN) for the PDP-10, and it is often regarded as one of the most influential and best-loved time-sharing systems of its era. Within DEC, its internal codename referenced TENEX: TOPS-20 is jokingly said to stand for "Twenty's Operating System, Not TENEX — But Like It."

Features

TOPS-20 offered demand-paged virtual memory (based on TENEX's paging design), hierarchical file directories, file version numbers, and the EXEC command interpreter, celebrated for its user-friendliness: it provided command completion, context-sensitive help with the "?" key, and recognition of commands by unique abbreviations. Its system call interface (JSYS monitor calls) and PCL (Programmable Command Language) supported sophisticated scripting.

Networking and culture

DECSYSTEM-20s running TOPS-20 were prominent nodes on the early ARPANET and later the Internet, and the system supported popular mail programs such as MM. The system was used at universities and companies including Stanford, Columbia, and many research labs; its friendly interface shaped expectations later systems were judged against. Hackers from the TENEX/TOPS-20 community influenced early Internet culture and tools such as Emacs's native environment on ITS is often contrasted with TOPS-20's polished commercial offering.

Decline

DEC canceled its follow-on 36-bit "Jupiter" project in 1983 and steered customers to VAX/VMS; the last TOPS-20 release was version 7.1 in 1988, and support ended in the 1990s. Enthusiasts preserve the system today through emulators such as SIMH and KLH10 and through archives that keep historic DECSYSTEM-20 software runnable.
