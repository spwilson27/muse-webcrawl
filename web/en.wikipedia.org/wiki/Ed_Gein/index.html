Edward Theodore Gein (August 27, 1906 – July 26, 1984), also known as the Butcher of Plainfield and the Plainfield Ghoul, was an American murderer and grave robber. His crimes, committed around his hometown of Plainfield, Wisconsin, gained worldwide notoriety in 1957 after authorities discovered that he had exhumed corpses from local graveyards and fashioned keepsakes from their bones and skin.

Early life

Gein was raised on an isolated farm by a domineering, religiously fanatical mother, Augusta, who preached that all women were instruments of the devil. After the deaths of his father in 1940, his brother Henry in 1944 (in circumstances some considered suspicious), and his mother in 1945, Gein lived alone in the farmhouse, sealing off the rooms his mother had used.

Arrest and discoveries

Gein was arrested in November 1957 in connection with the disappearance of hardware store owner Bernice Worden, whose decapitated body was found on his property. Searching the house, police found numerous objects made from human remains, as well as the remains of tavern owner Mary Hogan, missing since 1954. Gein admitted robbing graves of recently buried women who resembled his mother.

Trial and death

Gein was initially found unfit to stand trial and was committed to a mental hospital. In 1968 he was tried and found guilty of Worden's murder but legally insane, and he spent the rest of his life in psychiatric institutions. He died of respiratory failure at the Mendota Mental Health Institute in 1984 and was buried in Plainfield Cemetery.

In popular culture

Gein's story inspired some of the most famous figures in horror fiction, including Norman Bates in Psycho, Leatherface in The Texas Chain Saw Massacre and Buffalo Bill in The Silence of the Lambs.
