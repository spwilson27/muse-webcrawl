Exploding head syndrome

Exploding head syndrome (EHS) is a benign parasomnia in which a person experiences a sudden loud imagined noise or sensation of an explosion in the head, typically when falling asleep or waking up. Episodes may be accompanied by a flash of light, a sense of fear, and myoclonic jerks, but characteristically involve no significant pain.

Symptoms and causes

The phenomenon was described in the medical literature as early as 1876 by Silas Weir Mitchell and named by neurologist J. M. S. Pearce in 1989. Its cause is not fully understood; a leading theory involves a brief dysfunction of the reticular formation of the brainstem during the transition between wakefulness and sleep, in which the usual sequential shutdown of auditory neurons misfires. Episodes are associated with stress and fatigue and are more commonly reported in women and in people over 50.

Diagnosis and treatment

EHS is classified in the International Classification of Sleep Disorders as a sleep-wake transition disorder. It is considered harmless, and treatment is usually limited to reassurance and good sleep hygiene, though medicines such as clomipramine or calcium channel blockers have been reported to help in some cases. Surveys suggest lifetime prevalence may be around 10 percent or more, though it is underreported because sufferers fear being thought mentally ill.
