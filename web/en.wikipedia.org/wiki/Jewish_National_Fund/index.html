The Jewish National Fund (JNF; Hebrew: קרן קיימת לישראל, Keren Kayemet LeYisrael, literally "Perpetual Fund for Israel") is a non-profit organisation founded in 1901 to buy and develop land in Ottoman Syria (later Mandatory Palestine, subsequently Israel and the Palestinian territories) for Jewish settlement. The JNF is famous for its forestry work: it states that it has planted over 240–250 million trees in Israel. It also built dams and reservoirs, developed large tracts of land, and created parks and recreation sites.

Land purchased by the JNF was held as the inalienable property of the Jewish people and leased on long terms. After 1948 the JNF became closely integrated with the Israeli state — a 1961 covenant made it the state's agent for much public land development and reforestation, and JNF land constitutes a significant share of the land administered by the Israel Land Authority. Its practices have been both celebrated (afforestation and land reclamation) and criticised (land acquisition disputes, demolitions in the Negev, and the leasing policies challenged in court).

History

The idea of a national land fund was proposed by Hermann Schapira at the First Zionist Congress (1897) and adopted at the Fifth Congress (1901), with Johann Kremenezky as its first director. Early purchases began in 1903–1905, including land at Hadera, Kfar Tabor, and in the Jezreel Valley (Marj ibn Amir), where settlements such as Merhavia and later Nahalal's region were founded. A 1920s land-purchase agreement with the Sursock family of Beirut transferred much of the fertile Jezreel Valley. By 1948 the JNF owned roughly half of all Jewish-owned land in Palestine (about 936,000 dunams), including tracts that formed central parts of the new state's territory.

The Blue Box

The JNF's blue collection box (pushke), introduced in 1904 at Kremenezky's initiative, became one of the most recognisable fundraising symbols of Zionism, placed in Jewish homes and schools worldwide for coins toward land purchase. JNF stamps, issued from 1902, were another iconic fundraising device, pasted into children's albums alongside tree certificates given for planting donations on occasions such as Tu BiShvat.

Afforestation

From early plantings at Hulda and Ben Shemen, the JNF expanded forestry under Yosef Weitz ("father of the forests"). It claims over 250 million trees planted, creating forests including Eshtaol, the Jerusalem Forest, Yatir Forest in the northern Negev (one of Israel's largest, planted to combat desertification), and memorial forests. Some forests were planted over or near sites of depopulated Palestinian villages from 1948, a practice historians and critics have documented and debated. Ecologists have also criticised the historical reliance on fast-growing Aleppo and brutia pines as monocultures vulnerable to fire and disease, and JNF policy in recent decades has shifted toward mixed, natural-woodland management.

KKL-JNF also built over 200 water reservoirs, recycling wastewater and capturing floodwater for agriculture, and manages national parks and recreation forests open to the public.

Land and the Israeli state

In 1950 and 1953, the JNF sold and transferred land arrangements with the young state and in 1961 signed a covenant under which the Israel Land Administration manages both state and JNF land uniformly, while JNF retains ownership of its holdings (about 13% of Israel's land). A 2004–2005 legal challenge (the Ka'adan-related JNF case) questioned the JNF's policy of leasing its land only to Jews; an Israel Land Authority land-swap mechanism was devised to equalise access on JNF land, amid continuing political and legal debate and periodic Knesset bills on the JNF's status.

Criticism

Criticism targets: land purchases that displaced tenant farmers before 1948; afforestation over village ruins; repeated demolition and planting operations in the unrecognised Bedouin village of al-Araqib in the Negev (destroyed and rebuilt over a hundred times since 2010); and the JNF's activities and land registration work in the West Bank and East Jerusalem through its subsidiary Himanuta. Supporters respond that the JNF's charter dedicates its land to the Jewish people, that its forests and parks are open to all visitors, and that it performs national functions in water, forestry, and land development.
