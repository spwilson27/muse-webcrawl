Vicksburg Campaign

The Vicksburg campaign was a series of maneuvers and battles in the Western Theater of the American Civil War directed against Vicksburg, Mississippi, a fortress city that dominated the last Confederate-controlled section of the Mississippi River. The Union Army of the Tennessee, commanded by Major General Ulysses S. Grant, gained control of the river by capturing the city, splitting the Confederacy in two, in what proved to be one of the turning points of the war.

Background

Vicksburg sat atop high bluffs overlooking a sharp bend of the Mississippi River, and Confederate artillery there made Union passage of the river nearly impossible. Grant spent the winter of 1862–63 attempting several approaches to the city, including canal digging, bayou expeditions, and attempts to bypass the fortress through the swampy terrain of Louisiana and Mississippi. Most of these efforts failed, earning criticism in the Northern press.

The campaign

In the spring of 1863 Grant marched his army down the west bank of the Mississippi, crossed the river below Vicksburg with the help of Rear Admiral David Dixon Porter's fleet, and then struck inland. After victories at Port Gibson, Raymond, Jackson, Champion Hill, and the Big Black River, Grant drove the Confederate army of Lieutenant General John C. Pemberton back into the defenses of Vicksburg. Two assaults on the works, on May 19 and May 22, were repulsed with heavy losses, and Grant settled into a siege.

Surrender and significance

The siege lasted 47 days. Cut off from supplies and relief, Pemberton surrendered his army of nearly 30,000 men on July 4, 1863 — one day after the Union victory at Gettysburg. With the fall of Port Hudson, Louisiana, five days later, the Union controlled the entire Mississippi River, and the Confederacy was split. The campaign is regarded as one of the most brilliant of the war and cemented Grant's reputation, leading to his appointment as general-in-chief of all Union armies in 1864.
