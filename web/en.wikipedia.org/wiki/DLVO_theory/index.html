DLVO theory

DLVO theory is the classical theory of the stability of colloidal dispersions, developed in the 1940s by Boris Derjaguin and Lev Landau and, independently, by Evert Verwey and Jan Theodoor Overbeek — from whose surnames the acronym is formed. It explains the aggregation of aqueous dispersions quantitatively by modelling the interaction between two approaching particles as the sum of attractive and repulsive forces.

Theory

In DLVO theory the total interaction energy between two particles is the sum of two contributions:

  • the attractive van der Waals force, which pulls particles together and dominates at very short range; and
  • the repulsive electrostatic force arising from the overlap of the electrical double layers surrounding charged particles in solution.

The resulting energy-versus-distance curve typically shows a deep primary minimum at contact (irreversible coagulation), an energy barrier whose height determines kinetic stability, and sometimes a shallow secondary minimum at larger separations, associated with reversible flocculation. Adding electrolyte compresses the double layer (screening), lowers the barrier and can trigger coagulation; this underlies the Schulze–Hardy rule, which states that the critical coagulation concentration falls sharply with increasing counter-ion valence.

Applications and limitations

DLVO theory is fundamental to colloid science and is applied to emulsions, paints, ceramic processing, water treatment and the stability of pharmaceutical suspensions. At very short separations, real systems also show non-DLVO forces — hydration, steric and hydrophobic effects — which the classical theory does not capture, and extensions of the theory are used to account for them.
