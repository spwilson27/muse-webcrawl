Nansen's Fram expedition of 1893–1896 was an attempt by the Norwegian explorer Fridtjof Nansen to reach the geographical North Pole by exploiting the natural east–west current of the Arctic Ocean. Nansen had the ship Fram purpose-built with a rounded hull that would be lifted, rather than crushed, by pack ice, and deliberately froze her into the ice off Siberia to drift across the polar basin.

The drift and the dash for the Pole

Fram was frozen in on 22 September 1893 and drifted with the transpolar current for nearly three years under Captain Otto Sverdrup. Realising the drift would not cross the Pole itself, Nansen left the ship in March 1895 with Hjalmar Johansen, dogs and kayaks, reaching 86°13.6′N on 7 April 1895 — a new Farthest North record — before turning back. After wintering in a stone hut on Franz Josef Land, the pair were picked up by a British expedition in 1896 and returned to Norway days before Fram herself emerged from the ice near Svalbard, with all hands safe.

Scientific results and legacy

The expedition's oceanographic measurements proved the Arctic Ocean is a deep ocean basin with no hidden northern continent, and established the transpolar drift. Fram went on to serve Sverdrup's Canadian Arctic expedition and Roald Amundsen's South Pole expedition, and is now preserved at the Fram Museum in Oslo.
