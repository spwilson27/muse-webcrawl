Lewis Henry Morgan (November 21, 1818 – December 17, 1881) was an American anthropologist and social theorist, one of the founders of modern anthropology. A Rochester lawyer by profession, he conducted pioneering ethnographic research among the Iroquois, particularly the Seneca, and developed influential — though now largely superseded — theories of kinship and social evolution.

Work

His League of the Ho-dé-no-sau-nee, or Iroquois (1851), produced with the help of the Seneca informant Ely Parker, is regarded as one of the first scientific ethnographies of a Native American people. In Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family (1871) he compared kinship terminologies worldwide and distinguished descriptive from classificatory systems. His most famous work, Ancient Society (1877), proposed that all societies pass through stages of "savagery" and "barbarism" to "civilization", driven by technological development; the scheme was taken up by Marx and Engels, the latter drawing on it for The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State (1884).

Legacy

Morgan's unilineal evolutionism was rejected by twentieth-century anthropology, but his empirical work on kinship and his recognition that social organisation could be studied systematically remain foundational to the discipline.
