Bromide

A bromide is a chemical compound containing a bromide ion or ligand with the chemical formula Br− — a bromine atom with an extra electron, i.e. bromine in the −1 oxidation state. Bromides are salts of hydrobromic acid and occur naturally in seawater and brine deposits.

Potassium bromide, a typical ionic bromide salt.

Occurrence and properties

The most common bromides are ionic salts such as sodium bromide and potassium bromide, white crystalline solids highly soluble in water. Silver bromide is unusual in being insoluble and highly light-sensitive, the property that made it the light-capturing material in photographic film and paper. Covalent bromides include hydrogen bromide gas and numerous organobromine compounds.

Uses

In the 19th and early 20th centuries potassium bromide was widely used as a sedative and anticonvulsant — it was the first effective treatment for epilepsy, introduced by Charles Locock in 1857 — but chronic use caused "bromism", a toxic syndrome, and it was displaced by safer drugs. It remains in veterinary use for canine epilepsy. Bromides are still used in flame retardants, drilling fluids (calcium bromide brines), photography and as intermediates in organic synthesis, for example Grignard reagents prepared from alkyl bromides.
