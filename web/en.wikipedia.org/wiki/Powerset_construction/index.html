Powerset construction

In the theory of computation, the powerset construction or subset construction is a standard method for converting a nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA) into a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) which recognizes the same formal language. It is important in theory because it establishes that NFAs recognize exactly the set of regular languages. It is also important in practice for generating parsers, lexical analysers, and pattern-matching engines from regular expressions.

The construction

Let an NFA have states Q, alphabet Σ, transition function δ, start state q0, and accept states F. The equivalent DFA has as its states the power set of Q — that is, each DFA state is a set of NFA states. Its transition on a symbol a from a set S is the union of δ(q, a) over all q in S, extended with ε-closures when the NFA has ε-transitions. The start state of the DFA is the ε-closure of {q0}, and a DFA state is accepting if it contains at least one state from F.

Complexity

Because a DFA state is a subset of the n NFA states, the constructed DFA can have up to 2n states, and this exponential blow-up is sometimes unavoidable: there are languages recognized by n-state NFAs whose smallest DFA requires 2n states. In practice, however, only a small fraction of subsets is often reachable, and implementations generate DFA states lazily, on demand.

History

The construction is associated with Michael O. Rabin and Dana Scott, whose 1959 paper "Finite Automata and Their Decision Problems" introduced it and proved the equivalence of NFAs and DFAs; the authors received the Turing Award in part for this work.
