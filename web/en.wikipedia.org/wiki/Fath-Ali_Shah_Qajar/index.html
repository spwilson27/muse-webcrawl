Fath-Ali Shah Qajar (Persian: فتحعلى‌شاه قاجار; 1769 – 23 October 1834) was the second Shah of Qajar Iran. He reigned from 17 June 1797 until his death on 23 October 1834. His reign saw the forced and disastrous loss of Iran's northern territories in the Caucasus to the Russian Empire following the Russo-Persian Wars, resulting in the Treaty of Gulistan (1813) and the Treaty of Turkmenchay (1828).

Early life and accession

Born Baba Khan in Damghan, he was the eldest son of Hossein Qoli Khan Qajar and nephew of Agha Mohammad Khan, the dynasty's founder, who made him governor of Fars and his heir. On his uncle's assassination in 1797 he marched to Tehran and was crowned, taking the name Fath-Ali Shah.

Reign

Fath-Ali Shah consolidated Qajar rule, ruling through his able crown prince Abbas Mirza in Azerbaijan and grand vizier Mirza Bozorg Qa'em-Maqam. In foreign affairs Iran became a pawn in the Anglo-Russian "Great Game": France's Treaty of Finckenstein (1807) briefly promised support before Napoleon made peace with Russia at Tilsit, after which Iran turned to Britain.

Two wars with Russia ended catastrophically. The Treaty of Gulistan (1813) ceded most of Iran's Caucasian khanates; the renewed war of 1826–28, launched to recover them, ended with the Treaty of Turkmenchay (1828), which ceded the remaining territories north of the Aras River, imposed a heavy indemnity and granted Russia capitulatory rights. In 1833 his designated heir Abbas Mirza predeceased him; on Fath-Ali Shah's death in 1834 he was succeeded by Abbas Mirza's son, Mohammad Shah, with British and Russian support.

Court and legacy

Fath-Ali Shah is famed for his splendid court, his enormously long beard, deep wasp-waist, and his patronage of painting — the "Qajar style" of life-size royal portraiture — and of literature. He fathered a very large number of children (over a hundred sons are recorded), many of whom governed provinces. Relics of his reign include the Peacock Throne in the Golestan Palace and numerous rock reliefs in the style of ancient Persian kings.
