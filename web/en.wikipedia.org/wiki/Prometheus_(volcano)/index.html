Prometheus (volcano)

Prometheus is an active volcano on Jupiter's moon Io, located on Io's anti-Jupiter hemisphere at approximately 1.52° S, 153.94° W. It is one of the most closely observed extraterrestrial volcanoes, famous for its long-lived eruption plume and for a dramatic migration of its main eruptive vent observed between the Voyager encounters of 1979 and the Galileo mission of the late 1990s.

The Prometheus plume seen by the Galileo spacecraft

Discovery

Prometheus was one of the volcanoes whose plumes were imaged by Voyager 1 in March 1979, when the spacecraft discovered active volcanism on Io — the first active volcano observed beyond Earth. The plume, resembling an umbrella some 80 kilometres high, made Prometheus (nicknamed "the volcano with the plume") one of the emblematic features of the moon.

The moving vent

When the Galileo orbiter imaged Prometheus in 1996–1997, scientists found that the bright plume source had shifted roughly 75–95 kilometres west of its 1979 position. The explanation is that the lava flows from the original vent had advanced across a plain covered with sulfur dioxide frost; as hot lava spread over new frost fields, it vaporised the frost and generated a new plume at the flow front, while the original 1979 site cooled and developed a dark caldera-like feature. Prometheus thus became the type example of a "plume at the flow front," in which the visible eruption column is produced not directly above the vent but where lava interacts with volatile surface deposits.

Characteristics

The Prometheus flow field extends for more than 100 kilometres, among the largest on Io. Its plume consists largely of sulfur dioxide gas and dust lofted where lava crosses frost; it is optically thinner than the giant Pele-type plumes and is sometimes called a "Prometheus-type" or stealth plume. Near-infrared observations show the volcano has remained thermally active for decades, making it one of Io's most persistently active centres.
