Honorific speech

Honorific titles and verb forms encode the social relationship between speaker and listener.

Honorific speech is a style of speaking that encodes social distance, relative rank, or respect toward the addressee, the subject of an utterance, or an absent third party. Languages with elaborate honorific systems grammaticalize deference in pronouns, verb conjugations, vocabulary, and forms of address, so that speakers are obliged to select a style appropriate to the situation whenever they speak.

Honorific systems are especially developed in East and Southeast Asian languages. Japanese keigo distinguishes respectful, humble, and polite forms, while Korean speech levels mark formality and deference through sentence endings. Javanese has distinct speech registers, and Thai employs a special vocabulary, rachasap, for addressing royalty. Honorifics also appear in European languages in the T–V distinction of pronouns, such as French tu and vous.

Types of honorification

Linguists distinguish several dimensions of honorification. Referent honorifics elevate or humble a person spoken about, often through special verb forms. Addressee honorifics mark politeness toward the listener regardless of the subject. Avoidance speech, sometimes called a "mother-in-law language", uses a substitute vocabulary in the presence of taboo relatives, as in some Australian Aboriginal languages.

Social function

Honorific choices signal and negotiate hierarchy, intimacy, and solidarity. Using too familiar a form can insult; using too distant a form can seem cold or sarcastic. Because the appropriate style depends on age, status, occupation, and setting, honorific competence is a central part of pragmatic fluency for learners of languages such as Japanese and Korean.
