Vazha-Pshavela (Georgian: ვაჟა-ფშაველა; 26 July 1861 – 10 July 1915), born Luka Razikashvili (ლუკა რაზიკაშვილი), was a Georgian poet and writer, widely regarded as one of the greatest figures of Georgian literature. His pen name derives from his native region: "Vazha" is a Pshavian dialect word for a young man or "lad", and "Pshavela" means "of Pshavi".

Life

Vazha-Pshavela was born in the mountain village of Chargali in Pshavi, in the Caucasus highlands of eastern Georgia. The son of a village priest, he was educated at the Telavi theological school and the Gori teachers' seminary. After a brief period of study in St. Petersburg, he returned to Georgia, where he lived most of his life as a farmer, shepherd, and hunter in his native mountains, writing poetry steeped in the folklore, pagan beliefs, and customary law of the Georgian highlanders.

Works

His major narrative poems include Aluda Ketelauri (1888), Host and Guest (1893), and the philosophical epic The Snake-Eater (Gvelis Mchameli, 1901), in which the hero Mindia gains the ability to understand the language of nature. Vazha-Pshavela also wrote short stories, dramas, and essays. His works explore the conflict between the individual and communal tradition, the relationship between humanity and nature, and questions of freedom and morality.

Legacy

Vazha-Pshavela's poetry, translated into many languages, is considered the pinnacle of 19th-century Georgian romantic and philosophical verse. His house-museum in Chargali, films based on his poems, and numerous streets and institutions in Georgia bear his name.
