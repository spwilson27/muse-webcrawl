Wagenburg

A Wagenburg (German for "wagon fort"), also known as a laager or wagon fort, is a temporary fortification made of wagons arranged in a rectangle, circle, or crescent and chained together to form a defensive perimeter. The tactic was famously systematised by the Hussites of Bohemia in the 15th century under Jan Žižka, whose war wagons (vozová hradba) crewed by infantry with handguns, crossbows, flails, and light cannon repeatedly defeated armoured crusader cavalry.

History

Early uses

Wagon laagers are ancient: Chinese, Gothic, and steppe armies used baggage wagons defensively, and Gothic forces employed them at Adrianople in 378 AD. Hungarian and German armies used the Wagenburg on the eastern frontiers in the later Middle Ages.

Hussite Wars

During the Hussite Wars (1419–1434), Jan Žižka developed the war wagon into an offensive-defensive system. A standard wagon carried a crew of about 18–22 men — handgunners, crossbowmen, halberdiers and flailmen, and drivers — protected by heavy wooden sides and large shields (pavises) filling the gaps between wheels. Hundreds of wagons formed a mobile fortress that cavalry could not break; when the enemy was disordered, Hussite infantry counter-attacked. Victories at Sudoměř (1420), Vítkov Hill (1420), and Domažlice (1431) made the tactic famous across Europe.

Later use

The Wagenburg spread to Hungarian armies under Matthias Corvinus, to the Muscovite gulay-gorod (a portable wooden wall on wheels), and to Cossack and Ottoman warfare. In the 19th century, Boer laagers in southern Africa and wagon circles of American pioneers were direct descendants of the same principle, as were British Army laagers in the Zulu War of 1879. The tactic faded as field artillery and mobile firepower made static wagon forts untenable.
