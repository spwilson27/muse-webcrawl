Pertussis

Pertussis, also known as whooping cough, is a highly contagious bacterial disease of the respiratory tract caused by Bordetella pertussis. After an incubation period of about a week to ten days, it begins like a common cold (the catarrhal stage) before progressing to severe hacking coughs in paroxysms, followed by a high-pitched intake of breath that sounds like "whoop". Coughing may cause vomiting, exhaustion, and in infants apnoea and cyanosis; the illness can last about ten weeks — the "100-day cough".

Course and complications

The disease proceeds through catarrhal, paroxysmal and convalescent stages. Complications include pneumonia, seizures, encephalopathy, pneumothorax and rib fractures from violent coughing. It is most dangerous in infants too young to be fully vaccinated, who account for most deaths; worldwide, pertussis still causes tens of thousands of deaths annually, mostly in low-income countries.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on nasopharyngeal culture or PCR, supplemented by serology in later illness. Macrolide antibiotics such as azithromycin reduce infectiousness if given early and are recommended for close contacts at high risk. Otherwise treatment is supportive.

Prevention and resurgence

Whole-cell pertussis vaccine, introduced in the 1940s, slashed incidence, but was largely replaced in high-income countries by acellular vaccines (DTaP in childhood, Tdap boosters) because of side effects. Acellular vaccine protection wanes faster, and pertussis has resurged in many countries since the 2000s, prompting recommendations for vaccination during pregnancy to protect newborns.
