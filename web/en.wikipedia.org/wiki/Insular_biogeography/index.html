Insular biogeography

Insular biogeography, or island biogeography, is a field within biogeography that examines the characteristics and factors that affect the species richness and diversification of isolated natural communities — islands and island-like habitats surrounded by dissimilar ecosystems.

Theory

The equilibrium theory of island biogeography, proposed by Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson in 1963 and expanded in their 1967 monograph, holds that species richness on an island reflects a balance between immigration of new species and extinction of existing ones. Larger islands have more species because extinction rates are lower, and islands nearer a mainland source have more species because immigration rates are higher. The result is a species–area relationship found across many archipelagos.

Applications

The theory has been applied to habitat fragments, mountaintops, lakes and nature reserves as habitat "islands", influencing conservation planning and the SLOSS debate (single large or several small reserves). It is also used, with the general dynamic model, to explain diversification over island ontogeny.
