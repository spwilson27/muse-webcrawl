Phenol

Phenol (also known as carbolic acid, phenolic acid, or benzenol) is an aromatic organic compound with the molecular formula C6H5OH. It is a white crystalline solid that is volatile. The molecule consists of a phenyl group (−C6H5) bonded to a hydroxy group (−OH). Mildly acidic, it requires careful handling because it can cause chemical burns.

Phenol was first extracted from coal tar, but today is produced on a large scale (about 7 billion kg/year) from petroleum-derived feedstocks. It is an important industrial commodity as a precursor to many materials and useful compounds, including plastics, Bakelite, nylon, detergents, and numerous pharmaceutical drugs.

History

Phenol was discovered in 1834 by Friedlieb Ferdinand Runge, who extracted it (in impure form) from coal tar and called it Karbolsäure ("coal-oil acid" or carbolic acid). The French chemist Auguste Laurent obtained it in pure form in 1841 and derived its composition. In 1867, the British surgeon Joseph Lister pioneered the use of phenol (carbolic acid) as an antiseptic in surgery, dramatically reducing post-operative infection and laying the foundations of antiseptic surgery.

Properties

Phenol is appreciably soluble in water, with about 8.3 g dissolving in 100 mL. It is a weak acid, roughly a million times more acidic than water, because the phenoxide anion is stabilised by resonance delocalisation of the negative charge over the aromatic ring. Unlike aliphatic alcohols, phenol reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide to form sodium phenoxide. Phenol undergoes electrophilic aromatic substitution much more readily than benzene itself, and is readily oxidised, turning pink or red on exposure to air.

Production

Most phenol is produced by the cumene process, developed in the 1940s, in which benzene and propene are converted to cumene (isopropylbenzene), which is oxidised to cumene hydroperoxide and then cleaved with acid to give phenol and acetone as a valuable co-product. Because of the demand for acetone, the cumene process accounts for the great majority of world phenol production.

Uses

The largest use of phenol is in the production of phenolic resins such as Bakelite, one of the first synthetic plastics, made by condensing phenol with formaldehyde. Phenol is also converted to cyclohexanone and cyclohexanol, precursors of nylon; to bisphenol A, used in polycarbonates and epoxy resins; and to picric acid, detergents, herbicides, and drugs such as aspirin. Dilute solutions were historically used as an antiseptic and are still used in some throat sprays and oral analgesics.

Health effects

Phenol is toxic and corrosive: it causes chemical burns on contact, whitens and numbs the skin, and can be absorbed through the skin with systemic effects on the central nervous system, heart, liver, and kidneys. Concentrated solutions are handled with protective equipment, and contamination of a large area of skin can be fatal. It is also an environmental pollutant that is harmful to aquatic organisms, though it is biodegradable.
