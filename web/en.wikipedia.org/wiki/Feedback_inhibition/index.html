Feedback inhibition

Feedback inhibition (also called end-product inhibition) is a mechanism of metabolic regulation in which the final product of a biochemical pathway inhibits an enzyme that acts earlier in the pathway — typically the first committed step — thereby preventing overproduction when the product is abundant. It is a classic example of negative feedback in metabolism and a major form of allosteric regulation.

Mechanism

The end product binds to an allosteric site on the regulatory enzyme — distinct from the active site — changing the enzyme's shape and reducing its activity. Because the inhibition is reversible and non-competitive, production resumes quickly once product levels fall. The classic case, worked out in the 1950s in Escherichia coli, is the inhibition of aspartate carbamoyltransferase (ATCase) by cytidine triphosphate (CTP), the end product of pyrimidine biosynthesis; ATCase is also activated by ATP, balancing purine and pyrimidine production.

Examples

  • Cholesterol synthesis: intermediates and related steroids restrain HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme (also the target of statin drugs, which mimic this control).
  • Amino-acid pathways: isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase in its own synthesis pathway in bacteria.
  • Glycolysis: ATP and citrate inhibit phosphofructokinase-1, signalling energy abundance.

Significance

Feedback inhibition gives cells fast, fine-grained control of metabolism on a timescale of seconds, complementing slower regulation by gene expression (such as operon repression). Its discovery helped establish the concept of allostery in biochemistry.
