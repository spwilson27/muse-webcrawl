Social choice theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Social choice theory, or social choice, is the study of collective decision processes and procedures — voting rules, constitutions and aggregation mechanisms — analysed through formal models of individual preferences. Its modern founding text is Kenneth Arrow's Social Choice and Individual Values (1951) and Arrow's impossibility theorem, which showed that no ranked aggregation rule can satisfy a short list of fairness conditions simultaneously; its prehistory runs through Condorcet and Borda in the eighteenth century and the medieval and Renaissance theory of electoral methods.

The field's results structure much of political science and welfare economics: majority cycles (Condorcet paradox), agenda control (McKelvey), strategy-proofness limits (Gibbard–Satterthwaite), May's theorem characterising majority rule for two candidates, median-voter results, the theory of apportionment, and Sen's widening of the framework to rights and capability (the liberal paradox, 1970; Collective Choice and Social Welfare). Design questions — which electoral system, which committee rule, which auction or matching mechanism (mechanism design is its engineering cousin) — are social choice under other names.
