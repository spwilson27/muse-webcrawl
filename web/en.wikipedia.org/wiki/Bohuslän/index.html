Bohuslän (Swedish pronunciation: [ˈbûːhʉːsˌlɛːn]) is a Swedish province in Götaland, on the northernmost part of the country's west coast. It is bordered by Dalsland to the northeast, Västergötland to the southeast, the Skagerrak arm of the North Sea to the west, and the county of Østfold, in Norway, to the north.

Bohuslän is known for its rocky coastline of smooth granite skerries, its archipelago, and its fishing communities, which grew rich during the great herring periods of the 18th and 19th centuries. Popular summer resorts include Smögen, Lysekil, Fjällbacka, Grebbestad and Marstrand.

History

Bohuslän belonged to Norway from around the 9th century until the Treaty of Roskilde in 1658, when it was ceded to Sweden together with the rest of the Norwegian province of Båhuslen. The name derives from the medieval Bohus Fortress near present-day Kungälv. The region is rich in prehistoric remains, including the Bronze Age rock carvings at Tanum, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and thousands of stone-age and Viking-age monuments.

The herring fisheries periodically transformed the coast: during the great herring period of 1747–1809, hundreds of salting houses and train-oil cookeries lined the shore. Later, stone quarrying of Bohus granite became a major industry, and in the 20th century petrochemical plants were established around Stenungsund.

Geography

The terrain is a fissure-valley landscape of bare, ice-polished granite rising to about 220 metres at Björnerödspiggen. Sweden's only threshold fjord, Gullmarsfjorden, cuts into the coast near Lysekil, and the Koster Islands off Strömstad form Sweden's first marine national park, Kosterhavet, established in 2009.
