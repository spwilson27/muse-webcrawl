The Birman, also called the Sacred Cat of Burma, is a domestic cat breed. The Birman is a long-haired, colour-pointed cat distinguished by a silky coat, deep blue eyes, and contrasting white "gloves" on each paw. The breed name is derived from Birmanie, the French form of Burma. The Birman breed was first recognized in France by the Cat Club de France in 1925, then in England by the Governing Council of the Cat Fancy (GCCF) in 1966, and in the United States by the Cat Fanciers' Association (CFA) in 1967.

History

Breed legend holds that Birmans were the sacred temple cats of Burma (Myanmar), companions to Kittah priests, and that the breed's colouring came from the goddess Tsun-Kyan-Kse. Historically, the modern breed was developed in France: a pair of cats was reportedly shipped from Burma to France in 1919, and although the male died en route, the pregnant female survived and founded the European population. The breed nearly vanished during the Second World War and was rebuilt from very few survivors, possibly with outcrosses to longhaired breeds.

Appearance

Birmans are medium to large, stocky cats with a silky, medium-long coat that lacks a dense undercoat and resists matting. The coat is pointed — darker on the ears, face, legs and tail — in colours including seal, blue, chocolate, lilac, red and cream, in solid, tabby (lynx) and tortie patterns. The defining features are the four white paws ("gloves"), with white extending up the back of the hind legs in "laces", round deep-blue eyes, and a strong, slightly Roman nose.

Temperament and health

Birmans are known for a gentle, people-oriented temperament: affectionate and playful without being overly demanding. The breed is generally healthy, with a lifespan commonly in the mid-teens; reported concerns include hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and a hereditary kidney disease in some lines. Kittens are born white, developing point colour in the first weeks of life.
