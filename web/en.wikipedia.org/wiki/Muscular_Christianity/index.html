Muscular Christianity

Muscular Christianity is a religious movement that originated in England in the mid-19th century in the Church of England and developed a philosophy that physical strength and health are vital to the Christian because the body is the temple of the Holy Spirit. It valued manliness, the moral and physical beauty of athleticism, teamwork, discipline, self-sacrifice, and the expulsion of effeminacy. It was promoted by figures such as Charles Kingsley and Thomas Hughes, whose novel Tom Brown's School Days (1857) embodied its ideals. The movement spread widely through public schools, the YMCA, and organisations such as the Boy Scouts.

Origins

The term "muscular Christian" was first used by the lawyer T. C. Sandars in the Saturday Review in 1857, in a review of Kingsley's novel Two Years Ago; Kingsley at first disliked the phrase but it was rapidly adopted. Muscular Christianity combined Christian commitment with an enthusiasm for outdoor sport and physical culture, partly as a response to fears that Victorian Christianity had become effeminate and over-intellectualised.

Influence

The philosophy had a major influence on British public school education, codified sport, and empire-era ideals of character. In the United States it was closely associated with the YMCA, through which sports such as basketball and volleyball were created, and with college athletics. Churches built gymnasiums, and organisations including the Men and Religion Forward Movement promoted physical culture as moral reform. Muscular Christianity also contributed to the founding ethos of the Boy Scouts under Robert Baden-Powell, himself an admirer of Hughes's work.

Legacy

Later critics have regarded muscular Christianity with ambivalence, linking it to militarism and imperial masculinity, while its emphasis on sport as character-building remains deeply embedded in Anglophone school and youth culture. Related movements appeared in other countries, including the German Catholic and Protestant youth movements and the Sokol movement in Slavic countries.
