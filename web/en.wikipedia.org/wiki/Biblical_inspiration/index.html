Biblical inspiration

Biblical inspiration is the doctrine in Christian theology that the human writers and canonizers of the Bible were led by God with the result that their writings may be designated in some sense the word of God. This belief is traditionally associated with concepts of the biblical infallibility and internal consistency of the Bible.

[Image: Illuminated manuscript page depicting a biblical author writing under divine inspiration]

Biblical basis

The key text is 2 Timothy 3:16, which in the original Greek describes Scripture as theopneustos, "God-breathed." Second Peter 1:21 likewise says that "prophecy never had its origin in the human will, but prophets, though human, spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit."

Models of inspiration

Theologians have proposed several models. Verbal plenary inspiration holds that God inspired every word of the whole Bible. Dictation theory portrays the human authors as secretaries recording God's words. Dynamic inspiration holds that God inspired the thoughts of the authors, who expressed them in their own words and styles. Neo-orthodox views, associated with Karl Barth, treat the Bible as a human witness that becomes the Word of God in the event of encounter. Limited inspiration restricts inerrancy to matters of faith and practice.

Debates

The rise of biblical criticism in the Enlightenment challenged traditional accounts, prompting the fundamentalist–modernist controversy of the early twentieth century. The 1978 Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy articulated a widely cited evangelical position, while the Catholic Church, in the Second Vatican Council's Dei Verbum (1965), taught that Scripture teaches "solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation."
