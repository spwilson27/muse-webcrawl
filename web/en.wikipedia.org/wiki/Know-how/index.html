Know-how

Know-how is the practical, often tacit, skill of performing a task effectively.

Know-how (or procedural knowledge) is a term for practical knowledge on how to accomplish something, as opposed to "know-what" (facts), "know-why" (science), or "know-who" (communication). It is the knowledge of how to perform a task or function, often accumulated through experience. In philosophy, it corresponds to the distinction drawn by Gilbert Ryle between knowing how and knowing that: riding a bicycle, for example, is a skill one can possess without being able to articulate the physics involved.

Tacit knowledge

Much know-how is tacit knowledge — Michael Polanyi's dictum "we can know more than we can tell" captures the idea that skilled performance rests on cues and rules the performer cannot fully specify. This tacitness makes know-how difficult to transfer in documents alone; it is typically passed on through apprenticeship, demonstration, and practice. Knowledge-management theory (for example Nonaka's SECI model) studies how tacit know-how is externalised, combined, and re-internalised within organisations.

Legal and commercial aspects

In law and business, know-how is a form of intellectual property: confidential technical and commercial information — process parameters, recipes, troubleshooting skills — that gives a firm competitive advantage. It is licensed alongside patents, protected as trade secrets, and transferred in technology-transfer and franchising agreements. Because know-how lacks the formal registration of a patent, its protection depends on secrecy and contract.

Industrial context

In industrialisation and development economics, acquiring manufacturing know-how — not merely buying machinery — is often the binding constraint on building industries, which is why licensing agreements and joint ventures typically include training and the transfer of unwritten operating practice.
