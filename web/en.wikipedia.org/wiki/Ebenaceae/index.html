The Ebenaceae are a family of flowering plants belonging to the order Ericales. The family is best known for two economically important products: ebony, the dense black heartwood obtained from several species of Diospyros, and the persimmon fruits, produced by species such as Diospyros kaki (Japanese persimmon) and Diospyros virginiana (American persimmon).

Description

Members of the family are trees and shrubs, mostly distributed in the tropics, with some persimmons extending into temperate regions. The leaves are usually simple, alternate and entire-margined, without stipules. Flowers are typically unisexual (the plants often being dioecious), with a persistent calyx that enlarges as the fruit develops. The fruit is a berry, often seated in the characteristic woody calyx familiar from persimmons. Many species have exceptionally hard, dark heartwood.

Genera and distribution

The family contains around 800 species. By far the largest genus is Diospyros, with roughly 700–750 species spread through the tropics and subtropics worldwide. Euclea (about 18 species) is African and Arabian, Lissocarpa is South American, and Royena is sometimes merged into Diospyros. True ebonies — such as Ceylon ebony (Diospyros ebenum) and African ebony (Diospyros crassiflora) — have been heavily exploited for furniture, musical instruments and carving, and several species are now listed as endangered and regulated under CITES.

Uses

Besides ebony and persimmons, the family provides the Japanese persimmon's tannin (used in brewing and preserving), blackwood for inlay work, and various local fruits such as the black sapote (Diospyros nigra) and mabolo (Diospyros blancoi). Persimmons are an important commercial fruit crop in East Asia, Spain and elsewhere.
