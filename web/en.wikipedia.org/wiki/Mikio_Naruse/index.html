Mikio Naruse (成瀬 巳喜男, Naruse Mikio; August 20, 1905 – July 2, 1969) was a Japanese filmmaker who directed 89 films between 1930 and 1967. Working largely in the shomin-geki genre of lower-middle-class life, and often adapting the novels of Fumiko Hayashi, he is now ranked among the greatest Japanese directors alongside Ozu, Mizoguchi, and Kurosawa.

Career

Naruse joined Shochiku as a prop assistant in 1920 and began directing in 1930. Moving to PCL (later Toho) in the mid-1930s, he made the acclaimed Wife! Be Like a Rose! (1935), one of the first Japanese films released in the United States. After the war he made a string of masterpieces: Repast (1951), Lightning (1952), Late Chrysanthemums (1954), Floating Clouds (1955) — frequently voted among the greatest Japanese films — and When a Woman Ascends the Stairs (1960), many starring Hideko Takamine.

Style and themes

Naruse's films portray, with unsparing but humane pessimism, people trapped by poverty, family obligation, and incompatible desires — especially women struggling for independence in a changing Japan. His editing is brisk and elliptical, his camera placement precise; Akira Kurosawa compared the flow of his films to a deep river with a calm surface hiding a raging current underneath.

Legacy

Long overshadowed abroad by his more exportable contemporaries, Naruse has been steadily reclaimed through retrospectives and restorations, and his influence on later directors of domestic drama is widely acknowledged.
