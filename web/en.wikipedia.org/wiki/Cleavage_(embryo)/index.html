In embryology, cleavage is the division of cells in the early embryo following fertilization. The zygote undergoes rapid mitotic divisions with no significant overall growth, producing progressively smaller cells called blastomeres. The process converts the single-celled zygote into a multicellular structure — first a solid ball called the morula, then a hollow blastula (in mammals, the blastocyst) — while the overall size of the embryo remains roughly that of the original egg, constrained by the zona pellucida.

Mechanism

Cleavage divisions differ from ordinary mitosis in that the daughter cells do not grow between divisions: the cell cycle alternates rapidly between S phase (DNA synthesis) and M phase (mitosis) with little or no gap phases. Cell cycles are driven initially by maternal RNAs and proteins stockpiled in the egg until the midblastula transition, when zygotic gene expression begins and divisions slow and become asynchronous.

Patterns of cleavage

The amount and distribution of yolk strongly influence cleavage patterns:

  • Holoblastic cleavage — the entire egg divides, seen in eggs with little yolk (isolecithal, e.g. mammals and sea urchins; radial, spiral, bilateral, and rotational patterns) or moderate yolk (mesolecithal amphibians).
  • Meroblastic cleavage — only part of the egg divides, as in the very yolky eggs of birds, reptiles, and most fish (discoidal cleavage) and insects (superficial cleavage).

In mammals

Mammalian cleavage is rotational and slow, with the second division of one blastomere occurring out of phase with the other. Around the 8–16 cell stage the blastomeres undergo compaction, maximizing cell contact and setting up inside–outside differences that lead to the first differentiation: outer cells become the trophoblast while inner cells form the inner cell mass of the blastocyst. Identical twinning can result if the early embryo splits.
