Tijuca Forest

The Tijuca Forest (Portuguese: Floresta da Tijuca) is a tropical rainforest in the city of Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. It is claimed to be the world's largest urban forest, covering some 32 square kilometres, and since 1961 the core of the Tijuca National Park, which also includes the Corcovado mountain crowned by the statue of Christ the Redeemer.

History

The forest is largely the product of reforestation. During the nineteenth century the mountains around Rio were cleared for sugar, coffee plantations and charcoal, and the loss of forest cover threatened the city's water supply as springs dried up. Beginning in 1861, by order of Emperor Pedro II, Major Manuel Gomes Archer directed the planting of tens of thousands of seedlings — the work initially carried out by a small crew including enslaved labourers; the effort is regarded as one of the earliest large-scale ecological restoration projects. Over a hundred thousand trees, both native Atlantic Forest species and exotics such as jackfruit, were planted in the following decades.

Geography and ecology

The forest covers the Tijuca Massif, whose highest point, Pico da Tijuca (1,021 m), is the highest peak in Rio. It is part of the Atlantic Forest biome and supports hundreds of plant species and fauna including ocelots, howler monkeys, sloths, toucans and numerous endemic birds and amphibians. Landmarks within the park include the Cascatinha waterfall, the Mayrink Chapel with murals by Cândido Portinari, the Vista Chinesa lookout, and Corcovado. The area forms part of the Carioca Landscapes UNESCO World Heritage Site inscribed in 2012.
