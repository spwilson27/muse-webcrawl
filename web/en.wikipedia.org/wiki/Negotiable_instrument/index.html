Negotiable instrument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A negotiable instrument is a document guaranteeing the payment of a specific amount of money, either on demand or at a set time, whose payer is usually named on the document. More specifically, it is a document contemplated by or consisting of a contract, which promises the payment of money without condition, which may be paid either in cash or with another negotiable instrument.

Because negotiable instruments are transferable, a bona fide holder (a "holder in due course") can take the instrument free of many defences that could be raised against the original parties, a feature that historically made such paper an effective substitute for money in commerce.

Types

  • Promissory note — a written promise by one party to pay another a definite sum.
  • Bill of exchange — a written order by a drawer to a drawee to pay a sum to a payee; a cheque is a bill of exchange drawn on a bank.
  • Cheque (check) — payable on demand and drawn on a bank.
  • Bank draft, money order, and traveller's cheque — related payment instruments with negotiable features in many jurisdictions.

Requirements

Under Article 3 of the U.S. Uniform Commercial Code, a negotiable instrument must be in writing, be signed by the maker or drawer, contain an unconditional promise or order to pay a fixed amount of money, be payable on demand or at a definite time, and be payable to order or to bearer. Comparable rules exist in the United Kingdom's Bills of Exchange Act 1882 and in the laws of other common-law and civil-law countries, many of which were influenced by the Geneva Conventions of 1930–31.

Negotiation and endorsement

Negotiation is the transfer of possession of the instrument to another person, who becomes its holder. Order paper is negotiated by endorsement (indorsement) and delivery; bearer paper by delivery alone. Endorsements may be blank, special, restrictive, or qualified, each with different legal effects.
