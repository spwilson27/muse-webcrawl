Guide to anti-spam techniques

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Image placeholder: Guide to anti-spam techniques

An anti-spam technique is any method used to prevent email spam — unsolicited bulk email — from reaching recipients' inboxes. Because spam accounts for a large share of global email traffic, a wide range of techniques has been developed, typically used in combination by mail servers and email clients. No single method is fully effective, so modern systems layer several approaches.

Content filtering

Content filters analyse the text and structure of a message for characteristics typical of spam. Bayesian filtering, popularised by Paul Graham's 2002 essay "A Plan for Spam", learns from examples of spam and legitimate mail to compute the probability that a new message is spam. Machine-learning classifiers and rule-based systems such as SpamAssassin score messages against hundreds of tests.

Blocklists and allowlists

DNS-based blocklists (DNSBLs), such as those maintained by the Spamhaus Project, list IP addresses and domains known to send spam, allowing receiving servers to reject mail from them. Allowlists do the opposite, guaranteeing delivery from trusted senders but risking abuse if a listed source is compromised.

Sender authentication

Protocols including SPF (Sender Policy Framework), DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) and DMARC let domain owners publish which servers may send mail for their domain and cryptographically sign messages, making forged sender addresses easier to detect.

Other techniques

  • Greylisting — temporarily rejecting mail from unknown senders, relying on legitimate servers to retry.
  • Challenge–response — requiring an unknown sender to complete a task before delivery.
  • Honeypots and spamtraps — addresses planted to attract spam and identify senders.
  • Rate limiting and tarpitting — slowing connections from suspicious sources.
  • CAPTCHAs and proof of work — imposing a small cost on sending, as proposed by systems such as Hashcash.

Legal measures

Legislation such as the United States CAN-SPAM Act of 2003 and the European Union's privacy directives supplements technical defences, though enforcement across jurisdictions remains difficult.
