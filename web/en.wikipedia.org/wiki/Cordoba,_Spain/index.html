Córdoba (Spanish: [ˈkoɾðoβa]) is a city in Andalusia, Spain, and the capital of the province of Córdoba. Situated on the banks of the Guadalquivir River, it was an important Roman city and later the capital of the Islamic Emirate and then Caliphate of Córdoba, under which it became one of the largest and most cultured cities in the world during the early Middle Ages.

History

Founded as the Roman colony of Corduba, the city was the capital of Hispania Baetica. After the Muslim conquest of 711 it rose to prominence; under Abd al-Rahman III, who proclaimed the Caliphate of Córdoba in 929, it flourished as a centre of learning, medicine and philosophy, home to figures such as Averroes and Maimonides. The Great Mosque (Mezquita), begun in 785, remains the city's most famous monument. Córdoba fell to Ferdinand III of Castile in 1236 during the Reconquista.

Main sights

  • The Mosque–Cathedral (Mezquita-Catedral), inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1984; the historic centre was added in 1994.
  • The Roman bridge and the Calahorra Tower.
  • The Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos.
  • The Jewish Quarter, the Synagogue, and the patios celebrated in the annual Fiesta de los Patios.
  • Medina Azahara, the ruined palace-city built by Abd al-Rahman III, a World Heritage Site since 2018.

Climate

Córdoba has a hot-summer Mediterranean climate with some of the highest summer temperatures in Europe, frequently exceeding 40 °C (104 °F) in July and August; a temperature of 46.9 °C was recorded in 2021.
