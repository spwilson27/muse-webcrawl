The eszett (German: Eszett, pronounced [ɛsˈtsɛt]) is the letter ß used in German orthography. Also called the "sharp S" (scharfes S), it represents the voiceless /s/ sound after long vowels and diphthongs, as in Straße ("street") and weiß ("white"). It originated as a ligature of the long s (ſ) and z in blackletter handwriting and type. Uniquely among letters of major European alphabets, it traditionally existed only in lowercase; an official capital form, ẞ, was admitted into German orthography in 2017.

History

The eszett developed in medieval German handwriting from the combination of ſ and z, and it appeared in print from the era of early blackletter types. For centuries its use varied regionally, and Roman (Antiqua) type often lacked the character. Germany's spelling reforms standardised it: the 1901 orthographic conference confirmed ß in both Fraktur and Antiqua. Switzerland and Liechtenstein abandoned the letter during the twentieth century, writing ss instead.

Usage

Under the reformed spelling of 1996, ß is written after long vowels and diphthongs (Fuß, draußen), while ss follows short vowels (muss, Fluss). The letter never begins a word. Where ß is unavailable — in crosswords, older computer systems, or uppercase text — it is conventionally replaced by SS; in sorted lists it is treated as ss.

Capital form

Because German capitalises all nouns, an uppercase eszett is needed when words are set entirely in capitals (e.g. STRASSE vs. STRAẞE). Although typographers had long used a capital variant, it was only officially recognised when the Council for German Orthography added ẞ to the official rules in June 2017, as an alternative to the SS rendering.

Names and similar letters

The name Eszett derives from the letters S and Z. The letter is sometimes confused with the Greek beta (β), which it resembles but to which it is unrelated.
