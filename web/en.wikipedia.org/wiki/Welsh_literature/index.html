Welsh literature

Welsh literature is the body of written works produced in the Welsh language and, in modern times, works in English by Welsh authors. Welsh has one of the oldest continuous literary traditions in Europe, stretching from the heroic poetry of the 6th century to the present day.

Early and medieval literature

The earliest surviving Welsh poetry is attributed to Taliesin and Aneirin, court poets of the Brittonic kingdoms of the "Old North"; Aneirin's Y Gododdin laments a disastrous raid on Catraeth around the year 600. The medieval prose tales of the Mabinogion, preserved in the White Book of Rhydderch and the Red Book of Hergest, combine mythology, folklore and romance, including some of the earliest Arthurian material. In the 14th century Dafydd ap Gwilym perfected the cywydd metre in lyrics of love and nature, and is widely regarded as Wales's greatest poet.

Early modern and 19th century

William Morgan's Welsh Bible of 1588 standardised literary Welsh and secured the language's survival. The 18th-century Methodist revival and the eisteddfod movement revived bardic competition, while the 19th century produced poets such as Islwyn and Hedd Wyn, whose chair-winning ode at the 1917 National Eisteddfod was delivered days after his death at Passchendaele.

Modern literature

The 20th century saw the novels of Kate Roberts, the poetry of Saunders Lewis and Waldo Williams, and the English-language work of Dylan Thomas and R. S. Thomas. Contemporary Welsh writing in both languages remains vigorous, supported by the National Eisteddfod and publishers such as the University of Wales Press and Seren.
