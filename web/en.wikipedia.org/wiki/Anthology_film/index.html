Anthology film

Anthology film
DefinitionA film composed of several distinct short films or segments

An anthology film, also known as an omnibus film, portmanteau film or package film, is a single film consisting of several different short films, often tied together by a single theme, premise, location or a framing device. Each segment is usually directed by a different filmmaker or features a separate cast and storyline, although a single director may also shoot every segment.

The format has been especially popular in horror, where it descends from Ealing Studios' Dead of Night (1945) and the Amicus productions of the 1960s and 1970s such as Dr. Terror's House of Horrors and Tales from the Crypt. It has also been used for city portraits, literary adaptations and festival showcase projects.

Structure

Anthology films commonly use one of several organising devices:

  • A framing story or wraparound segment that introduces and links the episodes, as in Creepshow (1982).
  • A shared theme or object passing between segments, as in Four Rooms (1995) or The Ballad of Buster Scruggs (2018).
  • A shared place or occasion, as in Paris, je t'aime (2006) and its successors set in other cities.
  • Adaptations of stories by a single author, as in The Illustrated Man (1969).

History

Multi-story films date to the silent era — D. W. Griffith's Intolerance (1916) interweaves four storylines — and became a recognised format with films such as Grand Hotel-style ensemble dramas and the British portmanteau tradition. The horror anthology flourished with Amicus Productions, declined in the 1980s after Creepshow and Twilight Zone: The Movie, and revived in the 2000s with titles such as Trick 'r Treat and the found-footage series V/H/S. Anthology projects have also served as calling cards for emerging directors, who can contribute a short segment at modest cost.

Reception

Critics frequently note the unevenness inherent in the format: individual segments vary in quality, and reviewers often rank them separately. Defenders argue that the variety is the point, and that the format preserves a space for short-form storytelling within commercial cinema.
