Night blindness

Night blindness, also called nyctalopia, is the inability to see well at night or in poorly lit environments. It is a symptom of several eye diseases rather than a disease in itself. Night blindness may exist from birth, or be caused by injury or malnutrition, particularly vitamin A deficiency. It can be described as insufficient adaptation to darkness.

Causes

The rod cells of the retina, which mediate vision in low light, are affected in most forms of night blindness. Causes include:

  • Retinitis pigmentosa — a group of inherited retinal dystrophies in which night blindness and tunnel vision are early symptoms.
  • Vitamin A deficiency — vitamin A is required to produce rhodopsin, the photopigment of the rods; deficiency remains a major cause in the developing world.
  • Congenital stationary night blindness — a group of genetic disorders present from birth, without progressive vision loss.
  • Cataracts, severe myopia, glaucoma medications that constrict the pupil, and diabetic retinopathy can also impair night vision.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis involves a history of visual difficulty in dim light, examination of the retina, dark-adaptation testing, visual field testing, and electroretinography to measure the electrical responses of rod and cone photoreceptors. Blood tests can identify vitamin A deficiency.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. Vitamin A deficiency responds to supplementation and dietary correction and is reversible if treated before corneal damage occurs. Cataracts can be removed surgically. Inherited retinal diseases were long considered untreatable, though gene therapy has been approved for one form of Leber congenital amaurosis, and low-vision aids and orientation training can help patients manage.
