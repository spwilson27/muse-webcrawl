Spanish–American War

DateApril 21 – August 13, 1898
LocationCuba, Puerto Rico, Philippines, Guam
ResultAmerican victory; Treaty of Paris (1898)
BelligerentsUnited States; Cuban and Filipino revolutionaries vs. Spain

The Spanish–American War (April 21 – August 13, 1898) was an armed conflict between Spain and the United States. Hostilities began in the aftermath of the internal explosion of the USS Maine in Havana Harbor in Cuba, leading to United States intervention in the Cuban War of Independence. The war led to the United States emerging predominant in the Caribbean region and resulted in its acquisition of Spain's Pacific possessions. It also involved the Philippine Revolution and later the Philippine–American War.

Background

The 19th century represented a period of decline for the Spanish Empire, while the United States went from becoming a newly founded country to a regional power. In Cuba, the Ten Years' War and subsequent uprisings against Spanish rule culminated in the Cuban War of Independence beginning in 1895. American public opinion, inflamed by yellow journalism reporting Spanish atrocities against Cubans, grew increasingly sympathetic to intervention. President William McKinley sought a negotiated settlement, but the sinking of the battleship USS Maine in Havana harbor on February 15, 1898 — blamed by American newspapers on Spain — pushed the two nations toward war.

Course of the war

The United States issued an ultimatum to Spain demanding it surrender control of Cuba; after Spain declared war on April 24, the U.S. reciprocated the next day. Commodore George Dewey destroyed the Spanish Pacific Squadron at the Battle of Manila Bay on May 1, 1898. In the Caribbean, American forces landed in Cuba, winning the Battle of San Juan Hill, while the U.S. Navy destroyed the Spanish fleet at the Battle of Santiago de Cuba on July 3. Puerto Rico was invaded in late July, and Guam was captured without bloodshed in June.

Treaty of Paris

Fighting ended with an armistice on August 12, 1898. The Treaty of Paris, signed on December 10, 1898, ended the war. Spain recognized Cuban independence and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippine Islands to the United States in exchange for $20 million. The war marked the emergence of the United States as a world power with overseas colonies, and signaled the final decline of Spain as a colonial empire.

Legacy

The conflict lasted roughly ten weeks and is sometimes called a "splendid little war," a phrase coined by Secretary of State John Hay. It propelled Theodore Roosevelt, who led the Rough Riders at San Juan Hill, to national fame and ultimately the presidency. The war also set the stage for American involvement in the Philippines and debates over imperialism at home.
