A twinjet or twin-engine jet is a jet aircraft powered by two engines. A twinjet is able to fly well enough to land with a single working engine, making it safer than single-engine aircraft in the event of an engine failure, while offering better fuel economy than aircraft with three or four engines. Twinjets are today the most common configuration for commercial airliners, from narrow-body types such as the Airbus A320 and Boeing 737 to the wide-body Boeing 777 and Airbus A350.

History

The earliest production twinjets were Second World War fighters such as the Messerschmitt Me 262. In civil service, early twinjets (BAC One-Eleven, Douglas DC-9, Boeing 727-class short-haul types) were for decades restricted over water by the "60-minute rule", which kept them near diversion airports. The introduction of ETOPS (Extended-range Twin-engine Operations Performance Standards) from 1985 onwards progressively allowed twinjets to fly long overwater routes — up to 370 minutes from a diversion airport for the Airbus A350 — which, combined with their lower fuel burn, drove trijets and quadjets from most passenger service.

Configurations

Twinjet engines may be mounted under the wings (most airliners), on either side of the rear fuselage (business jets and regional jets such as the Embraer E-Jet family), or buried in the fuselage or wing roots (many fighters). A twinjet's remaining engine must be powerful enough for single-engine climb, so twinjets typically have the highest thrust-to-weight margin of any airliner configuration.
