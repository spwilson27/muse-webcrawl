The Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC; often called the housing credit) is a federal programme in the United States that gives state and local LIHTC-allocating agencies the equivalent of approximately $10.5 billion in annual budget authority to issue tax credits for the acquisition, rehabilitation, or new construction of rental housing targeted to lower-income households. Created by the Tax Reform Act of 1986 and made permanent in 1993, it is codified in Section 42 of the Internal Revenue Code and is the largest source of federal support for affordable rental housing production in the United States.

How it works

The federal government allocates credits to state housing finance agencies on a per-capita basis. States award the credits competitively to developers under a Qualified Allocation Plan that sets priorities consistent with federal requirements. Because the credits alone do not pay construction costs directly, developers typically sell ("syndicate") them to investors — most often banks and other corporations — who can claim the credits against their federal tax liability over ten years. The equity raised finances the project, reducing the debt it must carry and allowing lower rents.

There are two principal types of credit: the competitive 9% credit, intended to subsidize roughly 70 percent of a project's eligible costs, and the non-competitive 4% credit, subsidizing roughly 30 percent, which is generally used together with tax-exempt bond financing. To qualify, a project must meet one of several income tests — for example, at least 40 percent of units reserved for households earning no more than 60 percent of area median income — and rents for those units are capped accordingly. Properties must comply with the income and rent restrictions for a minimum compliance period of 15 years, and under extended-use agreements typically 30 years or more.

Scale and assessment

Since 1987 the programme has financed more than 3.5 million apartments and served an estimated 8 million low-income households. Supporters credit it with producing housing of good quality across urban, suburban and rural areas with strong private-sector discipline. Critics note its administrative complexity, the "leakage" of subsidy to syndicators and intermediaries, the risk of units losing affordability when extended-use periods expire, and its limited reach to the poorest households without additional rental assistance layered on top.
