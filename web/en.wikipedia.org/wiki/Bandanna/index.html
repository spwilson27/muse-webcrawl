Bandanna

A bandanna (also spelled bandana) is a type of large, usually colourful kerchief, originating in India as a brightly coloured silk and cotton scarf with spots in white on coloured grounds, chiefly red or blue. The name derives from the Sanskrit word badhnati ("binds" or "ties"), via the Hindi bāndhnū, a term for the tie-dye method used to produce the spots. Today, bandannas are most often made of cotton printed with paisley or other repeating patterns, and are worn as head coverings, neckerchiefs, dust masks, or fashion accessories.

History

The bandanna reached Europe and the Americas in the eighteenth century through the East India trade, and printed cottons manufactured in Lancashire and later in the United States made it a cheap, ubiquitous article of work clothing. During the American Revolutionary War, a famous bandanna printed in 1776 depicted George Washington on horseback surrounded by cannon and flags. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries bandannas became standard gear for sailors, cowboys, miners, farmers and railway workers, who used them to keep off sun, absorb sweat, strain water and filter dust.

Uses and symbolism

Bandannas have also carried symbolic meanings. In the 1970s and 1980s, coloured bandannas formed part of the "hanky code" in some gay subcultures, signalling preferences by colour and pocket placement. Coloured bandannas were adopted as identifying colours by street gangs, leading some schools to ban them. The red bandanna became a labour-movement emblem, and a red bandanna worn by rescue worker Welles Crowther during the September 11 attacks was commemorated after his death. During the COVID-19 pandemic, bandannas were widely improvised as face coverings.
