Porphyry copper deposit

Porphyry copper deposits are copper ore bodies formed from hydrothermal fluids that emanate from a large magma chamber several kilometres below the deposit itself. They are low-grade but enormous — often hundreds of millions to billions of tonnes of ore — and constitute the world's largest source of copper, besides providing most of its molybdenum and significant gold and silver.

Formation

The deposits occur above subduction zones, where porphyritic intrusions of diorite to granodiorite composition cool and fracture, allowing metal-bearing brines to deposit sulphide minerals such as chalcopyrite and bornite in stockwork veins. Concentric zones of hydrothermal alteration surround the core: potassic, phyllic (sericitic), argillic, and propylitic.

Mining

Because grades are typically only 0.3–1% copper, porphyries are exploited by large-scale open-pit or block-cave mining with high-volume processing. Giant examples include Chuquicamata and Escondida in Chile, Grasberg in Indonesia, Bingham Canyon in the United States, and Oyu Tolgoi in Mongolia. Supergene enrichment blankets of chalcocite often enriched the upper parts of deposits and were mined first historically.
