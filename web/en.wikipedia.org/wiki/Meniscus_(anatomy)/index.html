A meniscus is a crescent-shaped fibrocartilaginous anatomical structure that, in contrast to an articular disc, only partly divides a joint cavity. In humans they are present in the knee, wrist, acromioclavicular, sternoclavicular, and temporomandibular joints. The term is most commonly used for the two menisci of the knee: the medial meniscus and the lateral meniscus.

Structure

The menisci of the knee are two pads of fibrocartilaginous tissue that sit between the femoral condyles and the tibial plateau. They are concave on the top surface, allowing them to cradle the rounded ends of the femur, and flat underneath where they rest on the tibia. The medial meniscus is roughly C-shaped and covers a larger portion of the tibial plateau, while the lateral meniscus is more closed, approaching a complete circle or O shape.

The menisci are attached to the tibia by the coronary ligaments and at their horns. The medial meniscus is also firmly attached to the medial collateral ligament, which makes it less mobile — and more prone to injury — than the lateral meniscus. Blood supply reaches only the outer third of each meniscus (the "red zone"); the inner two-thirds are largely avascular and have limited capacity to heal after injury.

Function

The menisci serve several functions in the knee joint:

  • Load transmission: they distribute the load across the joint, transmitting roughly half of the compressive load in the knee and protecting the articular cartilage.
  • Shock absorption: their fibrocartilaginous structure absorbs impact during walking and running.
  • Stability: by deepening the tibial surface, they improve the congruity of the joint and contribute to stability, particularly the posterior horn of the medial meniscus.
  • Lubrication and nutrition: they help spread synovial fluid across the articular surfaces.

Clinical significance

Meniscus tear

A tear of the meniscus is one of the most common knee injuries. It may result from acute trauma, typically a twisting injury in sport, or from degeneration in older adults. Symptoms include pain along the joint line, swelling, stiffness, and sometimes locking or catching of the knee. Diagnosis is by clinical examination and MRI. Treatment ranges from physiotherapy and rest to arthroscopic surgery — either partial meniscectomy (removal of the torn fragment) or meniscal repair, which is preferred where possible because removal of meniscal tissue increases the risk of osteoarthritis.

Meniscectomy and osteoarthritis

The importance of the menisci was historically underestimated, and total meniscectomy was once a common procedure. Long-term studies showed that knees deprived of a meniscus develop degenerative changes at high rates, so modern practice aims to preserve as much meniscal tissue as possible.
