Temperate rainforest

Temperate rainforests are rainforests occurring in temperate regions, characterised by high annual rainfall, frequent fog, and mild temperatures with a modest annual temperature range. They are dominated in many regions by evergreen conifers or broadleaf trees and support exceptionally high biomass and rich communities of epiphytes — mosses, ferns, and lichens growing on the trees.

Definition

Definitions vary, but temperate rainforests typically receive more than about 140 cm (55 in) of precipitation annually, much of it falling in the cooler months, and have cool summers with mean temperatures generally between 4 °C and 12 °C in the coldest and warmest months. Fog and low cloud contribute significant additional moisture. Compared with tropical rainforests, temperate rainforests have fewer tree species but often taller trees and greater standing biomass per hectare.

Distribution

The best-known temperate rainforests fringe the Pacific coast of North America, from northern California through Oregon, Washington, and British Columbia to southeast Alaska, where Sitka spruce, western hemlock, Douglas-fir, and coast redwood dominate. Other major regions include the Valdivian forests of southern Chile and Argentina; the west coast of New Zealand's South Island and Tasmania in Australia; parts of Japan and Korea; the Colchic rainforests of Georgia and Turkey along the Black Sea; and small Atlantic fringe remnants in Ireland, western Scotland, Wales, and south-west England, sometimes called Celtic rainforest.

Ecology

Long-lived conifers in the Pacific Northwest rainforests may live for many centuries, and fallen trunks serve as "nurse logs" for seedlings. The forests store large amounts of carbon and provide habitat for species such as the northern spotted owl and marbled murrelet. In the southern hemisphere examples, evergreen southern beech (Nothofagus) is often dominant. Epiphyte loads are heavy, and the forest floor is typically carpeted with mosses and ferns.

Conservation

Temperate rainforests have been extensively logged for their valuable timber; large areas of old-growth forest in North America, Chile, and Tasmania have been cleared or converted to plantations. Remaining tracts are protected in parks and reserves such as Olympic National Park and the Great Bear Rainforest of British Columbia, though debates over logging of old growth continue.
