The Lumbricidae are a family of earthworms which includes most of the earthworm species well known in Europe. The family comprises around 33 genera and more than 700 described species, native originally to the Holarctic region — Europe, western and northern Asia, and parts of North America. Several species, most famously the common earthworm Lumbricus terrestris and the red worm Eisenia fetida, have been introduced worldwide by human activity and are now cosmopolitan, profoundly affecting soils in regions (such as North American forests) that lacked native earthworms since the last glaciation.

Characteristics

Lumbricids are clitellate oligochaetes with a cylindrical, segmented body, setae arranged in four paired bundles per segment (lumbricine arrangement), and a well-developed clitellum used in reproduction. They are hermaphrodites that exchange sperm during mating and deposit fertilised eggs in cocoons. Species occupy distinct ecological niches: epigeic species live in surface litter, endogeic species in the mineral soil, and anecic species such as L. terrestris maintain permanent vertical burrows and drag leaf litter down into them.

Ecology

Lumbricid earthworms are major ecosystem engineers. By burrowing, ingesting soil and leaf litter, and depositing casts, they aerate the soil, improve drainage and nutrient cycling, and mix organic matter into the mineral soil — effects praised by Charles Darwin in his final book, The Formation of Vegetable Mould through the Action of Worms (1881). In agriculture they are generally regarded as beneficial, and Eisenia fetida is the principal species used in vermicomposting. Conversely, introduced lumbricids can damage northern hardwood forests by consuming the protective leaf-litter (duff) layer faster than it accumulates.
