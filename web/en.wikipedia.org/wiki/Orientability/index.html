Orientability

In mathematics, orientability is a property of some topological spaces that allows a consistent choice of "orientation" throughout the space. For surfaces in three dimensions, it corresponds to the existence of two distinct sides: a surface is orientable if a figure drawn on it, carried around any closed loop, returns un-mirrored; it is non-orientable if some loop returns the figure as its mirror image.

The Möbius strip is non-orientable: it has only one side.
Orientability
FieldTopology, differential geometry
Orientable examplesSphere, torus, plane, cylinder
Non-orientable examplesMöbius strip, Klein bottle, real projective plane
CharacterizationExistence of a nowhere-vanishing volume form; triviality of the orientation double cover

Surfaces

The standard non-orientable surface is the Möbius strip: an ant walking on it returns to its starting point flipped upside down. Embedding experiments show that one-sided surfaces such as the Klein bottle and the real projective plane cannot be embedded in three-dimensional Euclidean space without self-intersection, though they can be immersed. A compact surface is orientable exactly when it contains no Möbius band; the classification theorem says orientable closed surfaces are spheres with handles (tori of genus g), while non-orientable ones are spheres with cross-caps.

Manifolds

For a smooth n-manifold, orientability is defined as the existence of a nowhere-zero n-form (volume form), or equivalently a consistent atlas whose transition maps have positive Jacobian determinant. In homology terms, an orientation of a closed connected n-manifold is a choice of generator of the top homology group Hn(M; Z); manifolds like even-dimensional real projective spaces fail to have one. Every non-orientable manifold has an orientable double cover. Orientability is essential in differential geometry and physics for defining integrals of forms and applying Stokes' theorem over a whole manifold.
