Chinese mythology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chinese mythology is mythology that has been passed down in oral form or recorded in literature throughout the area now known as Greater China. It includes creation myths and stories of culture heroes, tales of the legendary sage-kings, flood myths, and a vast body of material associated with Chinese folk religion, Taoism, Confucian state cults and Chinese Buddhism. Much of the repertoire survives in texts such as the Shanhaijing (Classic of Mountains and Seas), the Chu Ci, and later novels like Investiture of the Gods and Journey to the West.

Creation and early myths

In the best-known creation account, the giant Pangu emerges from a cosmic egg, separates heaven and earth, and after his death his body becomes the features of the world. The goddess Nüwa fashions human beings from yellow clay and later repairs the broken sky with five coloured stones after the water god Gonggong smashes Mount Buzhou. Her companion Fuxi is credited with the trigrams of the I Ching, fishing nets and the institution of marriage.

Culture heroes and sage-kings

A succession of legendary rulers civilize China: Shennong, the Divine Farmer, teaches agriculture and herbal medicine; the Yellow Emperor (Huangdi) defeats Chiyou and stands as ancestor of the Han people; Yao and Shun exemplify virtuous rule; and Yu the Great controls the flood waters and founds the Xia dynasty. Other figures include Cangjie, inventor of Chinese characters, and the archer Hou Yi, who shot down nine of ten suns and whose wife Chang'e flew to the moon.

Gods and cosmology

Heaven (Tian) and the Jade Emperor head a celestial bureaucracy modelled on the imperial state, populated by deities of place, craft and nature administered through the City God, the Kitchen God, and countless local cults. Dragons — benevolent bringers of rain — symbolise imperial authority and the forces of water. Later syncretism blended indigenous gods with Buddhist figures such as Guanyin, the bodhisattva of compassion.

Sources

Key sources include the Shanhaijing, the Huainanzi, Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian, Yuan Ke's modern compilations, and Ming-dynasty novels that fixed many myths in their popular form.
