Anfinsen's dogma

Anfinsen's dogma
The native fold is determined by the amino-acid sequence
FieldMolecular biology, biochemistry
Proposed byChristian B. Anfinsen
Also calledThermodynamic hypothesis

Anfinsen's dogma, also known as the thermodynamic hypothesis, states that the native three-dimensional structure of a protein is determined solely by its amino-acid sequence and the conditions of its environment: in a given environment, the native structure is the unique, stable conformation of lowest Gibbs free energy.

The ribonuclease experiments

The principle comes from experiments by Christian Anfinsen in the 1950s–60s on bovine ribonuclease A. Anfinsen denatured the enzyme in concentrated urea with a reducing agent that broke its four disulfide bonds, destroying its activity. When the denaturants were removed and the disulfide bonds allowed to reform, the protein spontaneously refolded and regained essentially full enzymatic activity — showing that all the information needed to specify the fold is contained in the sequence itself.

Exceptions and qualifications

Several qualifications are now recognised: many proteins require molecular chaperones to fold efficiently in the crowded cell; some proteins are intrinsically disordered; prions and amyloid proteins can adopt alternative stable structures; and the observed fold can depend on the environment (pH, temperature, binding partners) rather than sequence alone.

Significance

The dogma underlies efforts to predict protein structure from sequence, from early statistical methods to modern machine-learning systems such as AlphaFold, and earned Anfinsen a share of the 1972 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
