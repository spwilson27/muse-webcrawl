Analog models of gravity

Analog models of gravity are attempts to model the phenomena of gravity — above all general relativity and black holes — using other physical systems, such as acoustics in a moving fluid, superfluids, optical media, or Bose–Einstein condensates. The motivation is twofold: analogue systems can be probed experimentally in the laboratory, unlike real astrophysical black holes; and they may illuminate deep questions about the interplay between gravity and quantum mechanics.

History

The field began in 1981, when William Unruh showed that sound waves in a transsonically moving fluid experience an effective curved spacetime: where the flow exceeds the local speed of sound, phonons cannot propagate upstream, forming an acoustic horizon analogous to a black hole event horizon. Unruh further argued that such a system should emit a thermal flux of phonons analogous to Hawking radiation, with a temperature set by the gradient of the flow at the horizon. Later work broadened the idea to surface waves in water tanks, light in moving dielectrics, and excitations in superfluid helium and Bose–Einstein condensates.

Acoustic black holes

In the acoustic model, the wave equation for sound in an inviscid, barotropic fluid can be rewritten as a massless Klein–Gordon equation in an effective metric determined by the fluid's velocity and density profiles. When the fluid accelerates through the speed of sound, the resulting "dumb hole" traps sound the way a black hole traps light. Laboratory experiments using water tanks and, later, Bose–Einstein condensates reported evidence consistent with analogue Hawking radiation, including in a widely discussed 2016 experiment by Jeff Steinhauer.

Other analogues

Related systems include optical analogues, in which intense laser pulses create moving refractive-index perturbations in a fibre or dielectric; surface-wave analogues tested with flowing water; and superfluid analogues in which quasiparticles play the role of fields on curved spacetime. Analogue models have been used to study Hawking radiation, black hole superradiance, cosmological particle production, and analogue versions of the cosmological constant problem.

Limitations

Analogue models reproduce the kinematics of fields on curved spacetime but generally not the Einstein field equations themselves: the effective metric is imposed by the experimental setup rather than determined dynamically by matter. They therefore cannot, by themselves, resolve questions about quantum gravity, though they provide evidence that Hawking radiation is a robust phenomenon insensitive to short-distance physics.
