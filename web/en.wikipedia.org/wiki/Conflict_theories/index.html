Conflict theories

Conflict theories are perspectives in sociology and social psychology that emphasise the social, political or material inequality of a social group, critique the broad socio-political system, or argue that the system functions to benefit some groups at the expense of others. In contrast to structural functionalism, which stresses social stability and shared values, conflict theory holds that society is characterised by competition over scarce resources and by struggles for power.

Conflict theory portrays society as an arena of competition between groups with opposing interests.

Origins

The tradition originates above all in the work of Karl Marx, who analysed capitalism as a system generating class conflict between the bourgeoisie, owners of the means of production, and the proletariat, who sell their labour. Max Weber broadened the analysis to include status and party alongside class, and Georg Simmel treated conflict as a normal, even integrative, social process.

Twentieth-century developments

Lewis Coser argued that conflict can perform positive functions, such as releasing tension and reinforcing group boundaries. Ralf Dahrendorf located conflict in authority relations within organisations. C. Wright Mills applied conflict analysis to the "power elite" of American institutions. The perspective has since been extended to race, gender and other axes of inequality, informing critical race theory, feminist theory and conflict criminology, which examines how law reflects the interests of the powerful.
