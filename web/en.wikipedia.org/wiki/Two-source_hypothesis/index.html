Two-source hypothesis

Two-source hypothesis
FieldBiblical studies
SubjectThe Synoptic problem
Proposed sourcesGospel of Mark; hypothetical "Q" sayings source
Formulated1838 (C. H. Weisse); developed by Holtzmann, Streeter

The two-source hypothesis is the most widely accepted solution to the synoptic problem — the question of the literary relationship among the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, and Luke. It proposes that Matthew and Luke were written independently, each drawing on two sources: the Gospel of Mark (the earliest gospel) for their narrative framework, and a hypothetical collection of Jesus's sayings, known as Q (from German Quelle, "source"), for most of their shared teaching material not found in Mark.

Evidence

Supporters point to the "triple tradition" (material common to all three Synoptics following Mark's order and often Mark's wording) and the "double tradition" (some 200 verses shared by Matthew and Luke but absent from Mark, mostly sayings). Material unique to Matthew ("M") and to Luke ("L") is attributed to each evangelist's own sources or redaction. B. H. Streeter's 1925 formulation, sometimes called the four-source hypothesis, remains the standard refinement.

Rival theories

Alternatives include the Augustinian hypothesis (Matthew first, then Mark abbreviating it), the Farrer hypothesis (Mark first, Luke using Matthew, no Q — defended notably by Mark Goodacre), and the two-gospel hypothesis (Matthew, then Luke, then Mark). The existence of Q remains debated because no manuscript of it survives, but its defenders argue it best explains the double tradition's patterns.
