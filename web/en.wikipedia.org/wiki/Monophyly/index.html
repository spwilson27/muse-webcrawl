Monophyly

In biological phylogenetics, a group is monophyletic (from Greek monos, "single", and phylon, "tribe") if it consists of a common ancestor and all of its descendants. A monophyletic group is also called a clade. Monophyletic groups are characterised by shared derived characteristics, or synapomorphies, inherited from their common ancestor.

FieldPhylogenetics, cladistics

ContrastsParaphyly, polyphyly

Related concepts

A paraphyletic group includes a common ancestor and most, but not all, of its descendants — for example, "reptiles" excluding birds, or "fish" excluding tetrapods. A polyphyletic group unites organisms based on convergent characteristics without including their most recent common ancestor, as in older groupings of flying animals or algae.

Use in classification

The cladistic approach to biological classification, developed by Willi Hennig in the mid-20th century, holds that formal taxa should be monophyletic, so that classification reflects evolutionary history. Modern systems, and molecular phylogenetic research generally, follow this principle, leading to revisions of many traditional groupings. For instance, birds are now classified within Dinosauria, making "dinosaurs" (in the cladistic sense) a living group, and whales are placed within the even-toed ungulates.

Etymology

The terms monophyly, paraphyly, and polyphyly derive from Haeckel's 19th-century usage and were given their modern phylogenetic definitions in the development of cladistics.
