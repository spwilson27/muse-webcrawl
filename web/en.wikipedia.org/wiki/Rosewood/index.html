Rosewood

Rosewood is the name given to a number of richly hued, dark tropical hardwoods, most of them species of the genus Dalbergia. True rosewoods are prized for their strength, density, beautiful grain and the faint rose-like scent released when the wood is cut or worked.

Species and uses

The most celebrated species is Brazilian rosewood (Dalbergia nigra), long the premier wood for fine furniture, musical instruments and veneer; others include East Indian rosewood (D. latifolia), Honduran rosewood (D. stevensonii) and Madagascar rosewoods. Rosewood is widely used for guitar backs, sides and fretboards, marimba and xylophone bars, clarinets and other instruments, chess pieces, tool handles and luxury cabinetry. Its high oil content gives it durability and a natural lustre.

Conservation

Centuries of overexploitation have made most rosewoods scarce. Brazilian rosewood has been listed on CITES Appendix I since 1992, effectively banning international trade, and in 2017 trade restrictions were extended to all Dalbergia species under Appendix II (with an exemption later granted for finished musical instruments). Illegal logging of rosewood, driven especially by demand for Chinese hongmu furniture, has devastated forests in Madagascar, Southeast Asia and Central America, making rosewood among the most trafficked wild products in the world.
