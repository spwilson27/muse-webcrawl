Institutional economics

Institutional economics focuses on understanding the role of the evolutionary process and of institutions — laws, customs, norms, organisations and property rights — in shaping economic behaviour. It emphasises that markets are embedded in social and legal structures rather than operating in a vacuum.

Institutional economics
Founders: Thorstein Veblen, John R. Commons, Wesley Mitchell
New institutionalism: Coase, North, Williamson, Ostrom
Key concepts: Transaction costs, path dependence

Old institutionalism

American institutionalists such as Thorstein Veblen — author on conspicuous consumption — Commons and Mitchell criticised abstract neoclassical theory, studying law, corporations, labour and business cycles empirically and historically.

New institutional economics

From Ronald Coase's work on transaction costs and the firm, Douglass North on institutions and long-run growth, Oliver Williamson on governance, and Elinor Ostrom on commons governance, the new school uses mainstream tools to explain why institutions differ and matter for development.

Influence

Institutional approaches inform development economics, economic history, law and economics, and policy on property rights and regulation. John Kenneth Galbraith popularised an institutionalist view of corporate power.
