The Minamata Convention on Mercury is an international treaty designed to protect human health and the environment from anthropogenic emissions and releases of mercury and mercury compounds. It was adopted on 10 October 2013 at a diplomatic conference held in Kumamoto, Japan, and entered into force on 16 August 2017, 90 days after the deposit of the 50th instrument of ratification. The convention is named after the Japanese city of Minamata, where severe mercury poisoning — Minamata disease — was first identified in 1956.

The convention was named for Minamata, Japan, site of the mercury-poisoning disaster discovered in 1956.

Background

Mercury is a highly toxic heavy metal that persists in the environment and bioaccumulates in food chains, particularly as methylmercury in fish. The Governing Council of the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) decided in 2009 to negotiate a legally binding instrument, and an intergovernmental negotiating committee held five sessions between 2010 and 2013, concluding the text in Geneva in January 2013.

Provisions

The convention controls mercury throughout its life cycle. Key obligations include:

  • A ban on new mercury mines and a phase-out of existing primary mining within 15 years of entry into force for each party.
  • Phase-out and phase-down of mercury in products (including certain batteries, switches, lamps, cosmetics and pesticides) and manufacturing processes listed in the annexes, with a 2020 phase-out date for many products.
  • Measures to reduce mercury use in artisanal and small-scale gold mining, a major source of emissions, through national action plans.
  • Controls on emissions to air from sources such as coal-fired power plants, industrial boilers, smelting and roasting processes, and cement production, and on releases to land and water.
  • Requirements for the environmentally sound interim storage of mercury and the management of mercury wastes.
  • Provisions on contaminated sites, health aspects, information exchange, public awareness and financial resources, including a financial mechanism involving the Global Environment Facility.

Conferences of the Parties

The first meeting of the Conference of the Parties (COP1) was held in Geneva in September 2017. Subsequent meetings have adopted guidance on emissions, releases, waste thresholds and effectiveness evaluation, and have added further products — such as certain fluorescent lamps — to the phase-out lists.
