Skin grafting

Skin grafting is a type of graft surgery involving the transplantation of skin. The transplanted tissue is called a skin graft. Skin grafting is often used to treat extensive wounds or burns, skin loss due to infection, and specific surgeries that may require skin grafts for healing to occur — most commonly the removal of skin cancers.

Classification

Grafts are classified by their composition. A split-thickness skin graft (STSG) contains the epidermis and part of the dermis and is harvested with a dermatome, commonly from the thigh or buttock. A full-thickness skin graft (FTSG) contains the entire dermis and gives better colour, texture, and less contraction, but can only cover smaller areas because the donor site must be closed directly.

By source, grafts may be autografts (from the patient), allografts (from another human, often cadaver skin), xenografts (from another species, typically pig), or synthetic and cultured skin substitutes.

Procedure and healing

The recipient bed must be clean, well-vascularized, and free of infection. Split-thickness grafts may be meshed — perforated with a pattern of slits — to expand their coverage and allow fluid drainage. A graft survives initially by plasmatic imbibition, then by inosculation and revascularization over several days. Immobilization and pressure dressings protect the fragile graft.

Risks

Failure can result from hematoma or seroma under the graft, infection, shear forces, or a poorly vascularized wound bed. Other complications include abnormal pigmentation, contracture, and scarring at both donor and recipient sites.
