Clause IV

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Clause IV is part of the constitution of the British Labour Party, which sets out the aims and values of the party. The original clause, adopted in 1918, committed Labour to public ownership of the means of production.

Original text

"To secure for the workers by hand or by brain the full fruits of their industry and the most equitable distribution thereof that may be possible upon the basis of the common ownership of the means of production, distribution and exchange, and the best obtainable system of popular administration and control of each industry or service."

The wording was drafted by Sidney Webb and became the symbolic core of British democratic socialism.

Attempts at revision

Hugh Gaitskell tried and failed to remove the clause after the 1959 election defeat. In 1995, leader Tony Blair succeeded in replacing it with a new statement that retained socialist language but committed the party to a "dynamic economy" and market enterprise — a defining moment of New Labour.

Legacy

"Clause IV" remains shorthand in British politics for ideological orthodoxy and modernization alike. The 1995 rewrite is frequently invoked in debates over party identity.
