Reinforced concrete is a composite material in which concrete's relatively low tensile strength and ductility are compensated for by embedding reinforcement — usually steel bars (rebar), meshes or fibres — that resists tensile stresses. It is one of the most widely used building materials in the world, forming the frames of most modern buildings, bridges, dams and roads.

How it works

Concrete is strong in compression but cracks under tension; steel is strong in tension and has nearly the same thermal expansion as concrete, so the two act together. The alkaline concrete also passivates the steel against rust, provided the cover of concrete is adequate and chlorides or carbonation do not reach the bars — corrosion of reinforcement is the chief cause of reinforced-concrete deterioration. Ribbed bar surfaces and hooks provide bond so forces transfer between the materials.

History

The material was patented in the 1860s by the French gardener Joseph Monier for plant pots, then developed for beams and buildings by Hennebique and others in the 1890s. In the 20th century it enabled the skyscraper, the great dams and the architecture of Le Corbusier and Pier Luigi Nervi. Variants include prestressed concrete, in which the steel is tensioned to pre-compress the concrete.
