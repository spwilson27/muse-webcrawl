Late Cenozoic Ice Age

Extent of continental ice sheets during the last glacial maximum

The Late Cenozoic Ice Age, or Antarctic Glaciation, began approximately 34 million years ago at the Eocene-Oligocene boundary and is ongoing. It is Earth's current ice age, marked by the presence of permanent ice sheets on Antarctica and, later, on Greenland and the Northern Hemisphere continents. The term "Late Cenozoic Ice Age" is used to distinguish the present long-lived glacial epoch from earlier ice ages in Earth's history, such as the Huronian, Cryogenian, Andean-Saharan, and Karoo glaciations.

Onset

The glaciation of Antarctica began around 34 million years ago, when declining atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations crossed a threshold that allowed a continental-scale ice sheet to form on the southern continent. Contributing factors included the opening of the Drake Passage and the Tasman Gateway, which thermally isolated Antarctica by establishing the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, and a long-term decline in greenhouse gas levels driven by silicate weathering, including uplift of the Himalayas.

Northern Hemisphere glaciation

Although Antarctica glaciated early in this ice age, large-scale glaciation of the Northern Hemisphere did not begin until much later, roughly 2.58 million years ago, at the start of the Quaternary period. From that point, the climate has oscillated between glacial periods (ice ages in the popular sense), lasting roughly 80,000 to 120,000 years, and shorter interglacial periods of about 10,000 to 30,000 years. These cycles are paced by variations in Earth's orbit and axial tilt known as Milankovitch cycles.

Current state

Earth is presently in an interglacial period, the Holocene, which began about 11,700 years ago. Because permanent ice sheets persist on Antarctica and Greenland, the planet remains within the Late Cenozoic Ice Age. Without human influence, the current interglacial might be expected to continue for tens of thousands of years; anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions are expected to delay the onset of the next glacial period substantially.
