Arthur Balfour

Arthur James Balfour, 1st Earl of Balfour (25 July 1848 – 19 March 1930) was a British statesman and Conservative politician who served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from 1902 to 1905. As Foreign Secretary in David Lloyd George's coalition government during the First World War, he issued the Balfour Declaration of 1917, a statement of British support for "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, which made his name one of the most consequential in the history of the Middle East.

Early career

Nephew of the Marquess of Salisbury, Balfour entered Parliament in 1874 and rose as his uncle's protégé, serving as Secretary for Scotland and then Chief Secretary for Ireland (1887–1891), where his firm enforcement of law and order earned him the nickname "Bloody Balfour" from Irish nationalists, alongside land-purchase legislation intended to "kill Home Rule with kindness". He led the Commons and effectively the government during Salisbury's last years.

Premiership

Succeeding Salisbury in 1902, Balfour passed the Education Act 1902 and the Licensing Act 1904, created the Committee of Imperial Defence, and concluded the Entente Cordiale with France (1904). His government was torn by the tariff reform campaign of Joseph Chamberlain, which split the Conservative Party; Balfour's attempts to hold the factions together failed, and he resigned in December 1905. The Conservatives were crushed in the 1906 general election, and Balfour lost his own seat (returning via a by-election), leading the party until 1911.

Later career

Balfour returned to office in the wartime coalitions as First Lord of the Admiralty (1915–16) and then Foreign Secretary (1916–1919), issuing the Balfour Declaration (2 November 1917) in a letter to Lord Rothschild. After the war he represented Britain at the Washington Naval Conference (1921–22), and as Lord President of the Council he authored the Balfour Declaration of 1926 at the Imperial Conference, defining the Dominions as autonomous communities "equal in status" with Britain within the British Empire — the formula that underlies the modern Commonwealth.

Intellectual life

A philosopher by training, Balfour wrote A Defence of Philosophic Doubt (1879) and The Foundations of Belief (1895) and was long associated with the Society for Psychical Research and the "Balfour Declaration" of a more personal kind — his interest in spiritualism and in the cross-correspondences. He was also a keen golfer and a president of the British Association.
