Vehicle identification number

A vehicle identification number (VIN) is a unique code, including a serial number, assigned by the manufacturer to identify an individual motor vehicle, towed vehicle, motorcycle or moped. The modern VIN is 17 characters long and has been standardised since 1981; it is defined internationally by ISO 3779 and ISO 3780, and in North America by federal regulation (49 CFR Part 565).

The VIN is stamped on plates and labels in several places, including the dashboard, visible through the windscreen.

Structure

The 17 characters (digits and capital letters, excluding I, O and Q to avoid confusion with 1 and 0) are divided into three sections. The World Manufacturer Identifier (positions 1–3) encodes the country and manufacturer. The Vehicle Descriptor Section (positions 4–9) describes attributes such as model, body style and engine; in North America position 9 is a check digit computed from the others to detect errors or tampering. The Vehicle Identifier Section (positions 10–17) includes the model year (position 10), the assembly plant (position 11) and a sequential production number.

Uses

Because no two vehicles in service share a VIN, it functions as the vehicle's fingerprint. Registration and title records, insurance, warranty claims, recall campaigns, theft recovery and vehicle history reports are all keyed to the VIN. Manufacturers are required to make the VIN durable and visible; it is typically found on a plate at the base of the windscreen, on the driver's door jamb, and stamped on the chassis or frame.

History

American manufacturers began stamping serial numbers in the mid-1950s, but formats varied by maker. The U.S. National Highway Traffic Safety Administration standardised the 17-character format for the 1981 model year, and it has since become the global norm, though some markets and vehicle classes (and very low-volume manufacturers) use variations.
