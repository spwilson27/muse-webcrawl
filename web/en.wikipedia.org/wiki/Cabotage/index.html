Cabotage

[Image placeholder: A coastal cargo vessel loading at a domestic port]

Cabotage (/ˈkæbətɪdʒ, -tɑːʒ/) is the transport of goods or passengers between two places in the same country by a transport operator from another country. The term originally applied to shipping along coastal routes — from the French caboter, "to travel by sea along the coast" — but now covers aviation, railways and road haulage as well.

Maritime cabotage

Many countries reserve domestic shipping for their own flagged vessels and crews, for reasons of national security, protection of domestic industry, and maritime labour standards. The best-known example is the United States' Merchant Marine Act of 1920, the Jones Act, which requires goods moved between US ports to travel on ships that are US-built, US-owned and US-crewed. Similar restrictions exist in Brazil, China, Indonesia and many other states. The European Union, by contrast, liberalised maritime cabotage among member states under a 1992 regulation.

Aviation cabotage

In aviation, cabotage is the carriage of passengers or cargo between two domestic points by a foreign airline. It is almost universally prohibited: the Chicago Convention framework and bilateral air service agreements grant "freedoms of the air" up to the seventh, while the eighth and ninth freedoms (consecutive and stand-alone cabotage) are rarely granted outside the European Union's single aviation market, where any EU carrier may operate domestic routes in any member state.

Road transport

Within the EU, hauliers may perform a limited number of domestic deliveries (cabotage operations) in another member state following an international delivery. Rules cap the number of such operations and the period in which they may occur, intended to prevent social dumping while allowing efficient use of capacity.
